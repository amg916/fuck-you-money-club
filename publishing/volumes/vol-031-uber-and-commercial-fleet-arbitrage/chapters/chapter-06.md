## Executive Summary

Operators running weekly gig-driver rentals converge on one formula: buy a paid-off $3,000 to $6,000 economy car (Camry, Corolla, Civic, Prius, Fusion), rent it at $350 to $450 per week paid upfront, and protect it with a hardwired tracker plus kill switch. Alex Auto, Kel King, and Yanni pitch a three-to-four-month payback on $1,600 monthly gross, while King of Rentals scaled to 300+ Teslas at a $425 average after Turo delisted its fleet. Etienne Elsaesser strips the influencer math down to about $700 net per car after $300 insurance, $200 maintenance, and a $200 total-loss reserve. The sharpest splits: RevHostHQ warns Uber demands approved-partner rentals or deactivation while private operators sell multi-platform access, Kel King's Marketplace offer skips deposits while Elsaesser requires them, and self-insuring collision leaves every total loss on the owner.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator in this lane starts from the same asset rule: buy cheap, buy cash, and never carry a car note. Kel King frames the difference bluntly: a $5,000 cash car carries no monthly payment, while a $30,000 to $40,000 financed unit has to cover its note before it earns a dollar [swveqE_MJY0 @ 00:01:10]. Alex Auto's step-by-step build insists the vehicle be bought cash rather than financed, even if that means liquidating a credit card or loan to pay the seller outright, specifically to avoid negative equity [Q9fRvJfTKk0 @ 00:04:05]. CAMTHECEO, running 27 cars at 23 years old after leaving Turo, caps purchases under $4,000 and still rents those cars at $350 to $450 a week [grHcYj2iy-c @ 00:06:01]. The model list barely changes between speakers. Etienne Elsaesser names the Camry, Corolla, Civic, Prius, and Fusion because they combine high MPG with cheap repairs [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:09:08], overlapping almost exactly with the Camry, Corolla, Accord, and Civic lineup Alex Auto runs and the Camry, Corolla, Fusion, and Malibu lineup Kel King runs. The logic is wear-driven: these renters treat the car as a work tool, so operators want a durable commodity sedan whose brakes, tires, and engine parts are cheap and available everywhere, never a financed asset sitting idle while depreciation and payments stack up.

The weekly rate is the most repeated number in the field. King of Rentals pegs the market at $350 to $450 a week for 2010 Priuses worth about $5,000 [zIGomHkXMms @ 00:00:59], and nearly every other operator lands in the same band. Etienne Elsaesser explains why gig drivers pay more than Turo guests: the driver is 'buying a tool' while the Turo guest is 'buying a weekend,' so price follows the renter's alternatives, not the car's sticker [l8b3Oi2g48I @ 00:02:38]. That tool framing drives retention. Kel King reports tourists leave gap weeks while gig drivers rent for months at a stretch [swveqE_MJY0 @ 00:02:26], and Alex Auto puts the average rental at 3.5 months, with $400-plus a week producing a $1,600 monthly minimum per car [Sukdr8-ecMM @ 00:05:14]. The renter pool is wider than Uber alone. Alex Auto points to Amazon Flex, Walmart, and FedEx last-mile work as added demand, and notes poor credit is common among applicants, which is exactly why these drivers rent instead of buy. Payment terms are consistent too: the first week is collected before the keys move, renewals run weekly and prepaid, and nobody in this group lets a car leave on a promise to pay later.

Four protections show up as non-negotiable across the board. First, hardware: Alex Auto states flatly, 'If you don't install your trackers and kill switches, you will lose your car,' and runs both a kill switch and a tracker such as Advantage GPS or GoldStar by Spireon on every unit [bHUp-OA4bH8 @ 00:08:40]. Second, screening that goes beyond the platform. Uber and Lyft already run background and MVR checks, but Alex Auto warns screenshots can be faked, so the operation still runs its own background check and license verification [UJnOivSethI @ 00:04:00]. Etienne Elsaesser tightens that further, requiring an active driver profile with 500 to 1,000 trips because 'new drivers are definitely a gamble' [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:04:36]. Third, paper. Alex Auto's rule is 'If you don't have a contract, do not rent out your cars,' because without one the owner wears the accidents, tickets, and tolls [Q9fRvJfTKk0 @ 00:02:30]. Etienne adds that the contract needs damage terms, payment terms, and a repossession clause, and that ChatGPT-drafted contracts do not cut it. Fourth, insurance, the one with the most friction: every operator agrees coverage has to be layered and placed deliberately, because the platform's commercial policy only applies while the driver is logged in.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Sourcing runs through Facebook Marketplace, but buying discipline is where operators separate. Kel King's preferred unit is the Ford Fusion with the 2.5-liter gas or 2.0 hybrid engine, avoiding the 1.5 EcoBoost and the 2013 model year entirely [5liDEdRcRhY @ 00:15:08]. Kel King caps mileage at 150,000 to 170,000, asks for the VIN, pulls a Carfax, opens with 'cash in hand,' and brings a mechanic for a $50 to $60 pre-purchase inspection found through YourMechanic or a Google search. CAMTHECEO cuts reconditioning costs at the junkyard, pulling rims plus tires for $250 [grHcYj2iy-c @ 00:03:00] and a replacement engine for $250 that cost about $700 installed [grHcYj2iy-c @ 00:05:36]. The couple William Boston profiles compressed the whole acquisition into two days: a 2015 Mazda 3 with 180,000 miles bought for $5,000, checked by a mobile mechanic, title collected from the seller, insured with a printed card, state-inspected, then titled, registered, and plated at a walk-up DMV fleet counter. A mobile key maker cut a same-day duplicate for about $150, and on day two the mechanic installed the tracker and kill switch before a Starbucks handoff, with photos and payment taken before the keys moved [jir3mVMJd64 @ 00:00:59].

Lead generation splits into a free lane and a paid lane, both with exact scripts. Kel King posts on Marketplace as an item, not a vehicle, titled 'cars for rideshare,' with the copy 'unlimited miles, insurance included, maintenance included, no credit check' plus a walk-around video, no deposit, and the first week paid upfront [5liDEdRcRhY @ 00:30:47]. King of Rentals runs Meta video ads carrying a promo code, captures leads on a landing page, and retargets with texts about five times a month [zIGomHkXMms @ 00:05:07]. Their highest-converting hook is a $350 first week that already bundles the deposit and insurance, which they credit with 'multiple seven figures' in revenue [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 01:02:52]. The same operators charge daily payers a 20% premium, so a $60 day becomes $72, pushing renters toward the weekly commitment. Collections are written into the agreement in plain terms. CAMTHECEO sets payment due Tuesday before 5 pm with a $50 late fee [grHcYj2iy-c @ 00:18:02], and uses small service-recovery gestures, like $50 off plus a full tank after a delayed handoff, to keep renters loyal and the waitlist of roughly six people warm for the next open car.

The real math comes from the operators who publish costs line by line. Etienne Elsaesser's insurance stack has the owner self-insuring the car, Romly supplying off-trip, liability-only coverage for street compliance, and Hugo writing on-trip third-party liability at about $10 a day in the renter's name, roughly $300 a month per car [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:02:30]. Starting from $1,600 gross, Etienne subtracts at least $300 for insurance, about $200 for maintenance, processing, cleaning, and dispute costs, and a $200 total-loss reserve, landing near $700 net per car per month; anyone pitching $1,600 of pure profit is, in Etienne's word, 'lying' [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:06:15]. King of Rentals measures the fleet by the mile instead: all-in cost of 14 to 16 cents a mile against revenue of 28 to 32 cents [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 01:30:07], with a 5,000-mile monthly cap, $0.30 a mile beyond it, and a fleet average of 3,600 miles a month. Their repossession clock is equally precise: a 24-hour grace period, then daily pay, 48 hours maximum for new renters, three to four days for long-tenured ones, then the kill switch and a retrieval team arriving by Uber or tow truck [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 01:19:18].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Source a cash car and inspect it
   - Buy a $3,000 to $6,000 economy sedan off Facebook Marketplace with cash.
   - Ask for the VIN, pull a Carfax, cap mileage at 150,000 to 170,000, avoid the Fusion 1.5 EcoBoost and the 2013 model year, and pay a mechanic $50 to $60 for a pre-purchase inspection [Social Proof — 5liDEdRcRhY @ 00:15:08].
2. Title, insure, key, and arm the car
   - Complete paperwork and hardware within two days.
   - Collect the title, insure and print the card, pass state inspection, register at a DMV fleet counter, cut a $150 same-day spare key, then have the mechanic install the tracker and kill switch on day two [William Boston — jir3mVMJd64 @ 00:00:59].
3. List the car and vet the renter
   - Post the rental and screen beyond the platform's own checks.
   - Require an active driver profile with 500 to 1,000 trips, earnings screenshots, identity verification, and your own background check, because new drivers are definitely a gamble [Etienne Elsaesser — amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:04:36].
4. Hand over in person and collect upfront
   - Meet face to face and take payment before the keys move.
   - Match the renter's face to the license, take payment on the spot, refuse contactless pickup, and keep oil changes on a 30 to 45 day cycle [Alex Auto — Q9fRvJfTKk0 @ 00:06:22].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over platform rules. RevHostHQ teaches that Uber requires rentals to run through an approved partner or the driver risks permanent deactivation, that Lyft only accepts rentals through its own program, and that the operator should confirm the car is an approved option in that city before a driver ever logs in [3jfvZ2LM8zM @ 00:00:36]. The private-fleet camp sells the opposite edge: Alex Auto pitches private rentals precisely because the Uber and Lyft programs lock a driver into a single platform and carry mileage caps [bHUp-OA4bH8 @ 00:04:11]. Etienne Elsaesser makes the same pricing argument from the renter's side, saying the weekly rate follows the driver's alternatives and that Enterprise only covers a single platform, so the rate is set against what else that driver can actually get [l8b3Oi2g48I @ 00:05:15]. Yanni of Whip It Whips takes a third route entirely, niching into Uber Eats, DoorDash, and Amazon drivers and deliberately steering away from Uber and Lyft drivers because those renters pile on too many miles [fqYaTfdZsQ8 @ 00:11:33]. An operator has to pick a lane: approved-partner compliance, multi-app private rentals priced against the alternatives, or delivery-only renters chosen to protect the odometer and stretch each cheap car's working life.

The profit math is the loudest fight on the circuit. Etienne Elsaesser starts from $1,600 a month per car and strips out at least $300 of insurance, about $200 of maintenance, processing, cleaning, and dispute costs, plus a $200 total-loss reserve, landing near $700 net and calling anyone who claims $1,600 of pure profit either lying or inexperienced [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:06:15]. Kell King of LA Elite Rentals, interviewed on Social Proof, tells the opposite story: over $50K a month in profit at roughly an 80% margin across a 72-car fleet, with the claim that a solo operator keeps 90 to 95% and can run about 12 cars alone [5liDEdRcRhY @ 00:08:30]. King of Rentals sits between them, reporting roughly $1,400 per car per month after insurance on a Tesla-heavy fleet [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 00:23:17]. RevHostHQ gives the widest band, saying a $400-a-week car leaves somewhere between $300 and $900 a month once insurance, maintenance, and a loss reserve come out [3jfvZ2LM8zM @ 00:00:17]. The gap comes down to what each operator books as a cost: Elsaesser charges every car at least $300 of insurance and a monthly total-loss reserve, while Kell King self-insures the vehicles and has renters carry their own liability through Bonzah or RentalCover, so one totaled car is what tests which number holds.

Capital is the next fault line. Alex Auto's one-car playbook insists the vehicle be bought outright for cash, even if that means liquidating a credit card or loan to pay the seller, specifically to avoid sitting on negative equity [Q9fRvJfTKk0 @ 00:04:05]. Yanni goes the other way with business funding on a 0% card carrying a 12 to 18 month promo: $15K to $20K buys about three $6K cars, which grow to about six within a year at $375 to $450 a week each [fqYaTfdZsQ8 @ 00:24:12]. Investor structures split too. Kell King dropped the old 60/40 car-split model after investors complained that accidents cut off their payouts, and now takes $6,000 and pays a fixed $700 a month for 15 months, roughly $10,500 back, a structure the host frames as essentially a loan with interest and Kell King agrees with [5liDEdRcRhY @ 00:02:22]. King of Rentals keeps the management model instead, collecting all gross revenue and deducting $400 a month or 30% of gross, dropping as low as $250 for large owners, with investors free of any lock-in contract while drivers sign a one to three month minimum [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 01:45:05]. Cash buyers trade speed for zero debt; leveraged and investor fleets trade debt or partner obligations for faster scale.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is the insurance gap. Etienne Elsaesser lays it out: platform commercial coverage only applies while the driver is logged in, on-trip third-party liability through Hugo only pays when the renter hits someone else, and a $4K car totaled in week one gets eaten by the owner because the deposit will never cover it, a blind spot most operators only learn the hard way [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:04:22]. RevHostHQ's 'Sam' scenario shows the same hole from the compliance side: a cash handshake with a driver risks deactivation and leaves the car uninsured, so the fix is an approved route, a card on file, a check of the driver's insurance, and a weekly inspection [3jfvZ2LM8zM @ 00:01:26]. Even Kell King, who self-insures and leans on a commercial liability policy for street compliance, concedes under questioning that the commercial policy is typically not going to pay out when an uninsured renter causes the loss [5liDEdRcRhY @ 00:44:12]. The Kel King channel adds hard lines of its own: never run these cars on a personal policy, pair GPS with a kill switch, and back every handoff with a signed agreement and a deposit while checking local rules such as California smog testing [swveqE_MJY0 @ 00:07:23].

Payment and recovery failures come next. Alex Auto warns that Uber and Lyft account screenshots can be faked, so even with the platforms already running background and MVR checks, that operation still runs its own background check and license verification rather than trusting the platform's screening alone [UJnOivSethI @ 00:04:00]. King of Rentals is blunter about money in motion: do not take a Zelle payment and take the renter's word for it, because 'you will lose 99% of the time' [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 01:09:58]. Recovery hardware is treated as mandatory rather than optional. Yanni, interviewed on darius benders after four years of renting cars to Uber drivers in Michigan, runs two trackers per car and sometimes three, each tied to a remote cut-off [NFLtPz5bUp4 @ 00:13:20]. Etienne Elsaesser specs hardwired units such as Move Tracks, Trackhawk, or Fleet Bolt with a kill switch, budgets a couple hundred dollars per device and install plus a monthly fee, and repossesses once a renter runs 24 to 48 hours late [amfsF8yd5hg @ 00:07:58]. Across all four operators, verification that rests on a screenshot, a payment-app promise, or a single tracker is exactly where cars and cash leak out of the fleet, so the controls get installed and enforced before the first week is ever collected.

The last checks are pure arithmetic. King of Rentals holds up 'The General,' an operator carrying $1M of debt against $750K of cars, as the over-leverage warning every scaling fleet should study, and pairs it with the rule to always have an exit plan when buying cars [qwQ8yQbhunw @ 00:58:19]. Alex Auto shows why financed cars bleed in this model: a $20K Camry driven 3,000 to 4,000 miles a month is worth under $10K after a single year [Sukdr8-ecMM @ 00:00:53]. Kel King's $5K car test shows how headline math drifts: the pitch frames $400 a week as $1,600 a month and about $19,000 a year, after flagging maintenance, gap weeks, insurance, and bad renters as real costs, yet the reveal lands on $2,220 at $74 a day [swveqE_MJY0 @ 00:08:52]. CAMTHECEO recovers downtime instead of absorbing it, filing loss-of-use claims at about $57 a day so a 30-day repair returns roughly $1,600, while keeping repair bills small with junkyard rims, tires, and a $250 engine [grHcYj2iy-c @ 00:14:58]. Before adding a car, an operator should know the debt-to-fleet ratio, the cost of every idle day, and the gap between the advertised weekly rate and what the car actually cleared.
