# Chapter 08: Co-Hosting Arbitrage: Scaling a Turo Fleet With Other People's Cars

*Co-hosting with zero cars or credit, recruiting passive car-owner investors, revenue splits, shipping partner cars in, the three partners needed to run a fleet, systems for managing 99 cars, lessons from 100–400+ car hosts, and co-hosting 101 Q&As*

## Executive Summary

Turo operators who scaled to 100-plus cars agree co-hosting is property management for cars: owners supply vehicles and pay expenses, the operator runs trips, claims, cleaning and guests, then splits revenue. Operator cuts run from Fleeteer's 15% fee and Delano Green's 30% to Max's tiered 35-45% and Demario Mccowan's 40%, with Brandon Bartron charging 30% of gross or $400, whichever is higher. Mccowan and Bartron insist the cut comes off gross, in a signed written agreement, never a handshake. The proof numbers are large: Green clears about $100k monthly profit near 99 cars, Alvin manages 90+ owner cars, and Peak Auto Academy shows a Yukon owner earning a 23% annualized return. The fights are over promising owners profit, which protection plan to run, and whether scale adds profit at all; Green warns more cars does not mean more profit.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who scaled past a handful of cars describes the same core trade. Max of King of Rentals calls it 'kind of like property management but for cars' [7IyNOkXwrsg @ 00:00:16]: the owner keeps title and insurance, the operator runs listings, guests, cleaning, claims and turnover. Jared Cook of Peak Auto Academy defines the deal as someone who owns a vehicle but wants you to manage it, and explains why supply keeps showing up: workload is the 'number one reason' hosts quit [3kqux9OEEfY @ 00:00:56]. Cook went from a first car in August 2025 to 10 by December while making airport runs up to three times a day. Demario Mccowan built a rental business with no cars and no credit, rejecting the '30 cars in 30 days' approach and partnering with exhausted hosts instead, including a Detroit owner whose car went to Vegas for the winter [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:00:50]. Mccowan's point is that co-hosting has no credit cap; cars came in from DC to San Diego. Delano Green proves the ceiling, reaching about 60 cars purely through co-hosting [xTVl-1-TkSI @ 00:04:02]. The shared prerequisite is simple: owners want hands-free income, and operators win by absorbing the labor that burns owners out.

The second point of agreement is sequencing and money flow. Aubrey Janik is blunt that it is 'irresponsible' to run someone else's car before operating your own [Yjwp4WMIjbc @ 00:03:51], and the operators who scaled backed that up with years of hosting first: Alvin logged 8,000+ five-star trips over 5+ years and started co-hosting with relatives before posting results in Turo groups [uOTjoNGAa7w @ 00:02:01]. On money, the owner carries the car. Max's contract keeps ownership and insurance with the owner and puts maintenance on the owner [7IyNOkXwrsg @ 00:05:42], and Alvin's owners pay all expenses, which are deducted, at a 65/35 split after Turo's 90-plan cut. Demario Mccowan adds the rule every split debate returns to: take your cut off gross, not net. Mccowan's math is $1,000 gross minus $200 maintenance leaving the operator $320 instead of $400, and Mccowan later admits being paid on net yielded only $200-300 per car [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:06:35]. Owners also keep the tax deductions because they pay the costs, which Mccowan and Car Rental Coach host George both use as a selling point. The baseline stack: real hosting reps, owner-held insurance, owner-paid maintenance, and an operator cut taken from the top line before expenses touch it.

The third consensus pillar is paper and honesty. Brandon Bartron, running 173 cars in Arizona, learned it by losing: an early 20% deal that gave away all the extras crashed under owner micromanagement, and Bartron quit co-hosting for 1.5-2 years. Bartron's rule now is that the financial breakdown 'needs to be very clear in a written and signed agreement do not do co-hosting with a handshake' [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:10:25], with no revenue projections and a warning to expect minor damage. West Coast Dave sets expectations up front, records separate exterior and interior intake videos, and signs a contract where the owner keeps insurance [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:11:39]. The 400-car Florida operator Delano Green met says no 'fairy tales' when setting owner expectations [xTVl-1-TkSI @ 00:12:36], and Rick of M2M Rentals in Memphis says to be transparent with good and bad news and treat every car as your own [cp3QlVZWrog @ 00:16:10]. Payout rhythm is part of the same trust stack: Rick pays by check or wire on the 3rd with a portal showing receipts, invoices and GPS, Bartron sends a check on the 15th with a breakdown, and Green's owners prefer monthly payouts over biweekly. Clean statements and blunt warnings are the retention tool every one of them leans on.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Fee structures are where the street numbers get specific. Max benchmarks the market at 35-50% of gross, with one operator at 70%, and flat fees around $400 per car per month; the King of Rentals tier sheet charges 45% for 1-3 cars, 40% for 4-10 and 35% for 10+, plus a setup fee covering detail, photos, tracker and listing [7IyNOkXwrsg @ 00:08:07]. Brandon Bartron charges '30% of the gross amount that turo pays or $400 whichever is higher' [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:11:29], keeps extras like smoking fees, and uses the $400 floor to screen out low earners, refusing a lineup of 20 Fiats. Bartron's break-even is $398 per car: $200 payroll plus $198 lease, lights and internet across 7 employees. Alvin in Jacksonville runs 65/35 after Turo's 90-plan cut with tiers up to 70 and 75, passes owners 65% of incidentals, and bills $50 a month parking and $15 a month washing per car [uOTjoNGAa7w @ 00:06:25]. At the low end, Fleeteer manages for 15% of rental earnings and advertises a minimum of up to $1,200 a month [NINtu6BJvFY @ 00:03:14]. Mccowan reads the market at 20-40% or a flat fee, while running 60/40 of gross with about $250 upfront.

Intake and turnover systems are what let one operator carry dozens of owner cars. Demario Mccowan shoots 60-80 photos at intake, runs two trackers (Advantage and Linder), approves repairs under $150 without asking the owner, and handles W-9s and 1099s [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:24:32]. Rick sets up the One Step tracker while the car is in transit, lists after inspection, and sees cars booked within 3-4 hours; each car gets a number like M109 matching its keybox plus oil-change and wash stickers, with a 3-hour turnaround [cp3QlVZWrog @ 00:12:03]. Rick's fee capture routes incidentals to an email watched by office staff, logs everything in Excel, and answers denials by quoting Turo's terms; a 2018 Rogue returned about 24 hours late with roughly 1,000 extra miles drew improper-return, late-return and extra-day charges. Delano Green built photo and video folders per car, SOP training videos for staff, and color-coded key tags, and lets the ops manager approve only what Green has approved before [ziuX0-egB7Y @ 00:03:53]. West Coast Dave says going contactless 'changed everything' and calls it 'a key component to scaling' [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:28:07], running Carsync and then Turo automated messages, no deliveries, and an Uber pickup option on luxury cars billed as the delivery fee.

Sourcing and seasonal arbitrage drive fleet growth without buying cars. Mccowan works a 4,000-member Facebook group holding roughly 400-800 hosts, posting results like 'new car, $1,400-1,800/mo' with no call to action and letting owners come in [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:12:40]. Delano Green runs the arbitrage hardest: partner cars listed at $300-400 a day earn about a year's revenue in roughly three months, then ship to the next market, while the same cars earn $80-90 a day elsewhere [PSopHdn3T8M @ 00:01:54]. Green cycles cars through Miami, Texas and New Jersey, signs a service agreement with dispatch and a bill of lading with the driver, and pulls plates into the trunk so tolls don't show up months later. Alvin taps the same dormancy, taking 10 cars from Virginia and 24 from Maryland because those markets sit dormant six months a year [uOTjoNGAa7w @ 00:02:51]. Brandon Bartron gets referrals from car salespeople whose customers dislike trade-in offers, with one salesperson sending 6-7 cars, and pitches owners on subsidizing the car payment and depreciation for taxes rather than profit [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:18:59]. Aubrey Janik's guest Alvin also works Facebook investing and automotive groups, Reddit, LinkedIn and accounting groups as lead channels.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Earn hosting reps before taking OPC
   - Operate your own cars and build trip volume and ratings before managing anyone else's vehicle.
   - Aubrey Janik calls running other people's cars first irresponsible [Aubrey Janik — Yjwp4WMIjbc @ 00:03:51], and Alvin tells owners to demand a co-host with a minimum of a thousand trips and a 5.0 rating [Car Sharing Jho — uOTjoNGAa7w @ 00:15:51].
2. Lock the split in a signed agreement
   - Set a gross-based percentage with a floor, list every fee in writing, and add clauses for accidents, maintenance and plates.
   - Bartron charges 30% of gross or $400, whichever is higher, with a flat 30% for a mid-month first month, and refuses handshake deals [King of Rentals — xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:11:29].
3. Document and tag the car at intake
   - Shoot full photo coverage, install trackers, set a repair approval limit, and number each car to its keybox.
   - Mccowan shoots 60-80 intake photos, runs two trackers and approves repairs under $150 without owner sign-off [Demario Mccowan — b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:24:32]; Rick numbers cars like M109 to match keyboxes [Car Rental Coach — cp3QlVZWrog @ 00:12:03].
4. Pay out monthly with full transparency
   - Send owner payouts on a fixed date with receipts, invoices and GPS access, and honor exit terms.
   - Rick pays by check or wire on the 3rd with a portal, Turo team-member access and GPS visibility [Car Rental Coach — cp3QlVZWrog @ 00:06:57]; Mccowan requires a 30-day exit notice with booked trips finishing [Demario Mccowan — b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:15:57].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The split itself is the first fault line, and operators land in very different places. Demario Mccowan runs 60/40 of gross and pegs the market at 20–40% or a flat fee, arguing the base matters more than the headline number: on $1,000 gross with $200 of maintenance, a net deal pays the co-host $320 instead of $400 [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:05:56]. Jared Cook at Peak Auto Academy goes the other way, splitting 70/30 of net with 70% to the owner, and sells it with a 2022 GMC Yukon that netted $9,672 in eight months and paid the owner $6,770 on $44,680 invested [3kqux9OEEfY @ 00:01:24]. Brandon Bartron of King of Rentals uses a floor instead of a pure ratio: 30% of the gross Turo payout or $400, whichever is higher, with smoking fees and other extras kept by the operator, and the floor rarely triggers unless an owner overprices the car [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:11:29]. Fleeteer undercuts the field, fully managing operations for 15% of rental earnings while advertising a minimum of up to $1,200 a month [NINtu6BJvFY @ 00:03:14]. For a new operator, the percentage means nothing until the base, the floor and who pays expenses are pinned down together.

Operators also split on whether to sell owners a number at all. Bartron gives no revenue projections and calls promising owners profit 'a lie'; the pitch is subsidizing the car payment and taking depreciation for taxes, with high earners in sales told to confirm with their accountant and to expect they may run negative cash flow yet end up with a paid-off asset [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:18:28]. Peak Auto Academy leans into the math instead, framing the Yukon owner's payout as 'a 23% return on your money' on an annualized basis and steering prospects to an earnings calculator on its site [3kqux9OEEfY @ 00:02:47]. Fleeteer pushes the comparison hardest: about $11k of client cars producing roughly $1,800 a month versus the $200k rental property needed for the same income, backed by guarantees of up to $1,200 a month and up to 100% of the investment protected [NINtu6BJvFY @ 00:03:28]. Demario Mccowan splits the difference, quoting figures but never random ones, and only from comparable market data pulled before the owner call [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:35:53]. Bartron's benefits-first pitch and the yield pitch from Peak and Fleeteer are opposite bets on what an owner should be promised before handing over the keys.

Protection plans and claims handling produce the sharpest operator-to-operator contrast. West Coast Dave, on Aubrey Janik's channel, keeps owned cars on the 90 plan but puts investor cars on the 75 plan as the conservative choice, and left the 80 plan with its $750 deductible after running the math, gaining about $1,500 a month [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:20:02]. Alvin in Jacksonville prefers the 90 plan despite its $2,500 deductible and works every claim: third-party diminished value and loss-of-use within 20 days, renters pushed onto their own insurance, and a cheaper off-Turo fix such as $600 offered up front, with about 60% success. On an $8,000 estimate, Turo pays $5,500 and Alvin's repair runs about $3,500 [uOTjoNGAa7w @ 00:10:58]. Totals divide the camps further. Dave reports that all three totaled cars made money because they were bought below market, and that claim profits flow into the investor split [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:34:34]. Aubrey Janik pushes back in the same conversation, now preferring to keep a car earning over collecting a total [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:34:48]. Whether a total counts as a win depends on purchase price, which is why Dave buys below market and Alvin buys only on a good deal.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest failures start with the deal terms and the owner. Bartron's first co-hosting run charged only 20%, handed every extra to the owners, never warned them against listing heirloom cars and let them micromanage, so the arrangement 'crashed' [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:00:16]. Bartron then stopped co-hosting for about 1.5 to 2 years [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:00:54], and the rebuilt version puts every fee in writing after about 15 cars once ran on a handshake. West Coast Dave refuses hands-on investors outright after a $1.7M house flip with nine voting investors meant Zoom calls 'every other day' [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:13:33]; Dave's intake now sets expectations up front, records separate exterior and interior videos, and signs a contract with the owner keeping insurance. Demario Mccowan's loss was in the pay base: being paid on net yielded only $200–300 per car, which pushed the operation to a gross split [b4HG8nxaQlU @ 00:34:52]. Across all three, the co-host who underprices, accepts sentimental or controlling owners, or lets expenses come off the top before the fee ends up running a fleet for almost nothing. Bartron also tells owners up front to expect minor damage and refuses to give revenue projections at signing.

Operational bottlenecks hit well before a fleet looks big. RC Simon's team warns that past five cars this cannot run alongside an inflexible job; their 24-car Las Vegas fleet already runs check-ins or checkouts six days a week with work split between an operator, a mechanic and a visionary partner [E1lFWd_WIy8 @ 00:02:59]. Delano Green found 25–30 cars the hardest stage, before SOPs, KPIs and a team made near-100 lighter [PSopHdn3T8M @ 00:03:07]. At 99 cars on Martha's Vineyard the breakpoints were still brutal: an airport ban under threat of towing, with Uber and shuttle workarounds failing because the fleet ran out of cars [SPoQzQlFhXg @ 00:00:36], co-hosted owners able to watch guests go unanswered, and hotel and Airbnb drop-offs that stacked 15 cars due at 10 a.m. without 15 staff [SPoQzQlFhXg @ 00:03:27]. Cleaning quality slipped without SOPs once kids were cleaning and older staff were delivering, Uber refunds cut into profit even though many cancelled guests rebooked the same fleet, and Delano's summary is that 'even the overseer needs overseeing.' Owners see customer-facing gaps first, so every handoff, message and cleaning standard needs a written system before the next batch of partner cars arrives.

Car selection is where the economics quietly break. The 400-car host Delano Green met warns off a 2016 Camry that makes $600 and leaves $200 in pocket, and flags that photos uploaded at 26 hours are not covered [xTVl-1-TkSI @ 00:14:28]. West Coast Dave caps intake at 75,000 miles or less after a high-mileage car threw oil issues every other week, and holds off on supercars, citing Turo's $200,000 cap and the need for commercial insurance [qSzMy86MT7I @ 00:14:28]. Bartron's $40K Sprinter earned $199–250 a day and then slowed as the market saturated [xCgsQFz-kOM @ 00:15:49], and a 6 Series BMW on its second engine swap just cost another $5K. Philly Car Renting hit the same wall with a Range Rover that needed a $10k engine, while the Tesla held up fine [1amDBwhhsY0 @ 00:15:36]. The economics check that comes out of these losses is Bartron's: track a P&L for every car, weigh return against depreciation on color-coded sheets, keep a $400 floor that screens out low earners, and cut the bottom 10% of the fleet to reinvest in the top 10%. A Turo rep's warning to Delano that co-hosting is 'getting super saturated' makes that discipline more urgent.
