## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every price-reader in this lineup starts from the same lever: inventory on the dealer lot, not headline inflation, sets the auction number. Machinery Pete (Greg Peterson) told RealAgriculture that 2022 auction pricing climbed by the month, then by the week, then by the day, and pinned it on used supply at historic lows, with five-year-old high-horse tractors listed for sale down 79 percent versus 2019 [DSk-SojWGxU @ 00:04:00]. The same mechanism ran in reverse on late-model combines: Pete showed the dealer-lot supply line spiking from about December 2022, exactly when one-to-four-year-old combine bids started easing [8dTCmixzqP0 @ 00:01:45]. Nick Mitchell, who sells equipment across the country at auction, reads the identical signal from the other side, reporting that 100-plus-horsepower tractors saw supply drop in September while prices at dealers and auctions rose at least 0.53 percent [zK9IyH3CceE @ 00:00:14]. Patrick Shivers, a Georgia row-crop farmer working the bankruptcy auction circuit, saw the flood version: a third straight year of negative margins in the Southeast, banks unable to keep lending money that does not come back, equipment flooding the market, and buyers with no cash, so prices set what Shivers called all-time-record lows [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:13:07]. Same rule, opposite tide.

The second point of agreement: condition and hours decide which units escape the tide. Pete has repeated for five years that everybody wants a low-hour pre-DEF, pre-Tier 4 tractor, and the Bill McGrew retirement sale in Keymar, Maryland proved it when a 1998 John Deere 7810 with 914 hours brought $170,000, smashing the prior $137,600 record [Rz6KNtveaDU @ 00:16:33]. Shivers lands in the same place from the operator seat, calling DEF-and-regen tractors a hard sale because they are not as reliable regardless of manufacturer, and saying washed, barn-kept equipment brings a lot more when it goes to sale [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:06:34]. Luke Fritshaw, Ritchie Bros. sales director for the Canadian prairies, told Pete that solid work orders, documented history and remaining warranty add value that is hard to peg but real, because today's buyers are selective [3oDXLUOs8DU @ 00:06:12]. Pete's late-model example backs that up: a 24-model Case IH Steiger 645 Quadtrac with 781 hours sold October 15, 2026 in Hazlet, Saskatchewan for 710,000 Canadian, roughly $499,769 US, above a 430-hour twin that brought $493,595 a year earlier [RtJspDtsC2M @ 00:00:50]. More hours, higher price, because best-condition iron is what fleet planners are chasing.

The third shared habit is reading the sale bill before reading the price. Shivers' rule for the Southeast: late-model tractors at auction usually mean bankruptcy, because healthy operators trade new iron in on newer iron at the dealer, while old classic tractors at auction usually mean someone upgrading or clearing extra equipment [vxI956OLj9E @ 00:00:22]. Pete flagged the lender-driven version years earlier, reporting a 66 percent jump in machinery auctions in July and warning that ag lenders would push farm restructuring sales loaded with late-model equipment [F80Wku6kGE0 @ 00:00:47]. Through May 7, 2026, Pete counted machinery auctions up 19.5 percent year over year while one-to-three-year-old units sold at auction fell 19.9 percent, because dealers paying 8 percent interest dumped that age class to auction in 2024 and ate what Pete called astounding losses [t7mTa6TooFk @ 00:02:04]. Retirement sales are their own category: Pete and Fritshaw described Ritchie Bros. running roughly 80 farm and construction sales from mid-March through April, two to five a day, built around farmers retiring after a lifetime of farming [3oDXLUOs8DU @ 00:00:30]. Knowing which kind of seller is behind a lot tells you whether the bid reflects distress, rotation or a curated lifetime fleet.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Pete's pricing method is comp-stacking from sold auction data, filtered by hours. On Machinery Pete TV the free auction-price search was used to pull every John Deere 8345R sold between 4,000 and 5,500 hours: an average of $159,344, a high of $191,000 and a low of $127,250, against a 4,755-hour unit crossing the block in North Carolina [_yl0gY1cWj8 @ 00:15:05]. When a farmer emailed asking whether $50,000 was fair for a Case IH 7140 front-wheel-assist with 3,500 hours, Pete answered by pulling a 1988 7130 with 6,260 hours that brought $45,000 two weeks earlier in east central Nebraska, plus 7140s at $50,000 and up, including a 1987 unit in southeast North Dakota [XbQmSseBtI8 @ 00:02:19]. Deep history matters: Pete has compiled more than 3,500 sale prices on the John Deere 4430 alone, showing a 2010-to-2020 average stuck between roughly $10,400 and just under $13,000 before jumping to a current average of $15,147 and a $36,000 high [wuBUPvkp-d8 @ 00:01:15]. Nick Mitchell layers trend data on top, tracking both dealer asking values and auction prices month over month and year over year, so a seller can see used semi-trailers up 7 to 14 percent on the year while supply sank 35 percent [zK9IyH3CceE @ 00:02:04].

On the sale lot, Shivers reads iron like a forensic examiner. A John Deere 4640 showing 3,000 hours is almost certainly a 13,000- or 23,000-hour tractor because that meter lacks the extra digit, and spotless pressure-washed sides with hydraulic oil still sitting on top and two fresh drip stripes on the pad told Shivers a transmission or pump failure had been hosed off before the sale [vxI956OLj9E @ 00:05:14]. A peanut picker still holding last season's crop, rusted, chains off and cogs on the ground, signals a machine that broke mid-season, even though sellers sometimes leave peanuts in the head on purpose so it looks freshly run [vxI956OLj9E @ 00:12:20]. On late-model Deere R-series units Shivers prices in sensor headaches: a lockout costs a $500 dealer laptop visit or a $120 Deere sensor that Shivers has seen online for $9 to $12 [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:04:42]. Pat McGrew, who runs Key Machinery in Keymar, described the family discipline behind a record-setting lineup: inspect everything, ask owners which series need the least work, and keep only those [Rz6KNtveaDU @ 00:18:30]. Shivers reads the reverse signal too: paper towels and Armor All in a combine cab mark an owner who took pride in the machine.

Timing and age band are the last two dials. Pete's Case IH Magnum 250 sequence shows the swing: a 121-hour 2016 unit brought $155,000 in August 2018, a 496-hour twin brought $130,000 in August 2020, and a 1,929-hour unit sold for $194,250 on February 18, 2023, leading Pete to say the time to be aggressive is when other folks are pulling back [_yl0gY1cWj8 @ 00:18:48]. On age band, Pete told Fritshaw the data shouts three-to-seven-year-old large equipment with low hours, and Fritshaw added that a three-year-old unit that cost about a million dollars new can be bought at auction in the $500,000 range [3oDXLUOs8DU @ 00:06:42]. For reading direction, Pete treats four-wheel-drive and over-200-horsepower tractors as the bellwether, and says to watch your dealer lot for new planters or combines piling up after two years of empty space [DSk-SojWGxU @ 00:15:16]. Ceiling math shows how fast tops move: the highest auction sprayer price went from $115,000 in 2004 to $250,000 in 2014 to $484,000 on a 23-model John Deere 612R with 23 hours, which Pete extrapolates, at almost double every ten years, to about a million dollars by 2034 [obq_-8IhG18 @ 00:01:15].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Classify the Seller
   - Identify whether the sale is bankruptcy, lender restructuring, dealer dump, upgrade or retirement before looking at prices.
   - Late-model iron at a Southern auction usually means bankruptcy while old classics mean upgrading [PatrickShivers — vxI956OLj9E @ 00:00:22]; lender-pushed restructuring sales carry heavy late-model equipment [Machinery Pete — F80Wku6kGE0 @ 00:01:06].
2. Pull Hour-Banded Comps
   - Search sold auction prices for the exact model inside a tight hour range and note average, high and low.
   - The 8345R pull at 4,000-5,500 hours returned a $159,344 average, $191,000 high and $127,250 low [Farm Journal — _yl0gY1cWj8 @ 00:14:49].
3. Run the Lot-Side Tell Check
   - Verify hour-meter digits, look above washed surfaces for oil, and check for last season's crop left in the machine.
   - Pressure-washed sides with oil on top and fresh drip stripes signaled a hidden transmission or pump failure [PatrickShivers — vxI956OLj9E @ 00:05:14].
4. Read Supply Direction, Then Set the Bid
   - Check dealer-lot supply against search demand and watch the lot for new planters or combines piling up before committing.
   - Combine bids softened right as dealer-lot supply spiked in December 2022 [Machinery Pete — 8dTCmixzqP0 @ 00:01:45]; Pete watches lots for new planters and combines that have not been there in two years [RealAgriculture — DSk-SojWGxU @ 00:15:16].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split on the auction floor is whether used iron is crashing or climbing. PatrickShivers walked a Georgia pre-harvest bankruptcy sale and called the prices "bottom basement, as low as it gets, setting all-time records," tying the collapse to a third straight year of negative margins in the Southeast [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:13:07]. That read produced a blunt buy signal: "there's never been a time in the history of the world better than right now to buy used farm machinery" [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:00:37]. Machinery Pete, working national auction data, reports almost the opposite for good condition units: values held and climbed slightly from the very end of 2025, even as auction counts rose [t7mTa6TooFk @ 00:01:12]. Pete's proof point is a 24-model Case IH Steiger 645 Quadtrac with 781 hours that brought 710,000 Canadian, or $499,769 US, in Hazlet, Saskatchewan on October 15, 2026, against $493,595 a year earlier for a 430-hour twin [RtJspDtsC2M @ 00:00:30]. The operator reconciliation sits inside both accounts. Shivers is pricing distressed row-crop dispersals in a region where farmers have no cash, while Pete is pricing the best-condition late-model and pre-DEF iron that buyers chase because few new tractors have moved off dealer lots. Price the condition tier and the regional seller pool, not the headline mood.

Volume is the second fault line. Textbook logic says more auctions mean softer prices, and Machinery Pete calls that out directly: through May 7, 2026, machinery auctions ran 19.5% above the prior-year period, yet good used values did not break [t7mTa6TooFk @ 00:00:53]. The explanation is age mix. One- to three-year-old units sold at auction fell 19.9% over the same window because dealers had already jammed that inventory out to sale in 2024 [t7mTa6TooFk @ 00:01:29]. Pete saw the same unhooking in late 2022, when retirement and downsizing sales shot auction counts up and prices still refused to drop [DSk-SojWGxU @ 00:21:29]. The counter-playbook comes from Pete's own earlier warning, which named two factors that could move values: a July jump of over 66% in auction counts, with lender-pushed restructuring sales loaded with late-model equipment, and a wave of John Deere Financial and Case Credit lease returns hitting the market [F80Wku6kGE0 @ 00:00:47]. Pete calls the current stretch unprecedented across 36 and a half years of tracking. The difference between those readings is what actually crosses the block. Count the auctions, then count the late-model units inside them; a surge of auctions stocked with older dispersal iron is not the same supply shock as a surge of lender-forced and lease-return late models.

The buy window itself is contested. Luke Fritshaw of Ritchie Bros sees the most pressure and the deepest inventory in the 7-to-15-year-old bracket, with a bottleneck at 3 to 5 years old where farmers ask whether the move is really an upgrade, while Machinery Pete counters in the same conversation that the data shouts the 3-to-7-year range on large equipment, where a sharp, low-hour unit stays dynamite out to 10, 12 or 15 years old [3oDXLUOs8DU @ 00:02:47]. On timing, Pete has run two playbooks. In early 2021 the instruction was "whatever you need just get it, don't worry about what it costs," and a buyer who did could use the iron two years and sell at a profit [DSk-SojWGxU @ 00:14:09]. By 2023 the line was contrarian: a 2016 Case IH Magnum 250 brought $155,000 in a flat 2018 market, $130,000 in August 2020, then $194,250 with 1,929 hours in February 2023, so "when other folks are pulling back that is the time to be aggressive" [_yl0gY1cWj8 @ 00:19:10]. Nick Mitchell runs a seller-side calendar instead, telling planter owners to hold off through a quiet stretch and come back closer to the busier December buying season [zK9IyH3CceE @ 00:04:37].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest mistakes happen at the walkaround, and PatrickShivers catalogs them from a single Georgia sale. A John Deere 4640 showing 3,000 hours was really a 13,000- or more likely 23,000-hour tractor, because that hour meter has no extra digit, and a freshly rattle-canned 4430 showing 3,400 hours drew the same verdict: "that's 13,000 hours" [vxI956OLj9E @ 00:03:21]. The same 4640 had sparkling, pressure-washed sides under a top still coated in hydraulic oil, with two fresh drip stripes where it parked, and Shivers guessed the transmission likely doesn't work [vxI956OLj9E @ 00:05:14]. A rusted peanut picker still holding last season's crop, chain off and cogs on the ground, read as a machine whose season ended early, and Shivers flags the seller trick that mimics it: leaving a few peanuts in the head after harvest so bidders assume the picker just ran. The identity of the seller matters as much as the iron: newer tractors at a Southern auction usually signal bankruptcy, while classic tractors usually signal an upgrade [vxI956OLj9E @ 00:00:22]. Pat McGrew's family beat these traps by inspecting every purchase and asking owners which tractors they had to work on the least, then keeping only those proven series [Rz6KNtveaDU @ 00:18:30].

Modern electronics are the next economics check. PatrickShivers reports that John Deere R-series tractors, 8R or 7R, are "fraught with sensor issues" in the experience of every farmer Shivers knows: a sensor fault locks the tractor down where it sits, the dealership charges $500 per laptop visit, and the replacement sensor costs $120 from Deere versus $9 to $12 online in identical packaging [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:04:42]. Shivers treats DEF and regen as a hard sale on any brand, "not as reliable period," and warns that triple-screen tech that has banged through irrigation ruts for 15 years "is not so good" [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:11:02]. Front-dual spacers help traction behind a moldboard plow but prematurely wear upper A-arms and ball joints on independent front suspension. That aversion is priced into the market Machinery Pete tracks: buyers chase low-hour pre-DEF, pre-Tier 4 units, and a 2004 John Deere 7320 with 2,434 hours hammered at $82,500 against a prior no-loader record of $75,250 [Rz6KNtveaDU @ 00:04:29]. Luke Fritshaw adds the paperwork side: solid work orders, service history and warranty make buyers willing to spend more, a hard value to peg but a real one [3oDXLUOs8DU @ 00:06:12].

The big-money losses in this cycle were taken by people who misread it. In 2024, dealers carrying too much 1-to-3-year-old used inventory at 8% interest made the biggest collective push Machinery Pete has seen in 36 and a half years, jamming that iron to auction and suffering "astounding financial losses" [t7mTa6TooFk @ 00:02:19]. The farmer-side trap is the mirror image: waiting for a 20 to 22% drop because that is what happened in 2014 and 2015. Pete argues that consolidated dealer chains of 20, 30 and 50-plus stores now move used inventory programmatically and that manufacturers will defend their pricing power, so "if you don't like paying that triple premium now for good used equipment, I really don't think you're going to like paying a premium when times are bad" [DSk-SojWGxU @ 00:11:03]. The cash squeeze at the bottom is real: PatrickShivers relays a regional bankruptcy lawyer filing at least one farm bankruptcy a week from January to August, and a buyer pool so broke that beef cattle operators riding record prices are the ones scooping up cheap tractors [QI2WPiqDHqE @ 00:15:27]. Nick Mitchell adds that used medium-duty trucks are a poor sell right now, with asking values falling [zK9IyH3CceE @ 00:00:45].
