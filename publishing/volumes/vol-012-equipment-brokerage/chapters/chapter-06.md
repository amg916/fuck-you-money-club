## Executive Summary

Equipment finance practitioners agree the money is behind the seller, not in front of the borrower: Jermane Cheathem, Buzz Glover, and Equipment Finance Academy all source deals through equipment vendors, CPAs, and bankers who already hold a trusting buyer, then match the file to a lender who can resell the collateral. Street numbers run wide: 4% to 6% spreads paying $12,500 to $20,000 on heavy iron, medical, and vocational fleets; Cheathem's claim of up to 20 points; and the Academy's more grounded $2,000 to $5,000 per funded deal, illustrated by a $4,200 first-deal commission on a $180,000 excavator. Glover's workflow adds hard rules: credit-app-only up to $75,000, one monthly payment as a performance deposit, and phone-first lender pitches. Disagreements center on commission size, application-only ceilings from $75,000 to $350,000, and credit floors from 580 to 650.

## The Practitioner Consensus: Sit Behind the Seller, Lend Against Resellable Iron

The loudest point of agreement is where deals come from. Jermane Cheathem says most brokers build backwards by chasing business owners with bad credit who are already shopping themselves around, so Cheathem built the whole book on equipment sellers who already hold a buyer that trusts them and has picked the machine [6laX1_643JA @ 00:00:16]. Cheathem frames it as working the finance department of the car lot instead of the sales floor: the buyer already picked the color, the features, and the bells and whistles, so financing is just a formality, and the partner does 90% of the heavy lifting for free [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:08:19]. Buzz Glover of BizLoanConsultants.com teaches the same annuity approach, naming equipment sales reps as the main line, with bankers declining deals, CPAs, and attorneys as secondary feeders, and noting the vendor has already endorsed the broker before the first call [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:03:06]. Equipment Finance Academy widens the bench to accountants, bookkeepers, commercial realtors, equipment vendors, and business brokers, and insists a referral is a relationship, not a lead: treat it like a lead and the second one never arrives [YIQEV-r00oA @ 00:03:00]. Recruit five partners who genuinely understand the offer first, then use that case study to land the next fifty.

Every camp agrees the asset is what makes this vertical approvable. Cheathem says banks and underwriters love equipment deals because the customer has to have the machine to operate, so the note gets paid and submissions get approved; that appetite is the reason to deal direct with underwriters instead of chopping a commission four, five, or six ways through other brokers [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:05:09]. Equipment Finance Academy says lenders actually prefer high-ticket heavy iron because rock crushers, wheel loaders, and excavators hold resale value and carry a liquid secondary market at equipment auctions if the borrower defaults [uzLIopNJDJY @ 00:01:44]. Joe Camberato lays out the plumbing: the lender buys the machine straight from a legitimate, established vendor, leases it back to the operator, and takes it back on default, with the lien sitting against the equipment rather than the whole business [H6xXORaU7Jo @ 00:01:12]. Demand is not in dispute either. The Academy cites $1.51 trillion in equipment financed in 2025 and 87% of companies financing their equipment, almost nine out of ten, and reads that as demand already sitting there: owners are already hunting for a way to pay for trucks, machines, and shop equipment without draining their bank accounts [p-1FbIYoOCs @ 00:01:36].

The practitioners also agree on who sits across the table. Mehmi Financial Group maps four players: conservative banks that prefer established businesses, captives like John Deere Financial and Caterpillar Financial owned by the manufacturers, more flexible independents, and brokers acting as matchmakers; it adds that bank approval rates are dropping while independent approval rates have jumped [BStMEmq3lwU @ 00:03:20]. NerdWallet's Karrin draws the same gradient: online lenders take bad credit and startups but charge higher rates on shorter terms, banks and credit unions give the best rates with the strictest requirements, and SBA lenders want a 650 personal score, two years in business, and strong revenue in exchange for terms up to 10 years [yAx4SRG_bfs @ 00:01:27]. That gap is the broker's opening: the Academy says when buyers fail the manufacturer's captive financing, the dealer needs a reliable broker on speed dial [uzLIopNJDJY @ 00:02:05]. The baseline prerequisite Cheathem stresses is focus. The riches are in the niches; a broker doing real estate, SBA, working capital, bridge, and equipment loans all at once never gets picked from anyone's Rolodex, so Cheathem chose equipment finance alone because it is essential, evergreen, and a trillion-dollar industry [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:02:45].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: From Referral Call to Broker Invoice

Pricing starts with the spread. Equipment Finance Academy runs the high-ticket math: a $350,000 Caterpillar excavator package at a standard 4% to 5% broker spread pays $14,000 to $17,500, a $250,000 multi-platform laser system for a med spa at 5% to 6% pays $12,500 to $15,000, and a two-unit $500,000 hydrovac or concrete-pump fleet at a conservative 4% pays $20,000 [uzLIopNJDJY @ 00:01:24]. Cheathem pushes harder, saying equipment deals let a broker build up to 20 points into the deal, so a $100,000 transaction carries a $20,000 commission, and preferring ten of those over a hundred one-to-five-point deals in other financial products [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:06:16]. The grounded numbers come from the same Academy: Sam's own funded deals typically paid $2,000 to $5,000, and Michael Chen's $4,200 on a $180,000 excavator, a first deal with zero finance background, sits inside that range [p-1FbIYoOCs @ 00:04:07]. ION Capital adds the lever behind those spreads: its network carries both low-rate and high-rate lenders, and on higher-interest paper the broker gets paid a little better because a few more points can be added; Ryan floats a rate around 11%, with single digits possible on low-risk files [WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:03:51].

Buzz Glover's deal flow is the most granular playbook. The referral source calls, the broker phones the borrower, then emails a proposal quoting 36-, 48-, and 60-month payments alongside a credit application [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:06:01]. Up to $75,000 the credit app is typically the entire package; above that, Glover asks for the last three years of tax returns or audited financials on the first email. Glover follows up by the end of the next day rather than waiting four days, then collects the signed proposal plus one monthly payment as a performance deposit, which takes the deal off the street so the borrower cannot keep shopping it [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:09:33]. Next comes the lender write-up: cash price, term, company age, what the machine does for margins or sales, and any bankruptcy disclosed with the story of how the borrower recovered. Glover then phones funding sources with bullet points only, withholding the company name so nobody poaches the deal, and emails the package only after a lender says it fits, because bankers ignore unsolicited deals [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:15:12]. Application-only ceilings vary by lender: Joe Camberato cites programs that lease equipment up to roughly $100,000 to $150,000 off a simple application with about a 620 score [H6xXORaU7Jo @ 00:03:29].

The back half is paperwork and communication. Bigger packages go through Dropbox or another file-sharing link because many email services cap attachments around 10 megabytes; deals at $75,000 or less usually get an answer within a day or two, and a decline sends the broker back down the funding-source database to pitch the next lender [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:16:47]. After approval, the lender prepares documents or opens an online portal, the broker gets them signed and submits a broker invoice for the commission minus the performance deposit already collected, so a $500 deposit comes off the invoice; the funder runs a telephone verification, pays the vendor and the broker, and the referral partner gets a thank-you lunch or dinner [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:20:15]. Equipment Finance Academy pairs that workflow with its Equipment Finance Broker Mastery course and a broker CRM: lender directory, calculators, a deal pipeline, a borrower intake link, one-click lender submission, per-deal document checklists, an AI lead finder, and portfolio and renewals follow-up tools [p-1FbIYoOCs @ 00:05:27]. ION Capital's intake is lighter still: an intake form, an asset list, three months of business bank statements, and equipment invoices, treated like any business-funding file [WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:00:42].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the referral and send the proposal
   - Call the borrower the referral source handed you, confirm the real request, then email a proposal with 36-, 48-, and 60-month payments plus a credit application.
   - Ask outright whether the client wants financing to purchase equipment or wants to borrow against equipment already owned, so the deal does not go down a rabbit hole [ION Capital - Loan Broker University — WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:01:46]; then send the proposal and credit app, adding tax returns for deals over $75,000 [BizLoanConsultants.com — kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:06:01]
2. Lock commitment with a performance deposit
   - Follow up by the end of the next day, collect the signed proposal and credit app, take one monthly payment as a deposit, and update the referral source.
   - Get one monthly payment as a performance deposit once the borrower agrees to the quoted payment; it takes the deal off the street and was the gap that cost early deals to other brokers [BizLoanConsultants.com — kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:09:59]
3. Write it up and shop it by phone first
   - Build a write-up covering cash price, term, company age, what the machine earns, and any past bankruptcy, then phone lenders before emailing.
   - Call funding sources with bullet points only, email the write-up only after interest, use a Dropbox link for packages too big for 10MB email limits, and loop back to the next lender on any decline [BizLoanConsultants.com — kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:16:47]
4. Sign docs, invoice, and thank the partner
   - Get lender documents signed, submit the broker invoice net of the deposit, clear the telephone verification, and reward the referral source.
   - Invoice the remaining commission minus the performance deposit, e.g. less a $500 deposit, then take the referral partner to lunch or dinner sized to the commission [BizLoanConsultants.com — kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:20:15]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Jermane Cheathem runs a seller-only funnel, calling the standard approach of chasing business owners 'completely backwards,' because those borrowers often carry bad credit and are 'probably already shopping them around to different lenders,' and instead simply becoming the financing resource for equipment sellers who already have the buyer, the trust, and often the clean credit [6laX1_643JA @ 00:00:16]. Buzz Glover of BizLoanConsultants.com agrees equipment sellers are the main line, but widens the net to bankers who are turning down deals, CPAs, and attorneys, and names sellers of computers, office furniture, CNC machinery, mining equipment, and solar energy as targets [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:03:06]. Equipment Finance Academy pushes a broader roster again: accountants, CPAs, bookkeepers, commercial realtors, equipment vendors, and business brokers, starting with five partners rather than 100 prospects [YIQEV-r00oA @ 00:03:00]. The same channel also runs a direct-hunting play nobody else here teaches: pull local public municipal contract awards and infrastructure bids to find vocational contractors who just won major projects, then structure hydrovac or concrete pump truck fleet deals for them [uzLIopNJDJY @ 00:04:17]. The split is clean: Cheathem refuses to create demand and sits only inside existing dealer demand, while Equipment Finance Academy will go find demand through public bid records.

On pricing, the spread between practitioners is wide. Cheathem says equipment deals are lucrative enough that 'you can build up to 20 points or 20% as your commission,' turning a $100,000 deal into a $20,000 payday, and Cheathem would 'much rather do 10 deals than do 100 deals' for the same money [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:06:16]. Equipment Finance Academy models far thinner spreads on bigger tickets: a standard 4% to 5% on a $350,000 Caterpillar excavator package for $14,000 to $17,500, and 5 to 6% on a $250,000 multi-platform laser system for $12,500 to $15,000 [uzLIopNJDJY @ 00:01:24]. The Academy's own track record is lower still. Sam's commissions 'typically ran $2,000 to $5,000 per funded deal,' and student Michael Chen collected $4,200 on a $180,000 excavator, a figure Sam places inside that range [p-1FbIYoOCs @ 00:04:07]. ION Capital's Ryan frames margin as lender-dependent rather than fixed: higher-interest lenders pay brokers 'a little bit better' because 'we can add a few more points to it,' while low-risk lenders deliver single-digit rates for clean files [WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:03:51]. The operator takeaway is that 20 points, 4% to 6%, and a $2,000 to $5,000 band are three different businesses; your realized spread depends on ticket size, lender tier, and who brought the deal.

Lender access is the sharpest philosophical split. Cheathem says equipment underwriters love these deals so much that 'you don't have to work through Brokers,' and calls broker-to-broker chains 'bottom of the barrel' because the commission gets chopped 'four or five six ways' [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:05:09]. ION Capital sells the opposite model: a 'very large and vast' nationwide network spanning very high-risk and very low-risk lenders, plus the boast that setting up an ISO agreement with a new lender is easy because 'no lenders will say no to us' [DSEdy5KFvBs @ 00:14:16]. Buzz Glover sits between them, building a database of national, local, and regional sources but feeding one to three banks first looks on everything, knowing more than half might get declined, and in exchange those banks act as a back room that handles much of Glover's credit work [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:13:44]. Paperwork thresholds clash too. Glover treats $75,000 as the application-only ceiling, with three years of tax returns or audited financials above it, while Equipment Finance Academy advertises application-only up to $350,000, decisions in as fast as 4 hours, rates from 6.99%, and terms up to 84 months [Ua7mCs7DMsw @ 00:01:47]. Brokers quoting borrowers on documentation need to know which lender tier they are actually submitting into before promising a one-page process.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Buzz Glover owns the biggest early mistake: 'the first couple years of me owning my brokerage I did not get performance deposits,' so the borrower would take all that work and 'find somebody else and go through them' [kOdpTliR_HM @ 00:09:13]. Glover's fix is one monthly payment collected as a performance deposit once the borrower signs the proposal and returns the credit application, which 'takes the deal off the street,' with a warning that raising the payment for bad credit means regrouping because the broker broke the bargain. Glover also withholds the company name when phone-shopping bullet points because poaching 'does happen occasionally,' and never emails unsolicited deals, since bankers let those go unnoticed. Cheathem's version of the same trap is the borrower who is already shopping you, which Cheathem dodges by refusing to sell from the front end like a used car salesman whose prospects 'go to four to five' dealerships and pit them against each other [I8ZezTXbvqQ @ 00:08:00]. Equipment Finance Academy flags the relationship side: treat a referral like a lead and 'you'll never get a second one,' and run referrals on a handshake and nothing protects you when a deal falls through, so the channel insists on a one-page written agreement paying a percentage once the deal actually funds [YIQEV-r00oA @ 00:04:02].

ION Capital's coaching call is the clearest loss story. Ryan describes an Alaska client with oil rigs and photos of 'giant big fat gold nuggets,' an asset list that looked like an obvious deal until it proved 'extremely difficult to find somebody who was willing to take on that kind of risk,' because lenders must resell equipment on default [WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:03:22]. Christopher adds the valuation trap: lenders are 'not going to give you fair market value,' they price used equipment as if it had to be liquidated in a fire sale, so old equipment rarely raises much, and assets already carrying a UCC filing fail because lenders 'do not want to take a second position' [DSEdy5KFvBs @ 00:09:36]. When a caller floated using an MCA to buy auction equipment, Ryan laid out the death spiral: the borrower gets 'smacked over the head with a daily or a weekly payment,' repays the lender with the lender's own money, and can default and lose the machine before it ever earns, just as a farmer waiting six months for harvest will not get funded today [WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:12:07]. The fix is asking upfront whether the client wants purchase financing or to borrow against existing equipment, then sending the asset list to the equipment team.

The economics checks come from the borrower side and the beginner side. Joe Camberato's test is blunt: a $25,000 piece of equipment over a five-year term runs about $500 a month, so ask whether the machine can generate more than $500 a month in income, and if credit sits below the 620 to 640 range expect money down or extra financial documentation [H6xXORaU7Jo @ 00:02:53]. Equipment Finance Academy warns beginners against burning out on 10 different $20,000 micro leases 'just to walk away with a couple hundred bucks per deal' when one high-ticket transaction can pay $15,000 [uzLIopNJDJY @ 00:00:01]. The same channel says beginners usually get stuck not knowing 'what to ask for, what order to ask for it in, or what a lender will want next'; Michael Chen's first deal took 60 days even with training, and the course pairs lessons with a CRM, pipeline, and per-deal document checklists [p-1FbIYoOCs @ 00:02:29]. Contend Capital draws the borrower-side prerequisite from client Ethan Lear: file a business as soon as possible because 'the clock starts to tick,' and Lear's small lease of 'just a couple thousand' built the business credit behind seven machines Contend later financed [Hod3pJo62jY @ 00:02:04].
