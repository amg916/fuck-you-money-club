## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who talks money lands on the same point: the machine is not the asset, the hours it sells are. Money Machines frames a CNC lathe business as selling 'a component that solves a customer's manufacturing problem' [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:01:08], names machine time and utilization as the real asset [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:02:05], and puts it bluntly: 'A CNC lathe without orders is an expensive piece of industrial furniture' [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:05:08]. Subi Industries reaches the same conclusion from the Chinese side. Once machinists spun off their own shops on used machines from the 2000s onward, clustering spread the know-how and expertise stopped commanding a premium [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:03:42]. The result: 'When everyone owns a machine, the real shortage isn't equipment anymore' [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:14:37], and owners take loss-making cash work just to keep spindles turning, feeding a race to the bottom [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:18:37]. TITANS of CNC Machining supplies the counterweight: orders come from people, because 'industry is all about relationships' [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:08:53]. The operator takeaway is consistent across all three: price the order book before the iron, because an idle spindle drains cash whether it was bought new, used, or financed, and a fleet without a customer pipeline is dead weight on the floor.

The second point of agreement is that setups, not spindle speed, decide margin. CoxManufacturing Company runs about 100 bar automatics on three shifts Monday through Friday plus some weekends, and stacks many operations into one setup because it eliminates handling and potential error [t9zRrkzjFbA @ 00:02:52]; its six-spindle machines are the cost-effective play for high-volume, low-complexity parts [t9zRrkzjFbA @ 00:03:12]. Subi Industries, describing Chinese shops that bill a few dollars an hour, explains why a rate that low can still pay: a long run amortizes the setup [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:09:20], while frequent changeovers eat the profit [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:10:38]. MICHAEL WANG puts machining time at 'the largest component, accounting for 30-50%' of cost and pushes single-setup, multi-face machining as a design lever [M5DbDgTFVeY @ 00:07:12]. TITANS of CNC Machining turns the same logic into a quoting weapon, contrasting a job run in 6 operations against one run in 2 [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:23:30]. Across volume screw-machine work, Chinese contract shops, and a garage start, the playbook is identical: consolidate operations, chase long runs, and treat every changeover as unpaid time. Setup hours that never get billed are the leak every one of these operators plugs first.

The third consensus is lean capitalization and respect for old iron. TITANS of CNC Machining tells new owners to keep the day job [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:17:22] and sets a hard affordability test: 'if you lost everyone you could still make that machine payment for 6 months without a problem' [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:16:50]. MachineshopLaunchpad warns against over-leverage while disclosing that the speaker's own machine runs $1,726 a month over 32 months [BoQ7bHu0IZE @ 00:01:24], and the roadmap then scales to $50-100K machines once Xometry work is flowing [BoQ7bHu0IZE @ 00:13:24]. Subi Industries shows what happens when that discipline breaks at scale: shops financed machines in the 2020 boom [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:12:28], then demand collapsed while the payments kept coming [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:14:01]. On the equipment side, age alone does not disqualify a machine. MS Milling CNC still runs a Matsura bought in 1994 and says old machines 'hold great tolerance' [BCaYAmw5wQs @ 00:00:16], and the shop keeps three matching 400mm Kiwas side by side for operators [BCaYAmw5wQs @ 00:02:31]. The shared rule: size the payment to survive six months of silence, start on the smallest machine that does the work, and judge iron by the tolerance it still holds rather than its build year.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Money Machines lays out the cleanest unit math. A new Haas SD10 runs about $65,000 and used units trade around $27-30K [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:00:15], for a lathe with 6,000 RPM, 15 hp, and a 12-station turret. TITANS of CNC Machining attacks the setup line hardest: bill an NRE charge and engineer setups down from 30 hours to 4 [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:22:17], because efficiency matters more than whether the shop charges $75 or $100 an hour. MICHAEL WANG lists depreciation at 20-40% of the cost stack and machining time at 30-50% [M5DbDgTFVeY @ 00:07:12], so the two largest cost buckets are the machine itself and the time it spends cutting.

On used purchases, Contend Capital brings in Anthony Sero, nine years as a machinist and programmer and nine more in sales, 90% of it used equipment [rLDoLaoEZ-A @ 00:00:47]. The money leaks in fees: thousands go to rigging, prep, and inspection [rLDoLaoEZ-A @ 00:01:28]. ## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how big to swing on day one. TITANS of CNC MACHINING says a garage start with one machine is possible, then admits the host did the opposite: investor Jeff Weaver, four machines, 25 customers, sleeping in the shop, and a first check of $14,000 for paintball parts in February 2005 [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:12:33]. The brake on that aggression is a payment test: if every customer vanished, the shop should still cover the machine note for six months without a problem [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:16:50]. MachineshopLaunchpad runs the lean version, picking a Tormach PCNC 440 for weight, power and tooling cost, warning against over-leverage, and putting numbers on it: the host's own machine runs $1,726 a month over 32 months, while the 440 sits at $288.55 a month, half price for six months through Geneva Capital [BoQ7bHu0IZE @ 00:01:24]. Contend Capital adds a third lane: startups without a two-year history can still get financed, with the lender steering buyers toward dealers [rLDoLaoEZ-A @ 00:05:26]. Both shop builders converge on keeping the day job while the garage machine proves itself, but they part ways on whether outside money and multiple spindles belong in the opening move or only after the first jobs clear.

On pricing, the practitioners disagree about which number actually matters. Money Machines anchors on market rate, quoting $50 to $100 per machine-hour across US and Western markets for 2026 [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:03:14]. TITANS of CNC MACHINING argues the hourly figure is the wrong lever: a shop billing $75 that runs six operations can beat one billing $100 that runs two, and an upfront NRE charge can cut setup from 30 hours to 4 [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:22:53]. Subi Industries shows the extreme end, where Chinese shops reportedly bill a few dollars an hour with barely any profit, and explains that a low price is survivable only on a long run that amortizes setup, while frequent changeovers eat the margin [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:09:20]. CoxManufacturing Company lands on the volume side, calling six-spindle machines the cost-effective option for high-volume, low-complexity parts and running about 100 bar automatics on three shifts [t9zRrkzjFbA @ 00:03:12]. The operator takeaway is that rate shopping and setup engineering are competing playbooks: low or mid-market rates only hold when run length, operations per setup, and spindle count are engineered around the part rather than around the hourly sticker printed on the quote.

Automation and axis count split the room too, and each argument maps directly onto what a buyer should actually pay for in a used machine. TITANS of CNC MACHINING tells new owners to become experts in machining and push every toolpath to its highest level before buying automation [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:03:45]. On the shop floor, Strange Parts found JLCCNC running 75 machines where simple 3-axis units handle 80% of jobs, with only one 5-axis machine on site and a robot arm still under test rather than in production [YuSApjBn_zY @ 00:00:15]. That collides with the TITANS host's verdict at Tongtai, a builder holding in-house spindles within 3 microns, that three-axis machines are almost obsolete and that engineers must learn manufacturing to design cost-effectively [C2UEluRNT-M @ 00:06:34]. MICHAEL WANG splits the difference, calling 3-axis the value workhorse and placing 3+2, 4-axis and then 5-axis at the high end of the price and capability ladder [M5DbDgTFVeY @ 00:03:26]. For anyone brokering iron, that split matters: a used 3-axis machine still lands in the bucket most floors fill first, while 5-axis capacity sells on single-setup capability rather than on spindle count alone.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Contend Capital opens with the hardest rule in used iron: do not buy a machine that is not under power, because market value does not apply to a machine sitting in a dark warehouse corner [rLDoLaoEZ-A @ 00:00:00]. The same trap hits sellers: in a repo, a $50K market value collapses once the machine is off power, so never unplug it before the sale [rLDoLaoEZ-A @ 00:04:33]. Guest Anthony Sero, nine years as a machinist and programmer and nine in sales that ran about 90% used equipment, shows where fees hide: a machine priced at $10K loaded is billed to the buyer at $11.5K, then the dealer has the uninsured seller fork-load it, and if it arrives damaged the liability is muddy [rLDoLaoEZ-A @ 00:02:08]. SARK Engineers & COnsultants document the paperwork version on a 2005 European rotor grinder refit in 2026: writing 'Built in 2026' misleads customs, the two dates must be stated separately, and the physical nameplate serial outranks PO, project and packing references [Qiyq7yhmD2o @ 00:02:17]. Each trap turns a clean-looking deal into a loss before the machine ever cuts its first chip on your floor.

The economics traps start with confusing revenue and profit. Money Machines runs an $80-an-hour SD10 through 1,200, 1,600 and 2,000 productive hours for $96K, $128K and $160K, then warns that those numbers are revenue scenarios, not profit [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:03:54]. The cost stack it lists runs material, tools, coolant, operators, setup, rent, inspection, financing and downtime, and its bluntest line is that a CNC lathe without orders is an expensive piece of industrial furniture [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:05:08]. Subi Industries shows the leverage version at scale: shops financed machines in the 2020 boom, demand collapsed, and the payments kept coming, pushing owners to accept loss-making cash work just to avoid idle spindles, which becomes a race to the bottom [iZJI5Y5wLUo @ 00:14:01]. TITANS of CNC MACHINING names the same failure from the bidding side, warning that underbidding or scrap can literally put a shop out of business, and that customers stretch from 30 to 90-day terms and still pay 30 to 45 days late [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:24:39]. Price the job on true cost, carry cash for the lag, and size the note to survive empty months, as the TITANS six-month payment rule demands.

Shop-floor failures are smaller per incident but compound fast across a year of jobs. TITANS of CNC MACHINING watched a Haas VF-11 at 15,000 RPM stall on a half-inch pipe tap, forcing a switch to threadmilling; the lesson drawn is to match the machine to the material before buying it [bqSGI0L8zm0 @ 00:14:36]. Strange Parts saw at JLCCNC that sandblasting changes part dimensions, a trap when blasting comes after the tolerance is cut, while hand polishing stays a manual step [YuSApjBn_zY @ 00:19:38]. MachineshopLaunchpad flags cheap-kit traps: Tormach vises that cracked and cost $300 to $400 versus a roughly $70 Vevor vise, and a 6x6 granite plate that is almost unusable next to a 12x8x2, plus a $189.99 quiet compressor and a heater for the garage [BoQ7bHu0IZE @ 00:03:02]. Money Machines adds the risks behind the revenue math: no guaranteed customers, skills gaps, and hazards from chips and fluids [Q00deIDcQRw @ 00:05:45]. Each of these is cheap to avoid upfront and expensive to discover mid-job, which is why the practitioners teach them as checks before money moves rather than as lessons paid for after scrap and missed ship dates.
