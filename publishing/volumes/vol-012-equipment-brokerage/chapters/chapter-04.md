## Executive Summary

Undercarriage and fluids are where used tracked machines hide the most money, and the practitioners agree on how to expose it. Volvo's inspector puts undercarriage replacement at 12,000 to upwards of 70 grand, while C&C Equipment and dealer technicians from Cat and Komatsu measure link height, bushings, pitch, grousers, idlers, rollers and sprockets against OEM or ITR wear charts instead of trusting a seller's percentage. C&C scored a 4,700-hour Komatsu at 31 percent worn and warns wear accelerates past 40 to 50 percent. On oil, Carter Machinery, WearCheck, Caterpillar and Chevron's Shelly Eckert push clean, warm, well-labeled samples trended over time, with Carter recommending every 250 hours. Shelly quotes under 10 dollars per engine sample and 24 to 48 hour turnaround. They split on tooling: ultrasonic gauges and software versus straight edges, calipers and a trained eye.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who climbs under a tracked machine treats the undercarriage as the line item that quietly eats a deal. Volvo's inspector flags it as one of the single most expensive components to replace on a used track hoe, with replacement running from 12,000 to upwards of 70 grand depending on machine size, and says it is usually undervalued and missed [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:00:01]. Clinton and Kevin at C&C Equipment push the same point from the buyer's chair: listings that say undercarriage is 75 or 50 percent remaining are supposed to come from actual measurements, so if a seller is pulling that number out of their head, have somebody measure it and double check the work [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:10:48]. Michael at Hunter Valley Tracks frames the measured approach as a CTS, or custom track service, inspection, where link height, bush thickness, roller and idler wear, shoe lug height and sprocket wear across three teeth get plugged into a program that converts readings into wear rates against hours and kilometers [1B6uxG5WNzQ @ 00:00:16]. The Cat Products dealer technician adds the business angle: a full measure-up takes about half an hour, and the resulting report forecasts replacement timing, lets services be bundled, and cuts owning and operating costs [fP5ysbGB2H0 @ 00:00:19].

The component checklist barely changes from speaker to speaker. Rail or link height, bushing outside diameter, pitch extension across several pins, grouser height, idler tread, carrier rollers, bottom rollers and sprockets fill the C&C excavator sheet, and Clinton stresses that excavators wear differently from dozers because they sit and twist rather than travel, which stretches rails and wears pins and bushings out internally while the bushing outside diameter stays relatively healthy [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:00:37]. Sprockets are where the consensus is loudest, and reading them requires no tool at all. Volvo's inspector repeats the old adage that you can run new sprockets on old rails but never old sprockets on new rails, and says if a tooth is sharp enough to shave with, it is shot and needs replacing [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:05:16]. Jim Mack says worn teeth turn sharper and pointier until they look like a bicycle sprocket, and that the sprocket normally gets changed whenever pins and bushings are turned or the chain is replaced, because it has to match the new bushing surface [YyZcRwETdeA @ 00:04:46]. The EquipmentWorld dozer tech calls sharp-edged sprockets one of the biggest indicators most people go by when deciding it is time to turn or replace components [shzCS90v5wA @ 00:03:23].

On the fluids side, the consensus is just as tight: an oil sample is blood work for the machine, and its value depends on clean technique, accurate paperwork and trend history. Mike Dudley, the Carter Machinery fluid analysis expert, compares sampling to a doctor's blood test that catches small problems before they turn into the equivalent of a heart attack, and says to sample often and regularly [R9dHZ4P7yp0 @ 00:00:36]. Shelly Eckert of Chevron calls engine oil the bloodline of the engine and a cheap insurance policy that can back up warranty claims by proving maintenance was done [QYimn88ydQI @ 00:00:52]. MEVAS, the heavy equipment inspectors who pull SOS samples on used machines, set the non-negotiable prerequisite for any buyer: you need to know how old the oil is, because a fresh change leaves nothing to measure, and oil run 1,500 hours on a 500-hour interval throws the values off [enf_Ct89j8o @ 00:02:24]. Shelly reinforces in a second session that the lab needs the engine make and model, engine hours, oil hours, date taken and a consistent unit identifier every time, since OEM wear tolerances differ and mislabeling splits the sample history that trend analysis depends on [l4sJ-xx-mqI @ 00:02:39].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

C&C ran a full measure-up on a 2010 Komatsu excavator with about 4,700 hours and likely original undercarriage, using an ITR America measuring kit and ITR wear specs. Rail height spec is 105 millimeters new; Kevin read 104, which charted at 20 percent worn. Bushing outside diameter is 59.8 new and read 59, or 24 percent worn. Pitch is measured over four links, where new is 761 millimeters; readings settled at 762 after checking more than one spot, which is 15 percent worn [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:05:28]. Triple-bar grousers went from 26 to 24 millimeters for 20 percent, the front idler at 20 millimeters was 25 percent, one top carrier roller dropped from 120 to 115 for 45 percent while the other at 119 was 13 percent, and lower rollers sat at 157 against 160 new for 34 percent, with the first roller at 43. Summing everything, 3,200 points equals 100 percent and the machine totaled 1,010, so 31 percent worn and 69 percent remaining [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:11:05]. Pin counts vary by brand: Volvo's inspector says chains get measured across five, four or three pins and run through a conversion chart [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:03:05], while Komatsu Australia measures center of pin to center of the fifth pin with the track tight [JCDyzlYC0h0 @ 00:02:14].

Dealer-grade inspections lean on ultrasonics. The Cat Products technician carries the UWI 5 ultrasonic tool plus calipers, depth gauge, tape measures, ruler and a wire brush, checks for warm joints, logs hours since the last inspection, then runs a string over the track, measures the two lowest points and divides by two for overall sag [fP5ysbGB2H0 @ 00:01:29]. That tech measures shoes one third of the way in on top of the grouser, uses the ultrasonic on D8 through D11 carrier rollers but calipers on D7 and smaller, and tapes the sprocket across three teeth on the same segment. Komatsu Australia starts by printing the wear criteria sheet, loads model, serial number, service meter reading and sealed or lubricated track type into the tool, gels the probe and waits for the green LED, then rates sprockets good, fair or poor visually [JCDyzlYC0h0 @ 00:03:38]. Jim Mack shows the no-electronics version with a straight edge and Cat ruler 5P3277, reading 1.68 inches of grouser and 3.54 inches, or about 91 millimeters, of link height [YyZcRwETdeA @ 00:01:42]. On sag, the EquipmentWorld tech found a Deere 750K at three to three and a half inches against a decal spec of two inches plus or minus a quarter, then adjusted it with a grease gun [shzCS90v5wA @ 00:06:06].

Oil sampling mechanics are equally specific. Mike Dudley at Carter Machinery recommends a sample every 250 hours or whatever the maintenance guide calls for, with the machine run about 20 minutes so the fluid is fully homogenized for a representative sample [R9dHZ4P7yp0 @ 00:01:08]. WearCheck's dipstick method cuts tubing to dipstick length plus about a foot, angles the end at 45 degrees so it cannot suck flat against a surface, samples within a few minutes of shutdown while oil is warm, and fills the bottle two-thirds to three-quarters; mid-drain samples wait until several gallons have run out, and a running machine on a sample port is the preferred method [ypiqyyCR9MI @ 00:02:43]. Jeff Payne of Caterpillar pulls a throwaway clean-out sample first to flush dust from the valve hole, insists a valid SOS port has that hole, and keeps the good cap in its bag because pocket lint shows up on the report [_j1VRwfp3gU @ 00:02:47]. Shelly prefers sampling valves for consistency, falls back on a vampire pump through the dipstick tube, quotes under 10 dollars for an engine oil sample and under 20 on the high side, with results in 24 to 48 hours from labs like ALS Tribology or POLARIS Laboratories [QYimn88ydQI @ 00:07:11].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Set Up The Inspection
   - Pull the machine's wear criteria, record hours and serial, and clean the measuring surfaces.
   - Komatsu Australia prints the wear criteria sheet and enters model, serial number, service meter reading and sealed or lubricated track type before the first reading [Komatsu Australia — JCDyzlYC0h0 @ 00:01:30]; the Cat tech wire-brushes mud off rails, rollers and idlers first [Cat Products — fP5ysbGB2H0 @ 00:00:35].
2. Measure Every Component On Both Sides
   - Read link height, bushing, pitch, grouser, idler, carrier and track rollers, then sprocket, and repeat on the opposite side.
   - C&C measures pitch over four links and rechecks in more than one spot because the track can bunch up [C&C Equipment — Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:04:33]; the Cat tech replicates every step on the opposite side [Cat Products — fP5ysbGB2H0 @ 00:03:10]; on excavators, Komatsu Australia keeps the final drive at the rear so left and right are not mixed up [Komatsu Australia — JCDyzlYC0h0 @ 00:07:21].
3. Convert To Remaining Life And Discount It
   - Sum component percentages into one remaining-life figure, then haircut anything near the halfway mark.
   - C&C totals 1,010 against 3,200 for 69 percent remaining [C&C Equipment — Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:11:05], and warns that past 40 to 50 percent the softer metal wears far quicker, so they usually replace components arriving at that level [C&C Equipment — Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:12:22].
4. Pull And Label A Clean Oil Sample
   - Confirm oil age, label the bottle first, sample warm and clean, and ship to the lab.
   - MEVAS confirms how long the oil has been in use before sampling [MEVAS The Heavy Equipment Inspectors — enf_Ct89j8o @ 00:02:03]; WearCheck sticks the label on before sampling so it holds firmly [WearCheck Video — ypiqyyCR9MI @ 00:00:22]; Jeff Payne labels machine number and hours on every bottle [Cat Products — _j1VRwfp3gU @ 00:02:58].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Inspectors split first on tooling and on how to turn raw readings into one sellable number. Clinton and Kevin at C&C Equipment run an ITR America kit and inspection report, tell buyers a straight edge will do if they lack the fancy tools, and then sum every component reading: 3,200 equals fully worn, and their 1,010 meant 31 percent worn and 69 percent remaining on a 2010 Komatsu showing roughly 4,700 hours [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:11:05]. The Cat dealer workflow is instrument-heavy, calling for the UWI 5 ultrasonic tool plus calipers, depth gauge, tape measures, ruler and wire brush, about half an hour for a full measure-up, with ultrasonic carrier roller readings reserved for D8 through D11 while D7 and lower get calipers [fP5ysbGB2H0 @ 00:00:19]. Komatsu Australia front-loads the ultrasonic unit with model, serial number, service meter reading, inspection date and sealed or lubricated track type, and only trusts a reading once the green LED lights [JCDyzlYC0h0 @ 00:01:08]. Jim Mack stays fully manual, laying a straight edge across two shoes and reading a CAT 5P3277 ruler for 1.68 inches of grouser and 3.54 inches of link height, then averaging several locations instead of trusting a single spot [YyZcRwETdeA @ 00:00:34].

Pitch and sprocket calls are where the specs diverge hardest. C&C Equipment measures center of pin to center of pin over four links against a 761 millimeter new figure, logs 762 as 15 percent worn, and deliberately repeats the reading in a second spot because the chain may be taking up or sucked together at one end [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:04:49]. Volvo's inspector says the span depends on who built the undercarriage, anywhere from five, four or three pins, and the number only means something once it runs through that maker's conversion chart for life used or remaining [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:03:05]. Komatsu Australia demands a tight track before the tape goes on and measures across five pins or four links, checking several points around the chain [JCDyzlYC0h0 @ 00:02:14]. Sprockets split the room again. C&C has no number at all and reads them by eye: a flat middle with roughly a half inch of room is healthy, and a sharp point means they are done. Hunter Valley Tracks, by contrast, runs a tape across three teeth of one segment, plugs that into its own undercarriage program alongside link height, bush thickness, rollers, idlers and shoe lug height, and converts the lot into wear rates [1B6uxG5WNzQ @ 00:00:36].

Oil sampling has its own competing playbooks. Shelly Eckert of Chevron calls a sampling valve on the engine block the industry standard because the sample comes from the exact same place every time, with the vampire pump threaded down the dipstick to the sump as the fallback [QYimn88ydQI @ 00:09:35]. WearCheck agrees the sample port is preferred and that the machine must be running for oil pressure, but also teaches a dipstick-pump method, with tubing measured to the dipstick plus about a foot and cut at 45 degrees, and a mid-drain grab after several gallons have run out [ypiqyyCR9MI @ 00:02:43]. Timing and fill rules differ too. Mike Dudley at Carter Machinery wants about 20 minutes of operation so the fluid is fully homogenized, and sampling every 250 hours unless the maintenance guide calls for something else [R9dHZ4P7yp0 @ 00:01:08], while WearCheck pulls within a few minutes of shutdown and stops at two-thirds to three-quarters full. Jeff Payne at Caterpillar fills to the printed line, but only after pulling a throwaway clean-out sample first, because dust sitting in the little hole at the end of the valve contaminates the first draw, and that skipped flush is where a lot of people go wrong [_j1VRwfp3gU @ 00:00:37].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The cheapest traps on a used tracked machine are the ones a seller builds on purpose. C&C Equipment warns that someone may pump the tracks up really tight to hide a worn-out track link, so they jump on the rails and rock them back and forth, and check whether the front idler is pushed way out [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:02:25]. Volvo's inspector has seen owners cut a link out of a stretched chain and tighten it back up; the tell is a chain that looks like a piano string, so count links against factory spec and confirm proper sag, because an over-tight chain puts extra wear on every consumable in the undercarriage [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:06:20]. The same inspector flags idlers welded back up without changing the bearings inside, which shows as a wobble that takes a second person to spot, pins that walked out and got welded in from the inside, and track adjusters welded solid so they can no longer extend, which a grease gun tightening test exposes [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:07:20]. At auctions an L can show up on the side of a machine, so count for the nine bottom rollers of a long undercarriage. Jim Mack adds that a flat edge felt on top of a bushing means the pins and bushings have already been turned once [YyZcRwETdeA @ 00:02:10].

The economics checks are blunt. Volvo puts the undercarriage among the single most expensive components to replace on a used track hoe, from about 12,000 to upwards of 70,000 dollars depending on machine size, and calls it the item most often undervalued and missed [L3kAdkTSgI4 @ 00:00:01]. C&C Equipment's hardest warning is the 50 percent cliff: once a machine measures around 40 or 50 percent worn, the wear has cut through the hardened material into softer metal and goes way quicker, so 6,000 hours at 50 percent does not buy another 6,000 hours, and their own shop usually replaces undercarriage that arrives at that level [Hif9qgd9EEI @ 00:11:54]. Hunter Valley Tracks builds its maintenance plan around that curve, targeting pin and bush turns at 7,000 hours or 60 percent rail wear and using mid-life readings to decide when to rotate rollers and idlers and when to re-lug shoes [1B6uxG5WNzQ @ 00:01:12]. On the fluid side, Bryan of Equipment World asked whether an oil sample could clear a 60 grand used purchase in time; Shelly answered that turnaround typically runs 24 to 48 hours, and that an engine sample generally costs under 10 dollars through a branded volume program and under 20 on the high side, with prepaid kits more cost effective [QYimn88ydQI @ 00:05:07].

Most oil analysis losses come from the sample, not the machine. MEVAS warns that oil changed just before sampling leaves nothing to measure, and oil run 1,500 hours on a 500-hour interval skews every value, so the oil's age has to be known before the bottle is worth sending [enf_Ct89j8o @ 00:02:03]. Shelly confirms the lab can tell when a sample looks like brand new oil, which is exactly what a seller who just swapped the oil produces [QYimn88ydQI @ 00:05:21]. In a separate Equipment World sit-down, Shelly's field stories read like an incident log: a cap set face-up in dust put dirt in the bottle and drew an elevated silica flag with a review-sample-technique note; a batch arrived without paperwork, one bottle held water, and every sample from that day had to be pulled again; a technician filled several bottles from one sump, producing back-to-back coolant results; a blender with oil up both arms kept failing particle counts; and a gasoline-smelling fuel tank flashed at 78 degrees, leading to a 50,000 dollar repair on returning leased equipment [l4sJ-xx-mqI @ 00:05:24]. Jeff Payne adds that a clean cap carried in a pocket picks up lint, and the report then shows lint in the system [_j1VRwfp3gU @ 00:02:47].
