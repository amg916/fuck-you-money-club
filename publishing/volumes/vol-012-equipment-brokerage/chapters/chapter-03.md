## 1. Price seven acquisition and execution plays against earned commissions  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Start with three closed-deal cohorts, not an equipment inventory valuation. The Conservative illustration closes ten machines at $200,000 each with a negotiated 5% fee, producing $2,000,000 of equipment transaction value and $100,000 of broker commissions. Base closes thirty transactions at $300,000 and 5%, producing $9,000,000 of transaction value and $450,000 of commissions. Scaled closes forty at $400,000 and 5%, producing $16,000,000 of transaction value and $800,000 of commissions. These prices, counts, and fee rates are illustrative decompositions of the canonical scorecard, not observed market averages. The seller receives equipment proceeds; the broker recognizes only its contractual fee when its performance obligation is satisfied. A signed mandate, verbal buyer commitment, escrow deposit, or unaccepted machine does not automatically establish earned revenue. Record serial number, seller authority, fee payer, earning milestone, inspection conditions, and cancellation treatment in one deal record. HubSpot can hold the opportunity, while QuickBooks maintains invoices and expense coding. Link both through a stable transaction identifier so a duplicate invoice cannot inflate the cohort. Treat freight, taxes, lender payoff, and purchase principal as separately identified settlement items, never as a larger brokerage revenue headline.

The supplied acquisition mapping is fmcsa_dot_inspection_trigger_arbitrage, the FMCSA/DOT New-Authority & Roadside Violation Trigger Funnels play in TRACK_1. Schedule daily checks of authorized FMCSA Census, Licensing & Insurance, and available SMS inspection sources, but store each source's actual publication date: daily polling does not make every dataset a daily feed. Respect access restrictions and do not reconstruct restricted carrier records. Correct the timing premise before writing outreach: the 21-day period under 49 CFR 365.115 concerns protests to published applications, not a universal mandatory insurance-filing window. Verify insurance and authority status separately. A new authority or maintenance citation is a qualifying signal, not proof that a fleet must buy equipment. Illustratively, $4,000 of research and outreach across 200 qualified fleet accounts yielding four closed transactions creates $1,000 channel CAC and $60,000 of Base commission revenue. Allocate another $500 per closing for shared selling work to reach the modeled $1,500 fully loaded CAC. If conversion fails, switch to transparent private compliance-agency branding, free BOC-3 and MCS-150 status checks, and inbound hand-raisers; disclose the brokerage relationship and never suggest government affiliation.

The remaining six mappings are chapter-level execution workflows, not additional supplied pipeline identifiers: CNC replacement matching, construction disposal mandates, fleet refresh sourcing, lien-resolution coordination, inspection coordination, and buyer-alert reactivation. Each must improve a measurable commission cohort rather than create imaginary service revenue. A CNC replacement match at $300,000 supports a $15,000 Base commission; inspection spending still belongs inside that transaction's delivery budget. A $400,000 fleet unit supports $20,000 at the same fee rate, but four vehicles bought under one mandate are not automatically four independent acquisition wins. Define the cohort counting rule before calculating CAC. Use Airtable equipment attributes for working envelope, spindle hours, controller, emissions tier, attachment compatibility, and pickup location. Buyer alerts should match those attributes against an explicit buying mandate rather than blast every listing. Price paid research against closed business, including the cost of failed opportunities. The matrix treats its examples as alternative applications of the same model, not additive revenue streams. When a workflow cannot meet the contribution hurdle, narrow the mandate, require buyer-funded specialist work, or decline the assignment before advancing cash.

## 2. Reconcile every deduction from billed commission to owner profit  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Use the waterfall below as a management ledger with separate revenue, direct delivery, acquisition, loss provision, and overhead accounts. Conservative commissions of $100,000 less $15,000 contractor delivery labor, $8,000 verification, and $2,000 settlement expense leave $75,000 delivery gross profit. Deduct $15,000 acquisition spending and $5,000 expected commission losses to reach $55,000 contribution before fixed overhead. Subtract $30,000 overhead for the canonical $25,000 owner profit. Base starts at $450,000, spends $90,000 on contractors, $27,000 on verification, and $9,000 on settlement, leaving $324,000 delivery gross profit. Its $45,000 CAC and $13,500 loss provision leave $265,500 contribution; $108,000 overhead yields $157,500 owner profit. Scaled starts at $800,000, deducts $144,000 labor, $40,000 verification, and $16,000 settlement for $600,000 delivery gross profit. Acquisition of $64,000 and expected losses of $16,000 leave $520,000 contribution. After $160,000 overhead, owner profit is $360,000. Delivery gross margins are therefore 75%, 72%, and 75%; the scorecard's 25%, 35.0%, and 45% are net margins, not commission rates or gross margins. Keep those denominators visible on every dashboard to prevent a high equipment price from concealing a weak fee.

Translate annual totals into spending authority before hiring contractors. Per closed transaction, Conservative allows $1,500 delivery labor, $800 verification, $200 settlement, $1,500 acquisition, and $500 expected losses. That leaves $5,500 contribution against $3,000 allocated overhead and $2,500 owner profit. Base allows $3,000 labor, $900 verification, $300 settlement, $1,500 acquisition, and $450 expected losses, leaving $8,850 contribution and $5,250 profit after $3,600 overhead. Scaled allows $3,600 labor, $1,000 verification, $400 settlement, $1,600 acquisition, and $400 expected losses, leaving $13,000 contribution and $9,000 profit after $4,000 overhead. Contractor budgets cover actual delivery assignments, including field coordination and transaction administration; specialist testing belongs in verification instead. Fixed overhead includes employee compensation where applicable, insurance, software, accounting, workspace, and routine administration. The teams remain 1 FT plus 3 C, 1 FT plus 3 C, and 2 FT plus 4 C; contractor counts describe a roster, not full-time utilization. Owner profit is the modeled residual, not an additional salary expense. Document any owner payroll allocation explicitly, and do not deduct distributions again when reconciling operating profit to the scorecard.

Separate pre-closing scrubs from reversals of earned commissions. The ten, thirty, and forty transactions already represent completed revenue-producing closings; failed inspections and abandoned mandates consume the stated acquisition and verification budgets without becoming extra completed deals. The $5,000, $13,500, and $16,000 provisions address expected losses on the earned commission cohort, equivalent to 5%, 3%, and 2% of gross commissions. These are planning assumptions requiring replacement with actual loss history. They are not percentages deducted from equipment principal. Tag each event as fee refund, collection failure, contractual concession, or incremental claim expense so its accounting treatment can be determined correctly. Some concessions reduce recognized revenue rather than create an operating expense; the management waterfall preserves the same economic result without prescribing one journal entry for every event. Never subtract an actual refund and its covering provision twice. Maintain an opening balance, additions, utilized amounts, recoveries, and closing balance. Moving cash into a reserve account is also not another expense. One $15,000 Base commission reversal exceeds the $13,500 annual provision, demonstrating why both a loss allowance and separate liquidity planning are necessary for concentrated industrial brokerage cohorts.

## 3. Fund the advance-to-release gap without borrowing seller principal  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Build cash timing from dated obligations rather than applying a generic receivables percentage to commission revenue. For the illustrative cohort, day zero is the operator's advance of acquisition and delivery cash, day thirty is contractual fee earning and invoicing, and day forty-five is unrestricted receipt. Assume buyer funds clear on day thirty-five and a ten-day payout lock ends on day forty-five. DSO is fifteen days from invoice to cash; the ten-day lock sits inside those fifteen days and must not be added again. Total advance exposure is forty-five days. These are negotiated modeling assumptions, not standard escrow terms. Per-closing cash advanced is $4,000 Conservative, $5,700 Base, and $6,600 Scaled, comprising labor, verification, settlement, and acquisition. Assume those costs are paid at inception for a deliberately conservative timing estimate; vendor credit would reduce exposure. The loss provision is excluded from daily spending because a separately funded buffer covers it below. Track actual invoice aging and elapsed pre-earning days independently. A machine awaiting acceptance can consume cash without appearing in receivables, while a fully earned commission can remain inaccessible inside a disputed settlement.

At steady throughput, annual cash delivery and acquisition spending is $40,000 Conservative, $171,000 Base, and $264,000 Scaled. Add fixed overhead of $30,000, $108,000, and $160,000, then multiply each combined annual amount by 45/365. The resulting operating bridge is approximately $8,630, $34,397, and $52,274. For this liquidity policy, add cash equal to the full modeled annual loss provision, plus one additional average deal advance for concentration. Targets become $17,630, $53,597, and $74,874. These are rounded planning amounts, not universal minimum capital requirements. No owner draws occur during the modeled bridge, and transaction principal remains outside operating cash. The stated startup capital range of $10,000 to $75,000 therefore cannot fund every launch configuration equally. Before the first deal, two to nine months of fixed overhead alone consumes $5,000 to $22,500 Conservative, $18,000 to $81,000 Base, or roughly $26,667 to $120,000 Scaled. Add actual pre-closing spending and setup costs to that launch schedule. Do not mechanically stack overlapping runway periods; a weekly dated cash forecast must identify the true peak deficit before choosing cash, partner capital, or a documented credit facility.

Treat lien clearance and escrow instructions as economic controls because unresolved title can strand an otherwise profitable fee. UCC 9-315 generally preserves a security interest after disposition unless the secured party authorizes a disposition free of it or another applicable rule changes the result. UCC 9-513 addresses termination statements, but a transaction-specific release may be required without terminating a blanket filing covering other collateral. Search the seller under the correct debtor name and applicable filing jurisdiction; also check certificate-of-title systems where relevant. Match machine serials, payoff conditions, lender authority, and release documentation before treating the asset as transferable. Independently authenticate changed wiring instructions. The escrow schedule should specify lender payoff, seller proceeds, broker commission, acceptance evidence, and the treatment of disputes. Do not assume a broker can operate its own escrow service without examining state licensing and custody requirements. Use a provider capable of the asset class, payment size, and jurisdiction, with written fees and release conditions. If the sale requires an export license or unresolved end-user review, move the earning and collection dates in the forecast rather than presenting trapped funds as available operating liquidity.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Open the cohort cash record
   - Assign one transaction identifier across CRM, invoices, and settlement documents.
   - Record equipment principal separately from commission, then enter dated acquisition, inspection, contractor, and settlement obligations.
2. Authenticate transfer and payment conditions
   - Match seller authority, collateral identifiers, lender releases, and escrow instructions.
   - Confirm wiring details through independently sourced contact information and document which acceptance event permits the commission release.
3. Reforecast the peak cash deficit
   - Update the thirteen-week cash schedule before committing each material expense.
   - Include pre-earning exposure, invoice aging, embedded payout locks, expected loss cash, and overlapping transactions without double counting.
4. Close and reconcile the economic cohort
   - Match unrestricted commission receipts to the final settlement statement and expense ledger.
   - Resolve provision usage, distinguish timing delays from permanent losses, and compare realized contribution with the approved transaction budget.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Verify four conditions before advancing brokerage cash**
> 
> Release spending only after the transaction record supports the fee, asset transfer, settlement path, and cash capacity.
> - Verify the signed mandate identifies the fee payer, commission calculation, earning event, refund conditions, and any broker authority to bind the seller.
> - Verify serial numbers, seller authority, applicable title records, authenticated lender payoff instructions, and asset-specific release conditions.
> - Verify the settlement provider accepts this equipment transaction and documents inspection acceptance, payout locks, dispute handling, and commission disbursement.
> - Verify the weekly cash forecast funds the advance, overhead, and loss buffer without using equipment principal or assuming an unapproved credit draw.

## 4. Stress contribution, collection timing, and concentrated equipment failures  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Stress one variable at a time before combining shocks. With completed volume down 20%, assume all modeled variable costs and loss provisions fall proportionally while overhead stays fixed. Conservative falls to eight closings and $14,000 profit; Base falls to twenty-four and $104,400; Scaled falls to thirty-two and $256,000. This is optimistic if contractor minimums or acquisition commitments cannot adjust, so flag noncancelable spending separately. A 10% reduction in commission dollars at unchanged volume and unchanged dollar costs reduces profit to $15,000, $112,500, and $280,000. That isolates pricing pressure rather than assuming vendors automatically share the concession. A 50% increase in acquisition cost produces $17,500, $135,000, and $328,000 profit. Adding loss expense equal to three percentage points of canonical revenue produces $22,000, $144,000, and $336,000. Those are incremental losses beyond the existing provision, not a second charge for already reserved claims. Use the same denominator and hold the other assumptions constant when explaining results to a partner. Otherwise, a scenario that quietly changes deal count, average machine value, commission rate, and collection rate becomes impossible to diagnose or translate into an operating response.

Collection stress changes liquidity before it necessarily changes profit. Adding thirty days to the entire modeled cash exposure increases funding needs by approximately $5,753 Conservative, $22,932 Base, and $34,849 Scaled, calculated from annual cash costs divided by 365 and multiplied by thirty. This assumes the business maintains throughput and pays obligations while receipts move later; a single delayed closing needs its own dated analysis. Do not book a credit loss merely because an escrow provider delays release, but investigate disputes and collection evidence promptly. By contrast, one additional unrecovered average commission, with all other costs unchanged and beyond the baseline provision, reduces annual profit to $15,000, $142,500, and $340,000. A substantial equipment defect can also generate inspection disputes, legal expense, or indemnity demands larger than the commission itself. Those exposures are outside this simple fee-loss stress and require separate severity cases. State adopted UCC provisions, express representations, and the broker's contractual role determine responsibility; a broker label does not erase promises actually made. Keep manufacturer specifications, inspection reports, seller representations, and broker statements separately attributed so a sales description does not silently become an unsupported condition warranty.

Set commercial stop rules using contribution and capacity, not gross equipment volume. At unchanged unit economics, fixed overhead requires at least six Conservative closings, thirteen Base closings, or thirteen Scaled closings to reach positive annual operating profit. These counts round up $30,000 divided by $5,500, $108,000 divided by $8,850, and $160,000 divided by $13,000. They assume the stated per-closing costs remain valid at lower volume; retained staff and minimum contractor commitments can move the actual threshold. Review the thirteen-week cash forecast every week and the cohort waterfall every month. Use inventory matching and buyer alerts to reduce research hours, but measure saved hours against contractor invoices or increased qualified capacity before calling them savings. Require written change orders when a buyer requests additional demonstrations, teardown work, or remote-site visits. For export transactions, classify the equipment and evaluate destination, end user, and end use under the EAR before promising shipment timing. For vehicle transactions, verify applicable state dealer requirements before adopting a fee structure. Close the feedback loop by retiring channels with persistently weak contribution, renegotiating payout conditions, and preserving cash for verified mandates rather than speculative listings.
