## 1. Sell a documented transfer, not a machine you do not own  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Equipment brokerage is an agency service connecting an equipment owner with a buyer who can use, pay for, and remove a specific asset. The broker establishes the seller's authority, describes the machine accurately, qualifies demand, negotiates the commercial path, and coordinates inspection and settlement. The seller ordinarily retains ownership until the agreed transfer, while independent specialists handle surveys, rigging, transport, or escrow. That differs from a dealer buying inventory for resale, an auctioneer conducting a competitive sale, and a lessor collecting payments for use. Those businesses can overlap operationally, but their contracts, working capital, revenue presentation, and state licensing exposure differ. Start with a written engagement identifying the asset, commission payer, fee calculation, exclusivity, introduced-buyer protection, termination rights, and the event that earns the fee. Do not advertise inventory merely because someone forwarded photographs. Obtain marketing authority and establish who can bind the owner. A durable broker can explain who owns the machine, what evidence supports its condition, which creditor must be paid, and who bears removal risk. A fragile broker has only a price, a messaging handle, and an optimistic promise of a spread.

Recognized operator revenue is the broker's earned commission, excluding the equipment transaction value. On an illustrative $200,000 sale carrying a contracted 5% seller commission, the economic fee is $10,000; the $200,000 is underlying asset value, not brokerage sales. If an independent escrow provider receives buyer funds, pays the secured creditor, remits seller proceeds, and disburses the commission, those movements do not turn the entire settlement into broker revenue. Under an accrual policy aligned with ASC 606, identify the promised service, assess whether the broker acts as agent, determine when the performance obligation is satisfied, and evaluate variable consideration and refund provisions. Cash arrival and revenue recognition need not occur together. A refundable advance remains a liability until the relevant service is earned; a completed service with an enforceable receivable can precede collection. Write down the actual contract conditions instead of assuming every signed purchase order earns an unconditional fee. Record commission receivables, cash collections, reversals, and third-party pass-throughs separately. If the operator starts taking title, accepting inventory exposure, or controlling the asset before transfer, reassess the model rather than carrying agent accounting into a materially different business.

Choose one of five operating archetypes before building a broad marketplace. Construction specialists translate hours, undercarriage wear, attachment compatibility, and jobsite access into usable buyer decisions. CNC specialists establish control generation, spindle condition, tooling inclusions, electrical requirements, and rigging feasibility. Fleet specialists organize vehicle identifiers, title documents, service records, and staged release schedules. Process-equipment specialists verify materials, throughput, sanitation history, and site interfaces. Plant disposition specialists manage many assets against shutdown dates and removal dependencies. Each archetype can use public marketplaces for discovery without pretending that marketplace visibility establishes ownership or condition. MachineryTrader, MachineTools.com, TruckPaper, EquipNet, and BidSpotter expose different buyer communities and sale formats; obtain current commercial terms instead of inventing standard platform commissions. State rules follow the activity and asset category, not the operator's preferred job title. California Vehicle Code section 285, for example, defines a vehicle dealer broadly enough to warrant careful review of compensated vehicle negotiation activities. Record jurisdictions, vehicle categories, auction involvement, and custody of funds before accepting the first mandate. The initial moat is narrow technical competence combined with trustworthy execution, not the number of unrelated listings copied into a spreadsheet.

Three chapter-defined mappings convert sourcing into qualified demand: retirement sourcing, specification matching, and buyer alerts. Retirement sourcing targets scheduled replacements rather than emergency liquidation rumors. Thirty existing fleet or plant contacts yielding six planned dispositions and two signed mandates can produce one eventual $10,000 fee at a 50% mandate close rate; the fallback is a dated follow-up calendar when owners cannot substantiate timing. Specification matching starts with application requirements, not superficial model-name similarity. Ten qualified requests, three compatible assets per request, and two completed $150,000 purchases at 6% yield $18,000 of commissions across that cohort. Reject an uncertain match or commission an independent technical review rather than concealing a voltage, capacity, or control mismatch. Buyer alerts distribute verified opportunities to an opted-in segment with matching geography, budget, and machine class. Eighty recipients producing eight substantive replies, two inspections, and one $250,000 closing at 4% yield $10,000. Pause alerts when records are stale and reconfirm availability with the owner. These examples describe distinct funnel views, not additive revenue forecasts: one machine can appear in sourcing, matching, and alerts while producing only one contracted commission.

The remaining chapter-defined mappings are inspection coordination, lien-clearance orchestration, and fleet disposition packaging. Inspection coordination gives buyers an independent route to condition evidence: five $900 inspections cost buyers $4,500, while two resulting $200,000 closings at 5% produce $20,000 of broker commissions. The inspection spend belongs to the provider, not the brokerage. If testing is inconclusive, renegotiate inspection scope or withdraw the recommendation. Lien-clearance orchestration turns an encumbered asset into an executable settlement. A $200,000 price less an $80,000 secured payoff and a $10,000 commission leaves $110,000 for the seller before other agreed charges; only $10,000 is operator revenue. Stop settlement when the creditor will not supply asset-specific release instructions. Fleet disposition packaging can group ten $40,000 units into a $400,000 transaction at 4%, generating $16,000 rather than ten invented minimum commissions. Split the lot into documented individual releases if one title blocks the package. The comparison matrix shows where these mappings become defensible specialties. ## 3. Build the transfer file before exposing either party to settlement risk  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Treat the transfer file as the product that converts a listing into an executable sale. Start with the seller's exact entity name, formation jurisdiction, authorized signer, machine identifier, acquisition records, location, and existing secured-creditor information. Under UCC section 9-301, debtor location generally determines the law governing perfection for many ordinary security interests, subject to important exceptions. Section 9-503 makes debtor-name accuracy central to effective filings. Search the appropriate filing office using the correct debtor identity and investigate prior names, blanket collateral descriptions, fixture issues, and titled-vehicle systems where relevant. A serial-number web search is not a substitute for this work. Under section 9-315, a security interest generally continues in collateral after disposition unless the secured party authorizes a transfer free of that interest or another applicable rule changes the result. Obtain creditor-confirmed payoff instructions and a release covering the specific asset. A payoff quote alone does not establish the promised release. Section 9-513 addresses termination statements, but the transaction may require a partial collateral release rather than termination of a filing that still secures other assets. Preserve evidence of the actual release mechanism and its conditions.

Condition evidence needs its own discipline because clean ownership does not mean a usable machine. Separate seller representations, broker observations, and independent inspection findings in the offering file. For a CNC machine, record whether the spindle ran under load, which axes were exercised, which alarms appeared, and whether the inspection included geometric accuracy testing. For a loader, distinguish a walkaround from oil analysis, hydraulic pressure measurement, or an operating-temperature test. An inspector's limited scope should remain visible in the buyer's decision record. UCC section 2-313 addresses express warranties created by affirmations, descriptions, or samples that become part of the bargain; section 2-316 governs exclusions or modifications with requirements that depend on the warranty involved. An as-is phrase does not automatically erase contradictory factual promises. State enactments and the broker's actual role matter. Route purchase-contract drafting to the appropriate professional and keep marketing statements consistent with the signed allocation of condition risk. Record included attachments and consumables individually. If a seller replaces a spindle, removes tooling, or changes operating status after inspection, notify the buyer and refresh acceptance evidence before funds move.

Settlement choreography must connect money, creditor release, buyer acceptance, insurance, and physical removal without relying on a single forwarded email. Use an independent escrow provider whose actual service supports industrial equipment and the proposed payment flow; verify its identity and applicable authority directly. Confirm wire instructions through a previously established telephone number, with a second person checking changes. Define what releases funds, who confirms those conditions, and whether possession precedes or follows the release of funds. Record responsibility for disconnection, rigging, loading, transport damage, storage overruns, and failed pickup. Export transactions require a separate fact pattern review: determine whether the equipment is subject to the EAR, establish classification, and evaluate destination, end user, and end use. 15 CFR 734.3 addresses items subject to the EAR; Parts 738, 744, and 746 address important classification, end-use, and destination controls. Used machinery is not automatically outside those rules. Assign exporter responsibilities explicitly and preserve required records under Part 762. If classification or a restricted-party match remains unresolved, stop the affected export and seek a documented resolution rather than routing the asset through another buyer to obscure the original destination.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Open a controlled asset record
   - Assign one deal identifier and collect the signed seller mandate.
   - Attach owner evidence, machine identifiers, source permissions, commission terms, and the first verified availability date.
2. Resolve technical and ownership exceptions
   - Commission the agreed inspection and complete the relevant lien and title work.
   - Maintain separate exception lists for condition, creditor release, licensing, and any proposed export.
3. Reconcile the settlement sequence
   - Match the purchase agreement, payoff documents, escrow instructions, and removal plan.
   - Confirm recipients independently and identify which evidence authorizes each disbursement and physical handoff.
4. Close the commission ledger
   - Document the service completion event and record only the earned brokerage fee.
   - Track collections, contingent amounts, refund obligations, and post-closing exceptions separately from equipment value.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four checks before scheduling the closing**
> 
> Each check needs a named owner, dated evidence, and a specific resolution path. A persuasive seller message is not a substitute.
> - Verify seller authority, exact machine identity, applicable dealer or auction requirements, and permission to market the asset.
> - Confirm lien-search scope, creditor payoff validity, asset-specific release language, and any separate vehicle-title requirements.
> - Reconcile the inspection scope, sale inclusions, condition representations, and buyer acceptance requirements against the latest asset state.
> - Confirm escrow identity, payout instructions, export disposition where relevant, and the written sequence for funds, release, and removal.

At $200,000 per completed machine sale, that means ten, forty-five, and eighty closings annually; larger assets or different negotiated fees change deal counts without changing the canonical scorecard. Do not add buyer funds, creditor payoffs, or inspection invoices to those revenue totals. Likewise, the seven operating plays are stages and capabilities supporting these closings, not seven independent income streams. Count each earned commission once against a unique deal identifier and reconcile every reversal to that original entry.

Those ranges are planning inputs, not interchangeable promises of runway. Nine months at the lowest stated monthly burn consumes $22,500 before exceptional travel, setup, or transaction costs; a $10,000 starting balance cannot fund that path without lower spending or another documented source of cash. Annualizing the monthly range gives $30,000 to $168,000, which does not describe the complete cost stack implied by the Base or Scaled cases. Treat that range as baseline overhead only when using these scenarios, and separately budget all transaction-linked labor, selling costs, travel, insurance, and other operating charges so the annual totals reconcile. The contractor count does not specify full-time equivalents or compensation. Build role-based scopes for technical inspection, documentation support, and marketing rather than assuming three contractors cost nothing between closings. Maintain a thirteen-week cash forecast showing committed outflows, uncertain commission dates, and downside delays. Signed mandates and buyer inquiries belong in pipeline reports; neither is spendable cash nor earned commission without the required contractual performance.
