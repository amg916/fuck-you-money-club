## Executive Summary

Operators working salvage, surplus, auctions, consignment, finance brokerage, and import deals converge on one playbook: buy or broker equipment the market has mislabeled, and own the buyer network rather than the iron. EquipmentWorld's contractor has run a flood-submerged machine about 2,000 hours after a cheap rebuild; Vantor Surplus shows Code H bulldozers whose fix starts with a $10 filter and takes about half a day; CapitalCode turns a $1,500 F-150 into $4,000 of parts. Plain Finance's auction house stacks a 6% seller fee on a 10% buyer premium, earning $3,200 per $20K machine and $500K in year three. Jermane Cheathem reports $50M+ in brokered equipment deals. SwiftBridge Trading ships excavators only against verified purchase orders on 50/50 terms. The split: Plain Finance lost $150K on a net guarantee, while Flying Wheels refuses $5,000-$6,000 monthly rent and a three-year lease.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Across salvage yards, military surplus lots, and junkyards, the operators agree that the money in adjacent equipment plays is made on the buy side, by taking iron the market has written off. EquipmentWorld's Wisconsin contractor bought an East Coast machine that was completely submerged when the tunnel pumps quit, paid what they called a definitely cheaper price set by 'your checkbook,' pulled the oil pan, cleaned the underside, chased a few ground issues, replaced the battery wires, and has since run roughly 2,000 hours on it [w3saQAgnyLI @ 00:00:47]. Vantor Surplus describes the same pattern inside government disposal: brand-new bulldozers get coded unserviceable after a lapsed 36-month depot inspection, but storage damage is mostly seals, sulfated batteries, and gummed injectors while internals stay fine, and the fix often starts with a $10 filter because inspectors log units dead without a compression check [lExAdQTwxNk @ 00:05:59]. CapitalCode runs the identical logic at the vehicle level: a wrecked F-150 bought for $1,500 is pulled into $4,000 of parts, netting about $1,400 in operating profit after tow, labor, and other costs [5TO0VTTHS0w @ 00:01:54]. The shared rule is that the discount comes from a paperwork or condition label, not from a dead machine.

The second point of agreement is that the durable asset in brokerage, consignment, and auctions is the buyer network, not the inventory. Plain Finance's auction-house operator won a contested job because, in the speaker's words, 'What won the job wasn't a price, but a network he could see,' after building the business on hand-found specialist buyers, honest video, and inspections [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:13:17]. HGreg Lux sells consignment on the same lever: selling yourself means photos, advertising, vetting the buyer, getting a bank to pay a private seller, and paperwork, while selling to a dealer lands below market value, so the consignor's network, multi-platform ads, detailing, and paperwork deliver an agreed net price where the owner 'didn't have to do a thing' [8w7SLSenX5k @ 00:00:49]. Jermane Cheathem, who reports more than $50M in equipment deals as a broker, tells new equipment finance brokers to partner with equipment dealers rather than chase borrowers one by one, and points out the business runs nationwide while mortgage stays local [5RrEsxEZyes @ 00:01:03]. SwiftBridge Trading applies the rule to cross-border iron: no machine ships to Ghana until a verified buyer issues a purchase order, and only then does the shipment follow [XbLz7kvZIwc @ 00:05:16]. The broker's job is matching, and the match comes before the money moves.

The third consensus point is to keep fixed overhead small and verify before committing capital. Flying Wheels' Craig, who has opened three prior dealerships, is restarting in Florida on a shoestring budget under $5,000, refusing $5,000 to $6,000 monthly rent and a three-year lease, saying 'I don't want to just go spend three, four, $5,000 in overhead,' and targeting a shop with seven spots plus bays instead of carrying 30 cars [en-0Xomp1UQ @ 00:13:40]. Lanser Supply builds its whole pitch on vetting rather than owning: 'We don't own the factories,' but every plant is audited before it carries an order, with Zeiss and Hexagon CMM full inspection behind a plus-or-minus 0.002 mm tolerance claim [Vp-CvqpL_ZM @ 00:03:05]. Forgotten Factories Canada shows the downside when the opposite happens on the owner side: at Kelsey-Hayes Windsor, 'Equipment that needed upgrading was patched instead,' overcapacity crept in as a quiet killer, and after the closure the millwrights tagged the equipment that went south to Romulus, Michigan for $8.5M [XuXHM5is32c @ 00:23:09]. The 254,000 sq ft building itself went to Veltri in 1992, which went bankrupt in 2004, before passing to Flex-N-Gate.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Plain Finance lays out the auction-house numbers to the dollar. The operating company was bought for $175K, roughly $110K of it gear, paid as $55K cash plus a $120K seller note running about $2,900 a month for four years, with another $60K added as working capital; the prior owner had handled $3.2M in a year and kept only $22K [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:01:38]. The fee engine is a two-sided take: 'The seller pays us 6%,' and the buyer pays a 10% premium, so a $20K machine earns the house $3,200, and a grinder sold to hand-found specialist buyers hammered at $38K for $6,080 in fees [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:02:57]. Year one ran honest 'Unsold' passes and closed at $4.2M handled and $150K kept; by year three the house ran 10 auctions, handled $7.6M, produced $500K in operating earnings, and refused guarantees after a $520K net guarantee blew up [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:14:31]. Jermane Cheathem frames the finance-side version: equipment finance is the 'wild wild west,' where 'Most states don't require any licensing,' and the broker works a partner stack of underwriters, lenders, and sellers, with the speaker reporting more than $50M in equipment deals [5RrEsxEZyes @ 00:05:27].

The salvage-side mechanics are equally specific. EquipmentWorld's contractor sources from insurance companies and salvagedsale.com, buys underwater units, and passes on burned ones as 'a whole nother challenge,' then schedules the rebuilds for the Wisconsin winter so crews have work in the off-season [w3saQAgnyLI @ 00:01:46]. Vantor Surplus gives the revival sequence for stored dozers: battery disconnect, transfer pump, pilot hose, two 12V batteries in series, and clean diesel, roughly half a day of work, on targets like the D7G and D7R II with 3306 and C9 engines, the Deere 850J, and the Case M4K [lExAdQTwxNk @ 00:09:29]. The same speaker's pre-bid checklist matches the data plate to the DLA catalog, checks for milky fluids, pin bosses, and grouser height, and then reads the demil code: DEMIL A and Q transfer intact, higher codes mean the machine gets torch-cut, and buyers should expect a 50,000-lb lowboy, permits, and an off-highway-only title [lExAdQTwxNk @ 00:12:24]. CapitalCode scales the junkyard version to 40 cars a month at $3,500 each for $140K in revenue; at $2,300 variable cost per car that leaves about $48K contribution and $20K to $30K a month net, with scrap adding $400 to $600 on the F-150 example [5TO0VTTHS0w @ 00:07:00].

For cross-border and deal-flow brokering, the workflows are scripted. SwiftBridge Trading's intake captures brand, model, weight, engine, bucket, hours, and budget before sourcing, and works three machine tiers of 20 to 22 ton, 30 to 36 ton, and 45-ton-plus, with refurbished units also on the table, against a Ghana market that imported about US$205M of excavators in 2023 [XbLz7kvZIwc @ 00:05:39]. Its payment terms run 50% against a copy of the bill of lading and 50% within 15 days of arrival at Tema, varying deal by deal, and the stated expansion path after machines is parts, service, then rental and leasing [XbLz7kvZIwc @ 00:06:06]. Alex Zastre applies the same broker logic to oil: research the sector with GPT, build lists in Apollo, send a cold-email template, and close on a discovery call, charging $35K upfront for a year-long engagement plus a per-deal fee that runs 1%, or $500K on a $50M deal and $5M on a $500M deal [9EgxHbU-ajg @ 00:03:14]. Easy Business prices the service-machine version: an $8,500 laser rust remover billed at $40 to $60 per part or $70 to $90 an hour, with three to four jobs a day producing about $200 or more [SSM9tqoMGu4 @ 00:05:23].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Source Written-Off Iron
   - Buy flood-submerged or surplus-coded units through insurance and salvage channels.
   - Source from insurance companies and salvagedsale.com, take underwater units, and pass on burned units as 'a whole nother challenge' [EquipmentWorld — w3saQAgnyLI @ 00:01:46].
2. Run the Pre-Bid Inspection
   - Verify identity and wear before committing a dollar to a surplus machine.
   - Match the data plate to the DLA catalog, check for milky fluids, pin bosses, and grouser height, then confirm DEMIL A or Q and plan for a 50,000-lb lowboy and permits [Vantor Surplus — lExAdQTwxNk @ 00:11:28].
3. Lock the Buyer Before the Shipment
   - Take a full spec sheet and a purchase order before sourcing or shipping.
   - Capture brand, model, weight, engine, bucket, hours, and budget, require a verified buyer's purchase order, then ship on 50% against bill of lading copy and 50% within 15 days of arrival [SwiftBridge Trading LLP — XbLz7kvZIwc @ 00:05:16].
4. Price the Exit on Both Sides
   - Stack a seller commission and a buyer premium on every consigned sale.
   - Charge the seller 6% and the buyer a 10% premium so a $20K machine yields $3,200, and keep seller proceeds out of the operating account [Plain Finance — 9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:02:57].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether you own the iron or own the relationship. Lanser Supply runs a pure vetting model: “We don’t own the factories,” yet it routes one purchase order across 70+ CNC machines with 6 m beds and audits every plant before that plant carries an order [Vp-CvqpL_ZM @ 00:03:05]. Jermane Cheathem argues the same asset-light logic from the finance side, telling brokers to partner with equipment dealers rather than chase borrowers, because equipment finance runs nationwide while mortgage stays local [5RrEsxEZyes @ 00:01:03]. CapitalCode pushes back with an owner’s balance sheet: a junkyard sitting on a $1M property producing $300k a year must subtract roughly $100k a year in land opportunity cost before anyone calls it profitable [5TO0VTTHS0w @ 00:04:47]. Flying Wheels lands in the middle. Craig, a three-time dealership founder, is restarting in Florida on a shoestring budget, refusing $5,000-to-$6,000 monthly rent and a three-year lease, and says flatly, “I don’t want to just go spend three, four, $5,000 in overhead” [en-0Xomp1UQ @ 00:13:40]. The operator read: ownership buys margin control and inventory depth, while brokering and vetting buy speed and geographic reach, and each camp treats the other camp’s fixed costs as the main risk to survival.

Fee structure is the second fault line: commission on someone else’s asset versus taking principal risk. HGreg Lux sells consignment as the escape from two bad options, since selling yourself means photos, ads, buyer vetting, getting a bank to pay a private seller and paperwork, while selling to a dealer means a below-market number; consignors get an agreed net price and “didn’t have to do a thing” [8w7SLSenX5k @ 00:00:49]. Plain Finance’s auction-house case runs the commission stack explicitly: the seller pays 6% and the buyer pays a 10% premium, so a $20K machine earns $3,200, and by year three the house refused guarantees outright [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:02:57]. SwiftBridge Trading refuses to speculate on stock at all, sequencing verified buyer, then purchase order, then shipment, with 50% paid against a bill-of-lading copy and 50% within 15 days of arrival at Tema [XbLz7kvZIwc @ 00:05:16]. Alex Zastre goes furthest toward getting paid before any deal closes, charging $35k upfront for a year-long engagement plus a per-deal broker fee, and Zastre pegs the back end at 1%, meaning $500k on a $50M deal and $5M on a $500M deal [9EgxHbU-ajg @ 00:02:11].

The third split is salvage-and-repair versus buy-new-and-produce. EquipmentWorld’s Wisconsin contractor sources fully submerged units from insurance companies and salvagedsale.com, prices them off “your checkbook,” and has logged about 2,000 hours on one flood unit since repair, but draws a hard line at burned machines, calling them “a whole nother challenge” [w3saQAgnyLI @ 00:01:46]. Vantor Surplus makes the same contrarian bet on Pentagon disposals, arguing that stored condition-coded dozers often need only a battery disconnect, transfer pump, pilot hose, two 12V batteries in series and clean diesel, roughly half a day of work [lExAdQTwxNk @ 00:09:29]. The buy-new camp starts from a catalog instead. Garage Profits lists a $300 plastic shredder through a $2,200 Styrofoam densifier and argues “you’re not selling trash, you’re selling raw material,” yet posts no earnings figures [InQ7-NaPvxs @ 00:01:36]. Easy Business gives harder unit math: a CNC wood lathe turns $0.60 of cost into a $2-to-$5 piece, an $8,500 laser rust remover bills $40 to $60 per part, and a $650-to-$1,000 sublimation setup pays back after about 300 mugs in one to two months [SSM9tqoMGu4 @ 00:01:37]. Salvage players trade mechanical labor for discount; machine buyers trade capital for repeatable output.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure on record is the net guarantee. In Plain Finance’s auction-house case, the operator guaranteed a seller $520K net with no reserves, leaning on $620K in comps, three qualified inspections and $165K in cash, on terms that paid a 12% premium plus any upside, minus $50K in event costs [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:06:18]. The best machine carried $190K comps and hammered at $84K, the sale totaled $375K, and the $145K shortfall cost the house $150K, leaving $15K against a $9K payroll, with seller proceeds “not one dollar” usable because “the sellers’ proceeds never touch your account” [9kP-CdkchRk @ 00:11:48]. The lesson the narrator draws is brutal: comps are not bidders, and “Two is a market.” Recovery came from refusing to cover an $11K reserve gap and winning jobs on a visible network rather than price. Forgotten Factories Canada shows the slow version of the same arithmetic at Kelsey-Hayes Windsor: “Equipment that needed upgrading was patched instead,” overcapacity became “a quiet killer,” and the plant closed with 15 days’ notice before its equipment went to Romulus, Michigan for $8.5M [XuXHM5is32c @ 00:23:09]. Both cases punish operators who let fixed obligations outrun real, present demand.

Condition and buying discipline decide whether cheap iron stays cheap. CapitalCode shows how wide the spread runs: Yard A turns a $150k buying budget into $350k in sales while Yard B turns the same $150k into $220k, and the gap comes entirely from what each yard chose to buy [5TO0VTTHS0w @ 00:06:05]. The same breakdown flags luxury cars with damaged electronics and EV batteries as the inventory that wrecks returns [5TO0VTTHS0w @ 00:06:43]. Vantor Surplus warns that government inspectors log stored units dead without running a compression check, so the buyer’s edge is real, but the trap is the DEMIL code: A and Q transfer intact, higher codes mean torch-cut, and even a clean dozer demands a 50,000-lb lowboy, permits and an off-highway-only title, which is why the rule is “Read the demil code first” [lExAdQTwxNk @ 00:12:24]. EquipmentWorld’s flood unit still needed the oil pan pulled, battery wires replaced and “a few ground issues” chased down before it earned its 2,000 hours [w3saQAgnyLI @ 00:01:29]. The Wood Whisperer adds a finishing-side caution: the heated linseed, beeswax and carnauba blend is not durable, and spot repairs can become an eyesore [87nsgCs4Tzs @ 00:12:08].

Operational bottlenecks hit before the first sale. Flying Wheels walked Florida’s licensing chain from town hall to the Martin County dealer desk in Stuart to the regional FLHSMV office in West Palm, which books appointments only by phone from 8 to noon and hung up twice, then bounced the call to a compliance officer’s voicemail at what Craig calls step three [en-0Xomp1UQ @ 00:09:23]. The New Hampshire compliance officer Craig dealt with earlier was strict enough that visits felt like audits. SwiftBridge Trading lists the import-side breakpoints as payment, quality, port clearance, FX and after-sales, plus a hard refusal to sell to galamsey buyers as tracking and import controls tighten [XbLz7kvZIwc @ 00:10:21]. Keentel Engineering shows the same choke point at utility scale: missing dynamic models produce “a hard schedule stop,” cogeneration means “if the turbine goes down, your cooling might go down with it,” and OEM diligence has to pin contractual delivery dates, lifecycle cost and 2028 service depth [UxhrvcRGfdc @ 00:11:20]. Even adoption eats time; EquipmentWorld’s crew fought satellite and Bluetooth-to-rover-header problems for about six months before GPS became something they “wouldn’t really go without” [w3saQAgnyLI @ 00:02:59].
