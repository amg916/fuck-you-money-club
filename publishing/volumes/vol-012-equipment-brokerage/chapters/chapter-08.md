## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Scott Woods of Freight Profits opens every heavy-haul bid the same way: get the exact dimensions and weight of the piece, then let those numbers dictate the trailer before any rate is spoken [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:04:13]. Woods draws a hard line between oversize and heavy haul. A 105-inch-wide piece on a flatbed may need nothing more than a permit and can be priced close to a standard flatbed or step-deck run. True heavy haul is priced as the truck rate plus plus plus, where each plus is permits, pilot cars, police, or power assistance with a bucket truck running alongside to raise the lines [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:05:27]. Trinity Logistics frames the trigger points the same way. Anything over 75 feet long, 8 feet 6 inches wide, 13 feet 6 inches tall, or 80,000 pounds including truck, trailer, and cargo crosses the federal guideline, and every state layers its own limits on top [oQI9qiLLIwA @ 00:00:34]. Both treat the permit as mandatory. Trinity states that any load exceeding legal size or weight must be permitted in each state it travels through, with the exact route written onto the permit and significant fines for breaking its conditions [oQI9qiLLIwA @ 00:02:36]. Woods adds that the brokers who skip this homework are the ones calling for help after the truck is already rolling and the load is already losing money.

Practitioners agree the equipment and the driver have to be matched to the piece, not to whatever truck happens to be posted on a load board. HeavyHaul HQ walks a Trail King spec as a checklist. A flip neck extends swing clearance and pushes more trailer weight onto the truck. A flip axle puts three axles on the ground when scaling. A pony motor kit powers the trailer hydraulics without truck wet lines [W5IGVlejm0s @ 00:01:58]. The same channel notes that a 55-ton trailer runs a 54.5-inch axle spread while a 60-ton unit runs a 60-inch spread, which can earn more allowed weight depending on the state [W5IGVlejm0s @ 00:05:09]. Over the Road, through ATS heavy haul operations manager Shelby, lays out the human side of the same rule. Drivers start as 7-axle operators on three-axle and 3+1 trailers, handling pieces up to 15 feet 6 inches wide, 14 feet 11 inches tall, or 120 feet on an expandable, and up to 80,000 pounds of piece weight [xRwL0v4jyOs @ 00:01:31]. Moving to multi-axle typically takes one to two years, plus a doubles and triples endorsement, specific classes, multiple multi-axle loads, and a claim-free record [xRwL0v4jyOs @ 00:02:20]. Matching axle count and operator class to the piece is the first control on every move.

On the fleet side, practitioners agree that preventive maintenance and a work order system come before anything else. Yvette Aubin, CFO of Ebsary Foundation and chair of CFMA's National Heavy Construction Committee, ranks the lack of a preventive and predictive maintenance program as the top financial risk of owning heavy equipment. Next on Aubin's list is an underutilized fleet that keeps burning taxes, interest, registration, and insurance while it sits, followed by equipment rates that never make it back to the bidding department [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:07:20]. Aubin's first corrective step for any contractor lagging peers is to install a work order system and then focus it on preventive and predictive work [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:20:16]. Equipment & Contracting gives owners the same instruction: build a schedule off mileage, time, engine hours, and fuel consumed, with a task checklist per machine, an operator system for logging inspections and complaints, and periodic staff meetings on fleet status [HIhx8sY-aac @ 00:01:42]. Simply Fleet pitches the same workflow in software. Preventive maintenance is triggered by mileage, engine hours, or time. Inspections run on mobile, photo issue reports become work orders, and service history stays tied to each asset [z_9T-5cJEc0 @ 00:00:34]. The logic underneath is simple. Downtime on a machine becomes downtime on the job, and downtime on the job drives schedule and profitability.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Woods prices superloads by walking the move state by state. The reference job was a CAT 777 off-road rock truck, roughly 145,000 pounds, 18 feet wide, and 16 feet tall. It called for a 13-axle setup on an 80-ton Trail King, run by Henderson Heavy Haul out of Grand Junction, Colorado [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:04:13]. Loaded, the rig ran 125 feet long and 250,000 pounds, which made it a superload. Arizona required permits, pilot cars, and four state police on a night move that started at 10 p.m. Trinity Logistics confirms that extra-wide loads can trigger law enforcement escorts in some states, with their time usually paid by the hauling company [oQI9qiLLIwA @ 00:01:46]. Woods itemizes pilot pricing. The chase car bills $1.65 a mile, $85 per overnight, $250 a day plus hotel on a no-go, and a day rate near $600 when it moves under 300 miles. The pole car runs $1.85 to $2 a mile with a higher day rate because pole cars are harder to find [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:09:06]. Nevada bans weekend travel, so arriving Friday meant three no-go days at $250 each, about $1,000 with hotels, while the pilots played keno. The Kenworth W990 pulling the load gets only about 1.8 miles per gallon on the crawls [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:10:19].

The second layer of the bid is routing and time. Woods warns that PC Miler or Rand McNally might show 863 miles, but the state issues the route, and that route can come back at 1,700 miles [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:18:13]. The carrier is also pricing the empty leg. On the 777 move, the 13-axle had to deadhead about 1,200 miles to Gillette, Wyoming, because little big freight comes out of Seattle, and a carrier doing the job right bakes that leg into the number. That puts the move at $25 to $30 a mile on a 1,600-mile run, roughly a $50,000 job [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:14:07]. Permit timing breaks pickup promises. The Washington permit took three weeks, and 500,000-pound pieces on 30-axle trailers can wait four months for route surveys [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:15:56]. Trinity Logistics puts permit fees anywhere from $15 to over $70 depending on the state and size. Some states charge application fees that drop or disappear once a frequent mover sets up an account, and states offer one-time, multiple-move, and annual permits for similar hauls [oQI9qiLLIwA @ 00:02:36]. Woods also tells brokers to stay out of rigging entirely unless they know cranes. Woods has watched people lose $25,000 to $30,000 after bidding rigging into a job without a clue.

Fleet control runs on meter readings, logs, and allocation rules. In Onsite, a JCB is added as a meter-reading-based asset and tagged owned or rented, with the supplier named. The operator logs a start reading of 233 and a stop reading of 245, recording 12 hours of use, then enters 12 liters of diesel through save and add fuel and shares a PDF report with the team [djeL371Rgio @ 00:00:42]. The Profitable Contractor splits shared machines by the field log. Daily-rate rentals divide by each job's recorded equipment days, and hourly rentals divide by recorded hours. Off-rent is the confirmed billing stop, not the crew's last day of use [jLx7fLatA3A @ 00:03:08]. The channel's worked example budgets $8,000 for materials, $1,000 for rental, and $3,000 for subcontracting. At $200 a day, ten rental days cost $2,000, materials creep to $8,400, and job gross profit drops $1,400. Aubin's company runs HCSS Equipment 360 telematics. When a backhoe threw three error codes for fuel and pressure loss, the team opened a ticket and sent a mechanic out before the machine failed [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:16:49]. HCSS HeavyJob users describe replacing pen-and-paper timecards with iPad entry, where every job preloads its equipment and employees so foremen push a button instead of writing [Cbg13em0UgE @ 00:01:12].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Spec the piece and the trailer
   - Collect exact dimensions and weight, check them against the legal baseline, and pick the trailer and axle count.

2. Build the plus stack
   - Price permits, pilot cars, police, and power assistance as fixed costs on top of the truck rate.
   - Woods' Arizona leg needed permits, two pilot cars plus a pole car, and four state police on a night move, all checked before the quote went out [Freight Profits — qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:08:47].
3. Lock route, permit timing, and travel windows
   - Use the state-issued route mileage, add weekend bans and deadhead, and set pickup dates around permit lead time.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Scott Woods of Freight Profits runs heavy-haul brokerage on trust, not surveillance. Woods refuses the 9 a.m. check-call routine, lets crews run sun up to sun down, accepts a text, and when a location is needed calls the trucking company owner directly, mocking rookie brokers who demand mile-marker updates across Interstate 80 [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:07:16]. The filter sits upstream: hand the load only to a carrier already trusted, with the rate and the requirements spelled out. Matt Krause plays the opposite tempo, answering every call and email within one minute, sending rate confirmations fast, leaning on automatic invoicing and shipment trackers, and eventually outsourcing all operations to spend 100% of the day hunting shippers [QBNPafDHXLE @ 00:10:16]. Krause pegs the industry-average margin around 15%, with strong relationships pushing 20 to 25%. The two also split on how a newcomer enters. Krause says a broker with zero experience can launch for roughly $2,600 in startup costs and win by going deep in one niche, naming heavy machinery and flatbed as examples [QBNPafDHXLE @ 00:05:14]. Woods warns that everybody wants oversized and heavy-haul freight without knowing equipment, trailers, or fixed costs, and insists on a mentor who has actually moved loads before anyone bids this lane [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:16:32].

Yvette Aubin, CFO of Ebsary Foundation Company, comes down hard on ownership. The firm is not heavy on renting; the only rentals are lease-to-own trials, such as renting a crane for two to three months to confirm it performs before buying, and the purchase math leans on depreciation benefits plus a century-old pride in owning iron [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:10:11]. Aubin's counterweight is utilization: owned equipment sitting idle still burns taxes, interest if financed, registration fees, and insurance, so an underutilized fleet bleeds money [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:08:04]. Equipment & Contracting argues the other way on light units, saying there is no need to buy a work truck big enough to tow items rarely moved because a larger truck can be leased for the occasional super heavy load, while warning that leases punish operators who clock heavy miles or run jobs that are hard on a vehicle [Xv99UO78lCE @ 00:02:05]. The Profitable Contractor flags the rental trap from the cost side: the agreement controls minimum periods, weekend charges, and the event that stops billing, idle machines on site can keep accruing, and the crew's last day of use is not the same as off-rent confirmation [jLx7fLatA3A @ 00:02:32]. Each rental record carries the job number, start date, and return date.

Software choice splits by what the fleet actually needs on a daily basis. Simply Fleet pitches a maintenance-first stack for mixed fleets, where a truck follows mileage-based service while an excavator follows engine hours, and steers buyers wanting a broad construction ecosystem to Tenna or HCSS and buyers needing only general maintenance management to MaintainX or Limble [z_9T-5cJEc0 @ 00:02:37]. Aubin runs HCSS Equipment 360 and rates its telematics boxes as intuitive: one backhoe threw three error codes pointing to fuel loss and pressure loss, a ticket went in, and a mechanic went out while the machine was still running [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:16:49]. Onsite takes a manual-entry route instead, logging start and stop meter readings or clock-based hours, diesel litres, and a photo per record, then exporting a PDF to share with the team [djeL371Rgio @ 00:01:06]. Trailer specs carry their own trade-offs worth weighing. HeavyHaul HQ notes Hendrickson's Pro Max tire inflation can push air into or out of a tire, while Meritor parts are sometimes easier to find on the road [W5IGVlejm0s @ 00:03:24]. The same walkaround contrasts a 55-ton trailer with a 54.5-inch axle spread against a 60-ton unit at 60 inches that can sometimes scale more weight depending on the state.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest heavy-haul mistakes in Scott Woods's files are bidding errors. One broker bid a load without knowing pilot cars were required, lost about five grand on the move, and watched the truck hold the load hostage [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:03:03]. Former students who folded crane rigging into the job without understanding it lost $25,000 to $30,000 on a single load. Timing breaks naive bids too: the Washington permit on the Cat 777 move took three weeks, superloads can wait three weeks to a month, and 500,000-pound moves on 30-axle trailers can sit four months while states run route surveys [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:15:56]. Mileage lies on the bid sheet, because PC Miler may show 863 miles while the state-issued route runs 1,700 [qHs0sXdzamw @ 00:18:13]. Weekend curfews add dead cost on multi-state runs: Nevada bars weekend travel, so pilot cars sat Friday through Sunday at $250 a day plus hotels, roughly another $1,000 for doing nothing. The truck's 1,200-mile deadhead to Gillette, Wyoming, also lands in the rate whether the broker sees it or not. Trinity Logistics adds the enforcement edge: exact routes must be specified on each state permit, and missing a permit or breaking its conditions can bring significant fines [oQI9qiLLIwA @ 00:03:10].

On the fleet side, Yvette Aubin names the missing preventive and predictive maintenance program as the top financial risk, because it forces a reactive shop that likely spends more. The diagnostic is blunt: if only 25% of 100 annual work orders are preventive or predictive, 75% are breakdowns, major repairs, or surprises [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:13:15]. Aubin also reports from CFMA heavy construction committee polls that only 20% of contractors run a work order system, calling it low-hanging fruit [sNcACw9iXI0 @ 00:20:33], and warns that buying seven significant pieces without fleet in the room, and without enough mechanics, turns the program reactive. Equipment & Contracting puts the field version plainly: nobody wants the key crane dying halfway through the job, and neglected routine maintenance ends in expensive repairs or outright replacement [HIhx8sY-aac @ 00:01:42]. The Profitable Contractor shows how quietly rentals and material creep bleed a job. In its illustration, a $200-a-day rental budgeted for five days runs ten, costing $2,000 against a $1,000 allowance; with materials creeping from $8,000 to $8,400, the job lands at $13,400 against a $12,000 budget and gross profit drops $1,400 [jLx7fLatA3A @ 00:06:30]. Charging a shared machine's whole bill to one job distorts every project involved, so split it by logged days or hours.

Fleet control also breaks at the driver seat. thatDOTcop inspected a driver who logged 30 to 35 miles of personal conveyance to buy a sandwich at a spot closer to the next pickup; on paper it looked personal, but PC cannot be used to advance the load [zKmff6X7cQA @ 00:04:02]. The driver had no idea, and it became a teaching moment rather than a citation. Inspectors audit PC through GPS, distances, and timestamps in the eRODS view, and abuse can be written up as hours-of-service violations under falsified logs, triggering deeper inspections or out-of-service orders [zKmff6X7cQA @ 00:10:28]. Carriers can legally limit or ban PC outright, and driving to maintenance, a truck wash, or back to the company yard after unloading does not qualify. On the load itself, Trinity Logistics warns that overloading degrades steering, braking, and speed control, while poor positioning and a top-heavy profile raise rollover risk and loose cargo can fall into traffic [oQI9qiLLIwA @ 00:01:13]. Equipment failures strand rigs far from help: FMCSA Registration dot com flags corroded battery terminals in temperature extremes, worn suspensions on heavy loads, and clogged air filters that cut fuel economy [6q-JpVNYBwE @ 00:01:29]. Every one of these costs compounds on a heavy-haul tractor that Woods clocked at roughly 1.8 miles per gallon.
