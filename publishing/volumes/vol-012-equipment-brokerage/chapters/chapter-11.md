## Executive Summary

Imaging and data-center power brokers run the same play: buy equipment as a hospital or data center exits it, prove its technical identity, then resell it whole, refurbished, or as donor parts. MXR Imaging pegs reconditioned MRI and CT at 40 to 50% below new, with a one-year all-inclusive warranty as baseline, while Block Imaging says new can cost twice as much. Reclaim Atlas documents a 2026 Oak Ridge teardown where the owner was paid $12,000 because the generator and UPS resale covered removal. Sellers win on paperwork: tube scan seconds, software licenses, nameplates, battery date codes, acceptance test reports. Practitioners split on how close an independent refurb gets to OEM, whether as-is used gear is a value or a risk transfer, and whether OEM end-of-service actually ends a system's life.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The buy side runs in two hops. Reclaim Atlas reports that the party paying the original hospital is usually an equipment dealer or a manufacturer's refurbishment arm, and the clinical end buyer is another hospital, outpatient imaging center, or specialist provider [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:00:14]. MXR Imaging confirms its nationwide division buys used CT and MRI every day, and holds a refurbished fleet it puts at over 40 systems [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:28:58]. Block Imaging frames the seller's motive bluntly: hospitals dump working iron when a new AI package, contrast agent, or study type demands capabilities the secondary market cannot supply, and even gear two to three generations old still carries components that service machines one generation behind [4suol4m0dq4 @ 00:00:51]. The data-center side mirrors this. Reclaim Atlas describes specialist buyers chasing UPS systems, generators, switchgear, transformers, PDUs, transfer switches, and busway with the first goal of preserving resale value rather than scrapping, then placing that gear with another data center, hospital, factory, university, or commercial building [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:02:05]. The consensus is that the replaced unit is rarely broken; it left because the owner's requirements moved, which is exactly why a second operator can extract years of value from it.

Practitioners converge on one rule: price the documented configuration, not the shell. Reclaim Atlas warns that a listing can describe a complete system, a magnet with accessories missing, an as-is unit, or a package with refurbishment and installation, and the photos can look almost identical across all of them, so the buyer must match documented coils, software, and support to the exact exams they intend to run [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:02:17]. Block Imaging's intake list proves the same point from the sell side: year, make, and model first, then actual usage including tube age, scan seconds, and milliamp seconds on GE units, then configuration down to injectors, monitoring, Mac Labs, extra workstations, and the licensed software that reveals what the system was used for and can be used for next [4suol4m0dq4 @ 00:01:35]. MXR Imaging pushes buyers to demand an ISO 13485 certified vendor because the certification forces a documented cradle-to-grave paper trail, down to the detector map captured while reconditioning a VCT 64 [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:33:53]. On the power side, Reclaim Atlas reports buyers asking for nameplate, kilowatt rating, voltage, fuel type, operating hours, and maintenance history before paying serious money, because every major asset carries a technical identity and better records turn unknown surplus into credible equipment [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:02:36].

The baseline refurb standard is consistent across shops. Block Imaging says its team restores systems to OEM specification in three areas: image quality first, then overall performance including workstations and table movement, then cosmetics down to paint and decals, so the unit looks the way it did when it was born [Py7IK0wId1k @ 00:00:00]. MXR Imaging sets the same floor: every reconditioned system should meet all published OEM specifications and ship with an all-inclusive product warranty of at least one year, which is what produces the initial low-cost-of-ownership window [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:07:17]. Brown's Medical Imaging stresses that the interior has to be as clean as the covers, stripping a 2015 ET 660 system completely, sending covers to body and paint, running compliance, safety, and functional checks, and cleaning and coiling every cable so a customer pulling a panel never finds a dirty machine [DpGCQ1Ijs-Y @ 00:00:33]. Reclaim Atlas adds that the building becomes part of the deal: exit route, structural support, electrical supply, cooling, ventilation, and RF shielding all hit the buyer's budget and schedule, and an installation survey can turn a manageable equipment quote into a different project entirely [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:05:05]. The consensus: a refurbished system is only a bargain once the warranty, scope, and room are locked.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

MXR Imaging's Platinum workflow starts at the point of purchase with a service-life review of every documented repair and replacement date across the computer, RF, gradient, patient transport, console, table, magnet, and cryo subsystems, each plotted against OEM specs and mean time between failures, before the system is de-installed, shipped, disassembled, decontaminated, reassembled, and staged for verification [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:10:09]. The replacement triggers are hard-coded: a cold head past half its life is changed automatically with OEM remanufactured parts, while one under that mark is judged on hours and performance rather than swapped for the sake of it [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:29:43]. Tubes follow the same logic, with a fairly new tube still pulled if it has been spiking or causing image-quality problems, and the whole Platinum cycle runs 90 to 120 days, CT landing on the longer side because of all the electromechanical items [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:32:21]. Block Imaging runs a comparable relay: project manager, then a cosmetic crew that sands panels, primes, re-sands, and paints, then engineering for testing, part replacement, and full calibration, then warehouse crating for safe shipping, mechanical install on site, and a final imaging-engineer calibration before handoff to service [Py7IK0wId1k @ 00:01:10].

MXR Imaging's own cost-of-ownership math shows where the margin sits. On CT, the OEM quoted 375 against an MXR price $100,000 lower, and post-warranty service ran $89,000 a year from the OEM versus $72,000 from MXR, for $185,000 saved across the first six years [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:26:06]. On MRI the spread widens: 805 from the OEM against 595 from MXR, a 210 gap on purchase, with service at $85,000 instead of $110,000 a year, adding up to $335,000 saved over six years [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:26:53]. The warranty package backing that spread covers labor and travel 8:00 to 5:00 Monday through Friday, a 30-minute phone response, six hours on site, a 96% uptime guarantee, four planned maintenance visits a year, 100% parts and MRI coil coverage, next-morning parts delivery, and 24/7 remote monitoring of the cryo subsystem [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:24:11]. Block Imaging sets the outer brackets of the market: new can cost twice as much as the other categories, used ships as-is with every bump and bruise and only adds service parts and labor coverage at extra cost, and refurbished typically carries a one-year parts and labor warranty [VOR_RnHfhE4 @ 00:01:43].

On the data-center side, Reclaim Atlas shows how the deal actually closes. In a 2026 Oak Ridge, Tennessee shutdown, a specialist pulled a 225 kVA UPS, a 180 kW standby generator, cooling gear, and racks, cleared the room in 10 days, and paid the owner $12,000, because the recovered generator and UPS could be refurbished and resold for enough to cover the removal [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:00:13]. UPS buyers treat a 10-year-old cabinet as two assets, sending battery strings toward recycling while refurbishing the rectifiers, inverters, and controls, and they ask for model, kVA and kW, input and output voltage, age, service history, battery date codes, and matching bypass gear, since a 480 volt three-phase unit only sells to a site that can take it [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:03:37]. Vision Matrix Institute gives the switchgear number a buyer has to match: 25 kiloamp short-time withstand is the modern default for 33 kV systems, meaning the gear survives a 25,000 amp short circuit for a full 3 seconds [6nfvCdjM9lM @ 00:04:50]. Keentel Engineering's worked example shows why the IT number alone never sizes the iron: a 500 kW IT load grows to roughly 930 kW continuous once cooling, losses, support loads, and 20% growth are added, specified as two 1,250 kW generators in N+1 [nico3vxC_dA @ 00:11:16].

1. Build the Asset Dossier
   - Compile year, make, model, usage counters, configuration, licensed software, and photos before contacting any buyer or seller.

2. Walk the Site Before Demolition Crews
   - Photograph nameplates, check ratings, review service records, and calculate what can come out without destroying its value.
   - Specialist buyers get in ahead of demolition, then electricians shut systems down, riggers pull the equipment, and batteries split off toward recycling; careless cutting or a basement with no exit route can wipe out resale value [Reclaim Atlas — jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:09:34].
3. Test to Prove Technical Identity
   - Load bank the generators, inspect UPS wear items and bypass, and run oil and turns ratio tests on transformers.

4. Recondition, Stage, and Hand Off
   - Reassemble, test every coil and subsystem, stage the full system for verification, then ship, install, and calibrate on site.
   - After the physical process the system is reassembled for testing, all coils are evaluated, reconditioned, and system tested, computer subsystems are verified, and the system is staged before it ships to its new home [MXR Imaging — Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:10:55].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is over which tier of imaging iron to buy or broker. Jordan at Block Imaging lays out three lanes: new can cost twice as much as the other categories, used ships as-is with every bump and bruise from its prior home, and refurbished sits in the middle with all components tested, calibrated to OEM specs, repainted, and backed by a 1-year parts and labor warranty. Block calls refurbished the best balance of capital outlay and system longevity [VOR_RnHfhE4 @ 00:02:46]. Jordan still concedes that a used purchase, with due diligence and add-on service coverage from a reputable provider, can be a strong value. MXR Imaging pushes its reconditioned lane harder, stating that 40 to 50% reductions from new are not uncommon at all and that every reconditioned system should carry an all-inclusive warranty of at least one year [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:06:53]. Block Imaging's acquisitions team flips the logic for sellers: a facility chasing new studies built on new isotopes, contrast agents, AI, or software has to buy new, because that capability will not surface on the secondary market [4suol4m0dq4 @ 00:00:18]. Reclaim Atlas attacks the bargain-bin stereotype, pointing to Terveystalo choosing a refurbished MRI for its Tikkurila location, with selected components and software modernized, as a deliberate investment rather than an emergency compromise [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:01:16].

On service, the line runs between OEM loyalty and independent operators. MXR Imaging's product specialist argues the only thing separating an MXR refurb from an OEM refurb is that the OEM owns the software license and the 510k, and that otherwise MXR machines are as nice if not nicer [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:33:06]. Probo Medical UK attacks the OEM end-of-service declaration head-on: when an OEM says a system is end of service, that does not always mean end of value, and its OEM-trained engineers can often extend system life at a much lower cost than the manufacturer, without being tied to any single manufacturer's limitations [MZI2TSrdxAQ @ 00:00:58]. Reclaim Atlas applies the buyer-side brake, noting that Philips advertises warranty and service support on its circular edition MRI systems, but that this does not mean every used scanner comes with equivalent terms, so buyers must ask what is covered, which parts are excluded, and who responds when a fault appears [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:07:52]. Wear-part policy splits as well. MXR does not swap cold heads just to swap them; a cold head past half its life is changed automatically, anything younger gets evaluated against its mean time between failures, and replacements come from OEM remanufactured stock rather than being thrown in for show [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:29:43].

On the data center power side, practitioners split over what to buy whole, what to part out, and what to spec. Reclaim Atlas argues complete powertrains can be more attractive than isolated pieces, because a generator sold with its transfer switch and paralleling gear preserves equipment already designed to work together, while assets that fail the second-life test slide down the value ladder into donor boards, power modules, breakers, and eventually copper and steel [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:07:41]. Bridge time is the next fork. Keentel Engineering describes UPS batteries carrying the full facility load for a few minutes up to around 15, just long enough for generators to start and stabilize [kE7QqYqWub8 @ 00:03:02]. Keentel then pegs VRLA at a 3 to 5 year service life with lower upfront cost and tighter cooling needs, against lithium-ion at 10 or more years with an integrated battery management system, steering VRLA toward cost-driven or legacy installs and lithium toward space-constrained or long life cycle builds [kE7QqYqWub8 @ 00:03:19]. Vision Matrix Institute pushes the opposite edge, warning that a one-minute lithium UPS paired with generators needing 50 seconds to stabilize and take load leaves just 10 seconds between a smooth transition and total facility blackout, and asks whether the DG plant is truly designed fast enough to catch that load [6nfvCdjM9lM @ 00:12:40].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common imaging loss is a deal that looks cheap at quote and turns expensive at the building. Reclaim Atlas walks through a clinic that found an attractive scanner without checking its building: the equipment quote looks manageable, then the installation survey reveals additional work, and the actual project becomes a different proposition even though the advertised price never moved [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:05:21]. The same channel warns buyers to pin down the handover point, whether seller responsibility ends when the equipment is collected, when it reaches the building, or after agreed installation tests, because headline-price shoppers miss that difference entirely and inherit the unexpected problems [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:08:20]. Brown's Medical Imaging flags the access squeeze from the installer's side, noting crews sometimes have to strip covers just to clear challenging doorways, and that a buyer who paid for preconditioned equipment does not want beautiful covers hiding a dirty, nasty interior [DpGCQ1Ijs-Y @ 00:00:15]. MXR Imaging adds the shop-floor danger: a CT gantry spinning during a scan pulls G loading in excess of six Gs, and a CT without covers is a very dangerous machine, which is part of why CT reconditioning, with all its bearings, bushings, and adjustments, runs longer than MRI work [Nw9qRw-Y6TE @ 00:15:37].

Data center power gear loses its resale value during extraction more often than on the quote sheet. Reclaim Atlas reports that a desirable generator can be expensive to pull without a crane, a valuable UPS can be trapped in a basement with no practical route out, and careless cutting can destroy the exact bus sections and breakers that make switchgear worth reselling, which is why specialist buyers value the removal project as well as the machine [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:06:00]. The same channel says voltage can end the conversation quickly, because a 480 volt 3-phase UPS is only useful to a site that can accommodate it, and a bargain machine with the wrong configuration becomes expensive inventory [jfdR4HzT3cU @ 00:03:53]. Broma Engineering, working the commissioning side, warns that the logistics plan has to be thought out well in advance or a transformer delivery turns into absolute chaos, with doors left off, drywall openings enlarged, and corridors cleared so nobody ends up under a landing transformer [hukziWCxjUY @ 00:03:06]. Broma also flags the RACI matrix as a delay trap: if roles between vendor, electrical contractor, CXA, general contractor, and client team are not clearly defined before the project starts, testing the transformer-to-switchboard connection gets messy and causes delays [hukziWCxjUY @ 00:08:31].

Sizing errors are the quiet money-killers on the data center side. Keentel Engineering separates two failure modes: undersizing trips protective devices on the first hot design day, while oversizing never trips but strands capital, keeps no-load losses running, and drags down PUE [nico3vxC_dA @ 00:02:12]. Keentel's case study shows a design that sized generators on average PUE and used a simplified cooling calc; the first chiller restart would have pushed the generators toward an unacceptable operating condition, forcing a resized generator plant, staggered mechanical restarts, and a corrected heat load to avoid a potentially catastrophic summer outage [nico3vxC_dA @ 00:17:52]. Vision Matrix Institute calls sizing the main incomer for day-one load a classic junior engineer trap, because 18 months later a new tenant maxes it out and a live MV panel cannot be casually swapped. The fix is blunt: always size the incomer for the absolute future design capacity of the board, not just today's initial load [6nfvCdjM9lM @ 00:04:20]. NexLayer Tech closes the loop on operations: if PSU 1 fails unnoticed and PSU 2 fails days later, a redundant system has silently become a single point of failure, which is why monitoring of load, UPS status, battery condition, generator status, and alarms is essential [o7egpgd1LLI @ 00:11:39].
