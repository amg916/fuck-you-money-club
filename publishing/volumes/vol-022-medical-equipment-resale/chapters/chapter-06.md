# Chapter 06: FDA Device Regulation Fundamentals

*Device classification case studies, 510(k) vs. De Novo vs. PMA, FDA 101 webinars, CDRH industry basics, REdI conference sessions, CLIA-waived point-of-care IVDs, distributors filing 510(k)s for Chinese devices, and the 510(k) predicate loophole and litigation*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from FDA, Registrar Corp, leanRAQA, NSF, Invent Healthcare and independent consultants converge on one rule: the three-letter product code decides class, pathway and cost, and 'exempt' never means unregulated. Class I runs about 45-50% of devices, the 510(k) is the dominant route, and only PMA devices are 'approved.' Street numbers: the 2025 510(k) fee is $24,335 standard versus $6,084 small business, establishment registration hits $11,423 in 2026, and FDA's 90-day 510(k) clock runs about 200 days in practice. Small-business status can cut fees up to 75%, but paying before qualifying forfeits any refund. Isaac Erickson argues the 510(k) holder controls the US market and that distributors can file. Disagreements surface on wording ('approval' versus 'clearance'), inspection frequency (every 2 versus 3-4 years), and whether recalled predicates turn the 510(k) into a loophole.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The product code is the chokepoint every practitioner routes through first. Easy Medical Device's guest calls the three-letter code 'probably the single most important piece of information that a company needs to know about,' because it defines the device, the governing regulation and the intended use, and it dictates both class and pathway [USqn2QdZTCM @ 00:03:25]. Registrar Corp places the rules in 21 CFR 800-899 and confirms that every device type carries its own three-letter product code [GUA_JDVPQG0 @ 00:04:29]. NextGenMedTech flags product codes as the shortcut through the rulebook ('This will help you a lot') and maps class to pathway: Class I runs on general controls and is mostly exempt with a few 510(k)s, Class II stacks general plus special controls and mostly needs a 510(k), and Class III layers PMA on top of general controls [sB46aiZG8lw @ 00:30:42]. The DICOM Code & Cure operator, who took a computed tomography x-ray system to clearance under Class II product code JAK inside the 21 CFR 892 radiology regulations, adds the execution rule: 'you want to keep the same product code as your predict device because that will make more sense to substantiate its equivalence' [l_IqH_YvZ68 @ 00:06:58]. Code first; fees, testing, submission type and timeline all follow from it.

The second point of total agreement: exempt does not mean free. NSF puts Class I at about 47% of devices and states flatly, 'Many of them are exempt from 510k but that does not mean they are not regulated' [gfFy3QwozGE @ 00:05:00]. leanRAQA splits the market as Class I 45-50%, Class II 40-45% and Class III 5-10%, then warns that exempt products still carry registration, listing (contract manufacturers included), and complaint and quality-record procedures [EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:06:26]. FDA's Kendra Holter warns at the 2023 REdI conference that '510k exempt' does not mean no controls apply, then demonstrates with an air-fluidized bed, code INX under regulation 890.5160, that is Class II and 510(k)-exempt yet carries special controls and is not GMP-exempt [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 00:45:40]. At the top of the risk ladder, Registrar Corp closes the door: 'There are no exemptions in class three' [9Unzt431qg8 @ 00:16:32]. The same Registrar Corp session shows how counterintuitive the boundaries get: exam gloves, a mechanical wheelchair and oral lubricant are Class I yet need a 510(k), while a mercury thermometer, a bassinet and an AC-powered bed are Class II and exempt; even sunglasses are a device under code HQY. How simple a product looks predicts nothing; the code decides.

Practitioners also agree on the vocabulary and on who actually controls the market. Invent Healthcare's 510(k) presenter states that 'only products or devices that are approved through the pre-market approval process are considered FDA approved,' so a 510(k) device is cleared, not approved [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:05:23]. Registrar Corp adds two hard don'ts: FDA 'does not issue any registration certificates for registered establishment,' and the FDA logo is prohibited on products, with logo-bearing shipments forced into stickering that adds costs, delays and storage fees [9Unzt431qg8 @ 00:59:54]. Registrar Corp's second session repeats the instruction not to print 'FDA' on boxes, and in Q&A confirms that distributors need not register while the Chinese factory must [GUA_JDVPQG0 @ 00:44:31]. The commercial lever comes from Isaac Erickson, who says a distributor can file as the 510(k) applicant under 21 CFR 807 provided it has access to the manufacturer's documents, and who frames the clearance as intellectual property: 'If you have that 510k, you have control over the United States market' [TChhZNirpeM @ 00:01:20]. Citing 15+ years in the business, Erickson warns against trusting manufacturers to prioritize you. For an importer or reseller, holding the clearance rather than just the inventory is what Erickson treats as the controlling asset.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Fees are the first hard number. Registrar Corp quotes the 2025 510(k) user fee at $24,335 standard versus $6,084 for small businesses, with small-business status requiring under $100M in receipts and renewal every year [9Unzt431qg8 @ 01:02:38]. Registrar Corp's second session puts 2026 establishment registration at $11,423 and notes small-business approval can take up to 60 days [GUA_JDVPQG0 @ 00:27:25]. FDA's Jason Brookbank of the Division of Financial Management sets the tiers: at or under $100M in gross receipts including affiliates earns a 75% discount, and at or under $30M with no prior PMA, BLA, PDP or PMR waives the fee on the first submission [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 05:09:22]. Sequencing is everything: Invent Healthcare's presenter warns that 'if you submit the standard 510k user fee and then subsequently later get a small business determination qualification the FDA will not refund you the difference' [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:23:51]. Brookbank adds that registration fees get no discount, the determination is non-transferable and valid for one fiscal year, applications should go in at least 60 days ahead, and gross receipts mean total sales rather than profit. After an acquisition, the parent company's receipts count, so Brookbank recommends qualifying before being acquired.

The review clock runs on fixed checkpoints. Invent Healthcare lays out Day 7 for an acknowledgment or hold letter, Day 15 for the refuse-to-accept review, Day 60 for substantive interaction, a 90-day MDUFA goal (30 days for a special 510(k)), and additional-information requests that stop the clock and allow 180 days to respond [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:25:17]. Prime Path Medtech flags the cheapest failure, 'if you haven't paid the user free you'll get a hold letter,' and counsels building the file against FDA's own checklist [0CrtB8E2n30 @ 00:02:34]. FDA reviewer Andrew details the traps inside the clock: a non-accepted file gets 180 days to fix and restarts at Day 0, a clarification call on an AI letter must be requested within 10 days, eSTAR files skip refuse-to-accept in favor of virus and technical screening, and eSTAR is mandatory for all 510(k)s from October 1 unless exempted [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 02:54:31]. The clock also runs midnight-to-midnight Eastern regardless of weekends, so letters can land at 10pm on a Friday. leanRAQA's reality check: the 510(k) carries a 90-day FDA clock but runs about 200 days in practice, De Novo 120 versus about 400, and PMA 180 versus about 800 [EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:44:39].

Predicate hunting is a database grind. FDA's classification case study runs three methods (the classification database, clearance and approval searches, and listing searches) and tells users to use Quick Search, avoid plurals and not be too specific; 'traction' returned 11 results leading to product code ITH, 21 CFR 890.5900, Class II, 510(k) required, and the closing advice is to cross-check across all three databases [XMo038fEjH4 @ 00:17:20]. FDA's Andrew explains why older predicates go dark: 510(k)s cleared before 1990 have no summaries, 1990-2007 summaries were accepted but not regularly posted and need a FOIA request, and 2007 onward is typically posted; clearance letters are a 'snapshot in time' and are not reissued after a 510(k) is purchased [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 03:10:44]. Invent Healthcare allows multiple predicates if one primary predicate passes the whole flowchart, but 'split predicates are inconsistent with the 510k regulatory standard' [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:16:46]. leanRAQA warns that stacking too many predicates looks like a 'Frankenstein' that gets pushed to De Novo, that words like 'novel' invite trouble, and that a wrong product code gets refused at RTA [EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:36:54].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock intended use and the product code
   - Define the product, confirm it is a device, and pin the three-letter product code before the first prototype.
   - leanRAQA's five steps run define the product, verify it is a device, classify, gather evidence, submit, with intended use locked down first and regulatory strategy set before the first prototype [leanRAQA — EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:09:40]; Easy Medical Device searches the product code database with the most generic term, eliminates mismatches, shortlists a handful of codes and checks each regulation's intended use [Easy Medical Device — USqn2QdZTCM @ 00:03:45].
2. Select and document the predicate
   - Cross-check the classification, clearance and listing databases, then keep the predicate's product code.
   - FDA's case study tells users to cross-check across all three databases [U.S. Food and Drug Administration — XMo038fEjH4 @ 00:17:20]; DICOM Code & Cure keeps the predicate's product code to substantiate equivalence [DICOM Code & Cure: MedPhysics and FDA Insights — l_IqH_YvZ68 @ 00:06:58]; Registrar Corp notes FDA won't pick your predicate, but it can come from another company [Registrar Corp — GUA_JDVPQG0 @ 00:42:12].
3. Qualify for fee relief before paying
   - File the small business determination at least 60 days ahead, then pay the user fee.
   - Brookbank says to submit at least 60 days ahead, with each determination valid for one fiscal year [U.S. Food and Drug Administration — cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 05:11:08]; Invent Healthcare warns that FDA will not refund the difference if the standard fee is paid before qualification [Invent Healthcare — kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:23:51].
4. Submit via eSTAR and work the clock
   - File through eSTAR, track Day 15, Day 60 and Day 90 checkpoints, and register after clearance.
   - eSTAR is mandatory for all 510(k)s from October 1 unless exempted [U.S. Food and Drug Administration — cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 03:02:19]; AI requests stop the clock with 180 days to respond [Invent Healthcare — kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:25:17]; Kendra Holter says to register within 30 days of starting commercial distribution, waiting until clearance for devices that need it [U.S. Food and Drug Administration — cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 01:01:23].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is vocabulary, and it decides how stock gets described. DICOM Code & Cure describes a predicate as having "received a FDA 510k approval" [l_IqH_YvZ68 @ 00:00:47], and Patient Guard also calls the 510(k) outcome an approval. The opposite camp is blunt. Invent Healthcare's FDA panel states that only devices that go through the premarket approval process are considered FDA approved [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:05:23]. leanRAQA lists authorized for EUA, cleared for 510(k) and approved for PMA as terms that are not interchangeable [EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:45:43]. De Novo naming splits the same way. Registrar Corp calls it a "de novo 510(k)", while NSF frames De Novo as a separate pathway intended for novel devices that are still low to moderate risk [gfFy3QwozGE @ 00:11:55]. Fee numbers drift just as hard. Invent Healthcare's De Novo panel said there were no fees for pre-submissions or de novos, with the 510(k) fee charged only when the de novo starts as a 510(k). leanRAQA quotes $33,000 for De Novo, roughly $5,000 for a small-business 510(k) and $110,000 for a small-business PMA. Registrar Corp quotes the 2025 510(k) fee at $6,084 for small business against $24,335 standard, with small-business status requiring under $100M in receipts and yearly renewal.

Inspection cadence and what an investigator may open are the second fault line. NSF puts routine inspections at about every two years, possibly every five, lasting two to five days or up to two weeks [gfFy3QwozGE @ 00:34:07]. Registrar Corp splits it by class: Class III roughly every two years and Class II about every three to four years, with unannounced foreign inspections expanding after an India and China pilot [9Unzt431qg8 @ 00:45:28]. Registrar Corp also notes that inspections can occur as soon as the establishment is registered and listed. On internal audits the camps flatly contradict each other. Easy Medical Device's guest says FDA inspectors cannot look at internal audits, management review or supplier audits [USqn2QdZTCM @ 00:14:28]. FDA's own CAPA presenter at Invent Healthcare says the Office of Regulatory Affairs does not review internal audit findings or management review minutes, except where they relate to CAPA [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 03:25:45]. NSF counters that QMSR, in effect since February and aligned with ISO 13485, makes internal audits reviewable. The CAPA panel closes the gap from the other side: you cannot shield a CAPA inside audit documents, and internal audits that never feed CAPA are a red flag. NSF's warning is that the older assumption of private audits no longer holds under QMSR.

Predicate strategy and speed produce the sharpest alternative playbooks. Invent Healthcare's 510(k) panel allows multiple predicates only when one primary predicate passes the entire flowchart, and it calls split predicates inconsistent with the 510(k) standard [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:17:01]. leanRAQA warns that stacking too many predicates reads as a "Frankenstein" and gets pushed to De Novo, and it tells filers to avoid words like "novel" [EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:36:54]. On speed, leanRAQA says third-party review can cut about 100 days but costs an extra fee and has proved "far worse". Its table shows a 90-day FDA clock running about 200 days in practice. POCTCTR reports 3 to 4 months for a traditional 510(k) and up to one to two years for complex submissions [kiNTAANRFVM @ 00:07:34]. The critics reject the whole predicate model. 23ABC News reports that a recalled predicate can still count and that transvaginal mesh was cleared this way. It also reports that nearly half of 2017 to 2021 Class I recalls used a recalled predicate. Verdict Videos calls the result "leapfrog" chains where nothing gets adequately tested [Tb7dnxPHBNo @ 00:01:50]. FDA's own reviewers, by contrast, treat the predicate comparison as the core of the most common pathway.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive mistakes are fee sequencing errors that cannot be undone. Invent Healthcare's 510(k) presenter warns that if you pay the standard user fee and qualify for small business later, FDA will not refund the difference [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 01:23:51]. FDA's Division of Financial Management repeats it at REdI: get the SBD number before paying for the cover sheet, because there are no refunds [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 05:28:15]. The same speaker lists the recurring rejects. They include editing the PDF in a browser instead of Adobe Reader, a wrong fiscal year or Org ID, and not summing receipts on the tax forms. They also include a signer who does not match line 4 and certification by an accountant instead of the national tax authority. Submit at least 60 days ahead, since the SBD covers one fiscal year only. Qualify before any acquisition, because the parent's receipts then count. Front-door failures cost time instead of cash. Prime Path Medtech says an unpaid fee triggers a hold letter and that an RTA hold gives 180 days to fix [0CrtB8E2n30 @ 00:02:34]. leanRAQA reports that a wrong product code, such as FXX against an antimicrobial mask, gets refused at RTA [EnjRMOB1Uio @ 00:21:00]. FDA's reviewer adds that the acceptance clock restarts at Day 0 after a refusal.

Post-market, the 483 data keeps pointing at the same weak spots. NSF says CAPA is the top 483 observation, with complaint handling right behind [gfFy3QwozGE @ 00:35:17]. FDA's REdI speakers put numbers on it: complaint files drew 120 citations in FY2022 and non-conforming product drew 104 [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 06:16:18]. Tanya Wilbon's sharpest trap is the internal clock. "Timely" means immediately, because a 10-day internal procedure can miss Part 803's 5-day and 30-day reporting windows [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 07:25:57]. Invent Healthcare's CAPA presenter flags two self-inflicted failures [kwzaOqIZO2k @ 03:15:39]. The first is training showing up again and again as the corrective action, which should make you question the investigation itself. The second is identifying an action and then never implementing it. His baseline rule is that if it wasn't documented, it didn't occur. Physical controls matter too: Ruth B. Diaco says non-conforming product must still be physically segregated even when a system block exists. Invent Healthcare's eMDR panel says relabelers and repackagers are manufacturers for MDR purposes. It also says a production account takes two to six weeks, which is why the panel says to set up eMDR early against the 30-day deadline.

Distribution and labeling traps hit resellers and importers directly. Isaac Erickson says a distributor can file a 510(k) as applicant if it has access to the manufacturer's documents, and that whoever holds the 510(k) controls the US market. His warning, from 15-plus years of experience, is not to trust manufacturers to prioritize you [TChhZNirpeM @ 00:02:11]. Buying a clearance has its own catch. FDA's 510(k) reviewer says clearance letters are a "snapshot in time" and are not reissued after a 510(k) is purchased [cjw_q6QL1W4 @ 03:19:04]. Registrar Corp lists the labeling and agent errors that stall shipments [9Unzt431qg8 @ 01:15:55]. FDA does not issue registration certificates, and you cannot use a PO box for your US agent. Putting the FDA logo on product forces stickering, with added costs, delays and storage fees. Registrar Corp's 2026 session adds three more rules: do not print "FDA" on boxes, the Chinese factory must register, and distributors themselves need not register. NSF warns that a CE-ready label is not US-ready and that contract manufacturers must be registered every year [gfFy3QwozGE @ 00:30:02]. Easy Medical Device's guest adds a systems trap. One-size consultant quality systems turned out big and bulky, clients could not resource them, and that created compliance issues.
