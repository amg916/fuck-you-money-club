# Chapter 05: 21 CFR 820: From QSR to QMSR

*GMP for medical devices, ISO 13485 incorporation by reference, the February 2, 2026 transition, what inspectors now look for, risk management under the QMSR, legal and liability implications, and certification readiness*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the QSR is dead as of February 2, 2026: the QMSR incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference, keeps FDA add-ons for UDI, tracking, MDR and corrections, and makes risk the thread investigators pull. Certification buys no inspection exemption, and management review, internal audit and supplier audit records are now reviewable. Coverage follows activity, reaching relabelers, repackagers and sterilizers. Street mechanics: QSIT is replaced by CP 7382.850 with Model 1 and Model 2 inspections; the DMR, DHF and DHR collapse into the medical device file; standards cost a few hundred dollars, cheaper via Estonia; Matrix Quality starts at 10,000 euros a year; Kellerman budgets a 21-day habit window. Disagreements: Medical Device Academy rejects FDA's projected savings, Henry opposes dropping 'establish,' and Kellerman still sells certification as a marketing calling card while Henry says it means nothing to FDA.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus: Hard Switch, ISO 13485 Spine, Risk Everywhere

Every practitioner on this beat agrees on the calendar and the architecture. AMORIT EDUCATION lays out the clock: final rule in February 2024, a two-year transition, and a hard deadline of February 2, 2026, with ISO 13485:2016 incorporated by reference so the standard itself becomes binding and FDA-specific requirements stack on top [PhHUunSO_iQ @ 00:01:02]. Eric Henry of King & Spalding, a 35-year veteran speaking on Greenlight Guru, calls it a hard switch: the old QSR governs until February 1, 2026 and the QMSR governs the next day, after the proposed one-year transition was stretched to two while some commenters pushed for four [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 00:18:49]. Regulatory Compliance Associates frames the result as the best of both worlds, because pieces of Part 820 survive: clause 7.5.8 gets supplemented with Part 830 for UDI and clause 7.5.9 with Part 821 for tracking, which is exactly why certifying to ISO 13485 alone does not close the file [cNSAixRUu9Q @ 00:01:57]. Medical Device Academy supplies the backstory operators need when someone asks why the rewrite happened at all: the regulation dates to December 1978, added design controls in 1990, became the QSR in 1996, and has barely moved since, with only a wording swap as the recent edit and software mentioned just seven times [yX4mpyZCfjY @ 00:03:46].

The second point of agreement is blunt: a certificate on the wall does not buy a pass. AMORIT EDUCATION warns that neither MDSAP nor ISO certification exempts a firm from FDA inspection, and that management review and supplier audit records are now open to investigators [PhHUunSO_iQ @ 00:02:50]. Henry goes further, stating that ISO 13485 certification really does not mean anything to FDA, and flagging that management review minutes, internal audit reports and supplier audit reports all move into the reviewable pile [Cv26iLHCawk @ 01:03:00]. Denise Holiday of Capwell Consulting, an ISO 13485 auditor and expert witness speaking on Medmarc, confirms both points from the far side of the deadline: no certification is required, certification exempts nobody, and scope follows what an organization actually does rather than how big it is [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:07:32]. For anyone handling equipment downstream of the original factory, US Regulations Explained draws the coverage line: finished-device makers, sterilizers, relabelers and repackagers sit inside Part 820, while component makers supplying something like a screw or a microchip are not directly covered [3Qz3714kmCM @ 00:01:18]. Those four voices converge on one operating rule: map your actual activities against the regulation, because the badge on your letterhead will not shield the shop floor when an investigator walks in.

The third consensus thread is risk, and it changes how every process gets read. Holiday notes that under the old QSR the word risk appeared only in 820.30, while under the QMSR it is embedded system-wide; in Holiday's phrasing, risk is no longer a document [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:16:35]. FDA's own Tanya Wilbon of CDRH DICE treats risk management as something that permeates the entire quality management system and states that the response must match the risk level, whether that means a customer notice, a field safety notice, a recall or a CAPA [QFNSAlfJaic @ 00:19:34]. The downside of getting it wrong is uniform across speakers, and it is severe. US Regulations Explained spells out that a noncompliant device is treated as adulterated, meaning it can be stopped at the border, and that warning letters, fines and detentions can land on both firms and individuals, all justified by the core rationale of patient safety [3Qz3714kmCM @ 00:04:18]. Henry adds the definitional trap that catches newcomers: adulterated means failing to meet regulatory requirements, not physical contamination, so a perfectly clean device sitting on a shelf with a broken paper trail still qualifies as adulterated in FDA's eyes [Cv26iLHCawk @ 00:08:04].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The inspection engine has been swapped out entirely. Matrix One reports that from February 2, 2026 the QSIT technique is retired and CP 7382.850 governs, covering six QMS areas plus four other requirements (MDR, corrections and removals, tracking and UDI), with Model 1 routine inspections sampling at least one element per area and Model 2 baseline or PMA preapproval visits running a fixed minimum set that includes software validation [b_a74PE0Cn0 @ 00:01:28]. Regulatory Compliance Associates describes the program as a total product life cycle approach that pulls complaints, MDRs, recalls, servicing and supplier data into one view, with investigators using risk-based sampling and expecting a living dynamic system [cNSAixRUu9Q @ 00:02:49]. Holiday explains how that plays on the floor: there is no fixed order of areas, the inspector starts with your risk documentation, and threads get followed from one process into the next, so the inspection follows the quality system instead of forcing the quality system to follow the inspection [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:29:51]. QT9 Software adds that inspectors now test leadership decisions against risk and chase risk threads across records, which is exactly where siloed spreadsheets hurt, and that management review records get pulled when signals warrant it rather than on every visit [2VNYkzxS9nA @ 00:09:09].

The document stack needs relabeling and rewiring before an investigator ever arrives. Henry walks through the structural swaps: the DMR, DHF and DHR give way to the medical device file, and the word establish is replaced by documented, so a procedure that merely exists on a server is not the same as one that is documented and implemented; in Henry's words, just releasing a document is never enough [Cv26iLHCawk @ 00:25:32]. In the 2024 Greenlight Guru session Henry pairs that with a practical fix: build a glossary that maps legacy terms to the new ones, hand it to an auditor or investigator, and expect to be fine [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 01:25:07]. Holiday's cleanup list is more aggressive. Strip any 'do not show to FDA' language out of procedures, redact only with counsel, and stop treating CAPA volume as something to suppress; one client set an objective of fewer CAPAs, which Holiday calls the opposite of a quality objective, countering that CAPAs are your defense [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:21:58]. Regulatory Compliance Associates adds the terminology sweep: update references to ISO 9001 clause 3 terminology, carry the Class I design-control exemptions forward, and keep any Class I device previously required to hold a design file inside that requirement [cNSAixRUu9Q @ 00:01:16].

The cost and tooling numbers are concrete. Henry puts purchased copies of the incorporated standards at a few hundred dollars and notes that the free ANSI incorporation-by-reference copy is view-only [Cv26iLHCawk @ 00:48:58]. On software, QT9 Software insists the eQMS is something you validate yourself under clause 4.1.6, and pitches spiderweb linkages between records so that impact assessments run automatically when a change touches one node, replacing manual cross-checking across disconnected files [2VNYkzxS9nA @ 00:06:06]. Matrix One prices its Matrix Quality platform from 10,000 euros per year, quoting go-live in two to six weeks for teams of 20 to 1,000 people [b_a74PE0Cn0 @ 00:04:06]. On the people side, Kellerman Consulting sequences implementation as a gap analysis against both the QSR and ISO 13485, then document revisions and staff training, then a monitoring period of about 21 days, because in that consultant's experience change is hard and it takes roughly 21 days for something to become a habit [jUOtQFk5I3Q @ 00:30:08]. The operator read: budget money for standards and software, but budget calendar time for the habit window too, because a procedure nobody follows becomes the exact thread an investigator pulls on the next visit.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over what an ISO 13485 certificate actually buys you. Eric Henry of King & Spalding, speaking on Greenlight Guru, puts it bluntly: being certified 'really doesn't mean anything to the FDA' [Cv26iLHCawk @ 01:03:00]. AMORIT EDUCATION lands in the same place, warning that neither an MDSAP nor an ISO certificate earns a pass on an FDA inspection [PhHUunSO_iQ @ 00:02:50]. Denise Holiday of Capwell Consulting tells Medmarc viewers that the QMSR requires no certification at all and that holding one exempts nobody from inspection [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:13:12]. Kellerman Consulting runs the opposite commercial playbook. Its speaker concedes certification is voluntary, yet pitches it as a marketing 'calling card' and a competitive advantage, noting that FDA dislikes companies advertising that they are FDA-audited while a certificate can be promoted within logo rules [jUOtQFk5I3Q @ 00:13:53]. The two camps are not arguing about the law, because every one of these speakers agrees certification is voluntary. They are arguing about where the certificate earns its keep. Kellerman books it as a sales asset that opens doors with buyers and customers. Henry, AMORIT and Holiday book it at zero on inspection day, which means the FDA-facing system has to stand on its own records, risk files and CAPA trail whether or not a certificate hangs on the wall.

The second fight is about how much work the switch really is and who pays for it. Medical Device Academy rejects the projected savings outright: firms already run a single QMS, a consultant can finish the gap analysis in 'a few hours,' and the real cost lands on FDA, which must retrain its own inspectors [jUg2UuY-OlQ @ 00:01:10]. Eric Henry sat in the one-year camp on the transition period, arguing the job 'can be done in a year, especially if you've already got a 13485 program in place' [Cv26iLHCawk @ 00:46:10]. Industry pushed the other way, asking for two years, with one organization asking for four, and in the final rule FDA extended the proposed one year to two, landing the effective date on February 2, 2026 [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 00:17:29]. Regulatory Compliance Associates splits the market by starting point rather than by calendar: shops already following ISO 13485 face little change, while shops that only ever complied with Part 820 face significant change [cNSAixRUu9Q @ 00:04:06]. Read together, these four speakers price the transition by legacy system rather than by the regulation itself. A shop already running 13485 is looking at hours of gap analysis and a one-year job by Henry's estimate, while a Part 820-only shop is staring at what RCA calls significant change across its whole quality system.

The third split runs through the rule text itself. On risk management, FDA's Tanya Wilbon walks through Comment 9: FDA does not incorporate ISO 14971, and under Comment 32 the ISO 13485 clause 0.2 definition of risk is treated as sufficient [QFNSAlfJaic @ 00:03:43]. Eric Henry refuses to let operators read that as daylight. Henry's warning is not to use the non-incorporation as a loophole, because FDA has recognized ISO 14971 since 2007 and rates it above the P1/P2 risk structure of ICH Q9 [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 01:02:09]. Denise Holiday confirms 14971 is not incorporated by reference, yet Holiday and Henry both push operators to adopt it anyway [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:18:21]. Henry also breaks with FDA on vocabulary. The rule swaps the word 'establish' for 'documented,' and Henry disagrees with the deletion, fearing companies will slip on the 'implement' half of the old requirement [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 00:36:01]. That leaves two playbooks on the table. The minimalist reads the rule literally, leans on clause 0.2, and writes procedures that are documented on paper. The Henry and Holiday playbook runs a full 14971 risk process anyway and proves implementation with records, rather than trusting the word 'documented' to carry an investigator through a risk-driven inspection that now starts with the risk file.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The biggest trap under the QMSR is the paper you assumed FDA would never read. Eric Henry spells it out on Greenlight Guru: management review, internal audit and supplier audit records become reviewable, and 'that restriction is gone' [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 01:16:18]. Matrix One adds the retroactive sting: investigators can review records created before February 2026, ISO certificates buy no exemption, and investigators do not follow MDSAP audit plans [b_a74PE0Cn0 @ 00:02:59]. Henry had already raised concern about 'shadow documents' and possible statutory conflicts once these records open up [Cv26iLHCawk @ 00:54:21]. Denise Holiday has seen the rot firsthand inside client procedures: language reading 'do not show to FDA' that has to be stripped out, and one client that set an objective of fewer CAPAs, which Holiday calls the opposite of a quality objective [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:21:58]. Holiday's counter-rules are short. CAPAs are your defense, and redaction happens only with counsel. The economics check is simple and unforgiving. Every management review minute and supplier audit report on file, including records written before the deadline, is now potential inspection evidence, so the write-up discipline you apply to those records today decides what an investigator reads tomorrow, and a deliberately thin CAPA trail is a liability Holiday has watched clients build for themselves.

The second failure mode is a stale or siloed risk file, because under the new inspection model it is the map the investigator walks. Holiday puts it flatly: risk documentation is 'becoming the road map of the inspection,' and 'the inspector will start with your risk documentation' [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:29:51]. Matrix One issues the same order in fewer words: 'Keep your risk management file current, because it steers the visit' [b_a74PE0Cn0 @ 00:03:23]. QT9 Software describes inspectors testing leadership decisions against risk and following risk threads from one process into the next, and warns that siloed spreadsheets hurt a company once that thread-pulling starts [2VNYkzxS9nA @ 00:09:09]. FDA's own Tanya Wilbon shows what keeps the file alive between visits: under clause 8.2, a rise in complaints must trigger an update to the risk management file [QFNSAlfJaic @ 00:18:51]. The trap is treating the risk file as a design-phase artifact that gets signed once and shelved. Operators who feed complaint and post-market data back into the file can walk an investigator down a coherent thread from complaint to risk to CAPA. Operators who do not are handing the investigator an outdated document at the exact spot where Holiday says the inspection begins, and every downstream process then gets judged against it.

The third cluster of traps sits at the edges of the operation, where suppliers and automation do work the manufacturer still owns. Eric Henry notes that under Comment 2, FDA keeps enforcement discretion over component makers but 'strongly encourage[s]' QMSR use, and warns that the pressure will land anyway through customers' purchasing controls [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 00:31:19]. Denise Holiday reports that FDA is now focusing on supplier process control, using validated injection molding as the example [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:34:10], and closes the outsourcing escape hatch: the legal manufacturer stays ultimately responsible even when a contract manufacturer does the work, because 'they are responsible for everything' [L11OFtMOx3E @ 00:45:12]. Automation carries its own version of the same trap. Henry insists automated label checks still need a statistically significant human sample before anyone leans on the machine [yW4-Hn0IXI8 @ 01:00:32]. The economics check across all three warnings is identical. Outsourcing a process, buying parts from a component shop, or installing an automated inspection step shifts the labor but not the liability. Each of those handoffs needs a documented control, a sampling plan or a supplier audit that will hold up in front of an investigator who now has the right to read your supplier audit reports directly.
