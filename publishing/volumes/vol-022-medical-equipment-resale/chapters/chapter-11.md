## 1. Assign eight possible seats without building eight departments  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Build one shared pod around three explicitly mapped plays: diagnostic equipment refurbishment, surgical equipment resale, and veterinary/aesthetic equipment remarketing. These are operating lanes for Candidate #14, not separate businesses with duplicated managers. In the six-person configuration, the owner occupies the first full-time seat as commercial lead and capital allocator; the second full-time employee owns inventory operations, document control, and collections coordination. Four contractors provide biomed testing, rigging and freight, technical sales support, and bookkeeping with data-hygiene coordination. The coordinator does not personally certify unfamiliar device sanitization; qualified personnel must perform and document the actual work. Assign a named person, backup method, deliverable, and spending limit to each seat. Contractors are fractional capacity, not four hidden full-time salaries. At scale, add an employed technical operations lead and a fifth contractor covering specialist field service. That produces exactly three employees and five contractors, including the owner, rather than quietly introducing another management layer. The technical lead controls release evidence while the owner retains capital allocation. No person may buy equipment, accept its technical condition, authorize their own disputed invoice, and release the same payment without an independent documented review.

Each play needs a distinct acceptance packet inside the same inventory system. Diagnostic units require model-specific functional checks, accessory inventories, applicable calibration evidence, and software-transfer verification; an ultrasound console without a transferable option can be worth materially less than its listing suggests. Surgical equipment requires instrument compatibility, insulation or electrical testing where applicable, and a documented decontamination handoff. Veterinary and aesthetic devices require intended-use classification, consumable availability, service access, and verification that performance claims match the actual configuration. Veterinary devices do not automatically inherit every human-device regulatory obligation, and aesthetic branding does not remove medical-device obligations. Use Odoo Inventory or an equivalently controlled serial ledger, with service histories indexed by serial number and revision. Store signed reports in a restricted repository, not personal messaging threads. For each incoming machine, identify the legal seller, title evidence, installed software rights, accessories, recall status, and location before committing refurbishment money. A hospital asset tag establishes identity, not necessarily transferable ownership. Confirm financing releases and relevant UCC searches when warranted. This shared evidence architecture lets six people handle three plays without relearning the equipment's history at every handoff.

Set contractor compensation around bounded deliverables instead of vague availability. Offer the biomed contractor an agreed diagnostic fee, a separate authorized repair budget, and payment for a complete test packet, regardless of whether the machine passes. Paying only for passing devices creates the wrong incentive. Freight contractors receive route-specific quotes with declared value, loading responsibility, packaging scope, and claims deadlines. Technical sales support earns a fee on collected, positive-contribution transactions after the contractual return period, with written treatment of later refunds. The bookkeeping contractor receives a monthly close retainer plus a separately priced historical cleanup scope. Illustrative service targets are intake acknowledgment within one business day, initial biomed findings within three business days after receipt, and complete shipping evidence within one day of dispatch. Parts shortages pause completion clocks only through recorded exceptions. Do not use contractor labels to bypass employee classification; evaluate actual control, independence, applicable federal standards, and state tests. At $250,000 revenue, the entire annual expense envelope is only $225,000, including acquisition and refurbishment. If two genuinely staffed employee seats and required contractor coverage cannot fit, that scenario fails local underwriting; unpaid labor is not a repair.

## 2. Run the weekly scorecard from serial-level exceptions  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Run a forty-five-minute weekly operating review from a frozen Monday export, not an improvised slide deck. The operations coordinator publishes every owned serial, cash invested, days in each state, next action, accountable seat, and expected cash conversion date. Keep distinct states for received, quarantined, under diagnosis, awaiting parts, technically released, listed, contracted, shipped, accepted, and collected. The owner reviews cash commitments before discussing sales volume. Track rolling thirteen-week gross proceeds, collected cash, acquisition commitments, landed inventory cost, repair spending, contribution by device cohort, and warranty exposure. For contractors, show completed jobs and denominators: six accepted reports from seven submitted is more informative than a green quality badge. Separate first-pass documentation acceptance from actual device pass rate; difficult equipment can legitimately fail functional testing while the contractor performs excellent work. Establish initial internal targets of at least 95% complete packets and 90% on-time milestones, then recalibrate using observed job complexity. Never reward faster release by allowing unresolved safety defects. Review every disputed invoice, overdue receivable, and machine with no credible next action. Close the meeting with a named owner and dated correction for each exception.

Adopt a concentration policy capped at 25% for any single customer group's trailing-twelve-month recognized revenue and any single supplier group's trailing-twelve-month acquisition spending. Aggregate entities under common control; splitting purchase orders does not diversify exposure. Apply the same ceiling to individual customer groups' outstanding receivables and supplier groups' open advances, with a separate dollar limit where small denominators would distort decisions. Model post-transaction exposure before accepting the next purchase or sale. Early operations can exceed percentage limits mechanically because one first transaction represents all activity; disclose the breach and suspend additional unsecured exposure rather than presenting startup concentration as resolved. Use diversified prospecting, deposits, staged purchases, and alternate approved suppliers to reduce exposure. Contractor continuity needs its own metric: identify which released inventory depends on a single technician, software account, or service-key holder. Backup qualification is not an extra full-time seat, but paid backup work must still enter the expense ledger. Track aging alongside concentration because five unrelated buyers do not help if all owe overdue balances. Receivables follow-up belongs to operations; commercial concessions require the owner's authorization and a recorded effect on transaction contribution.

Translate exceptions into economically bounded decisions. At thirty days without diagnosis, stop buying the affected model until the technical bottleneck is understood. At sixty days awaiting unavailable parts, obtain a second sourcing assessment and compare continued repair with parts recovery or liquidation. At ninety days without qualified buyer activity, refresh comparable sale evidence, listing accuracy, and expected recoverable value; age alone does not determine the accounting write-down. These are internal escalation thresholds, not promises of market liquidity. For diagnostic equipment, track probe failures and unavailable software options separately. For surgical equipment, track incomplete accessory sets and failed safety checks. For veterinary/aesthetic equipment, track consumable lockouts and unsupported installation requirements. Inventory tracking and service-history indexing are Candidate #14's two automation levers: automate reminders and evidence links, not technical conclusions. A Zapier notification can flag an expired calibration certificate, but cannot establish that a device is safe. Contractor disputes should reference the agreed test protocol, missing evidence, and correction deadline. Pay undisputed accepted work on schedule. Repeated missed milestones trigger reduced assignments or replacement after continuity planning, while unsafe work triggers immediate shipment suspension for the affected units.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the weekly evidence set
   - Export serial inventory, contractor jobs, receivables, advances, and cash commitments each Monday.
   - Retain the dated export and reconcile totals to the current ledger before calculating ratios.
2. Review cash and concentration first
   - Calculate post-transaction customer and supplier exposure before authorizing new commitments.
   - Record common-control grouping, startup denominator effects, deposit protection, and the person resolving each breach.
3. Resolve technical and contractor exceptions
   - Inspect missing packets, failed tests, aging repairs, and disputed invoices by serial number.
   - Separate documentation quality from equipment condition and prohibit shipment while required release evidence remains incomplete.
4. Publish accountable corrective actions
   - Assign each exception one owner, one due date, and one measurable closure artifact.
   - Reopen unresolved items at the next review and preserve the decision history for buyer diligence.

## 3. Close accrual books that survive serial-by-serial diligence  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Maintain clean accrual accounts with an individual cost record for every machine and a consistent policy for shared costs. Candidate #14 recognizes gross equipment resale proceeds when acting as principal, not the hospital's original purchase price, downstream clinical billings, or financing principal. A brokerage arrangement in which the business acts only as agent requires a separate principal-versus-agent assessment and may recognize only the fee; do not mix that activity into the canonical resale scenarios without reconciliation. Capitalize equipment purchase cost and qualifying directly attributable inbound freight and refurbishment costs under the adopted accounting policy. Expense selling, administrative, and other period costs consistently. Recognize revenue under the contract's transfer-of-control terms, considering substantive acceptance conditions; a deposit or unsigned shipment receipt is not automatically earned revenue. Keep refundable deposits in liabilities. Release the matched inventory cost to cost of goods sold when the related sale is recognized. Accrue incurred but unbilled repair work and estimate applicable warranty obligations. Reconcile the serial subledger, general ledger, bank accounts, payment processors, and physical inventory each month. A spreadsheet can support this process, but uncontrolled overwrites and untraceable manual plugs cannot.

Reconcile every dashboard to the three canonical cases without turning an attractive unit spread into owner profit. Conservative revenue of $250,000 at 10% produces $25,000 annual owner net profit and $225,000 total scenario expenses. Base revenue of $1,025,000 at 16.0% produces $164,000 owner net profit and $861,000 expenses. Scaled revenue of $1,800,000 at 22% produces $396,000 owner net profit and $1,404,000 expenses. Acquisition and refurbishment sit inside those expense envelopes, alongside other costs included by the scorecard. Do not subtract acquisition twice or add inventory purchases directly to accrual expenses before sale. As an illustrative Base bridge, forty-one completed sales averaging $25,000 reconcile exactly to $1,025,000 gross proceeds; the actual mix can differ. The Base expense allowance averages $21,000 per completed sale, leaving $4,000 owner profit per sale after all allocated scenario expenses. These averages are budgeting checks, not device-level pricing rules. Track diagnostic, surgical, and veterinary/aesthetic cohorts separately because warranty experience, turnaround, and obsolete parts differ. Show purchases, recognized revenue, ending inventory, returns, repair overruns, and owner profit in separate columns so cash absorbed by unsold stock remains visible.

A transferable close package also connects technical records to legal and commercial rights. Classify each modification before authorizing work: FDA's servicing-versus-remanufacturing framework turns on the activity's effect on safety, performance specifications, or intended use, not the contractor's preferred label. Applicable manufacturer obligations, including the Quality Management System Regulation in 21 CFR Part 820, require a device-specific assessment; ordinary resale alone does not automatically create them. Document decontamination methods, materials compatibility, and exposure controls where employee occupational exposure invokes 29 CFR 1910.1030. Equipment containing electronic protected health information requires a defined custody and sanitization path. HIPAA applicability depends on the parties and functions; a business-associate relationship requires the applicable contract and safeguards, while covered entities retain their own duties. Use device-compatible sanitization procedures, verification results, and exception quarantine rather than a generic factory-reset claim. Never copy patient records into sale listings or buyer diligence folders. Check state device, radiation, and facility-related requirements for the actual equipment and activities. Record test instrument calibration, technician qualifications, software entitlement, and warranty scope beside the serial ledger. A profitable sale with missing rights or unresolved contamination can become a post-close liability.

>

> - Verify matched inventory cost, transfer-of-control evidence, deposit treatment, warranty exposure, and a reconciled contribution record before reporting completed-sale earnings.

## 4. Build transferable earnings before negotiating an exit multiple  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Transferability starts with rights and relationships, not a valuation spreadsheet. Obtain signed contractor agreements covering confidentiality, access termination, return of records, and ownership of commissioned reports, listing photography, original procedures, and inventory automation. Under 17 U.S.C. section 204(a), a copyright ownership transfer generally needs a signed writing from the owner or authorized agent; paying an invoice does not by itself transfer copyright. Do not rely casually on work-made-for-hire language for independent contractors. Schedule preexisting contractor materials and specify a durable license where assignment is unavailable. Neither arrangement creates rights to copy OEM manuals or transfer restricted diagnostic software. Keep vendor portals, domain registrations, CRM exports, telephone numbers, and repository administration under company-controlled accounts with individual access. Review customer, supplier, landlord, software, and service agreements for assignment and change-of-control conditions. For each material relationship, establish a second company contact and retain communication history. Demonstrate that operations can complete a purchase, technical release, shipment, collection, and warranty response while the owner is absent. A thirty-day delegation exercise exposes missing authority more reliably than a polished procedure binder. Resolve failures before asking a buyer to pay for institutional capability.

Value the business using a defined earnings measure and a clearly stated transaction perimeter. For planning sensitivity only, consider 2.0–3.0 times normalized seller's discretionary earnings for an owner-operated equipment trader and 3.0–4.5 times normalized EBITDA for a genuinely manager-run operation with transferable records and diversified counterparties. These are illustrative negotiating ranges, not observed comparable transactions or promised market pricing. Obtain current broker and buyer evidence before setting an asking price. The canonical $25,000, $164,000, and $396,000 owner net profits are neither automatically SDE nor automatically EBITDA. Bridge interest, taxes, depreciation, owner compensation, personal expenses, unusual costs, and necessary replacement labor explicitly. A buyer cannot add back the owner's work and simultaneously assume that work disappears. Only if diligence establishes Base normalized SDE at exactly $164,000 does the illustrative 2.0–3.0 range imply $328,000–$492,000 of operating business value on the stated earnings basis. Only if Scaled normalized EBITDA independently equals $396,000 does 3.0–4.5 imply $1,188,000–$1,782,000. Neither calculation changes the canonical scorecard. At Conservative earnings, replacement labor may absorb the economic return, making recoverable asset value more relevant than an attractive headline multiple.

Negotiate inventory and working capital explicitly before comparing offers. An asset sale may price equipment inventory separately, while another transaction includes a normalized working-capital amount in enterprise value. Define the treatment of saleable inventory, obsolete units, deposits, receivables, warranty obligations, debt, and cash; never add the same inventory value twice. Use a seasonal monthly working-capital history rather than one unusually lean closing balance. In a cash-free, debt-free structure with an agreed working-capital target, equity proceeds generally reconcile from enterprise value through debt, cash, working-capital adjustments, and transaction costs. Earnouts, escrows, seller notes, and retained liabilities can make a higher headline offer less valuable than a lower cash offer. If retaining the business, use the same discipline: distribute cash only after funding inventory commitments, expected repairs, taxes, and a documented liquidity reserve. The stated $75,000–$400,000 startup capital and $5,000–$22,000 monthly operating expense ranges are planning context, not incremental deductions from already reconciled owner profit. Promote the eight-person pod only when incremental capacity pays for its real costs. A durable cash cow is a repeatable cash-conversion system, not a warehouse whose paper margin depends on another large speculative purchase.
