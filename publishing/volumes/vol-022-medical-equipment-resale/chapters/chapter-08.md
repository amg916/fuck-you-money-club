## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one operating rule: a recall is a removal or correction of violative product, and under Part 806 the risk-to-health actions get reported within 10 working days of the decision to act, not the customer letter. Ex-FDA veteran Steve Needelman puts voluntary recalls at 99.999% and favors filing 80% of the facts on time over 100% late, while Tim Phillips warns against quiet recalls disguised as technical bulletins. On the data side, Abby Smart, Ibrahim Alawad and Christina Bernstein all hit MAUDE's 500-record cap; Smart and Alawad split date ranges, merge Excel exports and keyword-search narratives. Disagreements center on data quality and close-out targets: Health Watch USA finds 96.6% of reports come from manufacturers and 0.49% directly from physicians, Jeff Kasof says most firms check 100% of customers, and Needelman calls 25% retrieval acceptable.

## The Practitioner Consensus: What Counts as a Recall and Where the Evidence Lives

Every practitioner who works the recall side starts from the same definition. Amanda Johnston of Gardner Law frames a recall as the removal or correction of violative product, explicitly excluding market withdrawals and stock recovery [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:07:25]. Aaron Snyder compresses Part 806 into five words, 'report field actions to fda,' and states that all Class 1 and Class 2 recalls must be reported within 10 days of initiating the recall [8Pgfw2y5xe0 @ 00:01:52]. Steve Needelman, 34 years at FDA before joining King & Spalding, sharpens the trigger: the 10 business days run from the decision to act, not from the date the customer letter goes out [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:04:43]. Jeff Kasof repeats the number flatly: the deadline for issuing the report is 10 working days [1kYC2rBfPPA @ 00:41:45]. The classes line up the same way across speakers: Class 1 is highest risk, Class 2 moderate, Class 3 lowest, and FDA, not the firm, assigns the final class, although a company can propose one through its health hazard evaluation. Johnston adds that reportability is decided on the risk-to-health definition rather than the class definitions, and that unreported corrections and removals still require kept records. For anyone moving used equipment, that vocabulary is the filter for reading every recall entry attached to a unit.

The second point of agreement is that almost nothing here is forced, and that hiding a recall is the expensive move. Needelman says FDA's mandatory recall authority has existed since 1990 and been used fewer than 10 times, putting voluntary recalls at '99.999%' [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:05:39]; Johnston lands in the 'high 90%' range. Treomark warns against misreading that word: 'voluntary' describes how a recall began, not how severe it is, and not every unit is physically returned [GoXd4_jxKBg @ 00:00:16]. Tim Phillips, who spent 12 years as an FDA investigator and 20 as a compliance officer, is blunt that recalls cannot be disguised as a 'technical bulletin' and that there are no quiet recalls [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:34:48]. Needelman backs that with a Medrad warning letter as his silent-recall example and a bulb hypothetical: failures at 22 to 23 months are not a recall, failures at 8 to 9 months are, and a depleted backup battery is a recall [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:51:13]. David Graham, a litigator with 35-plus years, explains why it matters downstream: a recall makes information public, and an opaque one turns a 'bad device case' into a 'bad company case.' Graham's working assumption is that every document goes public eventually.

On the data side, the consensus is that MAUDE is free, public, and badly limited, and that operators should use it anyway. Abby Smart, an epidemiologist at Ofstead Insights, dates the database to 1991 and notes it is searchable for 10 years of reports [a-scTl1C2PA @ 00:01:12]; Dr. Ibrahim Alawad gives the same 1991 start and 10-year window, and Dr. Hashem stresses that no hospital data is needed because the database is free and public. Three speakers independently hit the same wall when pulling large device histories: output is capped at 500 records per search. Alawad says flatly 'It only gives you back' 500 records [a_T4LqfP6zE @ 00:25:33], and Christina Bernstein adds that roughly 500 records arrive every two weeks, so the cap bites fast [xbxXTRwdVRs @ 00:22:47]. Gene Parunak reports 'pushback' that not every event reaches MAUDE, and that exemptions, variances and alternative reporting are excluded [0dpHnJj93Ns @ 00:00:25]. Smart also lists underreporting, late-arriving reports, redactions, and FDA's own statement that MAUDE cannot assess prevalence. Bernstein's rule closes the loop: a record is not causation, so public data has to be cross-checked against manufacturer claims, patient records and FDA findings before anyone builds a decision on it.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Searching MAUDE and Running the Recall

The search workflow is consistent across three teachers. Smart skips simple search because it returns irrelevant hits and misses inexact terms, warning that MAUDE 'doesn't work like Google.' Smart starts with product class, then layers manufacturer, model or brand, widens the default one-month date range, and deliberately leaves the event-type filter off because malfunction reports may describe injuries [a-scTl1C2PA @ 00:02:55]. Results get exported to Excel; Smart reads the event description first, then the manufacturer narrative. Smart's worked example, a robotic surgical system from October 2018 to October 2020, returned almost 300 reports, which were scanned by 'device problem,' then 'event text,' using Find & Select [a-scTl1C2PA @ 00:06:59]. Alawad ran the same play on the da Vinci Xi: 293 records, then Ctrl+F for 'broken cable' produced 2 hits, one of them a death [a_T4LqfP6zE @ 00:21:38]. Ann McLain shows the faster general route: product-problem dropdown, product class, and event type; a battery issue plus malfunction for the first month of the year returned 332 records, sortable by manufacturer, brand or date [OS2unSk68KQ @ 00:01:52]. For a known product, McLain goes to the device classification database, pulls the product code, and opens the Total Product Lifecycle report, which lists every recorded device problem in one view.

When a search breaks, the fixes are mechanical. Zero results means widening dates, dropping brand, including all event types, trying manufacturer-name variants and checking spelling; Alawad's example of name variants is Rosh versus Rocher [a_T4LqfP6zE @ 00:24:33]. Too many results means splitting the window: Smart cuts the query into consecutive date ranges and merges them [a-scTl1C2PA @ 00:08:44], and Alawad used the same split-and-merge to pull about 1,000 bronchoscope records over 2 years. Empty narratives are sometimes filled in later, and gaps get backfilled from the IFU, manufacturer site, YouTube, journals and newsletters. Bernstein runs openFDA instead of the web form: querying the brand 'Pelace' took about 10 seconds and returned 656 injuries, 13 malfunctions and 2 other events, and the same query URL accepts any brand name substituted in [xbxXTRwdVRs @ 00:09:28]. Because CRB carries about 20 mesh brand names, Bernstein queries the exact product, finds codes by searching 'FDA product code, manufacturer name, brand name,' and treats the TPLC report as the recall history. The read is comparative: if one manufacturer has all the recalls, suspect that manufacturer; if all of them do, suspect the product type. Parunak adds that bulk voluntary-report zips go back to June 1993 [0dpHnJj93Ns @ 00:00:45].

On execution, the numbers are tight. Phillips lays out a timeline: issue identified on Day 0, health hazard evaluation on Days 1 to 3, the recall decision on Day 5 starting the 806 clock, strategy and notifications on Days 6 and 7, and Day 15 as the latest compliant report [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:21:52]. David Pudwell's checklist for the FDA package covers product name, catalog numbers, lot, serial and UDI, quantities, a consignee list, and notices to both ship-to and bill-to; notices should be directed rather than indiscriminate so customers are not desensitized [sNeRd4nIrG8 @ 00:14:34]. Needelman insists on certified mail or verifiable receipt rather than email or phone, separates units sold from units used, expects about 30% product return, and requires three good-faith attempts [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:39:10]. Pudwell wants monthly status reports carrying root cause and CAPA each time, while Kasof recommends status reports every two to four weeks and notes most firms run effectiveness checks on 100% of customers [1kYC2rBfPPA @ 00:46:51]. Kasof numbers each report by registration number, date, sequence and type, and sends customer letters by mail merge. Needelman's rule on completeness settles the tension: 80% of the information on time beats 100% late, and a report full of 'to be determined' entries has no value.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The 806 clock is the first place the experts split, and the split is over what starts it. Aaron Snyder of Quality Systems Explained gives the shorthand: every Class 1 and Class 2 recall goes to FDA within 10 days of initiating the recall [8Pgfw2y5xe0 @ 00:01:52]. Steve Needelman, who spent 34 years at FDA before joining King & Spalding, moves the trigger upstream: 10 business days from the decision to act, not from the day the customer letter goes out [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:04:43]. Tim Phillips, 12 years an FDA investigator and 20 a compliance officer, runs it as a calendar: issue found on Day 0, health hazard evaluation on Days 1 to 3, the recall decision on Day 5 starting the clock, strategy and notifications on Days 6 and 7, and Day 15 as the latest compliant report [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:21:52]. Amanda Johnston, the Gardner Law partner on the same panel, breaks from the class-based shorthand entirely: decide reportability with the risk-to-health definition, not the class definitions; FDA assigns the class itself, and a company only proposes one through its HHE [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:17:54]. Operators who count from letter dates, or who wait for a class before deciding whether to report, are working off the softer version of the rule.

Everyone who pulls MAUDE hits the same wall, then climbs it differently. Abby Smart, the Ofstead epidemiologist, says the database returns only 500 reports per search, so they split the window into consecutive date ranges and merge the exports [a-scTl1C2PA @ 00:08:44]. Dr. Ibrahim Alawad runs the identical split-and-merge in Excel and pulled roughly 1,000 bronchoscope records over two years that way [a_T4LqfP6zE @ 00:25:33]. Christina Bernstein, the ASQ-certified auditor working as an expert witness, skips the spreadsheet grind: MAUDE returns 500 records at a time while roughly 500 new ones land every two weeks, so they hit the openFDA query URL, swap in a brand name, and had 656 injuries, 13 malfunctions and 2 other events for the Pelace mesh brand in about 10 seconds, after warning that one maker sells about 20 mesh brand names, so the exact product must be queried [xbxXTRwdVRs @ 00:09:28]. The second split is filtering. Ann McLain filters on event type up front, and a battery problem plus malfunction plus one month returned 332 records [OS2unSk68KQ @ 00:01:52]. Smart refuses the event-type filter, because reports filed as malfunctions can describe injuries. For a reseller screening a model before buying, McLain's narrow pull is faster, while Smart's wider net catches injury stories buried in malfunction filings.

Recall execution draws the sharpest numeric splits. Jeff Kasof, the endoscopy quality director with 13 earlier years running regulatory affairs, says most firms run effectiveness checks against 100% of customers [1kYC2rBfPPA @ 00:50:28]. Needelman sets a far lower bar for product actually coming home: expect about 30% return, make three good-faith attempts, and in Q&A they called 25% retrieval potentially acceptable [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:39:10]. Cadence splits too. David Pudwell of Mr. Regulatory, walking through FDA's March 2020 guidance, runs status reports monthly and repeats root cause and CAPA in every one [sNeRd4nIrG8 @ 00:23:00], while Kasof recommends every two to four weeks and notes FDA may or may not specify it [1kYC2rBfPPA @ 00:46:51]. The delivery channel is contested as well. Needelman wants certified mail or verifiable receipt and rejects email or phone; Pudwell builds a phone script into the strategy and only bars website posting as the sole means; Kasof generates customer letters by mail merge. On publicity, Kasof treats a press release as required for Class I and optional for Classes II and III, while Needelman says Class I usually needs one and FDA will issue its own if yours is unacceptable.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive mistake the FDA veterans describe is timing, and the second is disguise. Phillips says a late 806 report invites a 483 or a warning letter scaled to how late it was, and a firm that hoped nobody would notice is still exposed when an investigator finds it months later [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:22:33]. They also flag the camouflage moves FDA sees through: a recall dressed up as a technical bulletin, or a quiet recall [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:34:48]. Needelman draws the silent-recall line with a bulb hypothetical: lamps failing at 22 to 23 months are not a recall, lamps failing at 8 to 9 months are, and a depleted backup battery is a recall too; they cite a Medrad warning letter as the live example [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:51:13]. Their fix for the clock is blunt: 80% of the information on time beats 100% late, amend later, and promptly reporting in those 10 days is critical [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:52:35]. The trap on the other side is an 806 report stuffed with placeholder entries, which they say has no value at all. Phillips adds that vague recall letters raise red flags, and that asking FDA what you should do is not a plan.

MAUDE punishes anyone who reads it at face value. The Health Watch USA pharmacology researcher ran roughly 7.5 million events from 1997 to 2018 through custom software and found manufacturers filed 96.6% of reports, possibly driven by warranty claims and lawsuits, while physicians filed directly only 0.49% and facilities 1.3% despite mandatory reporting [yKomHc_DWtw @ 00:05:20]. The same study hit Johnson & Johnson logged under multiple name variants, common duplicates, and a cobalt toxicity pull of 14,714 records where only 4% listed lab levels [yKomHc_DWtw @ 00:07:05]. Smart warns that narratives can be empty and get filled in later, that reports arrive late and underreported, and that FDA itself says MAUDE cannot assess prevalence [a-scTl1C2PA @ 00:09:23]. Bernstein adds that clinical sites and manufacturers each file narratives on the same problem, very ill patients confound causation, and a record is not causation until it is cross-checked against manufacturer claims, patient records and FDA findings [xbxXTRwdVRs @ 00:30:15]. Alawad's zero-result fix is to widen dates, drop brand and event type, and try every manufacturer name variant, as with Rosh versus Rocher. Gene Parunak still calls a cursory keyword look at similar devices well worth your time.

The economics bite hardest where records and contracts are thin. Pudwell calls a recall without distribution records a rough spot, wants both ship-to and bill-to consignees notified, and warns that indiscriminate notices desensitize customers [sNeRd4nIrG8 @ 00:10:55]. Needelman closes two escape hatches: if one unit of a 100-unit lot ships, the action is no longer a stock recovery, and an expired warranty is no excuse, so set an end-of-life date and resist that temptation [nJblY5CRIVY @ 00:14:26]. They also describe recall fatigue, the same device recalled for 6 to 12 reasons inside 6 to 9 months, and push sales staff into the field for visits. David Graham, the 35-year litigator, says an opaque recall turns a bad device case into a bad company case, so assume every document goes public and line up a communication plan or PR firm, supply-chain indemnification and cost analysis, contracts and recall insurance [aYQu_0ujhyU @ 00:45:18]. The early-warning cost is real: Alawad found Ethicon stapler reports sitting in MAUDE 2 years before a recall that covered 92,000 US devices [a_T4LqfP6zE @ 00:43:24]. Usually, as Alawad puts it, you're not the only one experiencing something.
