## 1. Days 1–7: Narrow Three Plays and Protect the First $75,000  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Treat the first week as an evidence sprint rather than an auction shopping trip. Candidate #14 has a 64/100 score because capital exposure and hands-on verification constrain leverage; a clean spreadsheet cannot neutralize an obsolete probe or disputed title. For this chapter, map exactly three launch plays: diagnostic ultrasound resale to outpatient practices, nonimplantable surgical equipment resale to surgery centers, and veterinary ultrasound resale to established clinics. These are execution lanes, not additional scorecard candidates. Start with one supported model family per lane and select one winner before buying. Aesthetic lasers remain a research-only adjacent category until the team documents model-specific service access, calibration capability, buyer restrictions, and insurance coverage. Assign FT1 to sourcing, buyer discovery, pricing, and collections; assign FT2 to custody, documentation, inventory control, and release coordination. The four contractors cover biomedical inspection, specialist repair and decontamination, equipment logistics, and device regulatory plus data-security review. Select contractors whose actual credentials cover those scopes; do not assume one generalist can certify every device. Their booked hours must fit the cash plan. No person substitutes sales urgency for independent technical acceptance, and neither founder releases their own undocumented repair.

Use the lower end of the stated $75,000–$400,000 startup range to make scarcity explicit. Illustratively allocate $45,000 to three months of operating cash, $15,000 to initial inventory exposure, and $15,000 to an untouched exception reserve. The monthly operating ceiling is $15,000, within the canonical $5,000–$22,000 range: $6,500 for combined founder cash pay, $3,500 for the contractor pool, $2,000 for storage and insurance, and $3,000 for software, professional review, sourcing travel, and general shipping. These are planning allocations, not market salary claims; record deferred founder compensation separately and include economically required labor in pricing. Device acquisition, committed repairs, and attributable freight together consume the $15,000 inventory envelope. Allocate contractor work between overhead and unit cost without counting it twice. Stop new discretionary commitments when actual plus committed monthly operating cash reaches $12,000; the remaining $3,000 completes existing obligations. Never bridge a shortfall with refundable buyer deposits. The initial $75,000 supports only this bounded test, not the full nine-month sales horizon. Before Day 60, secure additional runway or reduce future commitments enough to preserve orderly closure and customer obligations.

Build the evidence ledger on Day 1 in Airtable or an equivalent access-controlled inventory database, with QuickBooks Online holding accounting rather than technical truth. Give every candidate a permanent asset identifier and fields for model, serial, accessories, seller ownership, lien inquiry, service history, software entitlement, decontamination, data status, biomed results, location, and disposition. Index documents by asset identifier in a restricted document repository; never upload patient records merely to make service histories searchable. By Day 7, complete twelve discovery interviews, four in each play, and obtain at least six written sourcing leads. An interview counts only when an authorized buyer states their application, budget range, purchase horizon, required accessories, and acceptance criteria. A supplier lead counts only with model, serial evidence, asking price, and authority to sell. Use FDA classification resources and current device-specific requirements to separate ordinary resale from activities that could constitute manufacturing or remanufacturing. Check applicable state requirements for the seller location, storage location, and intended destination. Record the basis for each decision and the responsible reviewer. The weekly deliverable is a ranked model-family shortlist with explicit exclusions, not a warehouse full of discounted equipment.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Buy the Complete Clinical Configuration, Not the Attractive Console**
> 
> Experienced resellers price the missing pieces before they negotiate the visible machine.
> - Photograph probe model numbers, connector pins, cable strain relief, and serial labels; a console price without usable probes is an incomplete acquisition quote.
> - Ask the OEM or authorized service channel about software entitlement transfer before purchase; a working demonstration does not establish transferable feature rights.
> - Request the hospital surplus release and any lender payoff evidence together; the department disposing of equipment may not control encumbered title.
> - Quote liftgate, inside delivery, stairs, crating, and declared-value protection separately; a low freight estimate can disappear at the receiving dock.

## 2. Days 8–14: Turn Supplier Claims into Releasable Asset Evidence  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Week two converts a promising listing into an inspectable asset without assuming ownership prematurely. Request a serial-specific document packet before traveling: proof of authority, maintenance records, configuration screenshots with patient information excluded, accessory inventory, incident disclosures, and storage conditions. Search FDA recalls by manufacturer and model, then establish whether the serial falls within affected production and whether the corrective action was completed. The absence of a search result is not a service certificate. For financed equipment, obtain appropriate lien searches and a release or authorized disposition where required; UCC section 9-315 explains why a security interest can continue after disposition. Have the biomedical contractor write model-specific acceptance tests before inspecting: ultrasound image uniformity, probe integrity, ports, battery condition, and supported software are examples, not a universal test standard. Surgical tables require load, articulation, braking, accessory fit, and applicable electrical checks under the manufacturer's procedures. Veterinary use does not excuse unreliable hardware or contaminated accessories. By Day 10, require three complete inspection dossiers and two independent service estimates. A seller who blocks serial verification or controlled inspection leaves the acquisition queue immediately, regardless of the advertised discount.

Design custody so the team can prove what happened between pickup and buyer acceptance. The logistics contractor photographs the serial, cosmetic condition, accessories, packaging, and handoff at each transfer. Mark incoming equipment quarantined until decontamination and technical checks are complete. Follow manufacturer-compatible cleaning methods and document chemicals, contact times, operator identity, and limitations; surface cleaning does not establish that an invasive accessory is sterile or reusable. Exclude single-use accessories from any informal reuse plan. For data-bearing equipment, ask the seller to remove patient information before transfer and obtain evidence, then independently verify accessible storage and configured network destinations. Factory reset alone may leave DICOM studies, removable media, credentials, or service logs. Use manufacturer-supported erasure or media replacement methods that preserve safe device function and transferable software rights. Where the reseller performs services involving protected health information for a covered entity, assess business associate status and establish the required agreement before access. Apply 45 CFR 164.310(d) disposal and reuse safeguards where applicable. An accidental patient-data discovery triggers isolation and the agreed incident process, not screenshots in the sales chat. Release records contain verification results, not copies of the patient material removed.

Keep the servicing boundary visible in every work order. Restoring a device to its original specifications differs from changing intended use or significantly altering safety or performance; actual activities determine whether remanufacturing obligations arise. Do not represent an ordinary resale business as automatically subject to every manufacturer requirement, and do not assume the word refurbished creates an exemption. The reviewer determines the relevant obligations under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and implementing rules, including 21 CFR Part 820 when the activity makes the business a covered manufacturer. Prescription-device distribution and labeling under 21 CFR 801.109 require a separate device-specific review. Check intended application, buyer eligibility, labeling, and destination before issuing a binding offer. On Day 12, run a mock release using one complete dossier; every missing record becomes a named corrective task. On Day 13, compare the three plays using verified demand, supported service access, all-in cash exposure, and probable time to acceptance. On Day 14, select one pilot asset or record a deliberate no-purchase decision. Both outcomes are valid if they protect capital and document why the evidence did or did not support a transaction.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Independent Checks Before Money or Equipment Moves**
> 
> Commercial interest never substitutes for the evidence needed to own, handle, or release a device.
> - Ownership check: match seller authority, serial evidence, and applicable lien release before paying an acquisition balance.
> - Technical check: require signed model-specific biomed results and completed corrective work before removing quarantine status.
> - Data and contamination check: verify compatible decontamination and storage sanitization, with every exception resolved before outbound release.
> - Commercial check: obtain a qualified buyer profile, complete landed-cost worksheet, and cash forecast that preserves the $15,000 exception reserve.

## 3. Days 15–21: Sell a Documented Configuration and Test the Unit Economics  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Week three tests whether a buyer will pay for the exact configuration the pod can deliver, rather than for a generic model name. Prepare a serial-specific offer with dated condition photographs, included probes or accessories, supported software features, test results, delivery responsibilities, installation boundaries, and acceptance criteria. Use eBay Business and Industrial, DOTmed, direct outreach, and local service partners as discovery channels, while following each channel's applicable listing rules. Asking prices indicate competition, not realized value; preserve closed-sale evidence or actual written buyer responses separately. Send at least six offers by Day 21, covering all three plays, but describe unowned equipment accurately as subject to availability and inspection. Do not publish patient-bearing screen images or claim OEM certification without substantiation. A qualified offer identifies the buyer, application, configuration, total delivered price, expected decision date, and unresolved conditions. Require a named clinical or technical acceptance contact, not merely an enthusiastic intermediary. For diagnostic and veterinary ultrasound, demonstrate the required application using suitable test methods rather than repurposed patient studies. For surgical equipment, verify receiving-site dimensions, utilities, and accessory compatibility before treating the quote as actionable demand.

Use an explicitly illustrative pilot worksheet to expose margin leakage. A $20,000 equipment sale might require $10,000 acquisition, $1,500 refurbishment, $600 inbound freight, $400 decontamination and data work, $500 testing, $800 outbound freight, $400 transaction costs, and a $600 warranty provision. Those $14,800 attributable costs leave $5,200 contribution, or 26%, before general overhead and owner economics. This is a screening example, not a fourth canonical scenario. Its pre-sale inventory exposure is $13,000 through testing; a conservative cash forecast also reserves outbound and transaction payments. Count testing contractor charges once, even when the same contractor also consumes the monthly retainer allocation. A 10% realized-price reduction to $18,000 leaves only $3,200 contribution, about 17.8%, if attributable costs remain unchanged. That sensitivity explains why the launch requires at least 25% projected contribution and a positive downside contribution before purchase. The canonical annual cases remain $250,000 revenue at 10% net margin for $25,000 owner profit, $1,025,000 at 16.0% for $164,000, and $1,800,000 at 22% for $396,000. Gross equipment resale proceeds are revenue; taxes collected, refundable deposits, and hypothetical buyer treatment revenue are not.

Make the commercial experiment reversible until the asset passes its release checks. Prefer seller-held inspection windows or clearly documented conditional purchase arrangements over speculative stockpiling. If using consignment, specify title, custody, insurance, selling authority, service spending, and return rights; report commission activity separately rather than presenting it as gross resale revenue in the canonical cases. The core launch model here remains purchasing and reselling owned equipment. FT1 logs every objection and next decision date; FT2 updates actual and committed cost daily. Contractors execute bounded work orders with change limits, not open-ended restoration projects. Establish a maximum $1,000 unplanned repair allowance within the existing $15,000 exposure ceiling; anything larger requires re-underwriting or withdrawal before further discretionary work. A buyer deposit remains a liability until earned under the contract and applicable accounting treatment. Record acceptance conditions and refund exposure before accepting it. By Day 21, the pod should possess six issued offers, two written buyer responses with concrete objections, one complete pilot cost sheet, and a documented reason to buy or wait. A disciplined wait is superior to a purchase that creates inventory merely to make the sprint appear productive.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the configuration and custody record
   - Assign an asset identifier and capture model, serial, accessories, software rights, and present location.
   - FT2 controls the record; photographs and seller documents must identify the same physical equipment before inspection spending begins.
2. Obtain independent release evidence
   - Complete ownership review, decontamination, data verification, and model-specific biomed testing.
   - Each contractor signs only their qualified scope; unresolved safety, title, or patient-data exceptions keep the asset quarantined.
3. Underwrite the exact delivered offer
   - Calculate attributable costs, downside contribution, acceptance timing, and deposit refund exposure.
   - FT1 owns buyer terms while FT2 checks cash commitments; both sign the worksheet before any acquisition balance is paid.
4. Close acceptance and reconcile actual economics
   - Record delivery condition, signed acceptance, collected proceeds, warranty obligations, and final unit costs.
   - Compare actual contribution with the approved worksheet and update the model-family price ceiling before the next purchase.

## 4. Days 22–30 and Reviews at 60/90: Kill the Hazard, Fix the Constraint, Scale the Evidence  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

The final nine days convert scattered activity into a decision packet the six-person pod can defend. On Days 22–24, reconcile supplier claims against inspection findings, complete a delivery dry run, and have an independent reviewer sample the pilot dossier. On Days 25–27, conduct buyer follow-ups, update signed quotation assumptions, and build a thirteen-week cash forecast that distinguishes operating payments, inventory commitments, restricted deposits, and the exception reserve. On Days 28–30, score each play, reconcile all commitments, and issue a written Kill, Fix, or Scale decision. The metrics count distinct counterparties and serial-specific candidates; repeated calls and revised quotes do not manufacture progress. Define an active opportunity as a named buyer with application fit, stated budget, an identified decision contact, and a dated next step within thirty days. Define a release-ready asset as one with every mandatory technical, ownership, data, and distribution condition satisfied. Zero unresolved critical exceptions is mandatory in every window. Any suspected unsafe device, compromised patient information, or defective ownership evidence immediately stops movement of the affected asset while the responsible specialist determines containment and remediation. Commercial scores never offset those failures.

Apply the decision matrix in order: hard-stop conditions first, all Scale conditions second, and Fix otherwise. A Kill decision normally closes an asset or play, not automatically the entire company; inability to fund existing obligations or preserve the exception reserve stops new company-wide commitments. At Day 30, Scale means permission to run one controlled pilot under the original limits after eighteen qualified interviews, six eligible sourcing leads, six issued offers, three active opportunities, and one release-ready candidate. At Day 60, require twenty-four interviews, ten offers, five active opportunities, and two release-ready candidates to expand sourcing work, not inventory exposure. At Day 90, purchasing expansion requires one completed, accepted, collected resale with at least 25% actual contribution, three further active opportunities, and replacement working capital. Without that sale, a valid funded pipeline remains Fix, consistent with the canonical 3–9 month time to first deal. An asset aged over sixty days triggers a written liquidation quote and carrying-cost review; ninety days prohibits another purchase in that model family until the stock is sold or a funded exit is documented. Calendar pressure is never a reason to falsify readiness.

Give Fix a short clock and a specific experiment: ten business days, one accountable founder, and at most $2,000 of already available operating budget, with no increase to the monthly ceiling. Examples include five new buyer interviews after weak demand, a second service estimate after excessive repair pricing, or a manufacturer-supported erasure procedure after incomplete storage verification. Failed safety remediation kills the affected asset; repeated commercial weakness kills the lane under the matrix thresholds. At Day 90, a qualified Scale decision can permit a second concurrent asset only with separately funded working capital, the protected $15,000 reserve, and a forecast covering six months of planned operating outflows. Keep the launch pod at 2 FT + 4 C until actual workload warrants change; the canonical Scaled case uses 3 FT + 5 C and cannot be claimed by simply multiplying pilot contribution. Reconcile gross resale proceeds, acquisition and refurbishment costs, overhead, warranty expense, and economically required labor before comparing annual net margins. Inventory tracking and service-history indexing should reduce search time and missing evidence, not automate technical judgments. End every review with cash remaining, inventory age, open obligations, and the next dated decision. Scale the repeatable control system before scaling the stock.
