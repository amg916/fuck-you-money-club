## Executive Summary

Imaging resale operators agree on one rule: a magnet bound for the secondhand market is deinstalled cold and energized, never quenched, because thermal shock can kill it and the helium bill is brutal. Block Imaging prices replacement helium at $20 to $30 a liter on magnets holding about 2,000 liters and wants levels kept above 70 percent. MRIPETCTSOURCE puts a 1.5T or 3T fill at 1,200 to 1,500 liters, about $60,000, with older units boiling off 5 to 10 liters a day. Buyers are told to purchase the whole system (coils, software, service records, room, and support), not the magnet photo, with extended contracts running one, three, or five years past warranty. Practitioners split on planned quenches for relocation, legacy helium magnets versus sealed 7-liter designs, and how far to trust manufacturer quench-pipe details.

## The Practitioner Consensus: Keep the Magnet Cold, Sell the Whole System

The loudest point of agreement among imaging operators is that a superconducting magnet headed for the secondhand market stays cold and stays energized. Toby Gilk tells the FGI podcast flatly that if an MRI is going to be resold on the secondhand market, nobody wants to quench it, because quenching creates thermal shocks that can wind up damaging the system [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:34:49]. Block Imaging backs that up from the dealer side: the preferred route is to preserve the integrity of the magnet and take it down the proper way, because a quench is distressing for the magnet and can damage its inner workings to the point where it might not even come back [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:02:04]. The planned quench, triggered with the red button or done physically by an engineer, is the move for magnets being decommissioned, old magnets nobody needs anymore, and units going to scrap [1GAyLn0XcLI @ 00:02:07]. Imaging Notes explains why a resale magnet cannot simply be unplugged: a niobium titanium coil sitting in liquid helium at about 4 K has zero resistance, so the current started at installation keeps circulating forever, and taking a scanner out of service is a lifting job, not a switch off [fRqZHq_Gruc @ 00:02:36]. Every deinstall plan, every rigging quote, and every buyer conversation starts from that single fact.

Operators also agree that the helium level is the asset's vital sign. Block Imaging's rule is to keep helium above 70 percent under proper maintenance, because once a magnet drops below the recommended level the shrinking coolant can no longer hold it at negative 452 and it starts to heat up [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:03:19]. JHL Solutions lists the spontaneous-quench triggers that make this so frustrating: a leak, valves that were not tightened enough, ice forming to the point of causing a quench, and a power outage that stops the cooling system and is left unrepaired long enough for the helium level to fall into quench danger [1GAyLn0XcLI @ 00:02:24]. MRIPETCTSOURCE puts the stakes in dollars: the average 1.5T or 3T magnet holds 1,200 to 1,500 liters, roughly $60,000 of liquid helium for the initial fill, with prices nearly doubling in recent years [Bu466hadTu0 @ 00:00:23]. Gilk adds the supply-side warning that facilities have sat down for protracted periods after quenches because service contractors could not get sufficient quantities of liquid helium [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:32:11]. Put together, a cold, full magnet with a documented helium history is the inventory. A quenched magnet is a refill bill, a downtime problem, and possibly scrap.

On the buy side, the consensus shifts from the magnet to the whole package. Reclaim Atlas maps the chain: the end buyers are other hospitals, outpatient imaging centers, and specialist providers, but the party buying from the original hospital is often an equipment dealer or a manufacturer's refurbishment business connecting a machine leaving one service with a customer who can use it again [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:00:14]. The same channel insists buyers pin down exactly what is being sold, whether a complete system, a magnet with some accessories missing, equipment as it stands, or a package that includes refurbishment and installation, because listings with almost identical photographs can be very different purchases [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:02:17]. Block Imaging sells into that gap with extended service contracts and pitches its strongest help to owners of end-of-support systems that just got the OEM letter cutting off engineering or parts availability [3203LTd64a8 @ 00:01:33]. The Boundary Bay veterinary build shows what the receiving site has to be ready with: cable trays, the RF panel where all systems pass through the wall, medical gas, seismic anchors in the floor, static-dissipating vinyl, and a quench pipe running straight up and out of the building before the magnet ever arrives [xr3Vfmkli0Q @ 00:01:12]. Machine, paperwork, service coverage, and room move as one deal.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The cost model starts with cryogen. MRIPETCTSOURCE lays out the boil-off math: older systems lose as much as 5 to 10 liters a day, up to $500 a day in liquid helium alone, and need refills of about 1,000 liters every 9 to 14 months, while newer systems boil off under 1 liter a day and get filled every 24 to 48 months or more, all assuming no quench [Bu466hadTu0 @ 00:00:57]. Block Imaging prices the quench side of the ledger at 20 to 30 dollars a liter on magnets holding around 2,000 liters, before counting the damage to the magnet's inner workings [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:02:59]. That exposure is exactly what the low-helium designs attack. JHL Solutions describes a sealed magnet operating on only 7 liters of helium, placed in the magnet during manufacturing and sealed in for the rest of its lifetime, pitched with no compromise on speed, patient comfort, staff comfort, or clinical confidence [1GAyLn0XcLI @ 00:03:22]. Gilk says most manufacturers now offer systems using between 5 and 20 liters, cooling the superconducting coils through heat exchangers instead of direct contact with a helium bath, and because these units carry trivial cryogen with their own containment, they do not need a quench pipe at all [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:32:52]. That difference changes both the refill budget and the room build.

Block Imaging spells out how a full service agreement gets priced: the major components drive it, with the CT tube or glassware called out as a major price line, balanced against the labor that goes into the agreement [3203LTd64a8 @ 00:00:30]. What the extended agreement covers is all preventive maintenance, all parts, labor, and travel, plus an assigned customer service representative who handles incoming calls and gets to know the site, and eligible gear runs across MRI, PET/CT, CT, X-ray, cath labs, CR, and mammography [3203LTd64a8 @ 00:00:58]. On the refurbishment side, Reclaim Atlas walks through the Siemens Healthineers five-stage program of selection, removal, refurbishment, installation, and services, where selection screens on condition, service history, performance, and technology level before component checks, worn-part replacement, and suitable updates [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:05:36]. The same channel flags the line the FDA draws between servicing and remanufacturing that significantly changes a device's performance, safety specifications, or intended use, so the work performed matters more than the word refurbished on the listing, and restoring specified performance is not the same as delivering every feature of the latest model [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:06:22]. Philips advertising warranty and service support on its refurbished MRI line shows support can ride inside the offer, but not every used scanner carries equivalent terms.

Site work is where a used-scanner deal either pencils out or blows up. Gilk's expansion math sets the stakes: liquid helium grows about 8 times going to gas and roughly another hundredfold warming to room temperature, about 750 to 1 overall, so 1,000 liters becomes about 750,000 liters, around five times the volume of a typical scanner room [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:24:13]. That is why the door cannot be the relief path: a half pound per square inch rise across a 4,000 square inch hospital door puts about 2,000 pounds on the hinges and latch, the same physics as pennying someone into a dorm room [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:28:25]. Gilk's three-part fix for quench-pipe magnets is the pipe built to manufacturer spec with a complete weather head no matter what the vendor detail shows, an exhaust fan to pull helium out of the occupied room, and a pressure relief path that does not involve the doorway, such as a vent with a waveguide or a blowout plug [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:29:26]. Imaging Notes adds the five gauss line, half a milliTesla, which bulges through floors and ceilings and has to be surveyed with a meter, drawn on a plan, and marked on the floor rather than guessed from the size of the room [fRqZHq_Gruc @ 00:06:22].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the Unit Before Quoting
   - Pull the full configuration, coil list, software capabilities, and service records for the system.
   - Reclaim Atlas says service records and a detailed equipment inventory belong in the deal alongside the machinery, because the paperwork reveals what the machine can become and what it still needs, and a working magnet does not prove every exam the buyer wants is possible [Reclaim Atlas — XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:04:06].
2. Lock Down Magnet and Helium State
   - Confirm helium level is above 70 percent and plan a cold, energized deinstall with no quench.
   - Block Imaging wants helium kept above 70 percent so the magnet stays at negative 452 [Block Imaging — _-e12TPIFxw @ 00:03:19], and Gilk says secondhand-market sellers avoid quenching because the thermal shocks damage the system [FGI — HPAddrORIJU @ 00:34:49].
3. Survey the Route and the Receiving Room
   - Check the exit and entry route, structural support, power, cooling, ventilation, RF shielding, and venting.
   - Reclaim Atlas says engineers must consider access and structural support along the route, plus electrical supply, cooling, ventilation, and RF shielding at the destination [Reclaim Atlas — XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:04:21]; Gilk requires a weather-headed quench pipe, an exhaust fan, and a door-independent pressure relief path [FGI — HPAddrORIJU @ 00:29:26].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split on the exit side is whether you quench a magnet on the way out. Block Imaging lays out the planned quench as a standard exit path: when a magnet is being decommissioned, relocated, is old, or is headed to scrap, you either hit the red button or an engineer goes in and quenches it, and the helium gets vented [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:01:45]. VET talk TV filmed that play at ARH Homebush, where the team quenched an end-of-life magnet to clear the room for a newer 1.5 Tesla install, moving everyone outside and staging a temporary emergency unit before the countdown [Xn2oD7o0yyU @ 00:00:23]. FGI guest Toby Gilk flips the economics for anyone flipping iron: if an MRI is going to be resold on the secondhand market, the seller does not want to quench it, because quenching creates thermal shocks that can damage the system [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:34:49]. Block Imaging lands on the same side, saying you usually want to preserve the integrity of the magnet and take it down the proper way, warning that a quench can damage the inner workings to the point the magnet might not come back, on top of venting helium priced at 20 to 30 dollars a liter across roughly 2,000 liters [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:02:59].

Practitioners do not agree on the helium numbers a buyer should underwrite. Block Imaging quotes roughly 2,000 liters per magnet at 20 to 30 dollars a liter, while MRIPETCTSOURCE puts the average 1.5T and 3T magnet at 1,200 to 1,500 liters, about 60,000 dollars for the initial fill. MRIPETCTSOURCE then splits the fleet in two: older systems boil off as much as 5 to 10 liters a day, up to 500 dollars a day, and need about 1,000-liter refills every 9 to 14 months, while efficient systems lose under 1 liter a day and refill every 24 to 48 months or more [Bu466hadTu0 @ 00:00:40]. The alternative playbook is skipping the bath entirely. FGI's Toby Gilk says most manufacturers now ship magnets cooled through heat exchangers using between 5 and 20 liters of helium, versus the 1,000 and 1,800 liter examples on conventional units, and that those trivial-cryogen systems do not need a quench pipe at all, the difference between a polite burp and clearing the room [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:32:52]. JHL Solutions describes a sealed magnet running on only 7 liters, placed during manufacturing and fully sealed for the rest of its lifetime [1GAyLn0XcLI @ 00:03:22], against the 2,000-liter conventional load Block Imaging prices out [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:02:59].

On sourcing and support, the playbooks diverge between buying a refurbished package and buying iron then bolting on a service contract. Reclaim Atlas maps the chain: a hospital's outgoing scanner is often bought first by an equipment dealer or a manufacturer's refurbishment business, which then places it with other hospitals, outpatient imaging centers, or specialist healthcare providers [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:00:14]. The OEM route is spelled out through Siemens Healthineers' five-stage program of selection, removal, refurbishment, installation, and services, with selection screening condition, service history, performance, and technology level [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:05:36]. Block Imaging runs the service-contract play: a one-year warranty on the sale, then extended service contracts of typically one, three, or five years covering preventive maintenance, parts, labor, travel, and an assigned customer service representative, with the CT tube or glassware called out as the major price line in any agreement [3203LTd64a8 @ 00:00:30]. Block Imaging also pitches directly at end-of-support systems, the ones whose owners receive an OEM letter cutting off engineering or parts availability, promising agreements customized to fit every budget [3203LTd64a8 @ 00:01:33]. Reclaim Atlas adds two non-resale exits: parting the system out to an imaging parts business, or keeping the magnet in place behind an upgrade.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The ugliest firsthand failure belongs to the quench pipe. FGI's Toby Gilk served as an expert witness on an MRI whose scanner literally exploded and blew open inside the room after a spontaneous quench. On teardown, the thin graphite burst disc that should have turned to powder and ejected was largely intact apart from a couple of quarter-sized holes, a physical impossibility unless water had accumulated in the pipe, frozen solid from the venting cold gas, and braced the disc like reinforced concrete while pressure built [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:15:47]. The root cause traced to a vendor detail that rose vertically, turned 90 degrees, and ended in a 45-degree chamfered cut; multiple rings of water and bacteria inside showed the level rising and falling with the rainy season [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:19:33]. Block Imaging lists the more mundane triggers for a spontaneous quench: a leak, valves not tightened enough, ice forming, or a power outage that stops the cooling system and is left unrepaired long enough for helium to drop into danger [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:02:18]. Imaging Notes puts the downtime bill plainly: because the helium is gone, the scanner is out of service for days afterwards [fRqZHq_Gruc @ 00:07:35].

On the safety-liability side, the practitioner record shows that perfectly functioning equipment still injures people. Imaging Notes opens with the 2001 New York case where an oxygen cylinder carried into a scan room during an emergency was pulled across the room and struck a 6-year-old boy on the table, who died; the error was geography, someone crossing a line they did not know was there [fRqZHq_Gruc @ 00:00:23]. Metrasens presenters John Posh and Tobias Gilk walk through a MAUDE case where a patient entered the suite in street clothes with a loaded weapon in a pants pocket; it was drawn to the magnet, discharged, and shot the patient in the leg, after the patient said they had removed everything [DdLolB1kuRY @ 00:33:27]. The site had no change policy and no ferromagnetic detection, and Gilk's takeaway is that patient answers need to be treated with suspicion. The second Metrasens case is a burn on a large sedated patient mummified in a sheet and scanned for 90 minutes; staff checked the patient several times, but a bed sheet or single-layer blanket will not deliver the vendor's minimum bore separation of roughly five millimeters to a centimeter [DdLolB1kuRY @ 00:24:03]. Imaging Notes adds that the people who actually get hurt are usually the unscreened relative, porter, engineer, or cleaner [fRqZHq_Gruc @ 00:11:00].

The economics traps hit after the purchase order, not at the listing. Reclaim Atlas describes a clinic that finds an attractive scanner without checking its building: the equipment quote looks manageable until the installation survey reveals additional work, and the project becomes a different proposition even though the advertised price never moved [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:05:21]. The same breakdown warns that the cheaper offer cannot be judged until totals include repairs, removal, transport, site preparation, installation, testing, and operating costs, and that the contract handover point, whether responsibility ends at collection, at the building, or after agreed installation tests, decides who eats an unexpected problem [XEbn26lA8EE @ 00:08:35]. FGI's Gilk adds that once an MRI is up and running nobody wants to touch the built environment, because rework means interrupting patient care, additional expense, and lost revenue, so most facilities get one bite at the apple and should not treat the design as a copy-and-paste commodity [HPAddrORIJU @ 00:35:39]. Block Imaging's maintenance floor is keeping helium above 70 percent, the core reason it gives for keeping a magnet under proper maintenance, because below the recommended level the magnet stops cooling the way it is supposed to and can heat up [_-e12TPIFxw @ 00:03:19].
