This chapter maps three operating plays: CON-sensitive decommissioned imaging arbitrage, diagnostic equipment refurbishment, and veterinary equipment redeployment; the latter two are chapter-level applications, not additional canonical play identifiers. ## 1. Define the asset, the customer promise, and the revenue boundary  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Medical equipment resale is the purchase, preparation, and sale of identifiable devices whose remaining utility exceeds the cost of restoring buyer confidence. The operator acquires title or a clearly defined selling mandate, documents condition, arranges appropriate servicing, and delivers an agreed configuration to an identified buyer. Diagnostic ultrasound, surgical tables, veterinary monitors, and aesthetic lasers are different businesses because their accessories, technical support, installation requirements, and failure liabilities differ. A listing marked working does not establish measurement accuracy, software rights, electrical safety, or clinical suitability. The actual product is a serial-numbered asset plus evidence that its promised functions survive transport and acceptance testing. Five useful archetypes are diagnostic refurbisher, surgical package assembler, veterinary redeployer, aesthetic specialist, and imaging project trader. Each earns a spread by resolving a specific uncertainty rather than simply finding a cheaper seller. Bimedis, DOTmed, eBay, and hospital auctions expose opportunities, but none substitutes for a title review or a model-specific inspection. Start with a narrow supported model family, a qualified service partner, and buyers willing to specify acceptance criteria before the purchase order. The first competitive advantage is knowing which apparently cheap assets cannot economically become saleable inventory.

Revenue follows the contractual role and transfer of control, not the largest number passing through a transaction. When the business controls equipment before transfer, bears inventory risk, and is responsible for delivering the promised asset, gross equipment resale proceeds are the relevant principal revenue basis. Acquisition, refurbishment, contractor labor, freight obligations, and warranty exposure then reduce earnings. A $25,000 sale of an owned ultrasound system is not merely the difference between its selling price and its purchase price. Conversely, an agent arranging a $100,000 hospital asset sale for an earned 8% commission ordinarily records $8,000 of fee revenue, not $100,000 of equipment revenue. Labels such as broker or consignment are insufficient; assess control and the actual promises using ASC 606 principal-versus-agent guidance. Deposits received before performance are generally liabilities, and purchased devices held for resale are inventory rather than immediate revenue offsets. Separately assess freight, installation, taxes collected for authorities, returns, and warranty terms. A buyer's future patient-service receipts are outside the reseller's revenue boundary. Keep agency fees in a separate ledger so brokerage volume cannot inflate the canonical gross-resale scorecard or make an inventory-light operation appear comparable to a principal-owned trading business.

The durable moat is an evidence chain that competitors cannot recreate by copying photographs. For every serial number, retain acquisition authority, lien-release evidence where applicable, incoming condition images, configuration details, service findings, decontamination records, data-sanitization evidence, and signed acceptance results. Use Odoo or Zoho Inventory to track status and landed cost, with access-controlled document storage for supporting records. Service-history indexing should expose recurring fault codes, unsupported software releases, probe failures, and parts consumption across the same model family. It should not accumulate patient identifiers inside a general sales database. A contractor's reputation helps, but a reproducible test protocol and traceable instruments matter more than a reassuring verbal assessment. UCC Sections 2-312 and 2-313 explain why title promises and specific descriptions deserve operational discipline: a listing can create obligations that survive a casually worded as-is label. Describe tested functions precisely and distinguish cosmetic restoration from verified performance. A beginner sells an attractive photograph and hopes the buyer discovers no expensive defect. An established operator prices a documented configuration, budgets the residual defect risk, and knows which service organization can resolve a failure after delivery without consuming the entire deal spread.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Buy the evidence before buying the apparent bargain**
> 
> Veteran traders reduce uncertainty before committing scarce working capital. These tactics create a repeatable advantage without pretending that every used device deserves refurbishment.
> - Request the exact software version, enabled options, accessory serials, and service-access terms before negotiating the headline price.
> - Ask the biomed contractor to quote a model-specific incoming inspection separately from repair labor so rejection remains economically possible.
> - Photograph accessory layouts and connector condition before packing; these images resolve missing-part disputes faster than generic condition statements.
> - Maintain model-family defect histories and quote a stressed exit price before bidding, including the cost of a second freight movement.

## 2. Select sub-verticals by supportability rather than auction discount  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

A sub-vertical is attractive when customers can define the required configuration, service partners can measure performance, and enough comparable transactions support disciplined pricing. Asking prices on DOTmed or Bimedis are discovery signals, not completed-sale evidence. Auction catalogs from GovDeals or AllSurplus often make removal deadlines and buyer premiums explicit, but those terms vary by seller and lot. Read the actual auction conditions instead of borrowing a fee percentage from another transaction. Direct hospital purchases may avoid a marketplace charge while introducing procurement coordination, lien questions, and difficult deinstallation. eBay adds category-specific selling costs, payment timing, and return exposure; verify current terms before setting a bid ceiling. Establish a landed-cost worksheet that includes acquisition, premium, unrecoverable taxes, removal, packing, transport, inspection, parts, software transfer, and expected support costs. The bid ceiling is the conservative net selling price minus every remaining cost and the required contribution. Refuse inventory whose price works only if a missing accessory appears free, a password becomes available, or a hospital extends removal time. Those are unresolved costs, not entrepreneurial upside. A six-figure original purchase price means little when the manufacturer no longer supports the installed configuration or replacement parts consume the achievable resale price.

Map the mandatory play con_decommissioned_medical_imaging_arbitrage to equipment and site diligence as two separate workstreams. A hospital's approved capacity can influence a buyer's interest, but a used scanner does not carry portable permission to operate an imaging service. State Certificate-of-Need rules can address projects, services, facilities, ownership changes, and replacement equipment differently. North Carolina General Statutes Section 131E-178 illustrates the requirement to establish whether a proposed new institutional health service requires a certificate before undertaking it. Never infer transferable authority from a seller's historic utilization or an existing room. Consider an illustrative $125,000 equipment acquisition, $22,000 deinstallation and transport budget, $12,000 inspection and servicing budget, $6,000 holding allowance, and $10,000 transaction overhead allocation. A $190,000 principal resale leaves $15,000 after those $175,000 of specified costs. A $10,000 option credited against the purchase price is part of the $125,000 acquisition, not an extra profit source. This is deal arithmetic, not an annual forecast. Contract for inspection and required authorization contingencies, cap the option exposure, and verify refund terms. If the intended site transaction cannot proceed, exercise an available termination right or sell inspected equipment separately without representing transferable operating authority.

The two complementary chapter-level plays are diagnostic refurbishment and veterinary redeployment. For a diagnostic example, a $14,000 ultrasound purchase plus $3,000 of probe and system testing, $1,500 freight, and a $1,000 warranty allowance creates a $19,500 specified cost basis. A $25,000 sale provides $5,500 contribution before remaining business overhead, not $5,500 of assured owner profit. If inspection reveals an additional $6,000 probe replacement, that spread turns negative before overhead; reject the acquisition under negotiated inspection terms or reprice the device for a clearly described alternative sale. For veterinary redeployment, a $4,000 monitor package, $1,200 service and accessory budget, and $600 shipping cost against an $8,000 sale yield $2,200 before overhead and residual support exposure. Species-appropriate accessories, intended application, and buyer training needs remain explicit diligence items. A failed compatibility check means returning the asset when contractually allowed or selling it into a supported use, not improvising undocumented adaptations. Both examples reward standardization across repeatable configurations. Neither example changes the canonical annual profit cases. Keep these contribution calculations beside the inventory record so acquisition staff can distinguish a repairable exception from an asset whose eventual sale merely hides a loss behind impressive transaction volume.

## 3. Reconcile the scorecard with inventory turns and fully counted costs  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

The canonical Conservative case is $250,000 gross equipment resale revenue at a 10% net margin, producing $25,000 annual owner net profit with two full-time staff and four contractors. The Base case is $1,025,000 at 16.0%, producing $164,000 with the same staffing counts. The Scaled case is $1,800,000 at 22%, producing $396,000 with three full-time staff and five contractors, eight people in total. These imply total annual expenses of $225,000, $861,000, and $1,404,000 respectively. Contractor counts describe people engaged, not full-time-equivalent workloads. For an illustrative volume bridge, ten sales averaging $25,000 reproduce Conservative revenue; forty-one sales averaging $25,000 reproduce Base revenue; sixty sales averaging $30,000 reproduce Scaled revenue. These are arithmetic decompositions rather than promised deal frequencies or a required product mix. The corresponding fully allocated expense per sale is $22,500, $21,000, and $23,400, respectively. A higher absolute cost per Scaled transaction is compatible with a stronger margin because its average selling price is higher. Acquisition, refurbishment, paid labor, ordinary support, and operating expenses must fit within each total expense envelope. Do not append contractor invoices after calling the residual owner profit, and do not count inventory purchases twice when translating earnings into cash requirements.

Working capital explains why a profitable spreadsheet can still produce a failed trading business. The startup capital range is $20,000 to $95,000, monthly operating expenditure ranges from $4,000 to $18,000, and time to first deal is three to nine months. Those are planning ranges, not additional deductions layered onto already complete annual cases. Classify recurring operating costs separately from inventory acquisition cash flows and reconcile both to the expense model. Three months at the upper operating range consumes $66,000 before any equipment purchase, so the lowest capital starting point cannot support every inventory strategy. A hospital purchase paid immediately, followed by six weeks of servicing and thirty-day customer terms, can tie up cash far longer than the visible repair duration. Customer deposits help fund the cycle but remain obligations until the promised performance occurs. Maintain a rolling thirteen-week cash forecast with serial-level expected completion dates, committed contractor payments, removal deadlines, tax payments, and realistic collection timing. Set purchase limits against the stressed forecast rather than bank balance alone. Track aged inventory at thirty, sixty, and ninety days, then reprice, return where permitted, or liquidate under a documented plan instead of allowing yesterday's purchase decision to dictate today's valuation.

Sensitivity work should attack the assumptions most likely to break, not add attractive revenue categories. In the Base decomposition, a $1,000 average increase in unrecovered costs across forty-one sales reduces annual profit by $41,000, from $164,000 to $123,000, assuming revenue and other costs remain unchanged. A single $20,000 unrecovered warranty loss consumes roughly 12.2% of the canonical Base profit. In the imaging illustration, an additional $18,000 repair changes the specified $15,000 deal remainder into a $3,000 loss. These are sensitivities, not replacement scorecard cases. Use them to set purchase contingencies, maximum repair authorizations, and model-family concentration limits. Avoid booking speculative service subscriptions or buyer procedure income as a rescue. If a separate maintenance agreement is actually sold, evaluate its performance obligations and costs separately rather than recognizing the entire contract with the equipment shipment. Track contribution by model, realized warranty cost, days to cash, and repeat-buyer rate alongside gross sales. A business with fewer sales and cleaner acceptance can outperform one carrying impressive but disputed invoices. Inventory automation earns its place when it prevents duplicate purchases, catches missing accessories, and identifies unsupported configurations early; it does not make technician judgment or field service capacity disappear from the labor model.

## 4. Make technical evidence and bounded obligations the operating system  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Regulatory diligence begins with the device and the activity, not the reseller's preferred business label. FDA establishment registration and listing obligations under 21 CFR Part 807 depend on the role performed; buying and reselling used equipment does not automatically make every domestic trader a manufacturer. Importing, relabeling, repackaging, or remanufacturing can change the analysis. FDA distinguishes servicing intended to return a device to its original safety and performance specifications and intended use from remanufacturing that significantly changes performance, safety specifications, or intended use. Document the scope before modifying hardware or software, and obtain specialist review when a proposed change crosses that boundary. The Quality Management System Regulation in 21 CFR Part 820 became effective February 2, 2026; determine applicability rather than assuming a technician's invoice satisfies manufacturer obligations. Search FDA recall records using model, catalog number, and serial range, then resolve applicable corrective actions before shipment. Radiation-producing equipment adds device-specific federal and destination-state requirements; mammography facilities also face 21 CFR Part 900 requirements. State licensing, prescription-device restrictions, and installation rules require a destination-specific check. Neither veterinary placement nor export should serve as a casual escape route for a device whose actual condition and permitted use remain unresolved after inspection.

Patient-data control and physical decontamination are separate acceptance tracks. Ultrasound consoles, imaging workstations, printers, and removable storage may retain patient names, images, worklists, network credentials, and exported reports. Ask the transferring institution to identify storage and its authorized sanitization process before collection. Where the reseller acts as a business associate and handles protected health information, address the applicable contract and safeguard requirements under 45 CFR Sections 164.502(e), 164.504(e), and 164.308. HIPAA does not automatically apply to every reseller merely because equipment once sat in a hospital, but that distinction is no reason to accept uncontrolled patient records. The media disposal and reuse provisions in 45 CFR Section 164.310(d)(2) provide concrete requirements for regulated parties. Use an authorized, validated process consistent with current NIST media-sanitization guidance and manufacturer constraints; a factory reset alone may not erase all storage. Preserve necessary software entitlements while recording what was sanitized, by whom, when, and how completion was verified. Separately require model-appropriate cleaning or reprocessing evidence before technician handling. A generic clean sticker cannot establish that a flexible endoscope was properly reprocessed or that inaccessible contaminated components are safe to handle. Unresolved assets stay isolated from saleable inventory.

Build a small-team workflow around explicit custody changes and signed evidence, not verbal assurances between independent contractors. One full-time role can own sourcing, contracts, and buyer qualification while another owns inventory records, scheduling, and acceptance coordination; specialist testing, rigging, freight, and field service remain assigned to qualified contractors. That division fits the canonical staffing structure without pretending every contractor works a full week. Before acquisition, confirm seller authority, exact configuration, inspection rights, removal obligations, and the downside exit. At intake, record condition and isolate the asset until decontamination, data handling, and technical checks are complete. Before listing, reconcile the advertised functions against the test report, software rights, included accessories, warranty scope, and destination requirements. Before delivery, obtain buyer agreement on installation responsibilities and measurable acceptance criteria. Preserve the packed-condition photographs and freight handoff record, then close the sale against actual contractual performance rather than a convenient invoice date. Schedule a post-delivery check while the service evidence is fresh and track every claim against the specific model family. The commercial objective is not eliminating all returns. It is making defects discoverable early, responsibilities legible, and recovery costs small enough that repeat transactions compound operating knowledge instead of repeatedly financing the same avoidable mistake.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Underwrite the exact configuration
   - Create the serial-level asset record and calculate a stressed acquisition ceiling.
   - Attach seller authority, accessories, software terms, channel charges, removal quotes, and an equipment-only fallback value.
2. Receive into controlled inventory
   - Record custody and condition while restricting access until handling requirements are resolved.
   - Keep decontamination, storage-media disposition, and technical inspection as separate documented workstreams with named owners.
3. Release a defensible sale package
   - Match every advertised functional claim to test evidence and the agreed device configuration.
   - Confirm service scope, destination obligations, licensed options, warranty terms, accessories, and buyer installation dependencies.
4. Close against performance and learn
   - Document delivery and acceptance, apply the correct revenue treatment, and monitor claims.
   - Reconcile serial-level costs, release applicable deposit liabilities, update warranty exposure, and index defects for future purchase decisions.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four pre-flight verifications before money becomes stranded inventory**
> 
> Advance the asset only when the responsible person can produce the evidence. An attractive purchase price cannot compensate for an unresolved ownership, safety, or resale restriction.
> - Verify seller authority, serial identity, relevant liens, inspection rights, and enforceable removal terms before releasing acquisition funds.
> - Verify a qualified technician's test plan, supported parts supply, software rights, and a capped repair budget before accepting the asset.
> - Verify documented decontamination and authorized data sanitization before moving equipment into unrestricted handling or saleable stock.
> - Verify buyer destination requirements, installation responsibility, measurable acceptance terms, and a contingency exit for any CON-sensitive project.
