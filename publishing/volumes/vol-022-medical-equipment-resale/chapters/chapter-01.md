## Executive Summary

Operators converge on one engine: buy surplus hospital gear cheap, document and biomed-check it, then resell or rerent it. Bill Whittemore built a garage operation into $100M+ in refurbished sales over 32 years; Centurion Service Group moves about 6,000 pieces a month and turns inventory every 5 weeks; Medinas Health takes a cut of gross sales instead of seller fees. The rental side is the cash machine: the Earn Your Leisure founder bills 13-month Medicare rentals and rerents returned units, but started with $700,000 liquid and 6-7 licenses per location, while a Florida starter pitch claims a $4,000 lean launch. Disagreements are sharp: Joe Verschoor grades high-ticket medical drop shipping only B to B-minus because of Google ad policy friction, and Whittemore warns that wipe-down-and-ship sellers trash the industry's reputation.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The supply side is the one thing every operator agrees on: hospitals dump usable equipment, and the money is in capturing it. Chloe Alpert of Medinas Health saw CT scanners, dialysis and x-ray machines lining facility walls in 2016 and found upwards of $15 billion of electronic medical equipment ending up in the US waste stream, with volumes growing 14% a year, because insurers and hospitals favor the newest technology [NFqBMUIcgl4 @ 00:00:36]. Centurion Service Group has built on that same flow since 2001, representing well over 1,000 hospitals, surgery centers and manufacturers and selling both surplus equipment and manufacturer trade-in programs [dUZz08G_Dzc @ 00:00:36]. Bill Whittemore, who started out of a garage selling to veterinarians and then doctors hunting deals, mostly from Mexico, lists the acquisition channels: buy direct from hospitals wherever possible, work the auction market on an as-is basis, and lean on fellow dealers and wholesalers, because unlike manufacturers, a used dealer cannot simply make more inventory [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:09:11]. Block Imaging adds why sellers show up at all: a facility wants new capability such as AI software or new isotope studies, or its unit is outdated, and even gear two to three generations old still carries component value for servicing newer systems [4suol4m0dq4 @ 00:00:51].

The second consensus point: refurbished gear only sells when it leaves the dock biomed-checked and patient ready. The I Medical Equipment and Service showroom crew, formerly Hospital Direct Medical, stages monitors, defibrillators, infusion pumps and a full operating room labeled biomed certified, patient ready, so doctors can walk in, power units on, compare screens, and schedule delivery or carry small items home [ezF-botYEqQ @ 00:03:57]. All States MED founder Josino Fera pitches brand-new options alongside used and refurbished options at a quarter of the price, backed by an in-house biomed department for ongoing maintenance and repairs [60julptDir4 @ 00:02:07]. The Doctorpreneur Academy tells clinic owners that refurbished big-ticket machines mostly come from Europe and the USA, and to buy through Philips, Siemens or similar companies that provide AMC and CMC coverage, because a direct import leaves nobody to service it when something breaks; refurbished status changes nothing on loans, subsidies or government schemes [GZlFJf6akG0 @ 00:00:56]. Bill Whittemore frames the stakes: the customer who grabbed a cheap unit may have no biomedical team to check it before patient use, and the dealers who keep a biomed team to recheck equipment before retail are the ones who can stand behind it, versus pure buy-low-sell-high brokering sold as-is [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:12:05].

On the home medical side, practitioners agree the recurring rental is the profit lever and service is the moat that holds it together. The Earn Your Leisure founder, who runs 16 locations, qualifies a patient's prescription against Medicare's one-per-five-years rule, bills a 13-month rental on most equipment, then picks the unit back up when the patient dies, is hospitalized or no longer needs it and rerents the same item to someone else [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:08:26]. That founder also argues the middleman exists because manufacturers would need a whole separate division to fit wheelchairs, keep patients from falling and out of the hospital, and service them after delivery [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:04:08]. The Florida starter breakdown from How to Start a Business that Prints Cash names the same lever, recurring monthly rental of specialized hardware, as the one beginners almost always miss, and tells operators to lock in recurring contract revenue [Ahi8z5l5Yao @ 00:00:49]. Bundling is how the margin stacks: a bedbound patient may need a hospital bed, lift, commode, walker, wheelchair and oxygen, so a thin margin on the bed gets offset by a better margin on the wheelchair, which is why the founder calls margins good overall even while admitting some items suck [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:07:28].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Capital requirements split hard by model. The Earn Your Leisure founder says starting a Medicare-billing equipment company required $700,000 liquid back when they launched, admitting the number may have changed since [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:02:44]. Each location then carries at least six and sometimes seven licenses: state licensing, accreditation, Medicare, every other insurance plan, plus a separate oxygen license. Florida takes longer and charges more, which the founder was told traces back to South Florida Medicare fraud, and the founder stresses that Medicare guidelines govern every state, making the industry more regulated than pharmacy [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:05:40]. At the opposite end, the Florida starter breakdown claims a lean operation launches for about $4,000 covering legal formation, basic inventory and liability insurance, with Agency for Health Care Administration compliance as the licensing anchor and wholesale pricing tiers set strictly by purchasing volume, not business age [Ahi8z5l5Yao @ 00:00:16]. ELEGANCEPRENEUR lays out the paperwork sequence: pick a structure, with an LLC for liability protection, register with the Secretary of State, pull a free EIN from the IRS, and check the state Health Department for a Wholesale Distributor license before sourcing from ISO- or FDA-certified suppliers [z8FeVDQ8g_U @ 00:01:30].

On the sell side, the mechanics are about documentation. Block Imaging wants a list before you even pick up the phone: year, make and model; actual usage such as tube age, scan seconds and, for GE, milliamp seconds; and configuration, meaning injectors, monitoring, Mac labs, workstations and the licensed software, which reveals what the system can be used for next [4suol4m0dq4 @ 00:01:35]. Photos shortcut the condition check: at least one glamour shot of the whole unit, the series tag to confirm age and configuration, the computers, the gantry from back, side and front, and every accessory included in the sale, and anyone on staff, from the radiology manager to a tech, can shoot them [4suol4m0dq4 @ 00:02:26]. Robert Remitz runs the peer-to-peer version at Go Southern MD: sellers list new or used equipment free in five to ten minutes by clicking Sell, then experienced staff verify the listing is accurate, advise on what the market is demanding, and make sure buyer and seller both understand the transaction [_ue3Y22oMAA @ 00:03:15]. The I Medical liquidation crew walks entire hospitals, from ER and pediatrics to ICU and OR suites, documenting every asset before reconditioning, scaling from 40 to 60 patient rooms up to 300 to 500 room hospitals [IA2PW_vSjBc @ 00:03:47].

The revenue models vary but the numbers are concrete. Medinas Health charges hospitals no seller's fee, unlike some competitors, and instead takes a percentage of gross sales on each piece, pairing every exclusive hospital with a dedicated manager, regional warehouse space, inventory and sales software, and an approved-buyer list; by October 2019 it had 20 employees and was on track to break 1 million in gross merchandise value [NFqBMUIcgl4 @ 00:02:16]. Centurion pairs its Las Vegas warehouse, where buyers inspect goods and buy in real time, with timed online auctions that let buyers pick up fixed assets directly from the hospital after watching them run [dUZz08G_Dzc @ 00:00:52]. Down at the small-reseller end, Jon Sheppard calls medical stock the biggest profit return of anything picked up over years of reselling: a Repose inflatable support mattress listed at £55 sold for £47 on an offer, an earlier brand-new one went for £100 and another for £40, against roughly £120 to £140 new on Amazon [8meyld3BWGg @ 00:07:41]. For online high-ticket sellers, Joe Verschoor targets products priced $2,000 and up, filters Semrush keywords to a difficulty of 15 or lower with at least 1,000 monthly searches for SEO, and adds 'used' as a negative keyword in Google Ads [-23KPzW6CW8 @ 00:12:47].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how much money it takes to get in the door. The Earn Your Leisure founder, who now runs 15 to 16 locations with one or two warehouses each, said that when they started they needed $700,000 liquid to open an insurance-billed medical equipment operation, and flagged that the number may have changed since [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:02:44]. Their engine is rentals billed to Medicare and other plans: a 13-month rental on most equipment, then pick-up and re-rent of the same item when the patient passes away, goes into the hospital, or no longer needs it [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:08:26]. The How to Start a Business that Prints Cash narrator pitches the opposite entry point: a lean starter operation for about $4,000 covering legal formation, basic inventory, and liability insurance [Ahi8z5l5Yao @ 00:00:16]. That narrator also names recurring monthly rental of specialized hardware as the pricing lever beginners miss, but routes the first 10 paying customers through direct partnerships with regional home health agencies and local nursing facilities instead of a heavy warehouse footprint [Ahi8z5l5Yao @ 00:00:49]. All States MED founder Josino Fera sells a third lane: secure a franchise territory with what they call a small investment, then lean on comprehensive training, location selection, and marketing support from a company that started door-to-door with only a business card [60julptDir4 @ 00:04:05].

Practitioners also split on how much work a unit needs before it ships. Bill Whittemore, 32 years in refurbished equipment and over $100 million sold, argues resellers should benchmark against the manufacturer standards that got the product to market in the first place, and that wiping it down and shipping it off without due diligence is what gives the industry a bad name [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:02:09]. Yet Whittemore concedes that as-is brokering has its place: buy low, sell high, sold as is, take your chances, which works for dealers who have the expertise and a biomed team to recheck equipment before it reaches the retail side [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:12:05]. The I Medical Equipment and Service crew on the Used Medical Equipment channel plants itself at the high-touch end, displaying biomed-certified, patient-ready gear in Richmond, Virginia and San Diego, California so doctors can turn monitors on, compare screen sizes, and test brands before buying [ezF-botYEqQ @ 00:05:23]. At the street-flip end, Jon Sheppard buys medical items at car boots and lists them straight on eBay: a Repose pump-up mattress that runs roughly 120 to 140 pounds new on Amazon sold for 47 pounds, an earlier pair went for 100 and 40, and Sheppard calls medical stuff the biggest return in profits they have found because nobody else picks it up [8meyld3BWGg @ 00:07:41].

Where to buy and sell is just as contested. ## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure Whittemore describes is the lazy test: it works, the lights came on, it must be good, ship it away. They say this is going on all the time, the customer who got the great price often lacks the biomedical team to check the product before it is used on a patient, and because bad news travels faster than good news, reputation will tell [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:11:23]. Whittemore also admits that no deal they have done in all their years went without something coming up, whether operator error, a unit nobody plugged in, or a step the shop simply did not cover well [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:04:42]. Robert Remitz of Go Southern MD built a free seller-listing service specifically because of calls from people who bought new and used equipment from third parties, did not get exactly what they ordered, and had nobody to contact with questions, warning that a buyer who does not know exactly what they are doing ends up with gear that will not work or is the wrong size [_ue3Y22oMAA @ 00:00:22]. Medinas Health learned that a hospital cannot just list a machine on eBay: biohazard liability issues need documenting before any sale, and the market lacked the transaction trust to close those deals cleanly [NFqBMUIcgl4 @ 00:00:54].

Pricing is the next trap. Whittemore explains that the used market lacks the raw-material and R&D cost structure that sets new-equipment prices, so value is negotiated deal by deal; they once gave a buyer a great deal, then the buyer's buddy showed up demanding the same price, which Whittemore could not even replicate by going back out into the market [zLW0PtGvqQE @ 00:08:09]. On the rental side, the Earn Your Leisure founder admits some item margins suck and others are not that great, and the math only works because a bedbound patient needs a hospital bed, lift, commode, walker, wheelchair, and sometimes oxygen, so thin items get offset by better ones [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:07:13]. Inventory itself can trap capital. The How to Start a Business that Prints Cash narrator names overstocking obsolete technology that depreciates rapidly as the number one expensive mistake, locking working capital into non-income producing assets, and prescribes high-demand consumables plus just-in-time delivery of heavy equipment [Ahi8z5l5Yao @ 00:01:21]. Jon Sheppard shows the slow-mover version at small scale: a medical mattress that sold fast once listed had sat in a bedroom corner for over a year because they could not be bothered to list it, and by then they had no clue what they paid for it [8meyld3BWGg @ 00:07:59].

Regulation and platform rules punish operators who ignore them. The Earn Your Leisure founder says medical equipment is more regulated than pharmacy: a state license, accreditation, Medicare, every other insurance plan, and a separate oxygen license, adding up to six and sometimes seven licenses per location, with Florida slower and costlier to license, which they tie to South Florida's history of Medicare fraud [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:05:40]. Medicare-primary patients can only get one wheelchair per five years, so their staff run every patient through the system before anything ships [hQY0qIkQ-xY @ 00:07:57]. Joe Verschoor warns that Google ads in this niche can get you in trouble, because Google treats targeting people who might need medical equipment as possible exploitation of elderly or vulnerable users, and adds that no tool's PPC data is reliable, with Semrush often not even close [-23KPzW6CW8 @ 00:16:48]. Jon Sheppard took a return on a bundle a buyer claimed was broken and incomplete, leaving them 115 pounds down even though the listing never claimed completeness; the fix was relisting at 150 pounds with explicit wording that pieces may be incomplete or broken, and they note the pump-up medical mattress could come back the same way if it gets popped [8meyld3BWGg @ 00:05:00].
