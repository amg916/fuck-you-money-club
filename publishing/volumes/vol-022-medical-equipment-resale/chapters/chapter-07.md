## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one spine for the 510(k): lock the predicate, copy its indications for use word-for-word, mirror FDA's guidance headings exactly, and treat performance testing as the critical path that drives cost and calendar. Rob Packard pegs the RTA screen as the first kill zone, with more than half of submissions bounced and the clock reset to zero, while Qualio reports 30% not accepted for initial review and 15% failing on the wrong predicate. Street numbers run from a $10,953 standard fee (Packard) to almost $20,000 in 2023 (Qualio), $13,000 biocompatibility, $50-60k EMC, and consultants at $20K-$25K typical. Disagreements center on predicate age, whether pre-subs always pay, whether abbreviated or special 510(k)s save time, and how conservative FDA is on software changes, as the O.N. Diagnostics warning letter showed.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus - Predicate First, FDA's Exact Words, Testing on the Critical Path

Every practitioner who touched predicate selection treats it as the load-bearing wall of the file. The Cleared channel opens bluntly: every 510(k) is built on one comparison, and pick the wrong one and the whole comparison can fall apart [6WEZ6ovm-PE @ 00:00:00]. Qualio puts a number on the damage, saying 15% of submissions fail because of the wrong predicate, while about two-thirds of all files draw additional-information requests anyway [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 00:59:39]. Rob Packard's screening rules are the operator version: avoid predicates under a consent decree or warning letter, and choose the broadest intended use available rather than the closest-looking product [gGje01O9B44 @ 00:21:40]. The same agreement covers the indications-for-use language. Qualio says copy the predicate's indications for use word-for-word, and Packard says copy them and change the name, while warning that a technology swap such as pneumatic versus mechanical can raise new risks [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:16:52]. Split predicates get no support from anyone: decision points one through four ride on a single primary device, and any other device in the file is labeled a reference, not a predicate. A cleared predicate that has gone off the market is still usable, and the Part II workshop speaker reports filing on discontinued predicates directly.

The second point of agreement is that the refuse-to-accept screen kills files on form, not science, so the document must mirror FDA's own rule book. In the Part I workshop, Packard reports RTA rejecting 56% of submissions and tells operators to follow the guidance order with a nice big bold heading that uses the exact same words FDA uses, down to copying FDA's misspellings, because paraphrasing invites rejection [yIARvVKN5-s @ 00:13:17]. Medical Device Academy turns the same idea into a template method: convert every guidance item into a heading and, where a section does not apply, write 'This is not applicable because' rather than deleting it [bEKT9pNj8Sg @ 00:15:11]. The workshop's warning example is a condom submission rejected as 'patient contacting.' For project control, Packard runs an Excel table of contents color-coded red, yellow, blue, and green so every section's status is visible at a glance [gGje01O9B44 @ 00:43:41]. Dr. David Lim's conference panel lists poor document naming and version control as pitfall number five and prescribes an internal final-review checklist plus verifying the eSTAR before upload [aZkxlipyWRs @ 00:06:12]. Inconsistent device names in headers are a known RTA trigger on their own, and Form 3881 should be re-downloaded right before submitting.

The third consensus is that testing, not writing, sets the calendar and the budget. Packard calls performance testing the critical path item in the project and estimates it costs about ten times as much as the rest of the build [yIARvVKN5-s @ 00:19:37]. In the Joe Hage session the trigger is design freeze: 'This is the point at which it gets expensive,' and the play is to write in parallel with testing and submit the same day the last test report lands [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:06:40]. Dave Saunders adds the scale of what sits behind the file: a 510(k) runs 200 to 500 pages while the design history file behind it may run about 10,000, and that DHF cannot be created retroactively [6w27HXwMbmU @ 00:31:49]. Adam at Matrix One closes the loop with the same warning in plainer words, telling teams not to underestimate the time a 510(k) needs and to ensure full documentation traceability before paying the fee [dd7dQRVr-mQ @ 00:06:17]. Qualio adds that the quality system is never submitted and gets no pre-submission audit, yet it is still critical, and CS Medical's Kendall Ash describes years of traceability, risk documentation, clinical in-use testing, and human factors work feeding a single clearance.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers - Fees, Test Bills, the Review Clock, and Build Order

The money map starts with the FDA user fee, and the numbers move with the year each practitioner quoted. In the Joe Hage session Packard cites $10,953 standard and $2,738 for small business, and lays out the testing bill that dwarfs it: about $13,000 for biocompatibility, $40k at the low end and $50-60k typical for electrical safety and EMC, tens of thousands for orthopedic cyclic testing, $100,000-plus for animal studies, and up to $3M for a 500-600 patient clinical, with $1M-plus as the ballpark [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:10:04]. Qualio puts the 2023 standard fee at almost $20,000 and the small-business fee at about $5,000 for firms under $100M, requiring Form 3602/3602A plus a tax return, two to six weeks to process, and yearly renewal; wholly owned subsidiaries must demonstrate independence to claim the discount [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 00:37:33]. In a second Joe Hage session Rob quotes consultant fees from $10K to $150K, with most landing between $20K and $25K, and prices the job out loud: about 100 hours at $250 an hour is 25 grand [gGje01O9B44 @ 01:20:17]. Pre-sub prep runs about 10 hours, roughly $2,500, and Packard says pre-sub feedback, while non-binding, saves $10k-plus [-ceDuiClggg @ 01:20:57].

The review clock is the second set of numbers every operator has to run. The Part II workshop lays it out by day: receipt by day 7, acceptance or RTA by day 15 with a refusal resetting the clock to day 0, substantive review ending in an additional-information hold or interactive review by day 60, and a decision by day 90 [nUaPO0mfwKI @ 00:29:17]. Rob's realistic total is 120 to 130 days, with interactive review running about 30 days to decision and the e-copy accepted and assigned a K number within 24 to 48 hours once fees are paid a few days early [gGje01O9B44 @ 00:11:34]. The highest-leverage trick on this clock is annotating the RTA checklist with the exact location of every item; the workshop speaker once got acceptance on day 2 doing it, and Rob, who includes a completed checklist in Section 1 or Section 21, reports the last two consultant-led files passed RTA on the first try [nUaPO0mfwKI @ 00:31:54]. When RTA does bounce a file, Packard's move is to phone the reviewer before resubmitting, since RTA can reject the same submission multiple times [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:27:35]. Once a hold letter lands, there are 180 days to answer, only one hold allowed, and an extension available.

The build order is fixed across the MedicalMarcom sessions. Performance testing goes first, then Section 4 indications for use, then device description, substantial equivalence, the 510(k) summary, and the executive summary dead last; 'Write it last,' as the workshop puts it [yIARvVKN5-s @ 00:20:07]. Rob names the five critical sections as 4, 5, 10, 11, and 12, and the workshop treats sections 1, 3, and 6 as boilerplate while marketing owns labeling and the IFU early. Predicate research runs through FDA's own databases. Searching product code OSH returned 3 predicates in the classification database but 90 in the 510(k) database, and a company holding a 510(k) statement must supply a redacted copy within 30 days of request [gGje01O9B44 @ 00:20:07]. Qualio's search for pregnancy tests turned up 316 predicates and points operators to K-number summaries, the three-letter product code, MAUDE, IFUs, and FOIA requests that can take years [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 00:15:11]. Packard calls the TPLC database, with recent 510(k)s, recalls, a Pareto by failure mode, and MDR links, 'Tens of thousands of dollars worth of consulting' [yIARvVKN5-s @ 00:07:13]. Registration & Listing confirms the predicate is legally marketed. Keyword discipline matters too: 'adhesive' returns 42 results, and searching 'dental' beats 'implant.'

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Rob Packard is the loudest pre-sub advocate on MedicalMarcom. Since the 2016 guidance changes he always recommends one, says 'it really pays to have pre-sub meetings,' prefers a teleconference, and warns that the single hour is the whole allotment, with an email response as the alternative [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:24:56]. In Q&A he prices the payoff: the feedback is non-binding, but it saves $10k or more [-ceDuiClggg @ 01:20:57]. Adam at Matrix One lands softer, calling the step optional but 'really helpful in outlining the FDA's expectations' [dd7dQRVr-mQ @ 00:05:39]. The counter-playbook comes from the Part II workshop speaker, who sometimes skips the pre-sub because clients arrive with 'kitchen sink' question lists. When a meeting does happen, the move is to bring the physical device and ask narrow, specific questions. For fast answers without a formal Q-sub, that speaker routes clients to DICE, which takes free, anonymous questions by phone [nUaPO0mfwKI @ 00:10:37]. The calendar cost is real: Packard clocks 60 to 75 days just waiting for a pre-sub meeting, and his longer MedicalMarcom session puts scheduling at 75 to 90 days. Pre-sub prep itself runs about 10 hours at $250 an hour, roughly $2,500.

Predicate age splits the Qualio stage itself. Kelly Stanton treats a predicate older than 10 years as a no-go and prefers one under 5, while Kyle Rose counters that it is 'not like a 100% rule' [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 01:01:57]. Discontinued predicates expose an outright contradiction. Qualio says a cleared predicate that is off the market is still usable, and the Part II workshop speaker says flatly they have used discontinued predicates [nUaPO0mfwKI @ 00:08:57]. The 20-year professional behind the Cleared channel lists removal from the market alongside misbranding and adulteration as disqualifiers [6WEZ6ovm-PE @ 00:01:27]. Stacking predicates is the other fight. During the Invent Healthcare webinar, attendee LaVine argued that picking the primary predicate is arbitrary, essentially 'flip a coin,' and attendee Coombs disputed that different testing methods amount to new questions. Marjorie Shulman of CDRH held that split predicates are inconsistent with the 510(k) standard and that FDA is 'not insisting on multiple predicates' [V3Fob6FXlCo @ 00:16:27]. Cleared lands on the same side: decision points 1 through 4 run on one predicate, multiple predicates must share an intended use, a primary gets named, and a reference device is not a predicate. Its 2023 draft filters add MAUDE, MDR, and MedSun checks and a written reason for the choice.

Adam at Matrix One tells operators to investigate the abbreviated and special 510(k), but the practitioners who have filed them are skeptical. Kelly Stanton at Qualio warns that the abbreviated option 'takes about twice as long as the traditional process for the FDA to review and it doesn't require any less work on your part' [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 00:13:07]. The Part II workshop speaker agrees it is not faster and pegs abbreviated filings at roughly 10% of submissions [nUaPO0mfwKI @ 00:05:40]. The special 510(k) carries real upside on paper. Rob at MedicalMarcom quotes a 30-day review target against 90 days for a traditional, but only one functional area of expertise can be involved, and his rule is blunt: 'if you're in doubt, submit it as a traditional' [gGje01O9B44 @ 01:27:19]. The cautionary file is a nano-metal-coated PEEK cage that ran through Flowchart C as a materials change on a tissue-contacting implant. It went in as a special, and FDA converted it to a traditional [nUaPO0mfwKI @ 00:06:12]. Packard's alternative to shortcut pathways is speed through parallel work. He can write a 510(k) in 72 hours, runs the writing alongside testing, and submits the same day the last test report arrives, which is how his book promises a 510(k) in 100 days.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The refuse-to-accept screen is where most first submissions die. Packard says 'more than 50% of the submissions are rejected during that RTA screening,' that the checklist runs 26 to 27 pages, and that a rejection resets the review clock to zero [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:13:03]. His longer MedicalMarcom session drills into the cause: 54% of rejections cite missing shelf-life data. His countermeasure is to include a completed RTA checklist in Section 1 or Section 21, and his last two consultant-led submissions passed on the first try [gGje01O9B44 @ 00:13:09]. Qualio's numbers are lower but still brutal, with 30% of submissions last year not even accepted for initial review [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 00:54:07]. Inside eSTAR the failure points shift but do not vanish. Packard sees 'so many RTAs just because companies don't have a level of concern documentation in the package,' and he flags missing wireless coexistence testing as another common trigger [b1Ejw4mhVFU @ 00:15:58]. The small stuff bites too. In the Part I workshop, an inconsistent device name across headers triggers RTA, a condom was rejected over the 'patient contacting' field, and a wrong Rx/OTC checkbox costs about $5k and another 90 days.

Dave Saunders describes a company that got 48 hours to produce a 40,000-page DHF, hand-delivered it, and still failed clearance. His yardstick is that a 510(k) runs 200 to 500 pages sitting on a DHF of roughly 10,000 [6w27HXwMbmU @ 00:32:12]. He warns 'you cannot create this documentation retroactively,' and says a change log where every change is approved 'suggests a rubber stamp process' [6w27HXwMbmU @ 00:36:12]. Sagar Tipne's remediation interview supplies the field evidence. A Class 2 orthopedic implant drew a 483 for missing input-to-verification traceability and took months to fix, and a drug-delivery device had no FMEA or hazard analysis, so the team rebuilt both under ISO 14971 [hJg3slOsTT8 @ 00:00:57]. Departed engineers force rationale to be reverse-engineered from ECOs and interviews, as happened on a cardiac catheter. Software classification is its own trap: Packard notes that IEC 62304 classes A, B, and C do not map to FDA level of concern, and some firms lost a year on that mismatch [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:45:40]. Saunders' audit drill exposes all of it: an auditor picks a random component, asks you to trace it back to a user need, and reads empty matrix cells as gaps.

The Innolitics panel dissected a live enforcement case. O.N. Diagnostics cleared its VirtuOst VFA spine CT tool in 2017, then shipped version 3.0.0 on June 11, 2024 with a better ML model, a desktop-to-web move, and a newer platform, all without a new 510(k). FDA inspected in March 2026, issued the warning letter September 1, and called the device adulterated and misbranded [UdQw2LB0EYc @ 00:00:03]. David was surprised the web move needed a submission, while Mary read the letter as a QMS failure, with unaddressed CAPAs and a release from an uncontrolled system [UdQw2LB0EYc @ 00:13:44]. For GenAI products the timing math is ugly: LLM deprecation windows run 2 weeks to 6 months against a roughly 6-month 510(k), so the panel treats a PCCP as effectively required. Budget checks belong at the same gate. Packard prices biocompatibility near $13,000, electrical safety and EMC at $50,000 to $60,000 typical, animal studies at $100,000 or more, and clinical work up to $3M for 500 to 600 patients [-ceDuiClggg @ 00:10:04]. Qualio adds the predicate tax: '15% submissions fail because of the wrong predicate' [1GKnWm8UYVk @ 00:59:39].
