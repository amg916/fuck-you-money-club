## The Practitioner Consensus

Every serious operator starts from one baseline: the OEM is not doing most of the work. Crawford of Block told a TechNation FDA webinar, which drew over 390 concurrent attendees, that the majority of medical devices in the marketplace are being serviced by someone other than the original manufacturer [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 00:21:33]. Dave Franco of TK/Technology Associates, an independent service organization, has 35 years in the field. Franco reports that there was no evidence that non-OEM servicing was lower quality or less safe [lSIrqUmOamc @ 00:22:16]. Wang brought hard numbers to the same webinar: roughly 500 incidents, only 6 traced to maintenance, across about 10 million devices, which is a 6.5 Sigma result. Wang also cited a set of 1,500 responses with 12 adverse events and none from maintenance [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 00:42:32]. Scott Carson, a 30-year veteran who helped build eBay's healthcare marketplace, adds the economic case. Carson argues that most servicing is already done by third parties and that without resale 'we probably wouldn't be able to afford health care.' The bright line everyone respects is remanufacturing. Servicing cannot change intended use. Remanufacturing significantly changes performance, safety, or intended use and falls outside the servicing community's scope entirely. Independents protect their model by keeping repairs inside servicing [lSIrqUmOamc @ 00:11:38].

Ask practitioners what separates good service from bad, and the answer is not the logo on the truck. Tamore told the FDA webinar that there is no disparity between OEM and third-party work when staff are trained and parts are OEM-spec, and another panelist put it plainly: the common denominator is people [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 00:50:31]. Franco breaks safe servicing into five components: skill sets, resources, parts, tools, and location. Franco insists that it should not matter where your paycheck comes from, and that neither a 100% OEM nor a 100% non-OEM market can work [lSIrqUmOamc @ 00:46:29]. Aaron Snider of Quality Systems Explained lays out the floor for any servicer. Personnel must be trained to the servicing procedures and must understand the customer's requirements. Procedures, testing, and controls must be defined wherever a reusable device degrades over time, and Snider notes that a device requiring installation likely requires servicing too [ohnyLE9h6ok @ 00:01:10]. Larry Nolen of Summit Imaging closes the loop on parts, arguing that quality is what actually delivers the lowest long-term total cost of ownership and the best patient care [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 01:09:53]. The shared playbook is blunt: hire or train competent techs, source parts you can defend, and document every job.

The third consensus point is paperwork, because the record is what a servicer actually hands an auditor. Snider says every service report must be reviewed to decide whether it is a complaint and whether that complaint is reportable. Each record carries the device name, lot number, services done, performer, date, and related test records, and reports flow into CAPA for tracking and trending [ohnyLE9h6ok @ 00:01:30]. Matrix One's QMS walkthrough repeats the same requirement: servicing records must include the device, the date, who serviced it, the service performed, and any test and inspection data [URXL-ymHf4w @ 00:01:18]. Noak framed the CMMS as an extension of the OEM device file [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 00:11:06]. Unique device identifiers tighten the trail. Jonathan Bratz recalls that in their own distributor practice, proprietary item numbers made recalls difficult, which is the exact problem a single harmonized global UDI is meant to fix [fB0ZiAL0q20 @ 00:02:27]. Snider describes what working well looks like: servicing procedures in the device master record, trained in-house servicers, and compliant records. The failure picture is the inverse: no procedures or no DMR link, untrained personnel, and records that are lost, destroyed, or unavailable. A final check confirms that records point back to the device history record.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Jason of Bright Idea Dental Repair, a former USAF biomed, built the clearest launch template. Side work began at $50 an hour, until a dentist reported a major supplier billing $75 for travel plus $150 per half hour. Jason countered with no travel charge at $150 an hour, and the dentist said 'deal' [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:02:56]. The launch rode on a 'three-legged stool': Guard pay with health insurance, the GI Bill, and the business itself. Jason pulled $8,000 from savings for test equipment and started under the name JnD Medical Maintenance [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:05:49]. Michigan red tape was minimal. An LLC cost about $50, and x-ray installs required the 2579 form, mailed to the FDA and copied to the state, while Ohio requires training. Insurance was the real wall, because water-damage liability scared carriers off. Coverage started at 'a couple grand a year' and now runs through McCready [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:12:30]. Jason would start dental-only today, arguing that private-sector biomed has 'not enough pain.' By contrast, water-based dental delivery units, autoclaves, suction, and compressors all need service. Staying lean matters. Dave of TKA warns that a mandated ISO 13485 system would cost $50k to $200k and take 12 to 24 months, which is enough to ruin 'garage' ISOs [kmzmXxn2MBE @ 00:31:12].

Jason's current rate card is $175 an hour regular, $225 after hours, and $75 travel for any customer more than 30 minutes out. Invoices carry four lines: labor, parts, travel, and shipping, and Michigan taxes both parts and shipping [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:35:36]. Recurring revenue comes from a PM membership: $200 a month buys 2 hours plus a 25-point inspection. Seven offices are enrolled, and the target is 20 offices per tech, or $4k a month per tech, with a 5-gallon parts bucket staged for each customer [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:46:29]. Prospecting is pure street work. Jason runs a Yelp 'dentist' map search as a 'treasure map' and walks in for informational interviews, which once got Jason hired twice in 3 hours for $450. Business cards go out paired with 3D-printed earrings, and one dentist bought 25 sets at $5 each. Free 10-to-15-minute fixes double as recon [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:26:46]. The back office runs on FreedomVoice for the call tree, QuickBooks Online, a separate bank account, ServiceFusion work orders, per-tech expense cards, and site photos as timestamps. Owners tend to stall near $300k a year. Jason broke through by adding a part-time autoclave bench tech and now runs 6 people from a 6,000 sq ft warehouse on 4x10 shifts. Dave of TKA's blunt counsel on OEM access is to 'just buy a PM only contract from every manufacturer' [kmzmXxn2MBE @ 00:54:28].

Parts are where margins get made or destroyed. Summit Imaging reports facilities hearing of 42% to 75% savings from building in-house clinical engineering. Those savings come from diagnosing on site, getting parts the next morning, keeping downtime inside business hours, and supporting techs remotely by phone, text, FaceTime, Skype, and YouTube [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:10:03]. The catch is part quality. Knockoff IU22/IE33 video cards run at 60% of OEM performance and lock up with no error code. They also ship with the exact same serial number across a couple thousand units [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:30:14]. Summit's rule is to compare warranty and DOA rates normalized to the warranty term, because 2.5% at 90 days is not 2.5% at 2 years. Summit also says to watch for failures right after warranty ends and to demand complete assemblies. For the quality system, Matrix One quotes published QMS software pricing starting at 10,000 euros a year. Teams go live in 2 to 6 weeks, with migration support off paper, Word, SharePoint, or Google Workspace [URXL-ymHf4w @ 00:03:57]. For credentials, Michelle, a BMET serving Alaska village clinics, describes the CBET as 165 questions in 3 hours, all from memory, with roughly a 60% first-time pass rate. The prep stack was the AAMI online course, ChatGPT to explain wrong answers, and subject-sorted YouTube playlists [UatkfSp7sHs @ 00:01:19].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split in the third-party biomed business is whether non-OEM servicing is a real safety problem or a market-share grab dressed up as one. Dave Franco of TK/Technology Associates traces OEM pushes from 1996 to 2006 to 2016, when MITA reframed the fight as safety, and states that “there was no evidence to say that there wasn't less quality or less safe servicing of medical equipment by non-oem representation” [lSIrqUmOamc @ 00:22:16]. On the FDA webinar panel, Wang stacked the numbers: roughly 500 incidents, 6 tied to maintenance, across about 10 million devices at 6.5 Sigma [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 00:42:32]. Tamore argued there is no disparity between OEM and third-party work when staff are trained and parts are OEM-spec, calling people the common denominator [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 00:50:31]. Summit Imaging pushes from a different flank: the risk is less about who turns the wrench and more about what goes into the box. Their bench found knockoff IU22/IE33 video cards running at 60 percent of OEM performance, locking up with no error code, and carrying the exact same serial number across a couple thousand units [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:30:14]. The operator read is that the independent camp wins the labor argument on the data its members present, while the remanufactured-parts supply chain is where the safety case can still be lost.

The second fault line is how much regulation the independent channel should invite. Scott Carson, who helped build eBay's healthcare marketplace and founded U.S. Medical, calls regulators “flat-footed,” says Alibaba counterfeits are almost epidemic, and states flatly, “I think we need more regulation and we need it fast” [l108Vrz9wLo @ 00:30:28]. Franco takes the opposite posture and agrees that careless regulation would raise service costs “dramatically” [lSIrqUmOamc @ 00:16:19]. The cost math behind that fear is concrete: Franco puts an ISO 13485 build somewhere between $50,000 and $200,000 and 12 to 24 months, a bill that could ruin the “garage” ISOs, which is why the QMS workstream moved toward defining tenets instead of mandating one platform [kmzmXxn2MBE @ 00:31:12]. Matrix One frames the manufacturer-side reality: since 2 February 2026, 21 CFR Part 820 is the Quality Management System Regulation, 820.10 requires compliance with ISO 13485, and their software starts from 10,000 euros a year with teams live in 2 to 6 weeks [URXL-ymHf4w @ 00:00:37]. The playbook splits accordingly: a lean independent shop rides the KPI-and-tenets track Franco's committee is building, while any operation drifting toward remanufacturing or labeling budgets for a full quality system and the tooling that produces audit evidence.

Practitioners also split on where an independent should plant its flag. Jason of Bright Idea Dental Repair says that if starting over, they would go dental-only, because private-sector biomed equipment has “not enough pain,” while water-based dental gear such as delivery units, autoclaves, suction and compressors constantly needs service [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:06:51]. Summit Imaging pitches the hospital side instead: build in-house clinical engineering, diagnose on site, get next-morning parts, keep downtime inside business hours, and capture the 42 to 75 percent savings customers report against outsourced service [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:10:03]. Carson bets on neither a shop nor a department but a marketplace, stacking eight trust enablers including 1,000-plus owned pre-owned devices, 10,000-plus hand pieces and parts, and 750 technicians and trainers on an “Uber and Angie's” model aimed at growing, cash-based aesthetics [l108Vrz9wLo @ 00:35:09]. Franco refuses the purity test on every side: across skill sets, resources, parts, tools and location, “it shouldn't matter where your paycheck comes from,” and neither a 100 percent OEM nor a 100 percent non-OEM model can work [lSIrqUmOamc @ 00:46:29]. The real choice is niche depth in dental and autoclaves, hospital-embedded savings on imaging, or platform aggregation across pre-owned inventory, and each speaker's numbers hold inside their own lane.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures in the independent channel show up inside the parts bin. Summit Imaging's Larry Nolen and Carl Ravel tell operators to normalize warranty and DOA rates to term, because 2.5 percent at 90 days is not 2.5 percent at two years, to watch for failures landing right after warranty ends, and to reject computers that ship without video cards or with reused hard drives [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:14:22]. Their teardown of remanufactured cable lines found about 30 frayed lines, and because OEMs do not release specs, any “OEM spec” claim on those assemblies is a guess [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:36:57]. The deepest trap is the whole-probe swap, where an OEM body, can and serial number are refitted around remanufactured crystal and lines; Summit's verdict is blunt: “I've never seen a 510 K for a whole probe swap” [7d4lxsuW9Xs @ 00:55:33]. Information gaps compound the parts risk: on the FDA webinar, panelists reported that Olympus did not notify IMS of the TJF-Q180 recall, and that more OEMs are refusing to provide parts and manuals [C3pxzDnP6HM @ 01:14:29]. An independent running on untested parts and missing recall notices pays twice, first in an extra day of downtime chasing diagnostic doubt, then in the misdiagnosis and seal-failure liability Summit flags for drop-out sectors and TEE probes.

On the business side, the traps are pricing and ownership structure. Better Biomed Channel relays hearsay, openly hedged with “from what I heard,” that a large third-party repair company won a multi-million-dollar hospital contract by bidding about half the prior vendor's price, when that prior vendor was already barely in the black or in the red [UQh0OoupetI @ 00:00:25]. The theory: the underbid is a loss leader that pads the books and inflates valuation ahead of a reported $660 million sale to a North Carolina buyer, so the sellers exit before contractually obligated losses arrive, leaving the investment firm and the working techs to absorb the damage [UQh0OoupetI @ 00:01:43]. Jason of Bright Idea Dental Repair names the solo-owner trap: owners hit a ceiling around $300,000 a year, and “the business needs to be able to run without you,” which they broke by adding a part-time autoclave bench tech [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:16:58]. Insurance is the other silent killer: water-damage liability made carriers hard to find, premiums started at “a couple grand a year,” and 1099 contractors must carry their own coverage [YXLWY7VHfxM @ 00:12:30]. Jason also notes that big suppliers treat service as a loss leader and flex rates to each customer's supply spend, which is exactly the gap their $150-an-hour, no-travel opening offer exploited.

The paperwork failures are quieter, but they surface the moment a complaint or audit lands. Aaron Snider of Quality Systems Explained lists what broken servicing looks like: no procedures or no link to the device master record, untrained personnel, and records that are lost, destroyed, or unavailable [ohnyLE9h6ok @ 00:02:22]. The fix runs through every service report, each one reviewed to decide whether it is a complaint and whether that complaint is reportable, with device name, lot number, services done, performer, date and related test records captured and fed into CAPA for tracking and trending [ohnyLE9h6ok @ 00:01:30]. For any shop that relabels or acts as the labeler, Jonathan Bratz of Medical Device Academy found the business software changes “the most time-consuming and costly aspect” of rolling out UDI, and warns that customers now demand UDI on a publish-or-perish basis [fB0ZiAL0q20 @ 00:26:31]. LexisNexis Life Sciences Solutions adds the maintenance trap: a new version, a new model, or a changed label value needs a GUDID update before distribution, with only a 10-day grace period for attributes that do not appear on the label [nxzxvwNJELg @ 00:06:17]. Snider's bonus audit questions close the loop: is servicing required, is it done in-house or at the customer site, and where are the records kept, pointing back to the device history record.
