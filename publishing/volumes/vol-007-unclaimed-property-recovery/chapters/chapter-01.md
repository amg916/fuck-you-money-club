## 1. Sell Documented Recovery Work, Not Access to Public Money  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Unclaimed property recovery is an evidence and administration business. A holder, such as an insurer, employer, bank, or vendor, retains an owner's property until the applicable dormancy and reporting rules require transfer to a state administrator. The recovery operator identifies a plausible owner, establishes authority, assembles the administrator's required documents, and follows the claim through payment. The state generally holds custodial property for its owner; a public listing does not transfer ownership to the person who finds it. Corporate uncashed warrants need another distinction: a stale government check may require reissuance by the issuing agency rather than a treasury claim, and some public obligations have separate deadlines. Determine the payer and legal route before quoting a fee. The owner's ability to claim directly without a recovery service belongs in the first substantive communication, not a buried attachment. Paid value comes from resolving an old address, locating merger documents, reconciling duplicate records, and reducing employee time. MissingMoney and official state portals are discovery interfaces, not exclusive inventory. A browser result establishes a lead; accepted ownership evidence establishes a claim; an enforceable earned fee establishes operator revenue.

Five operator archetypes organize this market: corporate treasury sweep specialists, successor-entity researchers, estate documentation coordinators, institutional entitlement tracers, and jurisdiction-focused claim administrators. Their economics differ because difficult authority chains can justify substantial service effort while straightforward consumer claims often cannot. A corporate sweep might identify twenty vendor checks under four historical company names; the moat is the verified relationship between those names and the current claimant. An estate specialist instead needs current representative authority and the correct succession route, not merely a death record. An institutional tracer must distinguish an insurance benefit, securities proceeds, and a deposit balance because each creates different evidence demands. Begin with $5,000–$35,000 startup capital and a 3–12 month first-deal planning horizon, rather than funding operations from expected approvals. The stated $1,500–$8,000 monthly operating expense range is a planning envelope for recurring operations, not proof that every staffing configuration fits inside it. Registration, permitted representation, fee ceilings, waiting periods, and privacy duties vary by jurisdiction. California Code of Civil Procedure section 1582 and New York Abandoned Property Law section 1416 illustrate why one national finder contract is a weak foundation.

This chapter's twelve mapped plays comprise two named cross-platform plays and ten concrete recovery applications. The named plays are public_record_api_intelligence and state_film_historic_brownfield_tax_credit_brokerage. The ten recovery applications are corporate treasury sweeps, vendor-check recovery, payroll-check recovery, refund recovery, insurance-proceeds recovery, securities-entitlement tracing, merger-successor recovery, probate representation support, dissolved-entity authority reconstruction, and multistate claim-status operations. These applications are operating lanes, not ten additional promises of separate income. Corporate sweeps organize the account; vendor, payroll, refund, insurance, and securities lanes classify the asset; successor, probate, and dissolution lanes resolve authority; status operations coordinate submission and collection. Public-record intelligence supplies evidence across those lanes. Transferable-credit placement is an adjacent brokerage discipline, not a treasury recovery claim, and must retain separate contracts, accounting, and eligibility checks. Do not add twelve projected income streams together when the same claim traverses several lanes. Assign one property identifier, one owner relationship, one collection event, and one service-fee ledger entry. The durable operator sells accountable completion of a difficult administrative task. The fragile beginner sells an impressive spreadsheet of balances that may belong to someone else.

## 2. Reconcile Service Fees, Collection Timing, and Owner Profit  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

The revenue boundary is recognized service fees, not client asset value. If an owner receives $50,000 and an enforceable agreement entitles the operator to a permitted 10% fee after successful recovery, the operator's revenue is $5,000, not $50,000. The remaining $45,000 is not an operator expense because the operator never earned that principal. Prefer payment directly from the administrator to the owner, followed by a separately supported service invoice. That route reduces custody exposure and clarifies the ledger without eliminating collection risk. Record discovered value, submitted value, approved value, owner-paid value, earned fees, invoiced fees, and collected fees as distinct measures. Under an accrual policy aligned with ASC 606, a contingent success fee requires analysis of the performance obligation and variable-consideration constraint; a search hit is never enough. A conservative operating policy recognizes the fee only after documented owner payment when that resolves the contractual contingency. Cash-basis reporting instead follows actual receipt under the applicable accounting method. Retainers and separate research charges need their own lawful terms and performance analysis; renaming a restricted finder fee does not remove the restriction.

The canonical cases reconcile without treating recovered principal as sales. Conservative revenue is $60,000, expenses are $45,000, and annual owner net profit is $15,000 at a 25% margin with 1 FT + 2 C. Base revenue is $255,000, expenses are $159,375, and owner net profit is $95,625 at 37.5%, again with 1 FT + 2 C. Scaled revenue is $450,000, expenses are $225,000, and owner net profit is $225,000 at 50% with 2 FT + 3 C, or five people. These are annual scorecard cases, not commitments about monthly cash availability. At an illustrative lawful 10% realized fee, the corresponding recovered client assets are $600,000, $2,550,000, and $4,500,000. That percentage is a modeling assumption, not a nationwide fee rule. The same revenue totals could arise from sixty $1,000 fees, eighty-five $3,000 fees, and ninety $5,000 fees respectively. Those averages imply different claim selection and evidence burdens, not automatic productivity improvements. Reconcile the annual expense envelope to researcher payments, payroll, verification, acquisition, insurance, software, and collection losses. Contractor headcount does not specify contractor hours, and owner profit is not an additional revenue category.

Use contribution arithmetic before hiring a researcher for a promising list. Suppose a qualified corporate claim yields a $3,000 collected fee, requires six hours at $45 per hour, and consumes $130 of document and account-servicing expense. Its $2,600 contribution precedes acquisition cost, shared overhead, and owner compensation treatment. If four equally expensive qualified files produce only one payment, the cohort spends $1,600 to collect $3,000, leaving $1,400 before those shared costs. Averages that exclude rejected files conceal the central risk. Conversely, a $200 owner balance at a 10% fee produces only $20; even twenty minutes of paid review can exhaust the economics. Direct such owners to the official portal unless a lawful batch engagement makes servicing efficient. Maintain a rolling thirteen-week cash forecast because document requests, probate issues, and administrator queues can defer collection for months. The startup range must support that delay without depending on owner funds. Reconcile the stated monthly operating envelope separately from the scorecard's total annual costs, especially at scale. Reserve for fee disputes and billing reversals where entitlement fails; do not inflate a supposedly high-margin model by ignoring unsuccessful work.

## 3. Build a Sub-Vertical Evidence Moat Without Mixing Asset Classes  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

The public_record_api_intelligence play turns fragmented public records into a prioritized evidence queue, not permission to harvest every available personal detail. Combine official treasury ledgers, permitted county docket access, probate registries, secretary-of-state records, and documented public APIs where available. Many portals offer no public API; do not infer one from an internal browser endpoint. Store source URL, retrieval date, property identifier, historical address, and match rationale in SQLite or PostgreSQL. OpenRefine can standardize entity names, while explicit rules preserve differences between a parent, subsidiary, and unrelated business with a similar name. An illustrative authorized batch of 2,000 records might yield forty plausible matches and eight document-qualified claims averaging $12,500. At a lawful 10% realized fee, those eight successful payments would generate $10,000, not the $100,000 client principal. Before payment, that amount remains a contingent pipeline estimate. At $900 extraction cost and $1,800 review cost, the cohort leaves $7,300 before claim servicing and other expenses only if all eight pay. When access changes, switch to scheduled bulk FOIA or state public-record exports, official data feeds, and low-frequency cached queues rather than bypassing access controls.

The comparison matrix pairs actual discovery interfaces with claim types rather than implying that every portal exposes every useful field. California's Controller, New York's Comptroller, ClaimItTexas, Florida Treasure Hunt, and other state systems differ in displayed amounts, identity checks, document delivery, and representative procedures. Capture the official property's type before asking for supporting records. A payroll claim may turn on an old residential address; a corporate vendor check may require proof that a former trade name belonged to the claimant; an estate claim may depend on court-issued authority. Historical addresses are corroboration, not substitutes for identity. Corporate acquisitions create especially useful research opportunities because public records preserve old names while employees lose institutional memory. They also create hard failures: a stock sale, statutory merger, and asset purchase do not transfer rights identically. Review transaction documents before asserting successor entitlement. For securities, establish whether the state holds shares or liquidation proceeds and explain the payable basis before computing fees. The moat is a reusable evidence map with bounded access and documented corrections, not a secret claim database that an owner could search free.

The state_film_historic_brownfield_tax_credit_brokerage play belongs beside recovery only as an explicitly separate placement service. It matches certified transferable state credits with eligible taxpayers at negotiated discounts; it does not locate an owner's escheated cash. For example, $100,000 of verified transferable state credit sold for $92,000 creates $92,000 of seller proceeds. A disclosed placement fee equal to 2% of those proceeds produces $1,840 of operator revenue when earned under the engagement, not $100,000. Buyer eligibility, vintage, use limits, transfer registration, and recapture exposure require program-specific review. Georgia's film-credit statute, O.C.G.A. section 48-7-40.26, provides one state-specific starting point; historic rehabilitation and brownfield programs require their own enabling provisions. Federal rehabilitation credits under 26 U.S.C. section 47 are not thereby freely saleable, and section 50 contains important recapture rules. The fallback is to suspend settlement under diligence contingencies and rematch only verified eligible credits without guaranteeing tax savings. Keep this hypothetical fee outside all three canonical recovery cases. Combining credit face value, discounted seller proceeds, recovered owner assets, and service fees would erase the very business-model distinction this chapter establishes.

## 4. Operate an Evidence Queue That Survives Rejection and Distrust  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Build the workflow around a case register and a restricted document store. Airtable can track non-sensitive case metadata; a properly configured Microsoft SharePoint or Google Workspace repository can hold supporting files with named access, multifactor authentication, and access logging. Product selection alone does not establish security. Separate lead researchers from identity-document reviewers, prohibit identity documents in ordinary spreadsheet attachments, and set retention periods against actual contractual and statutory requirements. Record the state's submission channel rather than uploading documents to whichever link appears in an email. Outreach should identify the operator, describe the service, disclose direct claiming, and explain how the recipient can verify the official record independently. Never impersonate a treasury office or imply that paying the operator is required to obtain property. Customer distrust is rational in this market; reduce it with verifiable identifiers, restrained contact frequency, and a clear written fee basis. Initial contact does not need a full identity packet. Request only the documents required for the next validated claim step, and direct submission to the state when its process permits that safer arrangement.

Automate record matching and claim status tracking, the two scorecard levers, while keeping judgment with accountable reviewers. Normalize punctuation and corporate suffixes for candidate generation, but never merge entities solely because normalization produces the same string. Require corroborating address, transaction, or legal-entity evidence before changing a match to qualified. Store the reviewer and rationale so a later rejection improves the rules instead of disappearing into a spreadsheet. Track explicit states: research, authority pending, agreement eligible, submitted, evidence requested, approved, owner paid, fee earned, and fee collected. Keep administrator status separate from internal billing status; approval may still precede payment or further validation. Use permitted notifications and scheduled human checks when no authorized machine interface exists. Do not evade portal rate limits to make an automation dashboard look current. Measure qualified-match yield, document rework, days between requested and received evidence, collection lag, and contribution per completed cohort. Identity mismatch, low claim values, and distrust should each have a measurable stop condition. Scale only after repeat cohorts demonstrate that fewer manual touches do not increase wrongful matches or uncollectible fees.

A practical first ninety days establishes a repeatable jurisdiction-and-account combination rather than a national claim factory. Select one state, one corporate property category, and a narrow owner profile with accessible authority records. Build the legal requirements sheet from primary statutes and current administrator instructions; record effective dates and revisit it before contracting. Test a small authorized record batch, manually review every proposed identity match, and ask prospective clients whether internal treasury staff already pursue these balances. Price against expected collected fees, including failed files, instead of advertised property totals. Move into a second jurisdiction only after the first process reliably produces complete submissions and auditable accounting. The pre-flight checks below are operating stop conditions, not ceremonial signatures. A missing authority document means no submission; an invalid fee arrangement means no contingent billing; a disputed ownership chain means no claim of certainty. The business earns its small-team advantage by declining weak files early and completing strong files cleanly. Its real asset is an evidence system that owners and administrators can trust, not the volume of money visible in public searches.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the property and jurisdiction
   - Capture the official property identifier, holder, owner name, and applicable claim route.
   - Separate state-held property from issuer-level warrant reissuance; preserve retrieval dates and source links without treating search results as established ownership.
2. Establish authority and lawful engagement
   - Corroborate identity, verify representative authority, and apply jurisdiction-specific finder terms.
   - Explain direct claiming, check contracting restrictions, and request only documents necessary for the verified recovery path.
3. Submit and resolve evidence requests
   - Use the official permitted channel and maintain dated submission and response records.
   - Assign one accountable case owner, track outstanding evidence separately, and avoid duplicate filings or unauthorized portal automation.
4. Reconcile payment and close the case
   - Document owner payment, evaluate the fee-earning condition, and reconcile recognized and collected fees.
   - Keep client principal outside service revenue, record disputes or reversals, and apply the documented retention schedule to supporting identity materials.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Checks Before a Claim Becomes Billable Work**
> 
> Advance only when the evidence supports the owner, the engagement, the submission route, and the eventual fee calculation.
> - Verify owner identity and legal entitlement with corroborating records; distinguish subsidiaries, asset purchasers, estates, and similarly named unrelated claimants.
> - Verify applicable finder registration, waiting periods, fee limits, required contract terms, and direct-claim disclosure before requesting signatures.
> - Verify that the authorized representative can submit through the administrator's current process and that sensitive documents have an approved secure destination.
> - Verify the contractual earning event, recovered-amount basis, collection route, and reversal treatment before recognizing or invoicing any service fee.
