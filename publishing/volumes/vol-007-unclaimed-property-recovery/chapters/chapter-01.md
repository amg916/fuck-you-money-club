## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the asset class is custodial money: holders unable to deliver wages, deposits, or securities must send a due diligence notice and remit to the state after a dormancy clock of one year for payroll and three to five for bank accounts, and states hold it with no expiration. About $70 billion sits in state custody and one in seven Americans has a stake, yet the median claim is about $100 against a $2,080 average. Street mechanics start at MissingMoney.com, then unclaimed.org for states like California and Pennsylvania, then federal registries for wages, pensions, FHA refunds, failed banks, and bankruptcy courts. Disagreements center on fees, where consumer voices call self-filing free and paid help unnecessary while finders operate under caps of 10 to 30 percent, and on Treasury Hunt, which some still recommend and others call retired.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator working this lane starts from the same base fact: holders cannot keep money they owe and cannot deliver. Hoang Le frames unclaimed property as any asset a company, bank, or government agency owed, tried to deliver, and could not, usually because the owner moved, changed a name, or lost touch, after which the holder must transfer it to the state in a process called escheatment [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:01:06]. Base_Rate sharpens the point with an uncashed check: the paper goes stale after 180 days under UCC section 4-404, but the underlying obligation does not expire with it, so the payer carries it as a liability until the dormancy clock forces a report and remittance [RycXVSWGZ3I @ 00:00:48]. The Lorman panel adds the holder-side doctrine that matters when you argue about what is actually claimable: modern laws are custodial, not true escheat, so title never moves from owner to state, and the state pays owners who later come forward and prove a valid claim [2TghvbJiOis @ 00:14:20]. Money Grammar lands in the same place for the consumer side, calling the state a custodial holder that keeps property in perpetuity until the owner or a legal heir steps forward [5l8zepw8oWY @ 00:04:03]. That custodial frame is why operators treat old files as live inventory rather than dead leads.

The dormancy clock is the second point of agreement, and the numbers line up across camps. Hoang Le puts wages, payroll checks, and utility deposits at roughly one year, matured life insurance at three years, bank accounts and securities at three to five, and travelers checks at up to fifteen in some states [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:01:58]. The Lorman panel confirms the one-year payroll rule in almost every state, counted from the date of issuance, and reports the trend is toward shorter clocks: states that once sat near fifteen years are moving from five to three, with Maine running two-year periods on gift cards [2TghvbJiOis @ 00:21:48]. Before the clock runs out, the holder owes a due diligence notice; Hoang Le times that letter at 60 to 180 days before the reporting deadline, mailed to the last known address, which is exactly why movers never see it [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:02:28]. David Wilkerson of Surplus Funds 101 translates the same mechanics into recovery-agent terms: holders like Chase Bank or Safeco Life Insurance run a due diligence window Wilkerson pegs at 6 to 24 months, then the property escheats, and only after the 12 to 24 month dormancy period can the investigator step in to assist a claimant [-VDHxOAAA7A @ 00:04:56].

On the hunt itself, nearly every speaker gives the same entry order: state databases first, every state touched, then federal registries. Money Grammar warns there is no single place to look, so you search every state where you lived, worked, or established a business, because the system is decentralized and the state will not come looking for you [5l8zepw8oWY @ 00:05:42]. This Guy Knows Tech warns that state portals hold no federal assets, which is why millions of people search one state registry, find zero results, and wrongly assume nothing is coming [74IHdXzW4aQ @ 00:02:47]. On fees, the consumer-side voices agree that filing with the state costs nothing, while the recovery-side voices confirm the paid lane is real: Hoang Le says licensed locators, sometimes called heir finders, are regulated in most states and typically capped around 10 percent, and a legitimate one never collects before the owner is paid [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:07:03]. Eric Richardson of Money Making Juggernaut, working the business since 2018, argues the agent role is crucial precisely because states do a poor job of notifying owners, with some funds sitting since the 60s after a mailed letter most owners ignore or never receive [7WZONOtYbjc @ 00:02:11]. That gap between free self-service and owner inertia is where the agent fee lives.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

MissingMoney.com is the first keystroke in almost every playbook. Hoang Le calls it the free national database run by the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators and the single fastest first step, since one query hits most participating states at once [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:03:16]. Money Missed flags the hole in that net: a few states, including California and Pennsylvania, run their own separate systems, so the second stop is unclaimed.org, where an interactive map links straight into every state's official program [sfcrjWHv_7k @ 00:08:19]. Diane Lee of WSPA 7News gives the field-level workaround on the multi-state site: enter the name, choose the state from the dropdown, and if that state is not listed in bold, select it anyway to get a direct link to the state's own unclaimed property site [VF97sp7MzAw @ 00:03:06]. The money then hides in name variants. This Guy Knows Tech reports state treasuries sitting on millions under misspelled names, inverted first and last names, and outdated nicknames, where an old utility company logging Jonathan as John can strand a $500 deposit indefinitely [74IHdXzW4aQ @ 00:02:15]. Money Missed's own drill adds maiden names, nicknames, middle initials, and junior or senior suffixes on every pass, so treat a single clean-name search as incomplete.

State portals stop at state money, so the second sweep is federal. Money Missed lists the separate federal slices: unpaid final paychecks through the Department of Labor, forgotten pensions through the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, unclaimed VA life insurance, FHA mortgage insurance refunds through HUD, failed bank and credit union deposits through the FDIC or NCUA, and tax refunds that generally must be claimed within three years of the original filing deadline [sfcrjWHv_7k @ 00:09:19]. On savings bonds, Dollar Decoded reports the Treasury holding around $29 billion in bonds that stopped earning interest, says the old Treasury Hunt site was retired in 2025, and routes searchers to the state unclaimed property office instead [QVgTN5Lip0o @ 00:05:34]. For lost 401(k) money when the old employer is unreachable, Josie Sturman of InvestigateTV points to the Form 5500 a company files with the federal government about its plan; the Department of Labor lookup puts the plan administrator and a phone number on the first page [YWsshlY3kF4 @ 00:01:36]. This Guy Knows Tech pushes into bankruptcy court registries, where returned distribution checks owed to creditors, vendors, contractors, and even tenants sit in federal court accounts across individual districts until someone claims them [74IHdXzW4aQ @ 00:08:43].

Once a match hits, the paperwork scales with the size and type of the claim. Hoang Le says smaller claims often clear online with minimal documentation, while larger claims, securities, safe deposit boxes, and claims filed for someone else usually require notarized documentation proving identity and legal right to the asset [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:05:10]. Unclaimed Money: Find What You're Owed lists the standard packet as a photo ID, the Social Security number, and proof of residence at the listed address, where an old bill or lease works, plus a death certificate and heirship proof for a relative's property [J4RhFyo0R7c @ 00:04:47]. On the agent side, Eric Richardson says some states require you to register as a finder, which means fingerprints and a background check, and puts state-level payouts at 16 to 120 days; the county-level lane needs no registration, often runs off a one-page form, and carries lower values but low competition [7WZONOtYbjc @ 00:02:36]. David Wilkerson keeps the economics in perspective: knowing the vocabulary will not make or break the business, because the money is made in contacting claimants, following up, and converting, and confidence with the terms helps an agent project certainty of outcome on the call [-VDHxOAAA7A @ 00:01:12].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the unclaimed-funds world is whether an owner should ever pay a finder. David Wilkerson of Surplus Funds 101 frames recovery agents as people turning a hobby into a full-time business, assisting owners in reclaiming property for a fee or percentage of recovered funds, and notes that states label them heir finders, locators, or investigators [-VDHxOAAA7A @ 00:02:20]. Eric Richardson of Money Making Juggernaut justifies the cut by arguing that states hold the money and do not do a good job notifying people, with funds probably sitting since the 60s behind a mailed letter most owners never see or never receive [7WZONOtYbjc @ 00:01:57]. Money Grammar takes the opposite line flatly: locators monitor public databases and contact individuals offering recovery for a percentage, and there is absolutely no need to pay because the exact same searches and claims cost zero when filed directly with the state [5l8zepw8oWY @ 00:07:36]. This Guy Knows Tech goes further, warning that anyone demanding upfront cash or a 20% locator fee is charging for work an owner can do in five minutes on an official registry [74IHdXzW4aQ @ 00:09:15]. Net read: the finder's edge sits in the notification gap Richardson describes, not in any data the public cannot reach.

Practitioners also report different fee ceilings and conflicting pictures of how hard states hunt for owners. Hoang Le says licensed asset locators, sometimes called heir finders, are regulated in most states and typically capped around 10% of the property's value, and a legitimate one will never ask for payment before the owner receives the money [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:07:03]. Money Missed reports a wider registered-and-capped band, commonly 10 to 30%, describing paid finders as not illegal but never necessary [sfcrjWHv_7k @ 00:12:17]. On outreach, Money Grammar insists the state will not actively search owners out and that the responsibility for starting recovery lies entirely with the individual [5l8zepw8oWY @ 00:05:42]. InvestigateTV reports the opposite on the ground: two Virginia residents who had never met each got unexpected calls from the state's Treasury Department, one caller had been searching for weeks, and both calls were legitimate and both paid off, one from a 1980s insurance policy and one from a late mother's estate [IhEPmIRt3Ak @ 00:00:34]. For a locator, that means the competition in some states is the treasury itself, which can reach a claimant first and charge nothing. Fee pitches also collide with Hoang Le's point that owners can verify a match themselves for free before agreeing to work with anyone.

Timing numbers diverge more than any other figure the speakers quote. Money Grammar anchors dormancy at often around five years before a holder must report and transfer the asset [5l8zepw8oWY @ 00:00:00], while Hoang Le breaks it out by asset: wages, payroll checks, and utility deposits after just one year, life insurance about three years after the policy matures, most checking, savings, and securities accounts three to five years, and travelers checks up to 15 years in some states [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:01:58]. Lorman's presenters report the direction of travel is shorter clocks, from around 15 years in many states originally down to five, then three, and even two-year periods for gift cards in Maine, because of the revenue-driven nature of these laws [2TghvbJiOis @ 00:21:29]. The tool list splits too. Hoang Le's checklist still routes matured savings bond hunters to the Treasury Hunt tool, but Dollar Decoded reports Treasury Hunt was retired in 2025 and that bond searches now start with the state unclaimed property office, pegging Treasury's stranded bonds at around $29 billion [QVgTN5Lip0o @ 00:05:34], a figure that already conflicts with Hoang Le's $32 billion estimate. Operators running older scripts are sending claimants to a dead tool and quoting mismatched market sizes.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common failure the speakers describe is the notice that never lands. Hoang Le explains that holders must mail a due diligence letter to the last known address, usually 60 to 180 days before the deadline, and if the owner moved without updating an old bank or employer, that letter never arrives and the account quietly transfers to the state [kZMkghcevh8 @ 00:02:28]. Money Making Juggernaut sees the same break in tax-sale surplus work: counties send excess-proceeds notices to the very property that was just foreclosed, where the former owners most likely no longer live [7WZONOtYbjc @ 00:04:00]. The second leak is data quality. This Guy Knows Tech reports that state treasuries hold millions of dollars under misspelled names, inverted first and last names, and outdated nicknames, so a single missed letter can leave a $500 deposit in state custody indefinitely [74IHdXzW4aQ @ 00:02:15]. Dollar Decoded's walkthrough proves the point: an $800 security deposit was listed under Maya Rodriguez with a typo, and a plain search would have missed it entirely [QVgTN5Lip0o @ 00:03:41]. The practical failure is searching once, under one current legal name, in one current state, then concluding nothing exists. The speakers who address variations push maiden names, nicknames, misspellings, and every state lived, worked, or banked in.

Economics checks cut hard against the jackpot pitch. Money Missed cites NAUPA data putting the average claim at $2,080 but the median, the more typical claim, at only about $100, with a handful of large recoveries dragging the average up [sfcrjWHv_7k @ 00:00:39]. An InvestigateTV anchor found $420 in their own name and openly went back and forth on whether the search was worth it before settling on yes [YWsshlY3kF4 @ 00:02:22]. Unclaimed Money: Find What You're Owed warns that stocks and other securities are often sold after they are turned over, so the owner usually gets the cash value from the sale date rather than today's price, which makes delay itself a loss [J4RhFyo0R7c @ 00:03:15]. On the surplus side, Richardson of Money Making Juggernaut says second mortgages and liens can swallow a foreclosure surplus, so operators must do due diligence and verify a real surplus exists before chasing it, and should use an attorney on judicial and non-judicial mortgage foreclosure files [7WZONOtYbjc @ 00:04:58]. Cash flow is slow regardless: Richardson pegs claim payouts at 16 to 120 days. For an agent working on a percentage, a $100 median file with a four-month payout is the baseline unit, not the exception.

Holder-side traps are where small balances turn punitive. Unclaimed Money Recovery describes a live case where a state audit found one employee had failed to cash a $149 expense reimbursement check, and the state extrapolated it into a $2 million assessment, with due diligence penalties reaching up to 50% of the property turned over [ZDSZHh3DZGs @ 00:02:13]. The same firm puts audit look-back periods at 10 to 20 years. Lorman's presenters add that holders who disagree with an assessment face a very unclear administrative path, with most states lacking a clear appeal route, so disputes often end up in court rather than review [2TghvbJiOis @ 00:07:29]. Size does not save a holder either: most states let holders report de minimis items under $25 or $50 only in the aggregate, so dollar value is not an opportunity to skip reporting [2TghvbJiOis @ 00:16:51]. On the owner side, Money Missed relays FTC-flagged red flags: pressure like claim within 48 hours or forfeit it, upfront processing fees, demands for a full Social Security number before a claim starts, and law-firm letters about a distant relative's unclaimed life insurance [sfcrjWHv_7k @ 00:11:42]. Any outreach script that resembles those patterns gets filed alongside the scams by the very claimants an agent is trying to convert.
