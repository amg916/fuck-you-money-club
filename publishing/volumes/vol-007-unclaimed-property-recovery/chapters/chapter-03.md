## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree that states hold escheated money as custodians, the owner's claim never dies, and official search and claiming cost nothing. MissingMoney.com, run under the state administrators' association, is the consensus first sweep. Each state portal where the owner lived, worked, or rented comes next, searched under name variants, business names, and deceased relatives. The street mechanics are concrete: result pages of 20, 40, or 80, info-versus-claim routing, Delaware's over-50 and under-50 bands, logged claim numbers, Social Security and proof-of-address demands, and a Delaware check reported within two weeks. Return practice splits sharply. Illinois mailed 140,000 checks worth $13.2 million, and New York auto-mails claims of $250 or less. California waits for owners to file on $7 billion across 28 million accounts. Finders can take up to half.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same baseline. When a holder loses contact with an owner past the dormancy window, the money is escheated to a state, and the state holds it as a keeper, not an owner. The Explainer Point lays it out flatly: every state and the District of Columbia runs a program, the owner never loses the right to ask for the money back, and states charge no fee to search or to claim [NWkWT5QX3sM @ 00:00:54]. Off The Receipt describes the same handoff. The business, which the law calls the holder, must report the money and turn it over once the dormancy clock runs out, and the state holds it as custodian against any future claim [hSrCCZHDpUU @ 00:01:07]. Lunch Cash supplies the timing operators plan around: the waiting period is often 3 to 5 years before a company must hand the money to the state, and the owner can still claim it years later [R6nRcAkxVdA @ 00:01:00]. The payment rule is just as unanimous. Nobody Asked, I Checked tells searchers that any site demanding payment before it shows results is off the official path. Penelope Jane Smith is blunter: you never have to pay to reclaim your money, so anyone asking for cash to unlock or register funds should be deleted and blocked [z2nZM27w4qU @ 00:02:54] [26FSCbZczh4 @ 00:01:55].

The second point of agreement is search breadth. Companies send lost-account funds to the state of the owner's last known address. Penelope Jane Smith's rule follows from that: search every state you have lived in, run the same sweep for family members who have passed, and circle back about once a year because records change [26FSCbZczh4 @ 00:03:43]. FundForge widens the net to every state where you have lived, worked, or rented an apartment, plus relatives' names, and notes that most states set no claim deadline, so there is no reason to panic [hPHajccTyH0 @ 00:11:37]. Nobody Asked, I Checked adds a name-variation layer: full legal name, any old married name, and common misspellings. A small change can make a record appear or vanish, and a re-run a year later catches the new reports businesses file with states every year [z2nZM27w4qU @ 00:01:45]. On tooling, the consensus lands on MissingMoney.com. Jordan Liles, a reporter who mostly covers scams, went in expecting this to be one. He concluded it was legit after finding holder names, addresses, and amount bands organized in a way he says lazy scammers would never bother to build. He then confirmed through Google News coverage that the site is endorsed by the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators and the National Association of State Treasurers [lM8PdIOG7-E @ 00:02:31].

Practitioners converge most sharply on the gap between states that push money out and states that wait for owners to file. In Illinois, Treasurer Michael Frerichs mailed checks to 140,000 residents in a single week, totaling $13.2 million against roughly $5 billion held. His staff first cross-checked owners against other state databases, and he publicly told recipients the envelope is not a scam [4abzayS6yzk @ 00:00:17]. New York's Comptroller now verifies ownership and mails checks for claims of $250 or less without any filing, under a law signed by Governor Kathy Hochul, while paying out $1.5 million a day from a $19 billion account [wCk_H4rThTo @ 00:00:38]. California sits at the other pole. CBS News California found that the state does not automatically return money to people, nonprofits, or small businesses, even though unclaimed property generates about a billion dollars a year for its general fund and the state keeps the interest [1Grj5UvPLwo @ 00:00:30]. The Legislative Analyst's Office named the structural problem directly: when property owners do not benefit, the state does, so there is always a built-in tension. Ryan Miller's analysis recommended automatic returns so some owners would never have to file a claim [0xrXfpILRzI @ 00:01:38].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On MissingMoney.com the run is mechanical. Knowledge Work Nexus types a personal name, then repeats the search under every business name or alternate name ever used. Results page in sets of 20, 40, or 80, and a thousand-hit list takes a few minutes to scan because results cannot be sorted by state [wO5QkFr7vdQ @ 00:01:08]. The presenter knew one record was theirs because the address matched their best friend Gail's former home. Clicking claim drops the item into a cart, and the view claimed properties button at the top of the list opens a relationship selector: owner or several other roles. Then come the form, a signature, a review, and a submission that returns a claim number [wO5QkFr7vdQ @ 00:02:23]. Mike Bogdan of Financial Soup ran John Jones across all states with no city, pulled over a thousand properties, and showed the two buttons on each result. Info routes to the holding state's official site, while claim uses the cart [ubLk2A5a-2A @ 00:02:36]. Bogdan prefers the info route and files directly through the state. He notes that not every state subscribes to in-site claiming, so for some records you have to use info and file with that state's treasury department [ubLk2A5a-2A @ 00:03:29].

Direct state portals follow the same skeleton with tighter fields. Michael Curtis of forgottenloot.org walks through Delaware's official site. You enter a first and last name, just a last name, or a property ID if one arrived in a mailer, a newspaper notice, or an event handout. A common name returns 1,000 records [JGravBgJTJo @ 00:02:58]. Delaware shows over-50 and under-50 bands rather than exact figures. You pick a record, hit view claim, act as owner, fill every red-asterisk field, answer the how-did-you-hear menu, preview, and write down the claim number from the summary [JGravBgJTJo @ 00:04:06]. Curtis says he did not believe it until a check arrived within two weeks of filing, and the site banner claims over $300 million returned in three years. KFNETWORK searched Kim Davis and added a city to narrow the popular name. They call the over-50 and under-50 bands not transparent and note that other states show exact dollar amounts [0anLNGLjnlA @ 00:03:04]. The form then asks for a Social Security number and phone number, and the follow-up email may request copies of Social Security documents, a birth certificate, and proof of address. Heirs pick the heir relationship instead of owner [0anLNGLjnlA @ 00:04:22].

Verification and cost control are where the numbers live. Jordan Liles checks a portal by searching its domain in quotes on Google News, not regular Google. He then reads whether outlets like Good Morning America, CNET, and local affiliates tie the site to state programs. That route showed MissingMoney.com covering 41 states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and Alberta [lM8PdIOG7-E @ 00:03:18]. Bogdan's due-diligence stack has four parts: a whois lookup on the domain, a Google search of the domain plus the word complaint, a look at Reddit threads, and asking ChatGPT how the site is monetized [ubLk2A5a-2A @ 00:01:53]. Nobody Asked, I Checked puts the finder math on the table. Wyoming's treasurer warned that private finders can take up to half of what they recover. The channel's log records the name searched, the state, the amount, the claim number, and the date, so claims in two states never get mixed up [z2nZM27w4qU @ 00:04:59]. Lunch Cash tells searchers to type the MissingMoney address directly, because ads can lead to look-alike sites [R6nRcAkxVdA @ 00:08:06]. Delaware's law also limits what finders can charge and bans finder deals entirely for the first two years after the money reaches the state [R6nRcAkxVdA @ 00:08:43].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The deepest split in state practice is whether the treasury mails the money out or waits for the owner to come hunting. Illinois Treasurer Michael Frerichs sent checks to 140,000 residents over the last week after staff double-checked holdings against other state databases to confirm rightful owners, and Frerichs told recipients the envelope is not a scam and to simply cash it [4abzayS6yzk @ 00:00:34]. New York now runs the same play by statute: for any claim of $250 or less, the Comptroller's office verifies ownership and mails the check instead of just sending a notice, under a law signed by Governor Kathy Hochul [wCk_H4rThTo @ 00:00:38]. California is the counter-example. CBS News California found the state does not automatically return money to people, nonprofits, or small businesses, even with Children's Hospital owed $1.3 million and food banks sitting in the database [1Grj5UvPLwo @ 00:00:30]. Delaware lands in between: Lunch Cash reports the state returns $150 million to $200 million a year on average and sends some money back automatically, without a filed claim, to taxpayers it can match [R6nRcAkxVdA @ 00:03:50]. For an operator, home-state rules decide whether a dormant balance arrives in the mailbox unprompted or surfaces only when someone runs the search.

Practitioners also split on how to treat unexpected contact and which button to press once a match appears. Penelope Jane Smith teaches that, to their knowledge, U.S. unclaimed property offices do not reach out to owners, and that anyone who contacts you needing money to unlock or register a balance should be deleted and blocked [26FSCbZczh4 @ 00:01:55]. Nobody Asked, I Checked takes a more surgical line on a letter about money you do not recognize: do not assume it is a scam, do not assume it is real, and look up the holder name and amount in the state's official search before acting [z2nZM27w4qU @ 00:06:37]. On MissingMoney.com the routes diverge again. Mike Bogdan of Financial Soup shows the site offers an Info button that sends you to the state's official site and a Claim button that builds a cart, then admits having no experience with the cart and claiming only through the state route [ubLk2A5a-2A @ 00:03:09]. Knowledge Work Nexus ran the cart route live, chose the owner relationship, signed, submitted, and got a claim number on the spot, while still stressing that every state runs its own process behind the portal [wO5QkFr7vdQ @ 00:02:45].

Even the headline numbers practitioners use to sell the search do not line up across channels, so an operator quoting them to a client should name the source. Penelope Jane Smith puts the national pile at about $33 billion and relays NAUPA's figure that about 1 in 10 people have unclaimed property, defined as accounts with no activity or owner contact for over a year [26FSCbZczh4 @ 00:02:37]. The Explainer Point uses roughly $70 billion held, one in seven Americans affected, an average claim of about $2,000, about $4.5 billion returned in the 12 months ending June 2024, and states charging no fee to search or claim [NWkWT5QX3sM @ 00:01:13]. Delaware's own homepage figures shift too. Michael Curtis read a banner on the official Delaware search site showing over $300 million returned to rightful owners in the last three years [JGravBgJTJo @ 00:00:46], while KFNETWORK, landing on the same official site through a Google search, read over $375 million returned in the past three years [0anLNGLjnlA @ 00:02:22]. Treat each figure as a banner claim tied to the date of the recording.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure practitioners documented is not a lost check but a liquidated position. CBS News Sacramento reported a retired couple whose $275,000 investment account showed zeros after the owner went three years without touching it or having any contact, at which point the state required Schwab to transfer the holdings into California's program [80djyAsS-YE @ 00:00:35]. The state then blamed Schwab for errors in the transfer while Schwab pointed the owner back to the state [80djyAsS-YE @ 00:01:02], and although the couple eventually claimed all of the property, the owner called the program a disgrace. The deeper hit comes after the sale. CBS News California reports state law requires California to cash out stocks and investments within 20 months of receiving them, so the owner collects whatever they sold for, not what the position is worth on the day of the claim [1Grj5UvPLwo @ 00:02:52]. The Explainer Point walks through Colette Barf's 2024 suit: Microsoft shares sent to Delaware in 2015 after a letter went to an address in South Africa, sold for nearly $200,000, and worth almost $2 million by the time of the lawsuit [NWkWT5QX3sM @ 00:06:47].

The scam surface is wide, and practitioners mapped it from both directions. Nobody Asked, I Checked relays Wyoming's treasurer warning that private finders can take up to half of what they recover while the state charges nothing, plus Oklahoma's treasurer flagging a site that charges a monthly fee to search records the official site always shows for free [z2nZM27w4qU @ 00:05:15]. Lunch Cash tells searchers to type MissingMoney.com in by hand because ads can lead to look-alike sites, and to check their own state's official site as well [R6nRcAkxVdA @ 00:08:06]. KFNETWORK's own origin story shows the trap and the escape in one move: in 2017, while job hunting online in Missouri, they clicked an ad promising unclaimed state money, worked through the prompts, and only trusted it after an email supplied a number to call the state directly [0anLNGLjnlA @ 00:01:11]. Penelope Jane Smith warns that even a claimed government endorsement can be fishy, so always validate before clicking [26FSCbZczh4 @ 00:05:57]. Across these operators the screen is identical: free search, a government domain, no upfront fee, and an independently confirmed phone number before any personal data changes hands.

Owner-side unit economics are mostly small tickets with real paperwork friction. Jordan Liles, who went in expecting a scam, warns that finding millions from a distant relative is too good to be true and points instead to forgotten prepaid cards near $25 or a clinic refund around $150 [lM8PdIOG7-E @ 00:04:00]. CBS News Sacramento's street test turned up $70 owed to Jody Cole from State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance, pulled from 38,000 Cole results in California alone [0xrXfpILRzI @ 00:00:49]. Friction scales with name frequency: Knowledge Work Nexus hit a thousand results, could not sort them by state, and only spotted a real match because it carried a best friend's former address [wO5QkFr7vdQ @ 00:01:29]. On the holder side, the downside is far steeper. FundForge reports that in the Temple Inland audit, with complete files surviving only back to about 2003, Kelmar's estimation produced a first assessment of $2.1 million while only $147.30 of actual property was identified [hPHajccTyH0 @ 00:05:54]. The same channel's fix for holders is blunt: good records matter most, because without them an auditor may estimate, and estimates can be large.
