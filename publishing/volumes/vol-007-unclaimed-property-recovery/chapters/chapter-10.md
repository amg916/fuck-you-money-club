## Executive Summary

Eric Richardson, Will Whitman, Darius, and Spring Porter describe a recovery business built around bankruptcy payments that never reached their owners. The operating sequence is concrete: locate a balance, identify the entitled party, research the docket, explain the money, secure the agreement, and submit the court's documents. Richardson promotes centralized leads and a $300 monthly platform; Porter demonstrates district-specific searches, claims-register research, and persistent follow-up. Their economics range from Porter's smaller stacked commissions to Richardson's guest accepting 20% on a recovery slightly above $34,000. The contrasts matter: centralized searches omit districts, actual payouts can exceed Richardson's 30–60-day expectation, and Porter's finder-fee discussion raises state-level contract concerns alongside the speakers' emphasis on negotiation. Documented ownership, court-specific paperwork, and claimant follow-through drive the work.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

The business starts with a payment that failed to reach its owner, not with a promise that every bankruptcy produces a windfall. Eric Richardson describes closed cases with undistributed money, incorrect addresses, deceased recipients, and checks that somebody simply never claimed. The debtor is the party in debt; the creditor is the party entitled to payment on that debt. Those roles matter before any outreach because the money can belong to either side [EyQovsqVOnE @ 00:00:28]. Darius at ACE SURPLUS gives the same basic picture: a distribution cannot be delivered, a check remains uncashed, and the payment returns as unclaimed funds. Individuals and companies can be recipients, and a debtor can receive a refund [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:00:28]. Will Whitman frames the operator's job as identifying people separated from their money and bringing the two back together, whether the person filed the bankruptcy or participated as a creditor [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:03:36]. Richardson's practical sequence is research, skip tracing, marketing, contracting, and claim submission. The research must explain how the funds arose, because a person whose bankruptcy ended years ago may reject the premise immediately. Reading the docket is part of being able to explain the claim professionally, not just a way to collect another name for a calling list [EyQovsqVOnE @ 00:02:31].

The shared starting point is a searchable court balance followed by case research, but the national locator is not the whole inventory. Whitman describes a database sortable by amount and filterable by court, estimating participation at roughly 95% and suggesting a focused Northern District of Georgia search for claims between $10,000 and $20,000 [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:10:05]. Darius also directs searchers to the online registry while estimating 90% coverage and saying the missing courts require direct contact [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:04:22]. Spring Porter supplies the concrete district example: Western District of Missouri used its own PDF with more than 400 cases rather than appearing in the locator [ckzrLZgtcYI @ 00:03:12]. That means the district's own information remains part of the search work, even when the main locator makes national prospecting convenient. Richardson then separates locating a listing from establishing its current status. In PACER, the operator checks whether somebody submitted a claim, whether payment followed, and whether the filing covered only part of the balance. Richardson and Whitman also report encountering a false claim, so an application appearing in the docket is not their signal to automatically abandon the lead. Their instruction is to read what happened and determine whether money remains rather than treating every existing filing as the end of the opportunity [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:15:14].

The service pitch rests on explaining the record and handling the paperwork, not merely announcing that money exists. Richardson recommends sending the documentary proof with an introductory email, alongside the company's website and email identity. Whitman identifies the trustee's report of deposit as the written record produced when unclaimed money enters the court [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:12:04]. Richardson's guest makes the same point from actual conversations: understanding the bankruptcy lets the caller explain where the money came from, which makes the service easier for a claimant to understand and accept [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:04:11]. Porter describes the work as researching leads, contacting prospective clients, and preparing documents for filing; the scripts, letters, and contracts support that sequence rather than replacing it [CJMCHDgNh4M @ 00:01:54]. Baseline preparation also includes learning the receiving court's process. Darius says ACE SURPLUS handles application preparation and filing, emphasizes each court's administrative process, and points to errors as a reason clients experience delays or denials [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:03:22]. These practitioners sell the ability to connect the claimant, the documented balance, and the required submission. A name and amount provide a lead; the conversation needs the explanation, and the filing needs the forms. That is the common service underneath Richardson's marketing system, Porter's document work, and Darius's application-handling offer.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

For a focused lead search, use Whitman's district-and-amount example: Northern District of Georgia, $10,000–$20,000 claims. Richardson also describes sorting fresh entries above $10,000 or ordering balances from highest to lowest [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:10:25]. For a broad personal search, Badwolf recommends leaving the court field empty and running the name separately as creditor and debtor, with case number and time frame left open when those details are not being used [suC7b2tuNcc @ 00:05:00]. Porter demonstrates a different route in Eastern New York, where the district's search requires a case number, debtor name, or creditor name. Entering bank names, or simply 'bank,' produces creditor leads; the example selected is a $31,802 Citibank balance [5A3cah6ujY4 @ 00:02:03]. Porter tries CourtListener first, finds that the case does not appear, and moves to PACER. There, the sequence is Find Cases, docket report, the unclaimed-funds entry near the bottom, and then the claims register. The Citibank filing is claim number three; its supporting document identifies the person who filed and provides a telephone number. Porter treats that as a contact lead while acknowledging the employee may no longer be there. The document also supplies supporting information for the recovery application [5A3cah6ujY4 @ 00:05:37].

Richardson's tool-assisted route combines skip tracing and documentary proof inside Bankruptcy Surplus. Richardson names BeenVerified and Skip Matrix as other research options, then describes the platform's phone numbers, relatives' details, email addresses, social links, and notice-of-deposit documents [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:16:48]. The marketing stack includes calls, texts, voicemail drops, emails, and follow-up triggers; Richardson reports four to five incoming calls per day and describes asking interested callers for an email address so the proof can be sent immediately [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:18:33]. Porter supplies the manual follow-through. When a client returned something signed but omitted another requirement, Porter texted a photograph of the signed contract and used casual language to remind the client that the remaining documents were still needed. Porter also suggests asking about Facebook as another communication route [YKjd__UIhB8 @ 00:02:05]. The CRM must include prospects, not only signed clients: Porter recounts forgetting earlier mailings and being caught unprepared when recipients called back. Record working and nonworking numbers, email, preferred communication, case information, the fund amount, and the agreed percentage. Porter specifically warns against remembering a different fee than the one originally discussed. Those notes let the operator resume the actual conversation rather than ask the returning prospect what was offered [YKjd__UIhB8 @ 00:03:26].

The economics are negotiated percentages on particular recoveries, with different operators choosing different deal sizes. Richardson quotes Bankruptcy Surplus at $300 monthly and describes 30–60 days as the ideal payment window, while noting that probate and additional work can extend it [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:27:36]. Richardson's guest accepted 20% on a straightforward recovery slightly above $34,000, but reports three or four months for the New York process, including a court Zoom call and successive processing periods of 21–35 days [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:07:14]. Porter shows the smaller-deal version: a 30% fee on $1,117.83 in Western Missouri produced $335.34, and that recovery joined Alabama and an earlier Virginia deal in a combined $1,030.14 commission total. Porter estimates about an hour of work, including travel, notarization, copies, and mailing, while explicitly separating that work from the waiting time [ckzrLZgtcYI @ 00:02:52]. On pricing, Porter urges negotiation rather than losing a client over a demand for half the recovery. Porter describes starting high and settling somewhere in the middle, and explains that 35% or 40% will not always be accepted. The specific tactic is to discuss the work being performed and adjust the percentage instead of treating the opening request as immovable [YKjd__UIhB8 @ 00:07:01].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Eric Richardson sells bankruptcy recovery as a centralized alternative to hunting county foreclosure lists: one site supplies names, amounts, and court locations, then the operator researches the case. Will Whitman narrows that pitch in their webinar, estimating that the locator covers about 95% of bankruptcy courts rather than every court; they also describe filtering a particular district for claims between $10,000 and $20,000 [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:10:05]. Darius at ACE SURPLUS gives a different coverage estimate, saying 90% of courts appear and that missing courts require direct contact [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:04:22]. Spring Porter demonstrates what searching beyond the central locator actually involves. In Florida, Porter finds only the Southern District listed, then visits the Northern and Middle District websites separately. The Northern District offers application paperwork and an affidavit, while the Middle District calls for a motion; the same state does not mean identical filing mechanics [R8PpcOrRZc4 @ 00:07:24]. Porter also searches territories: Puerto Rico appears in the locator, Guam has a separate PDF, and other territory searches lead to clerk contacts. Puerto Rico introduces a practical language barrier: Porter encounters Spanish court documents despite English search pages and describes discovering that difficulty firsthand. These are alternative lead routes, not completed territory recoveries [lWmrc3x-B-E @ 00:02:42].

Fee advice splits between negotiated percentages and Porter's warning about what happens when a client relationship becomes a contract dispute. Richardson answers the webinar's fee-cap question by saying the percentage is discretionary and negotiable, immediately adding that asking for 50% or 60% does not mean the client will accept it [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:28:49]. Porter likewise tells operators to negotiate rather than abandon a claim because the owner will not surrender half. Porter recounts contacting someone whose earlier locator wanted half and would not negotiate, and describes starting high before settling somewhere in the middle [YKjd__UIhB8 @ 00:07:01]. But Porter's dedicated finder-fee discussion takes a different position on state rules: absence of fee language in bankruptcy materials does not, in Porter's account, remove state-level exposure when a deal turns into litigation. Porter describes considering an $1,100 Virginia contract suit against a client who had not paid, without reporting a court victory [sf-f4iZbpRo @ 00:06:45]. Richardson's interview guest supplies a completed-deal comparison: a claim worth a little more than $34,000 carried an agreed 20% company fee because the case was straightforward. That figure describes the recovered claim, not a $34,000 take-home commission. The guest's rationale was accepting a worthwhile fee rather than pushing for more [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:07:14].

The outreach playbooks range from a paid operating system to ordinary calls and self-directed name searches. Richardson describes Bankruptcy Surplus as a database with skip-traced contacts, supporting documents, a CRM, and claim processing; the webinar prices that service at $300 monthly. Operators can market inside the system and request filing when a prospect is ready, while the course also teaches independent research and filing [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:26:51]. Richardson's interview guest reports a simpler acquisition method: calling. Their partnership divides calls and paperwork, but several identified owners still procrastinate, including contacts associated with $232,000 and $65,000 claims. Porter favors text follow-up when contracts return incomplete, sometimes sending a photograph of the signed contract and using conversational language. Porter also asks about Facebook and records how clients prefer to communicate [YKjd__UIhB8 @ 00:02:05]. Badwolf Unincorporated takes a different route entirely: search for yourself or relatives, leave the court field empty, and run the name separately as creditor and debtor. Badwolf's broad search produces many similar names, followed by suggestions to request identifying information. That demonstration is personal discovery and inquiry, not a reported paid recovery operation [suC7b2tuNcc @ 00:05:00].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Porter's Missouri discrepancy shows why a listed amount can stall the entire job. A district PDF showed $1,983, while the trustee's final report showed more than $13,000. Porter found repeated deposit entries and withdrawals, could not reconcile the figures, and pursued clarification because the larger commission made the extra work worthwhile [y2j1Ux0R9jY @ 00:01:08]. The bankruptcy clerk could not resolve it; the trustee's office also struggled; a judge's law clerk redirected Porter to the courtroom deputy. Porter left a message rather than reporting a resolved balance. Porter explicitly declined to contact the owner and prepare an application with an amount that might be wrong, and contemplated moving on if nobody answered [y2j1Ux0R9jY @ 00:04:48]. Another amount trap appears in Porter's Northern Florida walkthrough: a displayed case total of roughly $2,300 was divided among five creditors, rather than belonging to one claimant. Opening the case exposed the individual allocations [R8PpcOrRZc4 @ 00:03:32]. Richardson and Whitman add a different docket trap: they encountered a false claim, and Richardson explains that an existing application does not automatically exhaust the funds. Their research includes whether payment occurred or whether somebody sought only a portion. The job is to understand the entries, not simply abandon every docket containing a claim [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:15:32].

Payout timing and headline earnings diverge sharply across the reported jobs. Richardson describes 30 to 60 days as an ideal payout window, with additional proceedings such as probate potentially extending it [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:28:30]. The interview guest's completed New York claim instead took about three or four months, including a court Zoom verification and successive processing intervals described as 21 to 35 days. The same guest reports two Texas filings delayed by a six-month backlog with one person handling the work; an Illinois filing had been accepted, but its anticipated payout had not arrived [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:02:30] [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:08:13]. Porter supplies another concrete timeline: a Middle District of Alabama claim submitted September 30 produced a check dated January 7. Porter's fee there was $294.80 at 40%, while the Western District of Missouri example produced a $335.34 fee at 30%. Porter describes Missouri as fast and Alabama as slow despite friendly staff and easy paperwork. The $1,030.14 headline combines those fees with an earlier Eastern District of Virginia deal; Porter estimates about an hour of work but explicitly says there was waiting and that this was not daily income. Some districts mailed checks rather than sending electronic transfers [ckzrLZgtcYI @ 00:00:32].

Institutional access and unfinished client paperwork create bottlenecks before payment processing even begins. Porter contacted Georgia's Department of Revenue about $9,718 still sitting in a court registry, returned after an earlier January call, and reached a representative who lacked authority. The manager was unavailable, leaving another message rather than a signed engagement. Porter describes government creditors as difficult to reach through someone who understands the recovery request [1fuOy7IsEMo @ 00:00:31]. In the Eastern District of New York walkthrough, Porter researches a $31,802 Citibank entry through PACER and the claims register, locating the original claim signer's telephone number. Porter does not report collecting that money and says banking contacts have promised to investigate, then failed to return calls [5A3cah6ujY4 @ 00:09:31]. Smaller administrative failures are just as explicit: Porter has forgotten prospects after mailing them, struggled to remember quoted percentages, and received signed contracts with missing notarization. The response is to record prospects and communication preferences, retain fee notes, and follow up on missing documents. Sending identification separately can also trigger a deficiency notice when a clerk expects one package [YKjd__UIhB8 @ 00:00:50]. Darius at ACE SURPLUS identifies filing errors as a source of client delays and denials, making document preparation a central part of the service [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:03:42].
