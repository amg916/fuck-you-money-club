## Executive Summary

State unclaimed funds operators agree the pool is enormous, estimated at 49 to 80-plus billion depending on the speaker, and that registration is usually a light lift of ID, paperwork, and sometimes a background check. The consensus launch order is: read the state's fee cap and claim-package rules, pick one niche and one or two states, set up a visible business footprint, and prospect daily with follow-up. Street mechanics center on downloading or buying state lists, filtering for big claims, with David Church refusing anything under 10,000 dollars at a 10% cap, skip tracing for free or roughly 14 to 15 dollars per 100 leads, loaded-opener scripts, and notarized agreements. The sharpest split is outreach: Wilkerson and Church lean on cold calls and follow-up, while Joe Green largely rejects cold calling and skip tracing for inbound social-media lead flow.

## The Practitioner Consensus: A Deep Market, an Easy Door, and a Narrow Lane

Every operator here starts from the same premise: the money is real, it is massive, and it is not running out. David Church of Surplus Institute pegs the national pool at roughly 100 billion dollars, split about evenly between state unclaimed funds and mortgage and tax overages, then pulls a February 2023 figure showing states alone holding 70 billion, and tells anyone claiming the money is not there that they are lying [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:00:00] [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:01:21]. David Wilkerson of Surplus Funds 101 puts state-level holdings above 80 billion and argues that volume is exactly what turns this into a predictable long-term career, because you will never run out of accounts to recover [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:01:28]. Eric Richardson of Money Making Juggernaut defines the asset class cleanly: state funds are dormant savings accounts, uncashed checks, unclaimed insurance payouts, utility overpayments, PayPal balances, and death benefits that holders cannot keep and must turn over to the state treasurer, while surplus funds are foreclosure overages held at the county level [kuCEYN64vtQ @ 00:00:25]. The working consensus is that state funds are the simpler pitch, because the claimant already understands a forgotten bank account, and the operators who stay in the game treat that simplicity as their entry point rather than their ceiling.

The second point of agreement is that registration is easy and reading the state rules is mandatory. Timepiece Dan says becoming a state investigator means sending in your ID and answering a couple of questions, that anyone can become one, and that he believes the check exists mainly for tax purposes and to confirm who you are [sDutuuk5RG0 @ 00:02:06]. Wilkerson says most states do not require a license at all, only registration, and usually charge nothing; he tells newcomers to use the unclaimed.org map, click into a state, and line out three things before dialing anyone: what registration requires, how leads are obtained, and the exact claim package instructions, sometimes published as an investigator handbook [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:10:47]. Church runs the same drill live on the Mississippi treasury site, scrolling past the claim buttons to the FAQ, where he confirms the state works with claim finders at a maximum 10% fee before deciding it is workable [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:05:22]. Richardson frames the identical checkpoint for any market: before you pull a lead, know whether the state has stipulations on how you file a claim, how much you can charge, and any time duration, and understand that you do not have to live in the state you work [_vsvjD8JvgU @ 00:01:57].

The third consensus is that the people who wash out treat this as a lottery ticket, not a business. Joe Green of Unclaimed CEO says the worst advice he got starting out was to just register and get going; with no website and no Google business footprint, claimants researched him and backed out of closed deals [XsLq9LUTb6w @ 00:01:05]. Green ties the backouts to trust: these people are not finding you, you are finding them, and they are handing over personal information. Green's six-step launch order starts by forcing beginners to pick one category, state funds or tax overages, because the filing process and the way you find people differ between them [i84F9CVghTA @ 00:01:03]. Wilkerson pushes the same narrowing even harder: one niche, then only one or two states, because the process is identical everywhere but each state's claim package quirks will stretch your mental bandwidth thin [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:04:19]. Church closes the loop on discipline, saying you may grind four to seven straight days with nothing before the yeses and call-me-back-tomorrows start, and that consistency is what eventually turns the light on [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:04:52]. Across all four, the baseline is the same: register, choose a lane, set a daily prospecting rhythm, and expect the first real results only after sustained volume.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Lists, Skip Tracing, Scripts, and the Claim Package

Lead sourcing differs sharply by state. Church downloads California's 500-and-up investigator file, opens the third file because it holds the highest claims, and warns that Mac Numbers choked on the data so he had to buy and download Excel [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:11:33]. Timepiece Dan scrolls the same California list live, counting close to 400,000 lines in just one of four files, and runs the math: a 30,000 dollar royalty claim at the 10% ceiling pays 3,000 [sDutuuk5RG0 @ 00:03:27]. Richardson says many states do not hand lists out free, quoting 25 dollars in Arkansas and 10 dollars in Massachusetts, and notes some states only offer an online portal where you run direct searches name by name [kuCEYN64vtQ @ 00:03:00]. When a state stays quiet, Church calls and asks for the list, and if the clerk asks why, he skips the heir-finder pitch and says it is public information, he is doing research, and he understands 70 billion sits unclaimed nationwide. States that refuse, he says, you simply cannot work [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:07:29].

Contact mechanics run from free to cheap, and every operator here builds them in the same order. Richardson's budget route is free TruePeopleSearch, with BeenVerified and Intelius as alternatives, while his volume shortcut is a batch skip tracing service where his promo code brings the cost to about 14 to 15 dollars per 100 leads, versus four, five, or even ten hours of manual research [_vsvjD8JvgU @ 00:02:55]. Timepiece Dan skips paid tools entirely and Googles the first name, last name, address, city, and state straight off the California list until numbers pop up, calling it the free way with no marketing spend [sDutuuk5RG0 @ 00:06:14]. The opener matters as much as the number. Church skips small talk; he loads the first line with the claimant's name, a former address, and the bank holding the dormant account, then says he is a licensed locator with the state and asks permission to retrieve the funds, a move he says stumbles the claimant into remembering the account [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:03:17]. Wilkerson treats delivery as theatrics, telling operators to read the script multiple times and then walk around the house rehearsing it, because how you react when someone says hello or hangs up matters almost more than the words themselves [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:13:30].

Closing the file is where state mechanics diverge most. Church says California pays the locator's 10% directly, so a 20,000 dollar check becomes 2,000 for him and 18,000 for the client, with no collecting from the client [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:01:43]. California also hands you its own two contracts, one notarized, and the package goes straight to the state, while other states make you assemble the contracts and information yourself, file with the state, and then collect your fee from the client after they are paid [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:03:55]. Wilkerson's rule of thumb is that most states require any claim agreement to be notarized once the account tops 1,000 dollars, some require it regardless, and nine times out of ten you will not get paid without it [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:15:44]. He sends the agreement and supporting documents to a notary near the claimant, who packages everything for the state, which he calls the most efficient way to run a transaction start to finish. Speed then depends on the treasury: Richardson says state funds typically process within 60 days because the check is literally already sitting in the State Treasury, unlike surplus funds that must clear a county legal review before payout [kuCEYN64vtQ @ 00:03:52].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Register and Map the State Rules
   - Pick one state, register as an investigator, and document registration, lead access, and claim package instructions.
   - Use the unclaimed.org map to reach the state site, confirm whether a license or only registration is required, how leads are delivered, and the exact claim package the state expects; call the state if anything is unclear [Surplus Funds 101 — PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:10:47].
2. Pull and Filter the List
   - Download or request the state list and filter for claims large enough to justify the work at the state's cap.
   - At a 10% cap, Church works nothing under 10,000 dollars and starts with a roughly 10,000 dollar claim; if no list is posted, he calls and asks for it as public information for research [Surplus Institute / David Church / Unclaimed Funds — RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:07:42].
3. Skip Trace and Open the Call
   - Batch skip trace the filtered leads and open each call with the claimant's name, former address, and holder.
   - Richardson's batch service runs about 14 to 15 dollars per 100 leads versus hours of manual research [Money Making Juggernaut — _vsvjD8JvgU @ 00:02:55]; Church's opener names the person, the address, and the dormant account, then asks permission to help retrieve the funds [Surplus Institute / David Church / Unclaimed Funds — IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:03:17].
4. Paper, Notarize, and File the Claim Package
   - Fill out the state's agreement exactly as instructed, get it notarized near the claimant, and submit the full package.
   - Wilkerson sends the agreement and supporting documents to a notary close to the claimant, who notarizes, packages everything, and usually ships it to the state, which he calls the most efficient way to run a transaction start to finish [Surplus Funds 101 — PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:17:08].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split is how the first conversation happens. Joe Green of Unclaimed CEO tells beginners to stop cold calling entirely, arguing nobody trusts a stranger delivering unbelievable news, and he labels skip tracing a waste of time while he closes deals with people who come to him through what he calls his Honeypot method [zI7m3acMjuY @ 00:00:15]. His build: drive people to a website or landing page form, research whoever submits, and even work small amounts pro bono in exchange for referrals, which he says flooded his inbox with brothers, sisters, parents and coworkers asking him to check for money [XsLq9LUTb6w @ 00:04:00]. David Wilkerson of Surplus Funds 101 runs the opposite playbook: skip trace the state data and cold call, which he calls the quickest, cheapest, fastest way to gain traction, inside a three-hour daily prospecting window [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:12:49]. David Church of Surplus Institute backs the outbound route with a direct opener that names the person, a former address and the dormant Bank of America account before he pitches himself as a licensed locator asking permission to retrieve the funds [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:03:17]. Green concedes cold calling works for some, but says the other 98 percent with jobs and kids cannot dial six to eight hours a day.

Practitioners also split on what has to exist before the first dial. Joe Green treats funding as step one: file with the IRS and the state, open a business bank account, and ask for a 0 percent business credit card running 12 to 18 months, which he says carries 1 percent monthly minimums, can be pulled as a cash advance to pay yourself, and does not touch personal credit [i84F9CVghTA @ 00:06:13]. He also insists on a credibility footprint, because early prospects who researched him and found no website or Google business listing backed out of deals he had already closed [XsLq9LUTb6w @ 00:01:24]. David Wilkerson pushes the reverse mindset: kill the everything-has-to-be-perfect thinking, spend only on what produces revenue, and remember that his own first couple of deals closed before he even owned business cards [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:05:27]. Eric Richardson of Money Making Juggernaut goes further and runs the full cycle for zero dollars with free county lists, free people-search skip tracing, his free call scenarios and self-typed service agreements, admitting the free route is inefficient and that you trade money for intelligence and time [sK5PqCLSzzg @ 00:03:56]. The real choice is whether credibility infrastructure and a funded runway come first, or raw dial volume on a shoestring.

State selection is the third fault line. David Church calls California pleasant because the state registers you and cuts your 10 percent check directly, so a $20,000 claim nets him $2,000 without chasing the client, though payouts can take six months or longer [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:01:43]. He avoids working Florida solo because of its investigator requirement, but his tax-overage lawyer offered a 50/50 fee split to work Florida state funds under the attorney, and Church plans to push that toward 70/30 by feeding three or four claims a week [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:15:11]. Timepiece Dan pitches California lists as the easy entry, scrolling past $12,000, $20,000 and $30,000 accounts and pricing a $30,000 claim at a $3,000 fee, noting the fee is negotiable up to the 10 percent ceiling [sDutuuk5RG0 @ 00:03:27]. Wilkerson walked away from California over its minimum six-month turnaround, ranks payout speed and bulk-lead access above fee size, points to Wisconsin and Michigan as faster payers, and leans toward 20 percent cap states because most states sit in the 10 to 20 percent band [qyRWV76TgwE @ 00:09:16]. Same state, opposite verdicts: one operator's easy entry is another's dead capital.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common failure the practitioners report is quitting before the pipeline matures. Joe Green says he has watched excited starters turn into a ghost town within two months because the business was more work than they expected and they never figured it out [Vy3iGS6ep4w @ 00:00:37]. David Wilkerson hears the same story from people who called their list one time, had one conversation, got no deal and wanted out; his rule is that today's work pays about three months down the road, so newcomers should lock in a six-month time frame [PGvJEv9Y_Go @ 00:09:12]. He also names state-hopping as a top killer: a beginner skip traces Florida leads, watches a guru pitch Utah, abandons the Florida pipeline, and eats the lost time, money and revenue [qyRWV76TgwE @ 00:04:08]. FundSleuth traces the quitting to a skills gap, reporting that course graduates who are excellent researchers get discouraged because their skip tracing source could not reach anyone or because they never learned to sell, since most courses do not coach the sales side, and even the hobbyist route of handing signed deals to a back-end partner demands research, skip tracing and closing skills [kGhoQfbhUG4 @ 00:04:11].

The second trap is bleeding personal money and credibility at the same time. Green says he burned his own cash and personal credit cards, slowed down the more he spent, and dialed hundreds of people a day from an unfamiliar out-of-area number while nobody picked up and those who did hung up mid-script [XsLq9LUTb6w @ 00:01:51]. He warns that pushing personal cards past 30 percent utilization drags your credit score down while you drain family money meant for bills [Vy3iGS6ep4w @ 00:01:22]. David Church faces a different leak: claimants who go around you and file directly after your call, which he says happens all the time and which he shrugs off because missing his weekly deal count matters far more than one lost claimant [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:09:55]. Wilkerson reports an internal leak, admitting that early on he subconsciously pushed deals away, including a claimant owed $50,000, because he did not feel he deserved to work a transaction that size [qyRWV76TgwE @ 00:05:20]. Each of these failures kills signed agreements rather than leads: the money is sitting on the list, but no ring, no trust or no nerve means no deal.

The economics checks are blunt. Under California's 10 percent cap, Church's $20,000 claim pays him $2,000 and the client $18,000, but one probate file took about eight months; New York only discloses whether an account is under or over $100, and Florida demands two years under a licensed investigator with fees capped at $1,000, so he refuses to work either state blind [IIBKDdChS-k @ 00:02:37]. His floor follows from the cap: nothing under $10,000, with a first claim sized around that mark [RjCjrBjQF7Y @ 00:07:42]. Eric Richardson adds that state lists are not always free, quoting $25 in Arkansas and $10 in Massachusetts, and he expects state claims to process within 60 days because the money already sits in the treasury, a timeline that collides with the multi-month California waits Church and Wilkerson describe [kuCEYN64vtQ @ 00:02:47]. Joe Green's volume check: if ten calls a day yields one pickup and doubling to twenty still yields one, more dialing is wasted energy, and you only learn that by tracking the ratio for two to three weeks instead of guessing [i84F9CVghTA @ 00:10:08].
