## Executive Summary

State unclaimed property portals across Texas, New York, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Indiana, Iowa, Montana, Colorado, Kansas, Maryland and Georgia run on the same engine: holders remit dormant money after losing contact, the state holds it, and you pull it back with a last-name search plus ID and address proof. Practitioners agree the search is free, name variations and old addresses unlock hidden records, and paid finders run the identical database. Street numbers: Texas averages 37 business days, New York pays most simple claims within 30 days, Colorado's average claim is $918, and utility deposits escheat after one year in Iowa, Montana and Kansas. They split on finder fee ceilings (15% in New York and Kansas, 10% to 30% in Georgia, 10% to 40% in Montana), on whether amounts are shown, and on how long states hold funds.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Nearly every speaker frames the state as a custodian, not a confiscator. KHOU 11 reports that by law businesses and financial institutions must turn money over to the state once they lose contact with the owner, and the Texas Comptroller is holding more than $10 billion [uY7XnXFfpv4 @ 00:00:18]. Как се прави pins New York's trigger at roughly three years without owner-directed activity, after which the holder runs a due diligence attempt to reach you and then remits, with the state holding the funds in perpetual custody and no statute of limitations, so money lost three years or thirty years ago is equally claimable [cF0HhlLh7NI @ 00:02:26]. GardenShop.Pro repeats the model for Kansas, describing the state as a perpetual safekeeper with no maintenance fees draining balances and no forfeiture [E2EtkM2OBvI @ 00:02:00]. The inventory is consistent: forgotten bank accounts, uncashed checks, utility deposits, insurance refunds, final paychecks, gift cards, stock dividends and safe deposit box contents. NBC New York adds that the money is usually uncashed checks sent to an old address, reported by banks, retailers and health insurers [qHskTBuAHTg @ 00:00:35]. The operator takeaway: anything a business owed you and could not deliver sits in a state ledger, indexed by the name and address that business had on file.

The second consensus point is that the search itself is a last-name game, and exact-match indexing punishes lazy queries. Как се прави tells New York searchers to start with the surname or business name, refine with first name, city or zip, and then grind through maiden names, common misspellings and previous addresses, because even a tiny typo in how a bank recorded your name years ago can hide a match [9BVp_yhswZY @ 00:02:54]. GardenShop.Pro pushes this harder for utility money in Iowa: accounts are recorded under the exact name given when the account was opened, so if the gas company misspelled your name a decade ago you have to search that exact typo [OFnbGkVuoHQ @ 00:04:13]. The same channel's Maryland walkthrough says utilities logged names exactly as they appeared on the paper contract, so test hyphenated and unhyphenated versions, maiden names, and any LLC or small business name the bill ran under [1IpkVjkkXHg @ 00:04:31]. NBC New York gives the same instruction in one line: if your name is often misspelled, check the list for misspellings [qHskTBuAHTg @ 00:01:24]. Geography is the other lever. The money is tied to the address the holder had, not where you live now, so old cities and zip codes are filters to work, not afterthoughts.

Third, every practitioner who addresses fees says the state route is free and the paid route duplicates it. Как се прави warns that location service providers mail letters offering to recover your money for a percentage, and says flatly that they are simply running a last name search on the exact same free public database, while going direct keeps 100% of the recovered funds [cF0HhlLh7NI @ 00:06:41]. GardenShop.Pro's Montana breakdown puts the ask at 10% to 40% of the deposit and lands on the same verdict: they are searching the same free database you now know how to use [3iluxKs2wP8 @ 00:07:21]. The consensus also extends the hunt beyond your own name and your own state. WFAA tells viewers to search the names of friends and family while on the Texas portal and to hit the unclaimed property site for every state they have previously lived in [0UUN2j2_dFU @ 00:01:59]. GardenShop.Pro's Georgia episode goes further, noting the database is public and free to search, so you can look up parents, siblings, friends, and even current or former employers, then call them with the find [DVmNNFdoDzE @ 00:07:34]. That network search is where the volume lives: one name is one shot, while a family tree crossed with every former address and state multiplies the number of shots you get at a hit.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Portals differ sharply in what they show you before you file, and that dictates how you triage results. On the Texas portal, WFAA reports that claimable property appears with the dollar amount listed [0UUN2j2_dFU @ 00:00:17], and KHOU 11 shows the claim page naming the exact amount and type, down to contents of a safety deposit box [uY7XnXFfpv4 @ 00:00:48]. Pennsylvania runs a color-coded grid. Michael Curtis walks through it: green rows mean you must contact the office to determine eligibility, white rows are single-owner records you can claim, gray rows are joint ownership, and each row shows the reporting company with amounts only bucketed as under 100 or over 100 [0YK26E_fzYA @ 00:03:35]. He notes an over-100 record could be $500 or more and you simply do not know until you request the claim form, and that the results grid shows only the first 400 rows. Maryland, per GardenShop.Pro, uses prefix matching, so a search for Doe finds Doe, John but can miss a record filed as Estate of John Doe because the first word is Estate [1IpkVjkkXHg @ 00:03:58]. Anyone working a deceased relative's money needs to search with the estate prefix as well as the surname, and anyone working Pennsylvania should narrow with city and address before hitting that 400-row ceiling.

Filing follows the same skeleton everywhere: claim button, relationship to owner, identity fields, then documents. WFAA lays out Texas click by click: hit claim next to the listing, open view claimed properties, pick your relationship from the dropdown as owner, heir or trustee, enter name, address and Social Security number, confirm you are telling the truth and submit; the state then sends a claim listing the items it needs, such as a driver's license and documents showing your relationship to the property [0UUN2j2_dFU @ 00:00:50]. In Iowa, GardenShop.Pro says a web wizard asks for current mailing address, phone and legal relationship, then emails a unique claim ID for tracking; minimum paperwork is a signed claim form, photo ID and Social Security number, plus historic address proof like an old lease [OFnbGkVuoHQ @ 00:03:40]. Maryland adds form COT ST-912, which must be notarized if the claim is over $500, with portal upload as the fastest path or mail to Baltimore [1IpkVjkkXHg @ 00:05:32]. Kansas splits the channels: routine ID and mail scans go through digital upload, but an original certified death certificate or notarized affidavit of heirship cannot be emailed or faxed and must be mailed to the division in Topeka [E2EtkM2OBvI @ 00:06:00].

Turnaround numbers are specific and swing with claim complexity. WFAA reports the Texas Comptroller's average turnaround last year was 37 business days across all claim types, with heirship claims running longer and incomplete documentation slowing things down [0UUN2j2_dFU @ 00:01:42]. Как се прави gives New York's clock: a confirmation email within 48 hours, processing within 30 days if no further action is needed, and up to 90 days if the state asks for ID or proof of an old address; the check then arrives in a perforated envelope and must be cashed by December 31 of the following calendar year or it goes abandoned again [9BVp_yhswZY @ 00:03:36]. GardenShop.Pro tiers Colorado: simple cash claims with clear address matches in a few days to about four weeks, manual address validation in 90 to 150 days, and heirship, probate or business claims commonly six to twelve months [GWa4tWyuEoA @ 00:06:02]. The same channel flags the haircut the quick news segments skip: in Montana, approved claims are cross-referenced against government debts, so delinquent state taxes, unpaid child support, IRS assessments or tribal court obligations come out first and you receive a paper warrant check for the net balance [3iluxKs2wP8 @ 00:06:37].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the name and address variant list
   - List legal surname, maiden and prior married names, nicknames, hyphenated forms, misspellings, business names, and every former address.
   - Test maiden names, nicknames, common misspellings and hyphenated structures, and do not limit the initial search to your current zip code because moved money is tied to the old address [Как се прави — cF0HhlLh7NI @ 00:04:25].
2. Run each state portal plus the national registry
   - Search the official portal for every state you lived in, then run missingmoney.com for cross-state holders.
   - Kansas searchers start at missingmoney.ks.gov and also run missingmoney.com, especially after living in multiple states, typing just the last name and then variations [GardenShop.Pro — E2EtkM2OBvI @ 00:03:21].
3. Triage results and file the claim
   - Match name and old address, read the property type and holder, then hit claim and select your relationship to the owner.
   - Click claim next to the listing, open view claimed properties, choose owner, heir or trustee from the dropdown, then enter name, address and Social Security number and submit [WFAA — 0UUN2j2_dFU @ 00:00:33].
4. Send a complete document pack the first time
   - Assemble photo ID, Social Security proof, current address proof, and documentation tying you to the prior address before submitting.
   - Gather all documents before you hit submit, because sending an incomplete file is the most common reason people face a frustrating 6-month claim denial [GardenShop.Pro — 3iluxKs2wP8 @ 00:06:22].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split runs through third-party finders. Как се прави, in its New York explainer, treats location service providers as a regulated lane rather than a scam: their fee is capped at a maximum of 15% of recovered funds, they need a written notarized contract, and the state still mails 100% of the check to the owner, leaving the owner to pay the provider afterward [9BVp_yhswZY @ 00:05:12]. The same channel's last-name walkthrough takes the hard line instead. If a finder letter shows up offering recovery for a percentage, do not pay, because the finder is simply running a last-name search on the identical free public database [cF0HhlLh7NI @ 00:06:28]. GardenShop.Pro adds a timing weapon from Iowa: any agreement to pay a finder made within the first 24 months of the state holding the property is unenforceable, and after that window the fee is still capped at 15% with a strict notarized contract [OFnbGkVuoHQ @ 00:05:17]. In Georgia, the same channel concedes that claimants designated representatives operate legally and can charge 10 to 30%, then runs the math: a $1,000 find means handing over $300 for a search the owner could run free [DVmNNFdoDzE @ 00:04:19]. Both camps agree the state itself never takes a cut.

Operators also split on how wide to throw the first search. Michael Curtis, working Pennsylvania's treasury database, pushes narrowing: last name, first name, middle initial, city, and the old address if it is known, because without those fields the operator faces a long list to scroll through [0YK26E_fzYA @ 00:11:35]. Как се прави argues the reverse for New York. Start broad, keep geographic filters wide, and never lock the first pass to the current zip code, because money left behind after a move is tied to the old address; a reporter pulled two claims linked to former addresses in Queens and Albany exactly that way [cF0HhlLh7NI @ 00:04:25]. GardenShop.Pro's Maryland walkthrough explains why simple input beats detailed input on some portals: claimitmd.gov runs a prefix matching algorithm, so a plain 'Doe' surfaces 'Doe, John' but can miss a record filed as 'Estate of John Doe' because the first word is Estate [1IpkVjkkXHg @ 00:03:42]. What comes back on screen differs too. KHOU 11 showed the Texas portal listing the exact amount and the kind of money, down to the contents of a safety deposit box [uY7XnXFfpv4 @ 00:00:48], while Pennsylvania only bands rows as under or over 100 and Maryland hides exact figures until identity is proven.

States also disagree on whether the owner has to lift a finger at all. GardenShop.Pro reports that Iowa's Money Match program cross-references the unclaimed property database against current state income tax filings and mails a check automatically, no claim form required [OFnbGkVuoHQ @ 00:04:46]. Montana's House Bill 88, passed in the 2025 legislative session, lets the Department of Revenue match and pay out up to $1,000 using state tax records, yet the same walkthrough still pushes manual searching because the auto-pay feature has limits and will not trigger on everything [3iluxKs2wP8 @ 00:02:43]. Colorado runs a similar automated identification match against state tax and motor vehicle records, and if one of those checks shows up in the mail the owner can cash it right away [GWa4tWyuEoA @ 00:04:04]. The holding clock is the other fault line. New York, Kansas, Maryland, and Georgia are all described as perpetual custodians with no deadline, but Indiana's attorney general told WTHR his office keeps unclaimed money for 25 years, after which it goes to the state, which is why Indiana set up a booth at the state fair so people walking by could run a quick search [pPow2SwrxBE @ 00:01:16]. For Indiana claimants, waiting carries a real cost rather than a safe default.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive mistake reported is an incomplete file. GardenShop.Pro's Montana walkthrough calls it the most common reason claimants hit a frustrating 6-month claim denial, and the fix is to gather a government-issued photo ID, Social Security proof, current address proof, and documentation of the prior address tied to the property before hitting submit; a Missoula rental from 10 years ago has to be proven [3iluxKs2wP8 @ 00:06:08]. WFAA heard that the Texas document request 'can be a pain,' with the state asking for a driver's license or supporting documentation showing the claimant's relationship to the property, and when an old utility bill is missing, most companies will hand over a copy [0UUN2j2_dFU @ 00:01:10]. Delivery-channel rules trip people next. GardenShop.Pro warns that Kansas will not take an original certified death certificate or a notarized affidavit of heirship by email or fax; those have to travel by physical mail to the unclaimed property division in Topeka [E2EtkM2OBvI @ 00:06:00]. Maryland's form COT ST-912 must be notarized once the claim tops $500, and the checklist adds a historical lease, a bank record from that year, or an old utility statement proving residence at the BGE or Pepco service address [1IpkVjkkXHg @ 00:05:32].

Approval does not mean the full amount lands. GardenShop.Pro describes Montana's interagency revenue offsets: once a claim is approved, the state must cross-reference the claimant against outstanding government debts, so delinquent state taxes, unpaid child support, IRS assessments, or tribal court obligations come out of the deposit first, followed by a written explanation and a paper warrant check for the net balance [3iluxKs2wP8 @ 00:06:37]. Kansas runs the same play through its mandatory setoff program, checking a centralized state debt database and intercepting delinquent income taxes, child support, and district court fines before the treasurer writes the check; only a refund larger than the debt pays out, and only the net [E2EtkM2OBvI @ 00:07:00]. The second leak is sitting on the check. Как се прави says New York owners generally have until December 31st of the following calendar year to cash it, or the funds become abandoned all over again and the whole claim restarts from scratch; property that was originally interest-bearing may also be subject to federal and state taxes [9BVp_yhswZY @ 00:05:56]. Iowa's Money Match envelope arrives looking like junk mail, and GardenShop.Pro warns against throwing it away, because it has to be cashed within 6 months [OFnbGkVuoHQ @ 00:05:01].

Search-side traps cost just as much. GardenShop.Pro stresses that utility accounts are recorded under the exact name given when the account was created, so a decade-old gas company misspelling has to be searched as that exact typo, along with maiden, hyphenated, and other spelling variations [OFnbGkVuoHQ @ 00:04:13]. Pennsylvania's results grid hides its own trap: Michael Curtis shows that green rows mean the claimant must contact the office to determine eligibility, white rows are single-owner records that can be claimed directly, and gray rows mark joint ownership [0YK26E_fzYA @ 00:03:14]. On the fraud side, WTHR's Indiana segment warns to make sure the search runs on the right official website and nothing else, because the government will not charge a fee to recover your own money [pPow2SwrxBE @ 00:01:33]. Как се прави lists the red flags in detail: anyone demanding an upfront fee, an administrative charge, or a made-up clearance tax; unsolicited text messages or cold calls asking for a full Social Security number or bank passwords; and pressure claiming the money is about to be permanently forfeited to the government [9BVp_yhswZY @ 00:04:47]. Every one of these warnings collapses into the same discipline: official portal only, the exact name on the old account, and no money paid up front.
