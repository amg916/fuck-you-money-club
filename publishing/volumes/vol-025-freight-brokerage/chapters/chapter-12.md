# Chapter 12: Cargo Liability, Insurance and Claims

*Carmack Amendment and why it doesn't apply to brokers, carrier limits of liability, cargo vs. contingent cargo coverage and its gaps, contingent auto pricing, policy evidence requirements, and step-by-step claim filing*

## Executive Summary

Freight brokerage practitioners agree on the liability map. Under the Carmack Amendment the motor carrier is automatically liable. Brokers sit outside Carmack and are governed by contract. A broker's contingent cargo policy only ever matches what the carrier already carries, and Freight 360 reports one contingent payout, $17,000, in 45 years at Peerless Road Warrior Logistics. The street mechanics are documentary: a written claim with a dollar figure and commercial invoice, the 30-day acknowledgment and 120-day disposition clocks, agent-emailed confirmation that excluded commodities are covered, and CIF-plus-10% valuation on international cargo. Carrier liability is a floor, and Cargolinked shows a $300,000 container returning about $22,000. The disagreements are over three questions: whether contingent is 'fake insurance' or a product that has come a long way, how long claimants really have to file, and whether shippers must prove carrier negligence.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every liability conversation starts with the Carmack Amendment, and the practitioners line up hard on who it hits. Transportation attorney Cassandra Gaines calls it the 'big fat cat' because it sits on top of state law and leaves motor carriers almost no wiggle room. The carrier is automatically liable, and the claimant only has to show three things: the goods were tendered in good condition on an un-notated bill of lading, they were delivered damaged or lost, and a commercial invoice backs the value [Ig6sI3O1a14 @ 00:11:37]. Brokers sit outside that box. Gaines states flatly that Carmack applies only to motor carriers and freight forwarders. For a broker, the governing law is the contract. If there is no paper, it is the oral agreement to arrange transportation. That is the line to throw back at an angry customer who insists the broker owns the claim [NyOX8UjzDAo @ 00:00:44]. TLI tells shippers the same thing from the 3PL seat. The motor carrier, not the broker, is the liable party, so the play is to maximize value with that carrier, file against it, and pass 100% of the settlement back to the shipper [fxzUlwnz1mE @ 00:00:52]. Cerasis frames the shipper's burden in three matching elements, citing Title 49 U.S. Code 14706: good-condition tender, damaged or unreasonably late delivery, and the dollar amount of the damages [mJw3vbSaJbw @ 00:03:54].

The second point of agreement is that contingent cargo is not primary coverage, whatever a customer's insurance requirement implies. Nate Cross of Freight 360 defines it as a secondary policy that depends on the carrier's primary cargo insurance already being in place. It is built as a safety net for the cases where that primary policy denies a claim or refuses to pay, and customers need to be told it is not a primary cargo policy [kAT7s21mZIE @ 00:01:33]. In the shorter Freight 360 clip the mechanics get blunter. The contingent policy only ever matches what the selected motor carrier already has. Take a $200,000 electronics load on a carrier with $100,000 in coverage and an electronics exclusion: it gets nothing from a broker's $250,000 contingent policy, even though that is exactly the scenario shippers think they are buying protection for [FtL-2SOSFOU @ 00:00:45]. The hosts name only rare triggers. One is a carrier policy lapsing mid-transit without anyone knowing. The other is the carrier's insurer folding or simply failing to respond. That is why they call contingent 'fake insurance' and a 'feel-good' for shipper management [FtL-2SOSFOU @ 00:02:14]. TLI lands in the same place. It describes contingent as cover for extreme outlier events, such as a carrier going bankrupt in the middle of a freight claim, and notes that not all commodities are covered [fxzUlwnz1mE @ 00:01:26].

Third, every practitioner who touches carrier liability treats it as a floor rather than real cover. Trade Risk Guaranty says carrier reimbursement generally covers only a fraction of the shipper's loss. Its prescription: the only way to ensure reimbursement for full value is to buy cargo insurance, which covers loss, damage, or theft between origin and final destination even when the carrier is at fault [pbs3U-vmpzM @ 00:03:18]. Cargolinked puts a number on the gap. A $300,000 container that goes overboard returns roughly $22,000 under the usual sea rules, about 7%, because carrier liability is capped by weight or package and never by value, and carriers still keep defenses like perils of the sea and poor packing [rbmdGEpBKCI @ 00:00:01]. The fourth shared baseline is that the paper a broker vets does not tell the whole story. Freight 360 points out that a certificate of insurance shows policy limits and coverage dates but does not always show the exclusions, which creates risk for both broker and customer. Freight 360 also says a cheap policy often has a ton of gaps [kAT7s21mZIE @ 00:03:00]. Jared Palmer of Epic Insurance Brokers is just as blunt. Good insurance costs money, cheap insurance and the best coverage are competitors, and a $50,000 premium that should have been $150,000 will not respond the way the buyer expects when the loss hits [xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:17:34].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Claim filing is where brokers get taken advantage of. Gaines lays out the minimum filing requirements from the federal regulations she tells operators to look up at 49 CFR Part 370. First, the claim must be a formal written communication to the motor carrier. Notating the bill of lading does not count, and neither does phoning in that a couple of pallets are missing. Second, it must state a specific dollar amount, normally backed by a commercial invoice, because retail value is off the table under Carmack. Third, it must name the carrier as the liable party [Ig6sI3O1a14 @ 00:06:19]. Brent of Anderson Trucking Service walks through the broker's side of the workflow. The broker logs the claim and forwards it to the claims department. The department requests the original BOL, the original paid freight bill, the original or a certified invoice, and any photo, video, or other proof. That package goes to the customer and the carrier, the carrier submits it to its insurer, and the agent facilitates communication from there [id2FXFnynHM @ 00:00:43]. Quote Factory adds the hard edges. The claim covers cost incurred, not profit, measured as original value minus damaged value. Concealed damage and shortages must be reported within five days. Damaged freight should be retained rather than refused, and the freight bill gets paid regardless of damage, loss, or theft [Ti9MeFBKijM @ 00:05:41].

On pre-load verification, Freight 360 runs the most detailed playbook. A certificate of insurance is usually one or two pages, while the actual policy contract is most likely about 60 pages. When a carrier says 'yeah, it covers it' on a load of eggs, the broker is just taking their word for it [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:23:12]. The fix is to pull the certificate from an authorized third party such as Highway, AssureAssist, or RMIS rather than from the carrier or dispatcher. Then have the carrier's insurance agent email the certificate, with the carrier on copy, and confirm in writing that the commodity is covered in the event of a claim. This matters most for the usual exclusions: eggs, frozen seafood, firearms, electronics, and alcohol [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:27:38]. Ben of Freight 360 warns that adding reefer breakdown can bring 10 to 20 more exclusions, so the carrier can end up with less coverage. The suggested workaround is to feed the policy to an LLM, have it find and summarize the exclusions, and use that list to ask the agent sharper questions [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:25:02]. Large brokerages also ask carriers on a recorded line whether anything on the vehicle needs service, because maintenance fine print can void cargo coverage. That might be an oil light the driver did not get to one of five approved mechanics. The hosts concede the recorded question still does not hold up anyway [FtL-2SOSFOU @ 00:04:23].

The numbers side runs through valuation and premium math. Logistics YouTuber IINO san lays out the international formula. The insured amount is usually 110% of the CIF price on the invoice. The premium is that insured amount times a rate of roughly 0.3 to 0.5%, with a minimum of about US$30 per case and extra war and SRCC charges on some routes. In the worked example, a $10,000 machine plus $500 in Thai inland and clearance costs plus $1,000 in ocean freight, times 110%, gives a $12,650 insured amount and a $37.95 premium at 0.3% [xxk_vmQarVo @ 00:04:12]. Cargolinked agrees on CIF plus 10% and warns that insuring for less cuts claims in proportion. It also notes that only Institute Cargo Clause A covers theft, non-delivery, handling damage, and breakage [rbmdGEpBKCI @ 00:01:07]. Domestically, Quote Factory quotes carrier coverage of about $25 per pound for new goods and 10 cents per pound for used goods, because nothing proves a used item worked when it was loaded [Ti9MeFBKijM @ 00:01:44]. TLI sells the gap-fill inside its platform. On a $300,000 dedicated truckload where the carrier tops out at $100,000, the shipper buys the extra $200,000 of cargo liability in Viewpoint TMS while generating the bill of lading [fxzUlwnz1mE @ 00:01:58].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Verify coverage before the truck is booked
   - Pull the certificate from an authorized third party and get written agent confirmation for commonly excluded or high-value commodities.
   - Source the certificate through Highway, AssureAssist, or RMIS rather than the carrier or dispatcher, then have the carrier's insurance agent confirm by email that the specific commodity is not excluded [Freight 360 — qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:27:38]
2. Lock down the delivery evidence
   - Have the consignee inspect every item, note damage or shortage on the delivery receipt, photograph it, and keep the damaged freight.
   - Consignees should not be rushed by drivers, should open crates to check for concealed damage, should never refuse the shipment, and should report concealed damage within five days [Quote Factory — Ti9MeFBKijM @ 00:04:20]
3. File a compliant written claim
   - Send a formal written claim to the motor carrier with a specific dollar amount, a commercial invoice, and the carrier named as liable.
   - A notated bill of lading or a phone call does not meet the filing requirements, and retail value cannot be claimed under Carmack. Then hold the carrier to its 30-day acknowledgment and 120-day disposition deadlines [LOGISTICS RISK EXPERT by Cassandra Gaines — Ig6sI3O1a14 @ 00:06:19]
4. Move inside 48 hours on theft
   - Hand CargoNet, law enforcement, and the insurer the driver identity, timeline, and location data immediately.
   - Palmer's instruction is to be a good victim: report properly and timely within about 24 to 48 hours, because calling three months later with no driver, time, or location drops recovery odds to roughly zero [CargoRex — xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:13:43]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split runs straight through Freight 360's own catalog. In the explainer, Nate Cross pitches contingent cargo as a huge recommendation for brokers, a secondary safety net that kicks in when a carrier's policy is cancelled, runs short on limits, or excludes a commodity like seafood [kAT7s21mZIE @ 00:03:16]. On Episode 342, the same channel repeats an insurance agent calling it fake insurance, and Nate drops the receipts: Peerless Road Warrior Logistics, 45 years in business, one contingent payout totaling $17,000 [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:14:27]. The newer position is blunt: the contingent form only ever matches what the motor carrier already holds, so a $200,000 electronics load on a carrier with $100,000 and an electronics exclusion gets nothing from the broker's policy. Nate also calls the customer demand a feel-good requirement, usually pushed by an attorney or upper management. Jared Palmer of Epic Insurance Brokers splits the difference. Palmer admits the old mystique was that some forms were not worth the paper they were written on, but says the product has come a long way and good contingent programs and forms now exist [xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:16:43]. TLI frames it narrowly as cover for extreme outlier events, such as a carrier going bankrupt while a claim is in process [fxzUlwnz1mE @ 00:01:26].

On who actually owes the shipper, Cassandra Gaines, a transportation attorney, gives brokers a clean script: the Carmack Amendment is a federal statute that applies only to motor carriers, so when a customer insists the broker is liable, dispute it on the spot [NyOX8UjzDAo @ 00:01:05]. For the broker, the contracts between the parties govern, and with no paper at all it is an oral agreement to arrange transportation. B3FinalMile shows the underwriting side of the same coin: contingent auto is priced on the assumption that a sued broker will argue federal preemption and non-liability, and once a brokerage leases assets and holds itself out as a carrier in a contract, that defense gets much harder and the underwriter would have declined or re-priced the account [pRXH47gm66A @ 00:00:57]. Freight 360 reports the street reality cutting the other way. The broker is not legally responsible to pay out, yet many brokers make the customer whole to save the relationship, often paying cash because money almost never comes back from the carrier [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:21:21]. Some brokerages, once they scale, self-insure theft and double-brokering second payments out of pocket rather than pay an insurer to cover it [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:48:44].

The claims math itself is contested. Gaines teaches strict liability: the shipper or broker proves good-condition tender, damaged or lost delivery, and a commercial invoice, and the motor carrier must then clear one of five defenses, for example with ECM data from the truck [Ig6sI3O1a14 @ 00:11:37]. Trade Risk Guaranty tells shippers the opposite burden, that they must prove the damage was the carrier's fault, a process that can take months or years [pbs3U-vmpzM @ 00:01:06]. The nine-month window splits the same way. Cerasis tells shippers flatly that they have nine months to file, while Gaines calls that a common misconception: courts require the limit to appear in the bill of lading or contract, so a claim branded too old should be checked against the paper before anyone walks away [Ig6sI3O1a14 @ 00:07:42]. On valuation, Gaines bars retail value under Carmack and Quote Factory says the claim covers cost, not profit, while Kingsley of Cargo Rant tells ocean claimants to price arrived sound market value minus arrived damaged value and include potential loss of profit [NxIispBOkz8 @ 00:03:49]. Quote Factory also splits carrier coverage by condition, typically twenty-five dollars per pound on new goods against ten cents per pound on used goods, because used freight may have been broken before pickup.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Freight 360's stolen-load case is the clearest economics check on record. A broker moving a high-value, commonly excluded commodity bought a third-party all-risk policy that happened to include theft, the load was stolen, and the deductible was $25,000 [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:32:11]. The broker reached for the contingent policy to offset it, only to hit language requiring the broker to use its best endeavors to obtain proof of the carrier's cargo coverage, a subjective phrase the insurer can use to argue the broker never vetted the carrier because it bought outside cover [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:33:52]. Nate's correction: contingent covers theft only when the carrier's own policy also covers theft. Denials on the carrier side are just as technical. Ben from Freight 360 describes a reefer claim voided because an oil light came on and the driver did not reach one of five approved mechanics, and notes that recorded-line questions about vehicle service still do not hold up [FtL-2SOSFOU @ 00:04:07]. Adding reefer breakdown can bolt on 10 to 20 more exclusions, and MyFullCoverage warns that spoilage claims without temperature records get denied [x_fzUHiNELw @ 00:01:10]. The certificate itself hides the trap: a one- or two-page COI fronts a policy that probably runs around 60 pages.

Cosmetic coverage is a recurring failure. When one Freight 360 customer demanded primary cargo or auto liability from a broker, the agent offered to list the owner's personal pickup truck on a primary policy that met the certificate and covered nothing the shipper actually needed [qTcDOmwZMjI @ 00:52:07]. A fully remote brokerage told to carry workers comp got the same treatment: real coverage was quoted around $25,000, so the agent charged $1,000 for an add-on whose binding language excludes everything. Palmer flags the internal version: a compliance policy you cannot actually execute becomes evidence against you, worse than having none [xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:06:28]. Overbuying limits backfires too. Palmer carried a $50 million umbrella at AFN after a truck accident case and dropped it after about a year, because plaintiffs' attorneys see big limits as 50 million reasons never to settle [xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:22:41]. On the ocean side, Cargolinked puts the cap in dollars: a $300,000 container lost overboard pays about $22,000, roughly 7 percent, and general average can demand 10 to 50 percent of CIF value as security with the final figure taking 2 to 5 years [rbmdGEpBKCI @ 00:02:04]. Insuring below CIF plus 10 percent gets claims cut in proportion.

Timing kills claims. Palmer, working alongside CargoNet, says victims who call three months later with no driver, no theft date and no location have near-zero recovery odds; the window is 24 to 48 hours before freight heads toward the port [xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:13:43]. The same practitioner lost Super Bowl TVs in 2006 after posting them on load boards, and now points out covert trackers that cost $750 then run about $15 [xQYjaYAvyms @ 00:26:34]. Palmer also ties premiums to loss frequency and severity, citing truckers whose rates jumped 51 percent or went from $13,000 to $28,000 per truck. Gaines warns that notating the bill of lading or phoning in missing pallets is not a claim; it takes formal written communication with a specified dollar amount [Ig6sI3O1a14 @ 00:06:19]. Quote Factory lists consignee errors that sink payouts: refusing or discarding damaged freight, missing the five-day window for concealed damage, and withholding the freight bill, which must be paid regardless [Ti9MeFBKijM @ 00:05:55]. Kingsley adds that calling in a recovery agent only after the shipping line rejects the claim can jeopardize it beyond repair. Freight 360's downstream cost is relationship damage: denied claims lose customers who then sit on unpaid invoices.
