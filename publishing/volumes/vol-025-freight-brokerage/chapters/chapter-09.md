# Chapter 09: Equipment Niches: Van, Reefer, Flatbed, Heavy Haul and Drayage

*Reefer basics for new brokers, booking differences by trailer type, oversize/heavy-haul pay, container drayage from port pickup to empty return, intermodal lane bidding, and load-board search walkthroughs*

## Executive Summary

Operators working van, reefer, flatbed, heavy haul and drayage agree that dry van is the weakest seat and that margin sits in equipment and paperwork most operators avoid. Chief Carriers reports a 15-truck heavy-haul carrier near $8 million in revenue, almost double a regular flatbed operation. Reefer and flatbed are priced like van but booked off tighter checklists covering commodity, temperature, dimensions and securement. Load-board operators grade posts on true rate over all miles, deadhead and broker pay history, with Cole Fairbrook calling $2.35 a mile a loss. Drayage runs on SCAC, UIIA, TWIC, last free dates and empty returns, and one live intermodal bid landed at $1,450 on 600 round-trip miles plus accessorials. Disagreements cover what a SCAC costs and whether brokers strictly need one, LoadMatch's value, and whether to bid high or anchor to recent runs.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Across the van, reefer, flatbed and specialized desks, the practitioners converge on one point: plain dry van is the weakest place to stand, and margin lives in equipment that fewer operators can run. Chief Carriers opens with 'the riches are in the niches' and reports oversized and heavy haul thriving while van, reefer and flatbed sit flat, with infrastructure, manufacturing, agriculture and construction driving demand [7kg1eMOTev8 @ 00:00:13]. Few equipped carriers translate into pricing power, and the work is often project-based one-offs with no standard price. AFT Dispatch puts numbers on the van weakness: van rejection ran just over 3% amid overcapacity, the lowest performer since May 2023, while flatbed averaged over 10% rejection in 2023 and hit 20% three times, and reefer ran 6% with holiday peaks [Gx_gladG4ps @ 00:06:00]. Bobby Loads' caller, running three trucks split across flatbed, reefer and van, says the move if starting over would be to avoid dry van entirely [jxL6kaRxoEo @ 00:00:00]. The same view surfaces on the load-board side: Cole Fairbrook quotes DAT per-mile averages without fuel surcharge at flatbed 279, reefer 264 and dry van 228, so van sits at the bottom of the national rate stack before a single phone call is made [bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:02:24].

The second consensus point is that each trailer type is sold with the same pricing logic but booked with a different checklist. Brandon Scott, who started in reefer with no experience, says reefer is priced the same way as van or flatbed, reefer fuel does not need to be factored precisely, and the marketplace 'will correct your price' once the number goes out [xPxwiwivMXE @ 00:03:33]. No dedicated reefer network is required; good-paying freight posted on a load board draws carriers, though scarce lanes may get no calls, and the driver sets and maintains the unit while the broker only supplies the temperature [xPxwiwivMXE @ 00:05:05]. Never Stop Trucking turns that into a booking discipline. On flatbed, confirm trailer length, spread-axle weight distribution, length, width, height and state-by-state overhang rules, with roughly 13 feet 5 inches to 13 feet 6 inches as the ceiling including deck height, plus straps, chains and tarps [K5sqFMIL3IQ @ 00:00:45]. On reefer, confirm the exact commodity, because some drivers refuse meat, fish or watermelons, along with temperature, pre-cool, continuous mode, air chute and e-tracks, and expect long dwell, overnight appointments and multi-stop runs in exchange for higher pay [K5sqFMIL3IQ @ 00:06:20]. Dry van still needs weight, pallet count and packaging confirmed, with straps on board [K5sqFMIL3IQ @ 00:10:41].

Drayage is where every speaker agrees the paperwork is the moat. FreightBrokers defines it as moving 10, 20 or 40-foot containers to or from a port or rail terminal and lists the baseline: a SCAC code from nmfta.org for both brokers and carriers at about $80, the UIIA agreement for carriers but not brokers, a TWIC card for port drivers, and fees on any delay in pickup, delivery or container return that truckload never charges [N1uEW-kxNvE @ 00:02:16]. Freightskills confirms brokers technically need no license to contract a dray carrier, still recommends the four-letter NMFTA SCAC at about $70, and stresses that UIIA is port-specific with no reciprocity, so a carrier approved for the California ports might not be able to go to Chicago, and New Jersey works the same way [o6WTbGsyLqg @ 00:10:07]. CONGO EXPRESS gives the carrier-side build order: authority, UIIA-friendly insurance, SCAC, UIIA, then LoadMatch [vSgjLSdmTKc @ 00:07:31]. Drayage Specialist frames why the niche matters: dray is the short-distance link between ships or rail and warehouses, it prevents port bottlenecks and supports just-in-time delivery, and specialist carriers who know port operations and permits are the ones who get it done [sv8u2e9X6Oc @ 00:01:16]. Freightskills adds that every truckload customer has drayage at some point, which makes it an upsell on existing accounts [o6WTbGsyLqg @ 00:03:09].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the load boards, the mechanics are arithmetic plus speed. Real Trucking Money runs both DAT and Truckstop because good loads book in minutes, searches a 100-mile radius by equipment, and grades every post on three numbers: true rate, meaning total pay over all miles including deadhead, the deadhead itself, and broker pay history, where 3 days at $2.80 beats 45 days at $3.10 [Xi2gMYEX18k @ 00:04:39]. Dispatch Desk USA shows the math: $1,600 for 400 loaded miles is $4 a mile, but adding 100 deadhead miles drops it to $3.20 per all miles, which then gets compared against a cost-plus-profit floor [w4fpPCLZG_I @ 00:04:29]. Cole Fairbrook sets the floor bluntly: $2.35 a mile is 'pretty much a loss,' a truck 'really got to be over the 250 mark,' 263 is barely above break-even, and a Tampa load at 1.88 means 'paying the broker' [bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:20:05]. Fairbrook filters Truckstop at a $3 minimum and a 250 to 500-mile length of haul, prices deadhead into the counter, and uses the paid Load Hunter Chrome extension, whose RPM Plus folds deadhead in and turned a 394 post into 376 [bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:13:40]. Dispatch Desk USA adds that DAT wins on van and reefer volume while Truckstop is stronger in flatbed and open deck, so the trailer type decides which board gets checked first [w4fpPCLZG_I @ 00:01:31].

Dispatching & Intermodal Training bid a Houston port to Wylie, Texas lane live, and the sequence is replicable. Mileage comes from Google Maps by zip code, with a check on whether the carrier has an EZ tag for tolls; the 299-mile estimate rounds to 300, and because dray miles are doubled for the round trip, the quote is built on 600 miles with no backhaul needed [SvfUq9Udy60 @ 00:03:31]. No target rate came with the request, so new dispatchers should ask the broker for one. Market sat near $2.80 a mile, carriers want about $3, which is 'hard to get,' and the stated rule is to bid high because the broker will negotiate down. Yet 600 times 2.80 produced $1,680 linehaul, which the speaker judged too high and likely to be overlooked [SvfUq9Udy60 @ 00:05:40]. The final bid was $1,450, anchored to a nearby Grapevine run at about $1,400 less than two weeks earlier [SvfUq9Udy60 @ 00:06:30]. Accessorials then go on top: chassis at about $25 a day doubled to $50, storage at truck-parking rate times two for $50, an overweight fee even on a declared-legal load, $125 pre-pull, and $60 an hour detention after 2 hours [SvfUq9Udy60 @ 00:08:00]. Freightskills explains why those lines matter: carriers borrow the chassis and box from the steamship line and port, carry the fee liability, and pass it to the broker [o6WTbGsyLqg @ 00:08:43].

For import boxes, Drayage Academy lays out a 12-step workflow that starts with the delivery order, covering number, container, steamship line, weight, size, type, terminal and delivery location, then holds, fees and gate PIN, then confirming that customs, the steamship line and the terminal have all released the box [fhPReUtDXkI @ 00:01:54]. The last free date is the hinge, because the carrier is liable if it misses an agreed pickup: 'So don't miss your LFD' [fhPReUtDXkI @ 00:04:54]. The back half covers chassis inspection and the EIR, container and seal verification, the out-gate EIR, logged appointment times for detention, the signed delivery order, the empty-return location from the steamship line, and the in-gate EIR proving the empty was accepted [fhPReUtDXkI @ 00:08:18]. On exports, Victor Mason reads the booking as 'a reservation of space' on a specific vessel, identifies lines by prefix, CMAU for CMA, 038BM for MSC and ONEYU for ONE, and tracks ERD as the opening of the receiving window, cargo cutoff as the last receiving day, and doc cutoff as the customs deadline [o2G4zRoNHt4 @ 00:12:10]. Mason's prospecting method is to drive a 3 to 5-mile radius around the port listing businesses with containers parked outside, while Freightskills uses Import Yeti as a free lead source that likely needs no account [o2G4zRoNHt4 @ 00:33:03] [o6WTbGsyLqg @ 00:05:11].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull the free market read
   - Check the weekly national report and state-level loads in versus loads out before choosing lanes.
   - Use dat.com, Resources, Blogs for the free weekly report and the Dean Croke video, then read the DAT state dashboard, where Alabama showed 392 loads in against 2,063 out and Texas had fewer than half of 3,500 deliveries reloading [Cole Fairbrook — bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:00:49].
2. Screen both boards every morning
   - Spend 15 minutes on DAT and 15 on Truckstop with tight filters on radius, equipment and pickup date.
   - A good load is posted within the hour, above the lane average, with a reasonable window and short deadhead; a $1.80 post on a $2.50-average lane or a post sitting 6 hours unbooked is a red flag [Real Trucking Money — Xi2gMYEX18k @ 00:03:09].
3. Price on all miles and verify the broker
   - Convert every offer to rate per all miles, check pay history and authority, and lock the rate in writing.
   - Verify MC authority on FMCSA SAFER even for Book It Now loads, get the rate confirmation in writing, and treat rates far above market, vague postings and bad factorability as red flags [Dispatch Desk USA — w4fpPCLZG_I @ 00:05:31].
4. Quote and run the dray move
   - Build the round-trip linehaul, stack accessorials, then manage LFD, EIRs and the empty return.
   - Anchor linehaul to recent nearby runs, as with the $1,450 Wylie bid [Dispatching & Intermodal Training — SvfUq9Udy60 @ 00:06:30], then confirm the LFD and get the empty-return location from the steamship line, because the empty does not always go back to the terminal where it was picked up [Drayage Academy — fhPReUtDXkI @ 00:08:18].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Desk-Level Edges From The Load Boards And The Port**
> 
> Small moves the practitioners use to find, price and pitch niche freight faster than the next desk.
> - Call the newest postings first, and read the posting comments, which hide costs such as tarps, lumpers and appointments [Dispatch Desk USA — w4fpPCLZG_I @ 00:03:35].
> - Let the broker quote first, ask for $3.10, then go quiet: 'Silence is your friend on the phone,' and sometimes 10% is sitting there that was not offered up front [Real Trucking Money — Xi2gMYEX18k @ 00:06:28].
> - 53-foot trailers are scarce, so brokers raise rates to cover them, while 48-foot trailers are more versatile; on short hauls cut the deadhead filter to 75 miles [Cole Fairbrook — bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:28:15].
> - On drayage sales calls, say you have 'capacity,' never 'empties' [Victor Mason — o2G4zRoNHt4 @ 01:14:06].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over whether dry van is worth running at all. Bobby Loads, fielding a caller with three trucks spread across flatbed, reefer and van, says that if starting over they would stay away from dry van [jxL6kaRxoEo @ 00:00:00]. Yet the same channel reports its own van averaging 240 a mile, up from 210, and credits non-domiciled drivers leaving the market for the bump [jxL6kaRxoEo @ 00:04:54]. AFT Dispatch frames van as the weakest segment on paper: tender rejection just over 3%, overcapacity, and the lowest performer since May 2023 [Gx_gladG4ps @ 00:06:00]. AFT then shows a van booking for Reggie at 545 a mile on short runs and 402 on the week, so a sharp desk can still beat a soft segment, a point Thomas sums up as 'all an averages game.' Brandon Scott pushes back on the idea that reefer is a separate science: reefer gets priced exactly the way van or flatbed does, reefer fuel does not need precise math, and the broker should take a number to the marketplace, which 'will correct your price' [xPxwiwivMXE @ 00:03:33]. For an operator, segment averages set the backdrop, but lane selection and booking discipline decide whether a given equipment type pays.

How much a broker must know about the reefer box itself is where Brandon Scott and Never Stop Trucking split hardest. Brandon Scott, whose first freight was reefer with no prior experience, says no dedicated reefer network is needed: post good-paying freight on a load board, and the driver sets and maintains the unit while the broker only supplies the temperature [xPxwiwivMXE @ 00:05:05]. Ennis at Never Stop Trucking runs a far heavier checklist: confirm the exact commodity because some drivers refuse meat, fish or watermelons, then confirm temperature, pre-cool, continuous run, air chute and e-tracks, and expect long dwell times, overnight appointments and multi-stop runs in exchange for higher pay [K5sqFMIL3IQ @ 00:06:20]. Ennis also warns that temperature trackers can produce a rejected load and an insurance claim. On damage, Brandon Scott argues reefer carries no special curse: most damage comes from forklifts during loading and unloading, a shipper may simply write off damaged boxes, and risk follows the commodity, such as potatoes, rather than the trailer type [xPxwiwivMXE @ 00:02:01]. Bobby Loads adds a seasonal twist that blurs the line entirely: Christmas-tree freight that ships dry flips to reefer once freezes hit, so the equipment a lane demands can change in the middle of a season [jxL6kaRxoEo @ 00:02:04].

Drayage practitioners split on paperwork, carrier sourcing and pricing posture. FreightBrokers tells brokers and carriers alike to get a SCAC code through nmfta.org for about an $80 fee [N1uEW-kxNvE @ 00:02:16], while Freightskills states that brokers technically need no license to contract a drayage carrier, yet still recommends the four-letter NMFTA SCAC at about $70 [o6WTbGsyLqg @ 00:07:47]. On sourcing, CONGO EXPRESS calls loadmatch.com 'the best kept secret,' reports 200 to 300 broker emails after posting truck capacity, and pays about $600 a year or about $150 per three months [vSgjLSdmTKc @ 00:00:30]. Freightskills, claiming no affiliation, calls the same site dated, noting carrier names are free while contacts sit behind a paid tier. Pricing posture diverges too. Dispatching & Intermodal Training says carriers want about $3 a mile, which is 'hard to get,' and advises bidding high because the broker will negotiate down. Yet on a Houston-port-to-Wylie lane, the speaker pulled 299 miles from Google Maps by zip code, rounded to 300, doubled it to 600 for the round trip, priced it at $2.80 for $1,680, judged that too high for the broker to take seriously, and bid $1,450 off a nearby Grapevine run near $1,400 [SvfUq9Udy60 @ 00:06:30].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Load-board traps hit operators who read posted numbers at face value. Cole Fairbrook warns that brokers double- and triple-post loads, inflating apparent volume [bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:03:05], spots a $15,000 post for 472 miles that is probably a typo for $1,500, and sets hard floors: $2.35 a mile is 'pretty much a loss,' the target has to be over the 250 mark, and Tampa at 1.88 means 'paying the broker' [bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:20:05]. Real Trucking Money flags a $1.80 post on a $2.50-average lane as a cheap-broker signal, treats $1,200 for 700 miles as under $2 a mile, and says a post sitting six hours unbooked should raise questions [Xi2gMYEX18k @ 00:02:31]. The same channel ranks pay history over headline rate: three days to pay at $2.80 beats 45 days at $3.10, because 'cash flow keeps your truck moving.' Dispatch Desk USA warns that posting comments hide tarps, lumpers and appointments, that 'a posting you cannot run is not a load,' and that even Book It Now loads need an MC authority check on FMCSA SAFER first, plus a written rate confirmation and a hard rule to never pay anyone to book a load [w4fpPCLZG_I @ 00:03:35].

Drayage failures are measured in fees that do not exist in truckload. FreightBrokers lists delays at pickup, delivery or container return as the core cost difference from truckload freight [N1uEW-kxNvE @ 00:05:48]. Victor Mason describes a Los Angeles carrier ingating a container on the wrong booking; a dray out runs $500 to $4,000 and up to about $5,000, and because that box sat on top it cost about $700, which Mason deducted from the carrier's invoice [o2G4zRoNHt4 @ 00:07:39]. On CMA reefer boxes in NY/NJ, Mason quotes 3 free days, then $690 a day, then $895, then $1,040, reaching about $13,000 over 14 days, and recalls a customer paying about $30k a month in demurrage who shut that division down. Keisha at Drayage Academy opens with the three classic losses, false port trips, unpaid demurrage and returns to the wrong location, then hammers two rules: 'don't miss your LFD,' since the carrier is liable for a missed agreed pickup, and 'do not assume the empty container goes back to the same terminal where you picked it up' [fhPReUtDXkI @ 00:08:18]. Freightskills adds that holding a UIIA 'is not the only part,' and brokers who skip due diligence put themselves in danger [o6WTbGsyLqg @ 00:06:13].

Lane and equipment traps punish operators who chase headline rates. Cole Fairbrook's West Coast check found Ogden to Seattle posted at $2,000 against a $2,400 average with only seven loads, and a Portland-to-Denver round trip near $8,500 whose return option was a single load from an unapproved broker [bEWh0t-c_5o @ 00:42:32]; the same speaker warns that van freight hides catches like round trips, 17 drops and 48-hour drop trailers. Bobby Loads avoids California unless the load is something like 3,000 miles for $11K, calling it saturated with undercutting, and notes Southeast flatbed rates stay low because brokers price in the Florida-to-Georgia deadhead [jxL6kaRxoEo @ 00:03:43]. On open deck, Ennis at Never Stop Trucking warns that flatbed loads need frequent securement checks because falling cargo 'can kill someone' [K5sqFMIL3IQ @ 00:10:23]. Andrew Winkler at Chief Carriers lists the heavy-haul bottlenecks: pilot cars, routing, limited operating hours and no moving after dark, and relays that the hardest move the featured owner faced was not a heavy piece but a delicate fiberglass or carbon-fiber load on a stretch trailer [7kg1eMOTev8 @ 00:04:02]. That owner reached 15 trucks and about $8 million in revenue, which Winkler calls almost double what a regular flatbed carrier does.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Gates Before Any Equipment-Niche Load Moves**
