## Executive Summary

Brokers price from the truck up. They collect every load detail, pull DAT and FreightWaves lane data from several sources, fix an expected buy rate, and only then add margin. ShipLeague quotes about $1,850 on a lane where the truck should take about $1,700 and calls 15% standard. FreightBrokers works four numbers ($1,300 buy, $250 target, $1,550 sell, $1,350 ceiling) and books the truck at $1,325. Every money-side speaker separates gross from net and profit from cash: a $300 gross becomes $225 net, and 30-to-90-day terms force a choice. Quick pay costs 1-5% and covers one broker's loads. Factoring costs roughly 3-5% and advances 80-90% inside 24 hours. Practitioners split on quick pay versus factoring, on which AscendTMS perks are free, and on whether factoring rescues a thin operation or just helps it lose money faster.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every pricing practitioner works backward from the truck. FreightBrokers refuses to touch a number until the pickup and delivery details, appointment times, commodity, weight, equipment, driver assist, extra stops and special instructions are collected, because a firm 6:00 a.m. delivery prices differently than "anytime Friday." The carrier figure (the truck or buy rate) then gets built from lane data, load boards, past company pay and direct carrier calls, never from one source alone [HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:00:56]. ShipLeague runs the same play with DAT RateView and FreightWaves. They set granularity to "Market" 99% of the time and fix an expected truck pay before a single dollar of margin goes on top [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:01:22]. Tony's Topics treats DAT's Quick Rate Lookup as the reality check, pulling all-in spot rates by equipment type and holding every offer against the market [wOpAqd5AALo @ 00:02:58]. The second FreightBrokers lesson frames the output: research the lane, the market and the carriers, then quote. The result is two separate agreements, one with the shipper and one with the carrier [Lpq9AHrz7nQ @ 00:01:55]. The order of operations never flips. Shipper price equals buy rate plus a deliberate gross profit, and the broker's spread lives between two contracts that are negotiated separately and paid on separate clocks.

Gross margin is not take-home pay. FreightBrokers' worked load has the shipper paying $2,100 and the carrier receiving $1,800. That $300 gross margin is "not necessarily what you actually keep." After roughly $75 of load board, TMS, insurance, bond, phone, accounting and factoring expenses, net lands at $225. The same lesson warns that invoiced is not paid: owe ten carriers and $18,000 leaves the building before the first shipper check clears, because "you can have a load that's profitable on paper and still have a cash flow problem" [Lpq9AHrz7nQ @ 00:05:20]. BLACK BEAR FREIGHT & LOGISTICS makes the identical point from the carrier seat. Brokers and shippers pay in 30, 45 or 60 days, while fuel, insurance, drivers, truck payments and repairs do not wait. Profit measures sustainability; cash flow measures the ability to pay bills [ZYkg1UepLL4 @ 00:01:47]. Porter Capital puts standard terms at 30-60 days [iJE2rZFL57E @ 00:00:00]. Bumble Bee Dispatch stretches that to 30-90 days and notes that startup costs drain cash before the first payment ever lands [dTOcCujNdaw @ 00:00:50]. The shared answer is to fund the float through working capital, a credit line, quick pay or factoring, and to price every load knowing none of those options is free money.

Paperwork is the trigger that turns a delivered load into money. FreightBrokers ties both invoices to the proof of delivery. The POD releases the $1,800 carrier invoice and the $2,100 shipper invoice, and any detention, lumper or extra-stop charge needs its own documentation to get paid [Lpq9AHrz7nQ @ 00:02:48]. In FreightWaves' Rose Rocket demo, Rob Dougherty automates that hinge: same-day invoicing fires the moment the POD is uploaded and syncs straight to accounting [MobEZyaIjV4 @ 00:04:37]. AscendTMS teaches the same discipline from inside the software. Supporting documents must be uploaded, typed and attached or they sit "unprocessed." Factoring runs through partner companies with an NOA stamp, and a load paid in full earns the "green rectangle of victory" [ICIyOtMiEqY @ 00:24:58]. Steve's longer Ascend training adds that the ready-for-accounting tab is a staging area for collecting paperwork, and that adding an income pay item "is what trips ascend tms to generate an invoice document" [idL8PXMlKjQ @ 00:45:31]. Across a vendor demo, a free TMS and a broker training channel, the operating baseline is identical. No POD means no invoice. No attached document means no payment. The back office runs off load status and logged documents rather than anyone's memory of what happened on the road.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

ShipLeague's worked lane is a van from Atlanta, GA to Vineland, NJ, 766 miles. June ran a $2,183 high and a $1,670 low. The 3-day pull showed a $1,609 average, a $1,678 high and a $1,532 low, but they distrust that dip because the 7-, 15- and 30-day figures sit within $8 of each other, so expected truck pay is about $1,678 [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:02:17]. The market read "+100 very tight" (red is tight, blue is loose). They ignore forecasts and let the 30-day and 7-day data rule. Expecting roughly $1,700 to the truck, they quote about $1,850 and call 15% margin "pretty standard" [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:04:10]. FreightBrokers formalizes the math into four numbers on a Buffalo, NY to Newark, NJ dry van carrying about 22,000 lb of non-stackable furniture. A $1,300 estimated carrier rate plus a $250 target gross profit equals a $1,550 customer rate. With a $200 profit floor, the maximum carrier rate is $1,350 [HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:04:26]. The quote goes out as "$1,550" with no apology and no "10-minute song and dance." It states what is included and holds through end of day. The carrier booked at $1,325 for $225 gross [HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:06:29], because knowing the ceiling lets the broker negotiate with confidence.

Data access is tiered, and the tier decides what you can see. Tony's Topics lays out DAT's ladder. DAT One Standard carries no lane rates, Enhanced shows a 30-day average, and the other three tiers show 15-day data, so the working minimum is DAT One Pro at $135 a month via Tools > Quick Rate Lookup. Los Angeles to Chicago at 2,025 miles priced van at $1.90 per mile, reefer at $2.21 and flatbed at $2.14, all-in spot [wOpAqd5AALo @ 00:02:14]. ShipLeague's new-lane safety net is "make or break even": price in 150 miles of deadhead so the worst case breaks even, and keep doing it until the lane is learned [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:04:55]. Margin goes up for an undesirable or loose destination, or for freight that is overweight, oversized, tarp-damaging, needs ramps or load levelers, needs driver assist, or loads slowly. Capacity comes from Lane Makers filtered to full truckload, van, 30 days. A carrier that posted the lane nine times gets the "three a month" pitch [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:07:16]. Freight Broker 911 sells the automated version: instant quotes in about 10 seconds built from market data, lane history, fuel and capacity. Pricing is $0 for the first 30 days and stays $0 with an active shipper and at least one shipment per 30 days; otherwise it is $149 a month [edDHTJiH5HM @ 00:00:34].

Inside the TMS, the numbers become documents and deductions. Steve's AscendTMS build sets income at $2.30 per mile, then cuts it to $1.90 per mile for $2,205.90 on 1,161 miles. The carrier gets a flat $1,800 with a $75 extra-stop option, and a 4% quick-pay fee comes off the top: "four percent of 1800 is 72," held back from the carrier's pay [idL8PXMlKjQ @ 00:48:51]. The 2024 Ascend walkthrough shows $3,000 flat income against a $2,000 flat expense, with gross profit and percentage displayed. It auto-generates the bill of lading, carrier confirmation, customer confirmation and invoice. A driver document request texts a link, and the upload attaches straight to the load [Eycc6Jod6t4 @ 00:16:17]. On the payment side, quick pay is priced broker by broker. Trucking From Scratch's David McGill describes 2-, 5- or 14-day tiers, each at a different percentage, with payment instructions printed on the rate confirmation [K9UPJJr_uzQ @ 00:00:17]. Brandon Manney puts broker quick pay at about 2-3% within roughly 7 days, against factoring at 3-5% within 24 hours, which on a $1,000 invoice works out to "$50 plus whatever other fees" [i6KVZJUOBqs @ 00:02:21]. The broker's spread and the carrier's cost of speed get settled on the same invoice.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the load details
   - Capture pickup and delivery times, commodity, weight, equipment, driver assist, extra stops and special instructions before pricing.
   - A firm 6:00 a.m. delivery prices differently than "anytime Friday," so never quote off origin and destination alone [FreightBrokers — HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:00:56]
2. Set the four numbers
   - Estimate the carrier rate from multiple sources, add target gross profit, set the customer rate, then derive the maximum carrier rate.
   - $1,300 carrier plus $250 profit equals $1,550; minus the $200 floor gives a $1,350 ceiling, and the load booked at $1,325 [FreightBrokers — HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:04:26]
3. Vet the carrier and build the load
   - Run the caller's MC through universal search, then build the load with stops, miles and financials in the TMS.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split in getting paid is quick pay versus factoring, and the practitioners land on different sides depending on how many brokers a truck hauls for. Porter Capital frames quick pay as a 2-to-7-day payout costing 1 to 5 percent, good only for that one broker's loads, and says one broker with solid quick pay may be all a carrier needs while multi-broker scaling calls for factoring [iJE2rZFL57E @ 00:01:14]. David McGill of Trucking From Scratch goes further and prefers factoring outright: twenty to fifty brokers means twenty to fifty invoicing processes, while a factor is one portal or one email, a standardized process that can be delegated to an admin hire, and some brokers do not offer quick pay at all [K9UPJJr_uzQ @ 00:02:43]. Brandon Manney prices the trade-off in plain numbers, quick pay at roughly 2 to 3 percent inside about seven days against factoring at 3 to 5 percent inside 24 hours [i6KVZJUOBqs @ 00:02:21]. Courtney at Bumble Bee Dispatch counters that quick pay is not as cheap as it looks once a 5 percent fee stacks with a $25 to $29 Comchek fee, and steers carriers toward same-day or next-day factoring deposits [dTOcCujNdaw @ 00:04:20].

On pricing, the split is percentage margin versus fixed-dollar margin, and how much data to trust. ShipLeague pulls DAT RateView at Market granularity, ignores forecasts, leans on 30-day and 7-day figures, expects about $1,700 to the truck on a 766-mile Atlanta to Vineland van lane and quotes about $1,850, calling 15 percent margin pretty standard [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:04:10]. FreightBrokers rejects any fixed rule, stating there is no magic profit amount, and instead stacks a $250 gross profit on a $1,300 carrier estimate to land a $1,550 customer rate, then works backward to a $1,350 ceiling for the truck [HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:04:11]. Tony's Topics takes the subscription route, insisting lane rates need at least DAT One Pro at $135 a month, and shrugs that there is not much difference between 45 and 135 [wOpAqd5AALo @ 00:03:16]. Freight Broker 911 skips manual research altogether with an AI engine that returns instant quotes in about ten seconds off market data, lane history, fuel and capacity, at $0 for the first 30 days and $0 after that only with an active shipper moving at least one shipment every 30 days, otherwise $149 a month [edDHTJiH5HM @ 00:00:34].

The playbooks also diverge on who owns the customer and the carrier relationship. Mike Gray, appearing on Entre Mike, onboarded 14 direct shippers in a single year and kept only 2 or 3 of them, warning that direct shippers pay in 30 to 45 days and demand a system just to track invoices [oytUZVvyF80 @ 00:24:33]. FreightBrokers offers the agent route as the alternative to building all of that alone: a 60 percent split of a $300 margin leaves the agent $180, traded for the host brokerage's authority, systems, credit and carrier network, with a blunt instruction to read the agreement first [Lpq9AHrz7nQ @ 00:06:52]. Dispatchers split the room hardest. Gray says they bring negative sales skills and that a broker like Gray would pay a confident carrier two or three hundred dollars more, while the host adds a firsthand case of a dispatcher booking lower rates than the host's fiancé did and says dispatcher contact info in the packet adds friction [oytUZVvyF80 @ 00:10:19]. Yet the same conversation concedes that a carrier who lacks sales skills should hire a dispatcher anyway, with the host warning that the dispatcher then owns the broker relationship instead of the carrier [oytUZVvyF80 @ 00:31:06].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap every practitioner hits is confusing invoiced with paid. FreightBrokers walks a $2,100 shipper and $1,800 carrier load through POD-triggered invoicing, then shows that owing ten carriers means $18,000 out the door before a single shipper check lands, adding that a load can be profitable on paper and still create a cash flow problem [Lpq9AHrz7nQ @ 00:03:21]. That same $300 gross shrinks to $225 net once roughly $75 of load board, TMS, insurance, bond, phone, accounting and factoring expense comes off, which is why the channel defines net profit as what remains after the expenses of operating the business [Lpq9AHrz7nQ @ 00:04:47]. BLACK BEAR FREIGHT & LOGISTICS hits the carrier mirror image: fuel, insurance, drivers, truck payments and repairs do not wait for 30-, 45- or 60-day terms, and factoring will not fix low rates or high costs, since it may only help a carrier lose money more efficiently [ZYkg1UepLL4 @ 00:01:47]. Courtney at Bumble Bee Dispatch adds the friction cost of chasing pay without a single point of contact: five brokers in one week are hard to track, mailed checks can take two to three weeks, and truck stops charge to cash Comcheks [dTOcCujNdaw @ 00:05:00].

Pricing errors cut both ways. FreightBrokers opens with the stakes on a first load: quote too high and the first customer walks, quote too low and 500 bucks evaporates, which is why the channel derives a maximum carrier rate, $1,550 minus a $200 floor equals $1,350, and refuses to escalate until the profit disappears; if every carrier on the board wants $1,500, the move is to re-check the numbers or go back to the shipper [HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:04:26]. When a competitor quotes $1,450, the instruction is to compare apples to apples and walk away if the margin does not work, because nobody needs to win every quote; the worked load still booked at $1,325 for $225 gross [HTQaqqy_CPs @ 00:05:55]. ShipLeague warns new brokers off starting on a loss, contrasting $300 to $500 lost per load against $500 to $600 made at peaks, and says to quote very high or pass on year-round pricing and to ask for help before committing [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:11:45]. The same ShipLeague walkthrough flags a street-level abuse: do not post loads you do not have just to hear what carriers will haul for [rMpa5pbBWtI @ 00:09:04].

The ugliest firsthand failure is fraud. Gray recounts a hotshot that deadheaded 400 miles near Billings, Montana for an 8:30 appointment and rolled in at 11:00, with the driver caught taping a handwritten paper sign over the truck's decal [oytUZVvyF80 @ 00:13:56]. The driver ran a new MC while the booked MC was 14 months old, the dispatcher could only get loads from TQL, and the fake trip lease meant the driver never got paid while every party, the broker included, carried fraud liability [oytUZVvyF80 @ 00:16:34]. On the factoring side, BLACK BEAR FREIGHT & LOGISTICS lists the contract traps that eat the advance: ACH and wire fees, same-day funding charges, minimum volumes, processing and credit-check fees, reserves, early termination penalties, auto-renewal and aging fees, with tiered rates stepping up every 10, 15 or 30 days, so the carrier should demand a written total-cost example at 15, 30, 45 and 60 days [ZYkg1UepLL4 @ 00:03:36]. Non-recourse is narrower than it sounds, usually covering only insolvency and excluding disputes, missing POD, claims, rate-confirmation mismatches and refusals, so non-recourse does not necessarily mean a carrier can never be charged back [ZYkg1UepLL4 @ 00:03:00].
