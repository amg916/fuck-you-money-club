# Chapter 11: Double Brokering and Freight Fraud Defense

*How double brokering evolved, warning signs, carrier vetting with Highway, RMIS and Truckstop MFA, Carrier411 policy changes, attorney-led prevention, and new federal rules targeting fraudulent brokers*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from Double Broker Bounty Hunter, Freight 360, Highway, Overdrive's Hank Seaton and ThinkFreight converge on one thesis: double brokering is now an identity-theft game, run through phished load board logins, look-alike email domains, dark-web DOT PINs and bought MC numbers, usually by one operator posing as both carrier and broker. Defense mechanics are concrete: Highway-style association insights that expose shared addresses, phones and equipment; a 20% off-market rate trigger; corporate-office callbacks on load number and lane; withheld PO numbers released only after geofenced photo checks; and carrier names on the bill of lading. Numbers frame the stakes: $2 to $3 billion in 2024 losses, a $75,000 bond that paid Convoy carriers under 10 cents on the dollar, and a 7-day bond replenishment rule enforced from January 16, 2026. Speakers split on rebrokering chain depth and Carrier411 reporting.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

John Cana, who runs the Double Broker Bounty Hunter channel, calls double brokering the root of every other freight crime, from cargo theft to ransomed cargo to nonpayment [3WxrmhqpsyY @ 00:02:15]. The playbook starts with a carrier identity. Scammers either file their own authority using a scrapyard VIN and a pencil-whipped DOT inspection from a bribed mechanic, phish a real carrier's setup packet by posting a fake load and sitting on those documents for as long as the insurance stays valid, or buy aged MC numbers that exiting carriers advertise on Facebook and X [J96nZa6x_Ek @ 00:12:13]. The broker side mirrors it: forged rate confirmations doctored in Adobe, look-alike domains such as ship-abc swapped in for abclogistics, and fake DAT authentication emails that harvest load board credentials. Cana insists the chain is short, not a game of telephone: the same person who books from the real broker while posing as a carrier is usually the one posing as a broker to the real carrier, keeping a smoke screen in the middle [3WxrmhqpsyY @ 00:19:58]. Keep Loadings describes the street result in plain terms, with scammers buying old clean MC numbers and cloning legitimate carrier names to book high-value cargo and steal the entire trailer [FbcZES6peVA @ 00:01:42].

The vetting consensus centers on identity data that does not line up. Nate Cross of Freight 360 lists five red flags: no inspection history or unverifiable equipment, rates that sound too good to be true, email or phone details that do not match the FMCSA registration, PO box or residential addresses on a carrier offering to move multiple loads, and community reports from Highway, the TIA Watchdog list, or the Excel lists brokers pass between each other [gnxgKnnz6Jw @ 00:02:35]. Highway's demos show the same pattern in data form. One carrier with active authority and a recent inspection still carried eight insights: a residential address within a quarter mile of a carrier holding broker authority, a claim of 10 or more trucks, an inspection-to-truck ratio in the bottom 10 of its peer group, and shared phones, emails, contacts, an address and even a vehicle with a second carrier, which Highway calls two carriers sharing identities [b-vDBH9GpuA @ 00:01:33]. A second Highway walkthrough flagged a carrier self-reporting 16 trucks with zero inspections in 24 months and contact details reused across three active MC numbers [sKKld4Wdyqg @ 00:01:17]. Hank Seaton tells Overdrive listeners to start any fraud screen by looking for common addresses and common names [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:42:57].

Everyone agrees the losses are large and the federal backstop is thin. Cana cites FreightWaves and Triumph Pay putting 2023 double brokering damage at a half billion dollars, with 2024 estimates of $2 to $3 billion before stolen or ransomed cargo is counted [J96nZa6x_Ek @ 00:10:19]. Bridge Logistics relays Carrier411 CEO Darren Brewer's figure of more than $100 million a year and warns that defrauded carriers chase shippers for payment while honest brokers end up paying the same load twice [uT4ymtB7YcA @ 00:01:49]. The $75,000 broker bond is treated as a floor, not protection: Cana notes some mega brokerages run bonds above $10 million, but when Convoy shut down with about $34 million in loads chasing a $75,000 bond, carriers collected less than 10 cents on the dollar [3WxrmhqpsyY @ 00:08:37]. ThinkFreight reports that since January 16, 2026, FMCSA can suspend a broker whose bond or trust falls below $75,000 and is not replenished within seven calendar days. Shortfalls can come from unpaid claims, a broker consenting to a drawdown, ignoring valid claim notices, or a claim turned into a federal court judgment, though the host, coming from a broker background, still wants bonds sized to freight volume [s-qzw5ZxjmU @ 00:01:41].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Cana's rate-confirmation forensics are the sharpest street mechanics on offer. The pricing test is a 20% rule: any load posted 20% above or below market gets heavy digging, 30% above market is most likely a scam no matter which broker is named, and a Minneapolis to Elk River post paying $2,500 for 32 miles was an instant scam call [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:14:19]. On paper, Cana checks whether the pro number font differs from the rest of the header, whether the email domain matches the real brokerage, and whether shipper and receiver codes are four-character alphanumerics, which signal an Amazon load that is a scam if it is not moving through the Relay app [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:24:56]. A Gmail address triggers questions every time; Cana has verified only two legitimate brokers using Gmail out of roughly 60,000. Behavioral tells include one-line rate cons with no fine print, a broker demanding the bill of lading at pickup, check-in instructions under another carrier's name, and status demands at arrival, check-in, loading, and paperwork. Freight 360's Nate Cross adds that a too-good rate usually means the carrier plans to rebroker, take quick pay, and vanish, leaving the broker to pay the second carrier again [gnxgKnnz6Jw @ 00:02:20].

For brokers, the working stack layers vetting software over physical proof. Nate Cross runs Highway at the Freight 360 brokerage for inspection history, registered equipment, community reports, and an ELD-fed map of equipment locations, then makes GPS tracking mandatory for the full load when fraud is suspected; most fraudsters quit once challenged for tracking or an ELD download before payment [uGu6NcZkt28 @ 00:01:57]. Cross also demands photos or video at the shipper showing equipment, the driver's face, registration, driver's license, and the MC on the truck door, zooming in for doctored images, and withholds the PO or pickup number until identity and location are confirmed. QuickScope automates that gate: the driver gets a text, takes a one-time photo inside a 2m geofence, and character recognition checks the company name, MC, and DOT before pickup details release automatically [uGu6NcZkt28 @ 00:03:45]. Hank Seaton estimates two-thirds to three-quarters of truckload shipments move without the carrier's name on the paperwork [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:25:28]. His protocol: put the authorized carrier's name on the bill of lading, send that name to the consignor at booking, show the contracting broker as the send-freight-bill-to party, and require the driver to present credentials, including a driver's license, satisfactory to the consignor [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:26:07].

Carriers get their own checklist. Cana tells them to call the corporate office of any medium or large brokerage, found through the DAT directory, to confirm the load number, the lane, and that they are listed as carrier of record; with small shops, ask the shipper which broker it uses, but never talk money, because that edges into back solicitation [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:29:21]. Cana also recommends drivers sign over a colored rubber stamp carrying the MC number, carrier name, and driver name, so a scammer cannot hide the real carrier or forge a clean receiver signature [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:11:25]. On onboarding, Cana walks dispatchers through the Truckstop MFA layered onto RMIS setups since January 2024: the owner completes a one-time ID check with a driver's license scan and selfie, then each setup needs an SMS, biometric, or authenticator code plus the usual emailed verification code, adding about 30 seconds once configured; delete cookies between setups or the flow hangs [aSSPaOdn5ZI @ 00:12:31]. Seaton adds a pressure valve: a carrier who suspects fraud mid-haul can insist on payment for delivery of the shipment, which Seaton frames as a possessory lien rooted in old commerce law [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:38:00].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The $75,000 broker bond is the loudest fault line. ThinkFreight, speaking from a broker background, calls the new FMCSA rule a slight step forward: a broker whose bond or trust drops below $75,000 and is not replenished within seven calendar days can be suspended, but the speaker argues the bond should scale with brokerage size, get reviewed every year, and that anyone moving a billion dollars in freight should carry far more than a replenishing minimum [s-qzw5ZxjmU @ 00:04:52]. John Cana of Double Broker Bounty Hunter pushes the same direction with harder numbers: some mega brokerages already run bonds upwards of $10 million while others still sit at $75,000, and when big brokers like Convoy shut down, carriers chasing roughly $34 million in loads against a $75,000 bond collected less than 10 cents on the dollar [3WxrmhqpsyY @ 00:08:37]. Attorney Hank Seaton, on Overdrive, describes a case where an operator ran up $1.9 million in carrier receivables, filed bankruptcy, and left 273 carriers with maybe four percent [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:20:28]. The split is about remedy, not diagnosis. ThinkFreight banks the seven-day replenishment trigger as progress, while Cana wants the bond itself sized to the freight a broker has on the road at any given time [3WxrmhqpsyY @ 00:09:20].

Practitioners also split on whether the federal government will fix this. The Commercial Carrier Journal guest argues the Household Goods Shipping Consumer Protection Act would give the government teeth and funding to fine and even shut down fraudulent businesses, but insists it is just another tool in the tool belt that should not stop carriers from deploying technology and getting close with partners [u2IgQ87ZELQ @ 00:01:19]. Seaton goes the other way: the statute already lets the government charge a fraudster up to ten thousand dollars per violation, yet FMCSA has no appetite for it, complaints can wait as much as a year for a response, and the real fix is self-help, an agreement among brokers and carriers large and small to quit the sloppy procedures [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:17:45]. Cana lands in the middle. FMCSA believes court challenges blocked its civil penalty power, Cana disagrees with that interpretation, and private outfits like the bounty hunter program and the freight fraud task force are stepping in to help the agencies do their job [3WxrmhqpsyY @ 00:15:57]. ThinkFreight still calls enforcement of the financial responsibility rule a step forward, noting the rule took effect in January 2024 but enforcement was delayed until January 2026, with the broker transparency rulemaking expected next [s-qzw5ZxjmU @ 00:04:06].

Watchlists split the same camp. Freight 360's Nate Cross tells brokers that community reports on Highway or the TIA Watchdog list, plus Excel lists of internal double brokering reports passed from broker to broker, are a strong indicator to avoid a carrier [gnxgKnnz6Jw @ 00:04:01]. Cana is more skeptical, estimating at least 10 percent of Carrier411 reports are false or exaggerated, while co-host Jimmy argues a bad experience earns an internal do-not-use flag, not a Freight Guard that tells the world to drop Joe's Trucking [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:17:59]. Cana later blasted Carrier411 for making deleted Freight Guard reports permanent, growing from 12 to 26 report categories, and allowing reports against carriers who file on a broker's bond or threaten legal action, calling it a weapon brokers use to intimidate and extort carriers [o7UDt2VXwwU @ 00:01:28]. Quick pay draws the same kind of split. Cana tells brokers that a carrier demanding quick pay from a well-established broker, one every factor already covers, is a warning sign, but Jimmy counters that most brokers run quick pay cheaper than the five to three percent a factor charges, and that it helps new carriers factors will not take on, calling it a gamble but the price of doing business [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:20:11].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures Seaton walked through started with nobody checking who actually backed into the dock. In one case, two reputable broker-carriers tendered loads to a fraudster posing as a broker, the bills of lading at pickup never showed the actual carrier's name, and roughly three weeks later the parties realized the loads were copper wire in a scam of over $2.6 million; all seven loads had been reconsigned to an empty warehouse, and six months on nobody, including the FBI, FMCSA, and state police, could find the freight [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:22:34]. In another, over two dozen victims were scammed by an outfit FMCSA showed running only 60,000 miles, freight was claimed broken down at a Montana truck stop, seals came back broken and resealed, and the home office was a McMansion in Ohio [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:32:16]. Bridge Logistics describes the same endgame: the real carrier is sent to an unknown location and the product is transloaded onto another victimized carrier with a new bill of lading [uT4ymtB7YcA @ 00:02:31]. Cana's tell is silence. When dispatch goes dark after booking or tracking fails, the cargo is gone, and getting law enforcement to take cargo theft seriously remains the problem [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:18:48].

The economics of a single double-brokered load hit everyone twice. Nate Cross warns that when the fraudster collects and never pays the hauling carrier, the broker typically ends up paying for the same load twice, once to the fraudster and again to the carrier that actually moved it [gnxgKnnz6Jw @ 00:01:27]. Bridge Logistics adds that defrauded carriers go after shippers for payment when they cannot track down the fake broker, that legitimate brokers are commonly on the hook for multiple payments, and that the rates promised to double-brokered carriers run much higher than what the legitimate broker negotiated [uT4ymtB7YcA @ 00:01:09]. Seaton describes collection agencies that find a hoodwinked small carrier, take an assignment for 50 percent of whatever they collect, and hammer upstream, so the honest broker catches heat from the customer over a $3,000 demand on a load the broker never knew was double brokered [jnoJihiGSEA @ 00:15:35]. George of Truck Driver Nation frames the carrier's side: that money is fuel money, the only path back in most scenarios is to back source the load all the way to the original direct shipper and bill them, and speed matters because the fraudster is racing to collect first [5dP6IV7sJW0 @ 00:02:47].

Identity hijacks are the trap that compounds fastest. Cana warns that DOT PIN numbers compromised in the March 2023 data breach are for sale on the dark web, and that the minute a carrier discovers its information was pirated, the problem has existed for at least 24 hours with at least 100 loads double brokered in its name [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:17:23]. Scammers also post a fake load, award it to a real carrier, harvest the setup documents, and sit on them for as long as the insurance stays good, up to three months, before booking freight under that identity, while brokers get phished by fake DAT emails asking them to authenticate their accounts [J96nZa6x_Ek @ 00:12:35]. Forged rate confirmations look clean at first glance: Cana caught a mismatched font on the pro number, lookalike domains such as brockb.net and logisticsdynamicsinc.com, and four-character alphanumeric shipper and receiver codes marking Amazon loads that only the relay app user gets paid on [3dI5z6SHDPc @ 00:27:54]. The Commercial Carrier Journal guest expects the curve to get worse, since very advanced AI lets fraudsters scrape large amounts of data, fabricate details like physical location, and hide their actions much more effectively [u2IgQ87ZELQ @ 00:04:31].
