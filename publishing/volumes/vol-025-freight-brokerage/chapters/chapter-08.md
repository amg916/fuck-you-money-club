## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who prices freight starts from the same split. Sarah at Anderson Trucking Service defines a contract rate as a fixed price and volume commitment on a set lane, usually for one year, a half year, or a quarter. It is built so a shipper can forecast spend and ride out swings. A spot rate is a one-time price that changes day to day and exists to find capacity for last-minute shipments across dry van, flatbed, and refrigerated [b2-QjzInPJo @ 00:00:21]. Miranda at Trucking Made Successful explains where contract rates come from: shippers run an annual RFP, and the winning bid becomes the rate. Those contracts are really not legally binding at all, which is why some people call them paper rates [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:01:41]. Dispatch Trucks adds the quoting difference operators trip over. Contract freight is priced as base plus fuel surcharge, while spot is quoted all-in, and contract terms generally favor shippers while spot favors carriers [5azQO8-P020 @ 00:08:40]. The shared takeaway is that the two prices run on different clocks. Contract rates lag by a few months, so carriers who locked reefer bids above $3 per mile in December 2021 out-earned spot carriers once 2022 crashed [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:03:09].

The second point of agreement is that tender rejections are the engine of the spot board. Miranda lays out the mechanism: when spot rates beat contract rates, carriers reject contract freight and that freight lands on the load board. That is why rejections fell sharply from late February 2022 and spot rates fell right along with them [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:04:06]. On the SONAR map, darker blue marks higher tender rejection and therefore higher spot rates. Elevation on the same map shows volume as tenders over the past seven days, so an operator reads height and color together. Because rejected freight reaches the spot market in three to five days, the map works as a short-range forecast [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:08:43]. Logistics Today reports the squeeze in its sharpest form: tender rejections around 14%, SONAR showing contract rates up 18%, new contracts up 12-13%, and backup carriers costing about 22% more. The outlet sums it up as contracts catching up [5cyp2cCCUiA @ 00:02:10]. It frames capacity as fewer trucks chasing more loads, with truck posts down 20-26% while van loads rose 17% [5cyp2cCCUiA @ 00:01:31]. The operator reading is simple: rising rejections mean contract freight is spilling into the spot pool, and the board will pay for it within days.

Every load-board practitioner also agrees that a colored map is a starting point, never a decision. Bobby Loads repeats it across episodes: don't just trust a red map, search the loads yourself [fzBhki-xaDc @ 00:13:38]. In another episode the line is that just because it's red does not mean it's hot [MDlMmII7ZrY @ 00:17:50]. In one update, Birmingham showed cool on the map while the actual loads were strong, including a $2,300 load at $6.61 per mile [2vUXF3RIbqg @ 00:04:02]. Bobby Loads also warns that load notes hide traps a map never shows: team loads, one-pick-three-drop runs, oversize loads, and $350K cargo. The geographic consensus is just as consistent. Bobby Loads calls the South money bags for flatbed and says to stay in hot areas. Miranda's regional breakdown independently ranks the South best for flatbed with about 49K loads and the West Coast worst, and puts the Midwest and South best for dry van with the West and Northeast worst [l6yFrAoUZ2M @ 00:03:13]. Both camps steer away from the Northeast and the West Coast. Both treat load boards the way Bobby Loads puts it: as tools, not money printers. The operator who wins checks the map, opens the actual postings, reads the notes, and only then books.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Bobby Loads opens every update the same way: pull DAT national averages, then set a target well above them. In one week dry van sat at $3.03, flatbed at $3.66, and reefer at $3.44, with diesel at $5.31. The targets were 3.40-3.50 for vans and about $4 for flatbed [Gz22R2KL_e4 @ 00:00:23]. Another week put dry van at $3.08, flatbed at $3.77, and reefer at $3.44, with targets of $3.40-$3.50, $4-plus, and $3.60-$3.80 respectively [MDlMmII7ZrY @ 00:00:11]. On a Sunday, the read uses the previous 8 days of data. When the market softened to $2.72 van, $3.44 flatbed, and $3.19 reefer, the van target dropped to $3.00-$3.20 [ZSqNEq3Cx_g @ 00:00:34]. The heuristic for raw load-to-truck counts is to cut the loads in half and double or triple the truck count. A market only counts as tight if it still looks tight after that haircut [Gz22R2KL_e4 @ 00:03:56]. Memphis cleared that bar at 3,752 loads against 405 trucks. Chicago ran about 6,000 loads to 1,000 trucks, and Indianapolis showed 5,000-plus loads against fewer than 500 trucks.

The unit economics are built per day, not per mile alone. The dry van daily target is $1,600-$2,000 or more. It is earned by running short hops between good markets instead of big miles into crappy areas, with sample hits like Decatur to Indy at $1,900 and $4.75 per mile [2vUXF3RIbqg @ 00:05:20]. Flatbed runs on a weekly rhythm: short runs during the week, then a gross pad load for the weekend, aiming for close to $10K per week. The same plan consolidates LTL out of Chicago and avoids Northwest Arkansas [Gz22R2KL_e4 @ 00:14:20]. The flatbed playbook stays South and Midwest, uses 800-1,200-mile Friday runs east, and then eats one on Monday. Bobby Loads' own drivers posted $12,120 on 2,773 miles at $4.37 and $13,700 on 3,099 miles at $4.42 [2vUXF3RIbqg @ 00:10:14]. Jonesboro, Arkansas flatbed freight paid $3,200 for 395 miles and $4,600 for 640 miles. Other specific loads show what the targets look like in practice: Montgomery to Gainesville at $7 per mile, an 822-mile run paying $4,450 for roughly $2,200 a day, Memphis to Dallas at $6,000 on 452 miles, and Shreveport to Oklahoma City at $9.90 per mile [MDlMmII7ZrY @ 00:11:55]. The rule underneath it all: the less you drive and the better the RPM, the better the profit.

The tool stack splits by job. Bobby Loads rates DAT best for dry van, reefer, flatbed, stepdeck, and Conestoga on volume and analytics, with a map similar to SONAR. Truckstop has the edge for double drops and RGNs but otherwise carries the same loads with weaker analytics [s-t9YGcOJ8U @ 00:00:37]. Contract Fortune calls DAT the McDonald's of posting. They prefer Truckstop's rate calculator, which lands within a couple hundred dollars, plus its national fuel averages. They recommend holding both memberships, noting DAT services ran about $300 against about $100 at Truckstop [ocF4fny2uH8 @ 00:08:23]. Trucking Made Successful runs the SONAR app plus Truckstop/FTR Intel because the daily register stopped showing new authorities [M0KZ3BZctSQ @ 00:00:15]. Bobby Loads pushes the DAT market-conditions package, priced at about $100 extra a month and pitched as worth 100 bucks on a single load. They pair it with DAT trend lines and RateView [ZSqNEq3Cx_g @ 00:00:10]. Load Hunter, a sponsored add-on, layers factoring credit scores, click-to-email and click-to-call templates, lane auto-email, a profit calculator, and broker reviews on top of the boards. Contract Fortune adds the clock on the broker side: brokers have 24-36 hours to cover freight, and that is the window a carrier negotiates inside.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to lock freight or chase the board. Sarah at Anderson Trucking Service sells contract as the shipper's safety play: a fixed price and volume commitment on a set lane, usually for a year, with predictable costs, negotiable fuel and accessorials, less admin time and capacity on each and every load [b2-QjzInPJo @ 00:01:05]. Dispatch Trucks flips the lens to the carrier side, arguing contract rates usually favor shippers while spot rates favor carriers, and that contract pays base plus fuel surcharge while spot is quoted all-in [5azQO8-P020 @ 00:08:40]. Miranda at Trucking Made Successful undercuts the whole idea of a lock, calling contracts really not legally binding at all, so-called paper rates. Yet the same breakdown shows contract carriers who bid reefer above $3 a mile in December 2021 out-earning spot carriers after the 2022 crash, because contract rates lag by a few months [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:03:09]. Guy in a Truck weighs a live offer: a floral nursery contract at $2.25 a mile plus $50 per stop, five to six stops on regional runs, grossing five to six grand a week with home time. They frame it as good stability versus the spot market, with the risk of losing out if in-season spot rates climb [6qAGWwbnmxA @ 00:04:02].

Which screen to trust is the second fault line. Bobby Loads rates DAT best for dry van, reefer, flatbed, stepdeck and Conestoga on volume and analytics and calls its map similar to SONAR. Truckstop gets the edge only for double drops and RGNs; otherwise it carries the same loads with weaker analytics [s-t9YGcOJ8U @ 00:00:37]. Contract Fortune splits the job differently: DAT is the place to post, like McDonald's, while Truckstop wins on a rate calculator accurate within a couple hundred dollars and on its national fuel averages. DAT services ran about $300 against roughly $100 at Truckstop, and the recommendation is to hold both memberships [ocF4fny2uH8 @ 00:00:51]. Trucking Made Successful skips the board debate for forecasting and pays for FreightWaves SONAR, because the data they needed was locked behind SONAR or ACT Research [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:00:14]. Later they layered the SONAR app with Truckstop and FTR Intel after the daily register stopped showing new authorities [M0KZ3BZctSQ @ 00:00:15]. The practical read: Bobby Loads runs one platform plus the paid market-conditions package, Contract Fortune runs two boards for posting versus pricing, and Miranda treats tender-rejection data as the leading indicator that board volume only confirms three to five days later.

Relationships and lane discipline split the field a third way. Bobby Loads plays pure geography: short runs bouncing between good markets beat stacking big miles into crappy areas. The daily target is $1,600 to $2,000 or more, with a standing refusal to run cheap inbound loads to California [2vUXF3RIbqg @ 00:04:45]. Dispatch Trucks pushes a blended book instead, rejecting the rates-are-crap, go-direct-to-shippers mindset and teaching six practices: assess risk tolerance, match operational capacity, analyze the market, diversify with contracts covering baseline costs and spot capturing peaks, build relationships, and review regularly [5azQO8-P020 @ 00:07:52]. On broker loyalty, Guy in a Truck invests deliberately. When a broker has $3,300 into a load and offers a $3,350 ceiling, they take $3,300 to build the relationship, figuring brokers make maybe a hundred bucks if they're lucky [6qAGWwbnmxA @ 00:12:59]. Miranda at Trucking Made Successful is colder, warning that relationships built during COVID didn't really mean anything and sticking to known, smaller brokers. They also insist posted rates are pre-negotiation and that the rates you can negotiate are going to make diesel not an issue [l6yFrAoUZ2M @ 00:05:22]. One camp spends margin to buy loyalty; the other treats every rate confirmation as a fresh fight.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most repeated trap is trusting the map instead of the loads. Bobby Loads warns that red on the flatbed map can be a false positive and that just cuz it's red does not mean it's hot, pushing operators to search the delivery market a day or two ahead [MDlMmII7ZrY @ 00:09:25]. The same warning lands in the 2026 lane breakdown: don't just trust a red map, search the loads yourself [fzBhki-xaDc @ 00:13:38]. The reverse error costs money too. Birmingham showed cool on the dry van map while the actual loads were strong, including a $2,300 run at $6.61 a mile [2vUXF3RIbqg @ 00:04:02]. On the West Coast, the numbers themselves are suspect: Bobby Loads says brokers repost the same flatbed loads, and the standing rule is I would always stay away from the West Coast, I don't trust these numbers [C4qKjPG0gcY @ 00:01:26]. Indy loads hide extra stops in the notes, so the rate per mile on screen is not the job actually being booked. Gross is the final illusion: in New England and on the West Coast, Bobby Loads repeats that gross does not mean anything, and flatbed operators are told to avoid bad areas that force layovers no matter what the posted number shows.

Broker-side traps show up as both fraud and friction. Bobby Loads flags a Kahaba load listing a Gmail contact, which they allegedly tie to a hacked account. The rule that follows is blunt: distrust rates far above market, so don't fall for this crap, please [ZSqNEq3Cx_g @ 00:05:56]. Contract Fortune exposes the margin squeeze on Florida outbound, which is cheap to begin with. On a load paying $1,100 to $1,200, the broker paid the driver $500 to $600, against a normal broker target of $200 to $300 per load [ocF4fny2uH8 @ 00:02:27]. Miranda at Trucking Made Successful reports that brokers are pleasant to work with but hold carriers verbally, including holding a truck about two hours waiting on a rate confirmation. They add that it has been a nightmare until just recently, which leaves them suspicious after the 2022 downturn [M0KZ3BZctSQ @ 00:11:26]. Shippers face their own reversal: at renewal time, contract carriers may also leave the market. A contract that looked locked can vanish when spot beats it and rejected tenders send the freight back to the load board [ZlWGSiCM45c @ 00:07:32]. These traps all punish the operator who books the headline number without checking the contact, the margin and the commitment.

The economics check that sinks carriers is diesel hiding inside the all-in rate. Logistics Today puts the DAT all-in van rate at $3.07 but only $2.24 excluding fuel, and says that 80-plus cent gap is mostly diesel, with diesel at $6.35 and roughly $2.50 above the prior year [5cyp2cCCUiA @ 00:01:10]. Bobby Loads quantifies the hit at the truck level: diesel at $5.64, up 4.44 percent, with fuel running $2,000 to $3,000 a week. The counter is to cite diesel prices directly when negotiating with brokers [ZSqNEq3Cx_g @ 00:12:11]. Contract Fortune cuts the bill at the pump, partial-filling about $150 in pricey states, filling up where fuel is cheaper and avoiding the mountains [ocF4fny2uH8 @ 00:04:37]. The mileage trap compounds it. Bobby Loads rejects LA to Houston at about $2.81 because it is not worth 1,500 miles, and sums the rule up elsewhere as less you drive, better RPM, the better profit's going to be [Gz22R2KL_e4 @ 00:15:38]. Run the check before booking: strip fuel out of the posted number, compare the linehaul to what the week actually costs, and refuse long cheap runs into areas that force layovers.
