# Chapter 10: Accessorials: Detention, Layover and TONU

*Definitions and differences, carrier tactics for getting paid, broker obligations and policies, rate disputes, dispatcher responsibility, and how brokers profit from or lose on accessorials*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Carriers, dispatchers and brokers converge on one rule: if detention, layover and TONU terms are not in the rate confirmation or broker-carrier agreement before the truck rolls, the money is mostly gone. The street baseline is two free hours, then roughly $30-$75 per hour, with $50 cited most often by brokers. TONU clusters at $150-$200, and layover runs anywhere from $100 per night to $500 or more. Collection is a documentation game: timestamped arrival emails, warnings before free time expires, in/out times on the bill of lading, and invoices chased until paid. The fights are over who eats the cost when the shipper refuses. Some brokers pay from their own pocket, others pay only what the customer approves. The other split is whether brokers should pass accessorials through at cost or mark them up for margin.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

The baseline every seat in the deal agrees on is two hours of unpaid dock time, then the meter starts. Nate Cross of Freight 360 calls it free time, pegs the industry standard at two hours, and says roughly $50 an hour is normal once a driver passes it [A7rlv69JAj8 @ 00:01:59]. Think Trucking quotes the same two free hours and a wider $30 to $75 hourly band, paid in half-hour or hourly increments [uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:00:52]. Ernest at Never Stop Trucking spells out the math that burns rookies: if a truck sits four hours under a two-hour policy, only the last two get paid, at anywhere from $25 to $50 an hour. Ernest treats $50 as the bare minimum because the broker is renting a running truck, its fuel, and a driver, not an hourly factory worker [tvnlRLMXRJ4 @ 00:03:18]. Everybody scraps over this money because of opportunity cost. Trucking Territory puts a one-load-a-day driver at $800 to $1,200 in daily revenue. A six-hour hold erases time that could have been spent driving and earning. Dispatchers only get paid when the driver gets paid, so they hate detention as much as drivers do [Q-HgB9nn1jI @ 00:00:22].

Second point of near-total agreement: if the terms are not written down before the truck is dispatched, they effectively do not exist. Think Trucking puts it as the rule that if it is not in the rate confirmation, it does not exist. Accessorial pay only becomes real when it is written before the work happens [uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:03:40]. The ShipLeague broker is stunned by how rarely dispatchers or drivers ask about detention. They grill a broker on commodity, weight, distance and rate, but skip detention. ShipLeague demands the amount and start time in black and white on the rate con, noting it takes no time at all because the driver is the one who will actually sit there [OzmQFxT30NM @ 00:00:18]. Tim at Freight Master Resources walked a carrier friend through exactly that failure. The appointment was noon, the driver was on site at 10:30, and the real appointment turned out to be 6 a.m. the next day. Nothing about detention or layover was in either the rate confirmation or the carrier setup packet, so the broker threw roughly $150 and walked [1Fdc8KYk8M0 @ 00:03:31]. Brandon Scott assigns the responsibility bluntly from the brokerage chair: the carrier has to make sure the broker has a detention policy in place. Without one, there is a very real possibility of collecting nothing [V11p0IBJsTk @ 00:04:21].

The third consensus: the carrier bills the broker, and paper beats phone calls. Pauline Nabacwa, speaking as a dispatcher and company owner, is explicit that the broker pays detention. The broker may later chase the shipper for it, but the carrier side bills the broker directly [fpu1Fhdo9Vs @ 00:06:03]. Brandon Scott explains the plumbing: the shipper's relationship is with the broker, not the carrier, so the detention policy that governs is the broker's. The broker's job is to negotiate a policy with the shipper up front so the carrier can be paid when hours stretch [V11p0IBJsTk @ 00:03:41]. Craig Bliss has worked the shipper, broker and carrier sides. Craig says verbal conversations do not prove detention, especially against brokers that go quiet once the request lands; the paper trail does. Any phone call gets recapped in the same email chain with a 'per our conversation' line [WU9uuEFffXU @ 00:04:53]. Yvonne of Freightbrokerchic runs the identical playbook toward the customer. The moment an on-time driver is being held, the shipper is notified and the driver is told to keep track of time. Every phone call is followed up by email, so nobody can later claim they did not know a detention was coming [zvX7UreND4E @ 00:09:20].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The street rate card is wide, and practitioners quote it to the dollar. The spread between low and high offers is where negotiation lives. Trucking Territory works with $30 for each extra hour past two, and $100 to $150 per night of layover when the broker is reasonable [Q-HgB9nn1jI @ 00:03:17]. Pauline Nabacwa reports the floor going lower: brokers offering $12, $15 or $20 an hour, or a flat $100, $150 or $200 for the whole wait. Pauline calls that cheap change against what the truck gives up [fpu1Fhdo9Vs @ 00:03:41]. Ernest at Never Stop Trucking adds the cap buried in many policies: a typical broker policy pays $50 an hour up to a $250 maximum, then flips to layover. Layover commonly lands at $200 to $250 a day, roughly $10 to $12 an hour once divided by 24. TONU is a flat $150 whether the driver deadheaded two miles or 200 [tvnlRLMXRJ4 @ 00:05:33]. Nate Cross at Freight 360 prices layover higher on the broker side, at around $500 depending on equipment. Nate sets TONU near $200 and extra stops at $50 to $100 each. Lumpers run roughly $75 up to $200, reimbursed against a receipt or advanced to the driver up front [A7rlv69JAj8 @ 00:05:06].

Collection runs on a timed email sequence, and Craig Bliss hands it out nearly word for word. On arrival, the carrier emails the broker something like: driver has arrived at the shipper as of 1300, detention will start at 1500 at $50 per hour, and a heads-up will follow before it starts [WU9uuEFffXU @ 00:02:33]. At about 90 minutes, a second email in the same chain notes the driver is still not loaded at 1430 and offers to help speed loading. At expiry, a third email says detention is now running. After loading, a final email carries total charges plus the bill of lading with in and out times. Think Trucking runs the same five-step drill. First, the clause goes on the rate con. Second, the carrier sends a timestamped written arrival notice rather than a phone call. Third comes a warning before free time ends. Fourth, in and out times go on the BOL, initialed by facility staff, with gate tickets and photos kept. Fifth, the invoice is chased until it lands on settlement [uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:07:00]. Trucking Territory adds a field tactic: the two-hour call itself can push the broker to get the truck loaded faster to save money. If the next pickup is at risk, call that broker too and try to move the appointment to the next day [Q-HgB9nn1jI @ 00:04:47].

On the broker side, the mechanics turn on approval paperwork and, for some, margin. Brandon Scott emails the shipper the situation and the PO number, then gets the PO approved for detention. Brandon then issues the carrier a new rate confirmation showing the accumulated detention hours, so the final invoice includes it [V11p0IBJsTk @ 00:08:16]. Freight 360 does the same on TONU. The broker confirms what the customer typically pays on cancellation and invoices the customer the $200. The broker then cuts a fresh rate con to the driver for $200 and makes zero margin on the transaction [7sfi19fbIzU @ 00:02:52]. Brandon Manney teaches the opposite money play: ask every shipper its detention and layover rates during the sale, then pay the carrier less than collected. In Manney's example, a shipper pays $200 an hour for two hours, $400 total. The broker pays the carrier $100 an hour and keeps $200, or collects $500 on a layover and pays $400 or $300 [8Mhf3iqBflw @ 00:04:09]. Carriers can flip the paperwork too. Tim at Freight Master Resources has carriers draft their own detention and layover terms as a PDF and send them for signature through DocuSign or similar. That lets them negotiate from $75 an hour down to $40 or $50 instead of accepting $20 to $25 after three or four hours [1Fdc8KYk8M0 @ 00:07:39].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock Terms at Booking
   - Ask the broker about the detention rate per hour, when detention starts, and the layover and TONU terms, then get them all written on the rate con.
   - Ask before booking whether the broker pays detention, at what hourly rate, and after how many hours. If they refuse, do not stack a tight reload behind that load, and try negotiating a better line rate [Trucking Territory — Q-HgB9nn1jI @ 00:04:12]
2. Timestamp Arrival in Writing
   - Email the broker the moment the driver checks in, stating the arrival time, the detention start time and the hourly rate.
   - Use a script like: driver arrived at the shipper as of 1300, detention will start at 1500 at $50 per hour, and a heads-up will follow before it starts [Craig Bliss — WU9uuEFffXU @ 00:02:33]
3. Warn Before Free Time Expires
   - Alert the broker before the two hours run out, and use that window to start negotiating.
   - Tell the broker the shipper has not loaded the truck yet and that detention starts if the shipper does not move in the next 20 minutes. This opens room to push for a higher rate [Pauline Nabacwa — fpu1Fhdo9Vs @ 00:04:45]
4. Close Out With BOL and Invoice
   - Get in and out times initialed on the BOL, keep the gate tickets and photos, invoice, and follow up until the charge lands on settlement.
   - Have someone at the facility write and initial the in and out times on the bill of lading, and photograph everything. Then submit the claim with the documentation attached [Think Trucking — uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:07:16]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is what happens when the shipper says no. ShipLeague's broker-side host is blunt: if you ask your customer for detention and the customer refuses, you still pay it, because the brokerage is the customer to the carrier and a shipper refusal is no excuse [OzmQFxT30NM @ 00:02:22]. Craig Bliss runs a brokerage the same way, paying carriers off the brokerage's own policy rather than the customer's, while admitting the common practice is to pay carriers only what the paying customer agreed to [WU9uuEFffXU @ 00:06:32]. Freightbrokerchic's guest Dexter takes the opposite line: as a carrier bills detention, the broker can only pay out what the customer has already authorized, and nobody reaches into their own pocket to lose money on a load [zvX7UreND4E @ 00:06:27]. Nate Cross at Freight 360 sits in the middle, carving out a messy zone where the customer refuses a TONU but the driver already rolled toward the pickup or turned down a good load, and the broker makes a judgment call to cover a small TONU out of pocket to protect a solid carrier relationship [7sfi19fbIzU @ 00:05:05]. For carriers, the payout depends on which of these three philosophies sits on the other end of the rate confirmation.

Whether accessorials are a profit line or a pass-through is the second fault line. Brandon Manney teaches brokers to ask every shipper what it pays for detention and layover, then quote the carrier less: in the worked example the shipper pays $200 an hour, the carrier is told $100, and on two hours the broker banks $200 while the driver never learns the shipper's number [8Mhf3iqBflw @ 00:03:47]. Manney frames layover the same way, collecting $500 and paying the trucking company $400 or even $300 depending on what it will accept. Freight 360 teaches the reverse: detention is invoiced directly to the customer and passed directly through to the carrier, with no additional profit made by the broker [A7rlv69JAj8 @ 00:02:31]. On the Final Mile podcast the Freight 360 hosts went further, describing an agent whose customer approved a TONU the carrier never requested and who planned to keep it, and telling that agent flatly it was the carrier's money for wasted time [xe94bfEYHEU @ 00:11:49]. Think Trucking flags the same leakage on the carrier side: some carriers pass detention straight to company drivers, others keep it or pay less, so a driver should get the pass-through policy in writing before sitting at a dock where the carrier might collect $60 an hour and pay out $20 [uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:07:49].

Even the headline numbers are contested between camps. ShipLeague calls $50 an hour after two hours free the industry standard [OzmQFxT30NM @ 00:03:56]. Never Stop Trucking says the two-hour window only works in the shipper's and broker's favor because most trucks load in 30 minutes to an hour and a half, and treats $50 an hour as the bare minimum for idling equipment, burning fuel and a stranded driver [tvnlRLMXRJ4 @ 00:03:38]. Pauline Nabacwa reports brokers offering $12, $15 or $20 an hour, or flat fees of $100 to $200, and calls it cheap change against the day lost. Layover bands swing wider still: Freightbrokerchic's guest pegs a customary $250 a day [zvX7UreND4E @ 00:04:50], Freight 360 says expect around $500 depending on equipment, and Brandon Manney says $500 to $1,000 or more. The TONU trigger splits as well. Never Stop Trucking says the common $150 is paid whether the driver deadheaded two miles or 200, but only once the truck physically reaches the pickup, not halfway there [tvnlRLMXRJ4 @ 00:07:56]. Freight 360 instead pays when the driver is actively working the load, including one already rolling toward the shipper an hour before pickup, and expects the customer to cover around $200.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most repeated firsthand loss is booking blind. Tim at Freight Master Resources walked a carrier friend through a first load with a Georgia brokerage: the driver arrived around 10:30 for a noon delivery, was told to take a nap, and at three o'clock learned the appointment was actually 6 a.m. the next morning, putting a hurricane relief pickup at risk while the broker ignored calls, emails, texts and Telegram [1Fdc8KYk8M0 @ 00:02:28]. Neither the rate confirmation nor the carrier agreement mentioned detention or layover, so the broker threw roughly $150 and that was it [1Fdc8KYk8M0 @ 00:04:16]. ShipLeague says it is literally rare for a dispatcher or driver to ask about detention while asking commodity, distance, weight and rate [OzmQFxT30NM @ 00:00:18]. Brandon Manney describes having to school callers who book off the load board without ever asking. On Final Mile, Freight 360 traced a carrier who checked in at 10 a.m. for a 5 p.m. pickup, sat until 9 p.m. with no product and bailed, back to nobody setting expectations, and named three things to settle before every pickup: what happens if it is not loaded on time, the detention terms, and the cutoff between detention and layover [xe94bfEYHEU @ 00:04:39].

The second trap is the clock itself. Think Trucking warns the two-hour grace is measured from appointment time, not arrival, so an hour spent waiting early may not count at all [uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:01:10]. Never Stop Trucking reads three or four free hours on a rate confirmation as a big red flag that the broker already knows the shipper loads slowly, and adds that detention usually carries a cap, such as $50 an hour up to $250, after which a $250 layover works out to roughly $10 to $12 an hour [tvnlRLMXRJ4 @ 00:05:33]. Freight 360 picked apart a case where a new broker moved an 8 a.m. delivery to 11 a.m. mid-load, the receiver could not start until noon, and the broker recognized detention only from 1 p.m., leaving one billable hour on a lost day. The hosts said they would have paid three hours from the original 8 a.m. appointment, and that most of the time this pattern is a broker lying to cover a hard load, then trying to stiff the carrier [xe94bfEYHEU @ 00:09:49]. They have also watched carriers file on a broker's bond, and told one of their own brokers to pay up because the bond would likely pay and the brokerage would owe it back [xe94bfEYHEU @ 00:08:07].

Even when the paperwork is clean, the economics stay thin. Pauline Nabacwa sent a truck 160 deadhead miles, sat until 9 p.m., watched the load cancel with no rebook that night or the next day, and the most the broker offered was $150 TONU [fpu1Fhdo9Vs @ 00:07:53]. Pauline learned to reopen TONU terms the moment detention starts counting; on a later load that slid from noon to a 5 p.m. pickup, Pauline had warned the broker about a turn-away, the missing freight piece proved the point, and that earlier warning helped push the TONU to $540 [fpu1Fhdo9Vs @ 00:09:13]. Alfa X Logistics tells dispatch clients it is realistically 50/50: half the time the money is collected, the other half the broker caves, stops responding, and little can be done unless the amount justifies legal action [CNjYZO0c1o4 @ 00:02:55]. Craig Bliss adds that most customers refuse detention when the broker got no heads-up before charges started, and that agents and mega brokerages tend to go MIA once detention is requested. The cost of losing it compounds: Think Trucking puts five lost hours a week at $60 an hour at $300 weekly, or $15,600 a year sitting still [uHIZpxSLN-c @ 00:09:27].
