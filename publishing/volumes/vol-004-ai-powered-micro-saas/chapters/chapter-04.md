# Chapter 04: Launching on Product Hunt and Securing Multi-Tenant Apps with PostgreSQL RLS

*An app launch on Product Hunt, plus PostgreSQL tenant isolation and row-level authorization using Supabase, EF Core, Prisma, Claude Code, and AI-generated policies.*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Jack Smith's Product Hunt launch finished with 17 upvotes after late social promotion and a bespoke video that received two views. Their next attempt would emphasize a clearer tagline, earlier outreach, and more engaging content. Across the database demonstrations, practitioners move row authorization into PostgreSQL rather than trusting every application query to remember its filter. The mechanics are enabling RLS, defining policies, supplying caller context, and testing through the actual application role. Milan Jovanović uses an EF Core connection interceptor; Alex Rusin builds transaction-scoped Prisma access with Claude Code. Supabase demonstrates dashboard templates, AI-generated policies, and relational authorization. Alternatives differ on policy granularity, session handling, and encryption architecture. Repeated traps include owner bypass, privileged views, unrestricted policies, missing write permissions, and misleadingly successful interfaces.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Jack Smith describes Product Hunt as a place to reach early adopters, feedback, and sometimes press, primarily among people already in technology. Products receive a homepage listing for 24 hours; the opening four hours hide votes and randomize ordering, after which vote totals determine placement. Their launch strategy separates inbound votes from people browsing the platform and outbound votes from people brought there through an existing audience. The execution starts with the icon, product name, and especially the tagline, because those are the pieces visible before someone opens the listing. Smith's mobile app, Quotable, did not solve a pain point shared by much of that audience, and their retrospective focuses on making its relevant benefits clearer rather than abandoning the platform [hJtOsLAfxxE @ 00:07:21]. On the application side, Alex Rusin demonstrates an equally concrete dependency: a family-tree endpoint returns another account's person when its account filter disappears [ctJy5AxgVXM @ 00:00:00]. Milan Jovanović reproduces the same failure with notes belonging to two users after removing an EF Core filter. Both developers move the authorization condition into PostgreSQL so the omitted application filter no longer exposes the other user's rows [OwCgDPa0DnA @ 00:02:22].

The database consensus is an authorization rule attached to the data, not a promise that every caller will remember the right condition. Paul Copplestone, speaking on Microsoft Developer, explains RLS as an implicit WHERE clause: a profile query can omit its own user filter while the policy still evaluates which rows the caller may access. Their distinction is precise: authentication determines whether a user or role can enter; authorization determines what that caller can access afterward [vZT1Qx2xUCo @ 00:02:05]. Past The Diagram applies the same mechanism to unpaid invoices, with the database comparing tenant_id against the current session's tenant rather than relying on the developer to add that comparison. The application must supply the tenant resolved after authentication, and the policy needs attention to both existing rows and incoming values [h1KAW_izGl8 @ 00:00:43]. updownx adds the operating prerequisites: enabled RLS without a policy denies rows by default, while superusers and BYPASSRLS roles bypass enforcement. Their recommended application connection uses a dedicated non-superuser role with the required table privileges. They also use the pg_policies view to inspect policy names, roles, commands, and USING and WITH CHECK expressions, making the configured rules visible rather than assuming that a table toggle explains every permission [MEkM0aC1JaI @ 00:02:46].

The dashboard walkthroughs agree that enabling RLS is a table-specific operation and that allowing the intended actions is a separate task. Money Tools Academy starts by choosing the correct project, opening Table Editor, selecting the actual table, and checking that its RLS status remains enabled afterward. They emphasize that changing one table does not change every related table in the project [OBXv-uTBD7A @ 00:01:01]. SmartMoneyTutorials takes the authentication, configuration, and policies route, finds a table marked disabled, and enables it. Their important qualification is that enabling alone grants nothing: without policies, access is denied, so the intended reads and writes still need rules [Sctm5VrwWTw @ 00:02:09]. Shortcut Academy reaches the same controls from the database and tables area and says a table created in the dashboard may already have RLS enabled. Check the current toggle rather than assuming its state [4ncm-XBGErs @ 00:00:55]. Codewithguillaume demonstrates the consequence: enabling a read-all template makes orders appear in Postman, including another user's orders. They then remove that broad policy and choose the own-data template. Getting data back establishes that a policy permits the query; their demonstration then narrows the permission to the intended owner [Vx1q8Nfp0BE @ 00:01:48].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Build the first policy around an explicit operation and condition. SmartMoneyTutorials opens the chosen table's policy creation screen, selects a template or starts from scratch, gives the policy a clear name and description, selects the operation, and compares the row's user identifier with the authenticated user. They finish by saving and testing through the application's authenticated calls [sfzubTrNiiU @ 00:02:00]. Shortcut Academy separates the expressions: USING controls accessible rows and the rows targeted by update or delete, while WITH CHECK validates new or changed rows for insert and update. Their ownership example mirrors the condition in both places [4ncm-XBGErs @ 00:01:53]. Supabase demonstrates the AI version without changing those mechanics: ask the SQL assistant to enable RLS, accept the generated change, and run it; then request read access only when the published column is true. The policy editor displays a diff before acceptance and saving. Next, switch from the dashboard's privileged postgres role to anonymous or a specific authenticated user and inspect the resulting rows [hu2SQjvCXIw @ 00:02:44]. Codewithguillaume's orders example shows why the selected template matters: read-all returns other users' orders, so their replacement is the template for viewing only one's own data [Vx1q8Nfp0BE @ 00:02:09].

Milan Jovanović implements caller context with an EF Core database connection interceptor. Their API extracts the user identifier from JWT claims, and the interceptor overrides ConnectionOpenedAsync so the session setting is established before EF Core continues executing the query. They inject the HTTP context accessor, read the name-identifier claim, create a database command, and set the application user value. Registration includes the accessor, the interceptor service, and the database context's interceptor configuration [OwCgDPa0DnA @ 00:07:44]. Alex Rusin uses Claude Code to generate Prisma migrations and a transaction-scoped account wrapper instead. The sequence is enable RLS, create the account policy, discover owner bypass, force enforcement, and then supply account context. Their wrapper uses set_config with its local flag true so the setting reverts at transaction end rather than following a pooled connection into another request [ctJy5AxgVXM @ 00:05:31]. Rusin then replaces repeated route-level wrapping with a Prisma client extension: the raw client stays inside db.ts, while the exported account-scoped interface wraps every model operation in a scoped transaction. Past The Diagram likewise requires transaction-local context and warns that ordinary SET survives on the connection after it returns to the pool [h1KAW_izGl8 @ 00:02:09].

Supabase's advanced email client starts with an identity mismatch: application users have integer IDs, while auth.users has UUIDs. The implementation adds a nullable, unique supabase_id foreign key, producing a one-to-one link without deleting the application user's email history when the authentication user disappears. A trigger function inserts the public user or updates the existing email match with the new authentication ID [PdEutzhsrws @ 00:02:17]. The email read policy then resolves the public user ID through that link and allows emails where the user is sender or recipient. Folder visibility follows email visibility with an EXISTS subquery, so the referenced email policy governs which folder records contribute to the display. Supabase's blog walkthrough uses the same relational technique: a comment is readable when its associated post can be selected, avoiding a duplicate implementation of the post's visibility rules [Ow_Uzedfohk @ 00:10:45]. Write permissions remain separate: the email example checks that the authenticated user is the sender, and the blog example combines post visibility with comment ownership. Copplestone also explains USING as checking existing values and WITH CHECK as checking incoming values, including preventing an update from changing ownership to another user [vZT1Qx2xUCo @ 00:10:59].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Policy granularity is a genuine difference in implementation style. Milan Jovanović creates one user-isolation policy for all commands in the notes example, explicitly choosing SELECT, UPDATE, INSERT, and DELETE together [OwCgDPa0DnA @ 00:04:01]. Supabase's introductory blog walkthrough also begins with an all-actions ownership policy, then adds a separate published-post read policy and later a moderation policy. That demonstration uses additional permissions to expand what the application can do without granting another user ownership of the post [Ow_Uzedfohk @ 00:04:25]. Copplestone recommends the opposite organizational default: avoid FOR ALL, create one policy per operation and role, and place the relevant conditions inside that policy. Their reason is the mental model of multiple policies: permissive policies combine with OR, while restrictive policies combine with AND. They find the combination harder to reason about than a granular policy containing its own logic [vZT1Qx2xUCo @ 00:12:01]. updownx teaches both mechanisms as available controls, illustrating a permissive admin rule alongside a restrictive local-socket requirement. The alternatives are not competing claims about whether authorization belongs in PostgreSQL; they are different ways to arrange and reason about the conditions that admit a row [MEkM0aC1JaI @ 00:01:22].

Caller context is where the implementations diverge more sharply. Jovanović obtains the user from authenticated claims and sets the session value after opening the EF Core connection. They prefer establishing it once per API request rather than repeating the database round trip for every query. They also explicitly identify the boundary of their helper: an HTTP context accessor returns no user in a message consumer or background job, so those paths require another way to supply identity [OwCgDPa0DnA @ 00:12:32]. Rusin's demonstration obtains the account from a request header and feeds it to the account-scoped Prisma client, with transaction locality handling pooled-connection reuse [ctJy5AxgVXM @ 00:09:56]. Past The Diagram draws a stricter trust boundary: a tenant header is caller-chosen, so the application must use the tenant it resolved after authentication and must not simply trust client input. They also reject connection-persistent SET in favor of SET LOCAL inside a transaction [h1KAW_izGl8 @ 00:01:49]. These are specific differences in provenance and lifetime, not interchangeable recipes. Jovanović demonstrates where EF Core can inject context; Rusin demonstrates how to make scoped access the exported interface; Past The Diagram specifies which source of tenant identity their isolation model accepts.

The Postgres Conference playbook pursues cryptographic isolation rather than only row authorization. Rajan, presenting Baffle's architecture, describes shared tables or logical databases whose tenants' data is encrypted with different keys. The proposed system combines a SQL proxy, database extensions, and a management console. Applications connect to the proxy, which identifies protected values and transforms them before forwarding queries to PostgreSQL [VRoHkeV_-iI @ 00:15:17]. Tenant determination can use a tenant column, a SQL comment, or a session variable; separate logical databases instead allow the database name in the connection to select the tenant key. Their bring-your-own-key model places management with the SaaS provider, while hold-your-own-key retains the tenant's own key-management environment and its associated burden. Copplestone's database-centric alternative focuses on SQL authorization and the security posture inherited by applications above PostgreSQL [vZT1Qx2xUCo @ 00:04:41]. Product Hunt timing also has competing choices in Smith's account: they wanted a weekend for less competition, chose Monday under a deadline using Mark Lou's advice, and afterward questioned whether Monday morning outreach had hurt participation. Their next move is to try a weekend, not claim that the alternate day has already produced a better result [hJtOsLAfxxE @ 00:10:25].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Smith's launch economics are an allocation-of-effort story with a visible result: 17 upvotes, not the product-of-the-day win teased in the opening. They spent hours writing and producing a bespoke video aimed at Product Hunters, but it received two views. Their retrospective redirects that effort toward more engaging outbound content instead of treating an elaborate listing video as essential [hJtOsLAfxxE @ 00:09:04]. The social posts mostly requested support without providing something funny, interesting, or valuable, and promotion began on the day rather than building interest earlier. Their small following also constrained the number of people who could encounter those posts. Rusin reports a different operating cost after isolation works: row policies add evaluation overhead, may change query plans, and need measurement on the application's own data. Logging the claimed and resolved account IDs improves debugging, but their implementation adds a round trip, so they discuss putting it behind a production debug flag [ctJy5AxgVXM @ 00:11:37]. Copplestone likewise revises the attractive idea of blanket SELECT queries: explicit WHERE conditions remain useful for reducing the data considered before policy evaluation. Their performance advice keeps the database-enforced rule while still writing selective application queries [vZT1Qx2xUCo @ 00:15:54].

Several demonstrations show enforcement appearing present while the actual access path escapes it. Jovanović enables the notes policy and still receives every row because the current postgres connection has elevated privileges. The result changes after creating and using the less-privileged application role [OwCgDPa0DnA @ 00:04:51]. Rusin encounters a related but distinct failure: their Prisma role is not a superuser, yet owns the protected tables, so the first migration does not stop the leak. FORCE makes that owner subject to the policy; missing account context then produces empty results [ctJy5AxgVXM @ 00:03:40]. Supabase's advanced email client exposes another path: enabling RLS on emails does not hide the displayed emails because the application reads a view. Setting security_invoker true fixes that path, but folder counts remain wrong until the related email_folders table and its policy are addressed [PdEutzhsrws @ 00:09:50]. Codewithguillaume's orders walkthrough adds the broad-policy trap: the read-all template restores visible data but includes orders belonging to another user. They delete it before installing the own-data rule. These demonstrations identify different causes behind apparently working requests: elevated roles, table ownership, view execution, related-table coverage, and permission conditions that admit too many rows [Vx1q8Nfp0BE @ 00:01:48].

Test the denied action, not just the successful screen. Past The Diagram asks for a test that establishes tenant A's session, queries a known tenant B row, and receives zero rows through the real application role and connection path. They extend that exercise to insert, update, and delete rather than stopping at a read assertion [h1KAW_izGl8 @ 00:03:52]. CyberSecurityTV similarly calls for allow and deny assertions against two seeded tenants in a temporary CI database, plus checks of REST endpoints, views, and RPCs. Their warning is that ordinary functional tests can succeed while another tenant's data remains accessible [3yDr4mrZXAw @ 00:04:45]. Rusin runs a generated concurrency script and reports 800 concurrent requests without contamination, explicitly describing that as a result for the tested configuration rather than proof of every property. They also insist on reading generated tests and making the isolation test fail by removing FORCE [ctJy5AxgVXM @ 00:07:51]. Supabase demonstrates a smaller negative test directly in the table editor: selecting as an authenticated user works, but inserting a post with a blank ownership column fails. Supplying that user's ID succeeds. The exercised action and supplied identity expose whether the intended permission actually exists [hu2SQjvCXIw @ 00:05:33].
