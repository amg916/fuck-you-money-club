## Executive Summary

Build where customers already work, then remove a specific piece of friction. Jack turned client spreadsheets into Profit AI; Andy adapted an established Google Sheets pattern to Airtable; Superpower ChatGPT added missing features inside an existing interface. Shopify operators repeatedly emphasize merchant research, distribution, useful differentiation, and customer support. Their economics vary sharply: Data Fetcher reports $23,000 monthly revenue, while Vlad describes moving a small acquisition from $130 to $200 MRR. Free launches, paid upgrades, newsletters, affiliate partnerships, and acquisitions offer different routes to revenue. The disagreements matter: Andy avoids crowded AI platforms, while Superpower built on ChatGPT; Essential Apps integrates closely with Shopify, while Delight Chat pursues independence. Firsthand failures expose weak validation, delayed user testing, churn, support burdens, and distracting expansion.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with work somebody already does, not an entirely new destination they must learn to visit. Jack built spreadsheets for founders and marketing teams, making profit information easier to read and use. Those spreadsheets contained fixed costs, variable costs, customer-value calculations, an advertising analyzer, and scenario planning. Profit AI brought that reporting into a Shopify app after Jack had already spent time doing the work for clients. The problem was not inventing another dashboard for its own sake; Jack described data collection as time-consuming and the spreadsheets as awkward. Andy reached a similar opportunity through an IPO newsletter project that required financial data in Airtable. Data Fetcher connects other platforms through APIs and schedules imports instead of making users pull data manually. AI Tomorrow Desk describes Blue Dot's starting point as an agency with 15 recruitment consultants whose meeting notes were messy and difficult to keep current. Its Chrome extension records Google Meet calls, transcribes them, and summarizes the discussion without sending a bot into the meeting. Across these accounts, the starting work was already visible: client reporting, moving data, and recording what customers said. Each product removed a repeated task from an existing working environment rather than asking users to adopt a completely unrelated activity. Research means looking at existing behavior and the products people already compare. The Super Lemon founders searched every letter of the alphabet in Shopify's autocomplete, collected the resulting keywords, and examined competing apps. They looked at review velocity as well as average ratings: recent reviews suggested continuing demand, while a weaker leading app suggested room to compete. They did not want to challenge a well-reviewed leader with no obvious opening. Justas likewise looks for competition, merchant problems, differentiation, development difficulty, and whether an app helps merchants earn revenue. Their warning is direct: an empty category can mean there is no real problem, rather than an overlooked prize. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Jack's spreadsheet conversion began with a CSV uploaded to Cursor and an instruction to build it. The next work was identifying incorrect calculations and telling Cursor what the right calculation should be. Jack says the initial build took roughly three and a half to four months, not a single unattended prompt. Their later toolset includes Claude Code, terminal-based Codex, Framer, and Google Sheets. Andy's Data Fetcher uses TypeScript, React, and Airtable's extension SDK, with TypeScript, Postgres, GraphQL, and Node behind the extension. Before building, Andy checks whether the target platform has a public API, marketplace, or extension SDK, then considers platform size, problem frequency, and prices on an established comparison platform. W3 NOTES teaches a separate Chrome implementation path: Manifest V3, Plasmo or WXT for framework support, and chrome.storage for state. The channel warns that service workers sleep after about 30 seconds of inactivity, so simple in-memory variables do not preserve state across that interruption. It also presents XPay.js as a way to integrate Stripe payments without operating a separate payment backend. These are different build arrangements: Jack repeatedly corrected generated calculations, Andy used Airtable's SDK, and W3 NOTES focuses on browser-worker state and extension-specific tooling. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Free access serves different jobs in these businesses, and the founders do not agree on when it should end. Superpower ChatGPT's founder kept every feature free for roughly nine months, started a newsletter after three or four months, and earned the first sponsorship money before charging for the extension. The founder now thinks monetization should have started earlier, while also crediting free access with audience growth. When paid features arrived, existing features remained free. Ericas follows a different transition: launch an app free, collect reviews, wait for organic installs, grandfather existing users, and monetize new users with subscriptions and usage tiers. Jack's Profit AI combines a continuing free plan with a 30-day trial of the paid plan. The paid price fell from $5,000 a month to $800 after only a couple of existing business relationships took the higher offer. Connor Gross describes still other models: Honey earns affiliate commissions from retail partners, while Adblock Plus allows paying companies through its blocking system. Connor calls that latter arrangement ethically questionable while reporting its commercial scale. The practical contrast is explicit in their accounts: subscription upgrades are one route, newsletter sponsorship is another, and some extensions monetize relationships with companies rather than charging the person who installed the tool. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Positive interviews did not save Session Fox. The Super Lemon founders had interviewed 30 people, built for three months, and paid a freelancer to help development. When the SDK reached those prospects, only one integrated it after a month; the other 29 did not, despite follow-ups. Reading The Mom Test changed the questions. Instead of accepting enthusiasm, they asked why somebody claiming to need the product was not already using available alternatives. Reinterviews revealed that people were getting by with analytics or did not care enough to solve the problem. Justas describes an even longer wrong turn before Oberlo: the team invented a problem internally, worked behind closed doors for about a year, and earned 30 cents. With roughly a month of salaries left, they switched to problems some teammates actually experienced in dropshipping and released several apps. Andy reports a different research failure after shipping: almost an entire year passed without speaking to users. One afternoon of user testing exposed UX problems whose fixes improved revenue and usage almost overnight. These are concrete reversals: interview enthusiasm failed to become integration, a year of development failed to produce demand, and an operating product kept carrying usability problems until its founder watched and questioned the people using it. hexdevs [KP61wa0qiSU @ 00:07:53]; hexdevs [KP61wa0qiSU @ 00:11:58]; The SaaS Hub - The Shopify Podcast [Trud8JKFPok @ 00:03:28]; Starter Story [NvtsM8Nk72c @ 00:09:53].

Blue Dot's reversal was about the installation decision as much as the transcription feature. AI Tomorrow Desk reports that the founders first built a Zoom integration, then discovered companies did not want to test a new tool across their entire Zoom account. They pivoted to a Chrome extension focused on Google Meet. Their Product Hunt launch also disappointed: fifth product of the day and a few hundred website visits did less than the founders hoped, while newsletter mentions reportedly multiplied traffic. Superpower's founder faces a different bottleneck: the host platform can remove the business opportunity. The founder says OpenAI could kill the product any day, describes having worried about that for a long time, and expects rebuilding elsewhere would make life harder. Jack's Profit AI dashboard shows another limitation beneath a large revenue headline. Jack reports 43 uninstalls against 47 installs, 122 current users, most of them free, and about 38 or 39 paying customers. Jack also cut the paid price after very limited uptake at $5,000 a month. Their closing advice is not to aim for laziness: the market moves fast and idle builders get replaced. These accounts retain the friction alongside the revenue: reluctance to install, disappointing launch traffic, platform dependence, and customers leaving almost as quickly as new ones arrive. Operating costs and support work remain visible even in the lean businesses. Andy reports roughly $2,500 a month in hosting, $1,000 for SaaS tools, and $150 for coworking, alongside a claimed 85% margin. Ericas reports around 90% profitability, but Kaching also provides technical support around the clock and offers full refunds without questions because a one-star review is too costly a risk in their view. Super Lemon's founders kept support between five and fifteen messages a day by removing repeated confusion inside the product. One question fell from roughly ten daily messages to one or zero after two explanatory sentences were added. They also watched server dashboards, investigated unusual traffic, and added capacity as usage grew rather than starting with elaborate infrastructure. Justas describes a heavier portfolio operation: support staff are developers with Shopify knowledge, and onboarding can take a couple of months to half a year. Automated tagging, workflows, and escalation handoffs support those people rather than eliminating them. Their warning to a lone developer considering ten apps is that success itself can overwhelm support capacity. The economics therefore sit beside specific operating arrangements: Andy's hosting bill, Kaching's review-sensitive refunds, Super Lemon's contextual help, and Essential Apps' trained technical support teams.
