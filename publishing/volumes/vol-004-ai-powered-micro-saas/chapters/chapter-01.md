## Executive Summary

Start where people already spend money or repeatedly do work by hand. Rob Walling reports that 48% of surveyed founders found their problem at work; Thomas turned application packaging into Packager, while Abhishek found dissatisfied Typeform users. ## The Practitioner Consensus

Look first at work somebody already has to finish. Rob Walling reports that 48% of surveyed founders found their opportunity at their day job, where they already understood the workarounds, buying process, and potential customers. Their examples were not abstract inventions: support software missing boring operational features, repeated spreadsheet preparation, and meeting notes written up by hand. Thomas followed that route with Packager. Packaging an application for Microsoft Intune could take an hour, and other IT administrators were describing the same frustration in forums. Existing solutions felt too technical or too expensive, so Thomas built a browser-based tool around one job: deploying applications. Zues Ordaz describes a comparable problem inside their agency, moving between Calendly, Gmail, Typeform, Google Calendar, and a spreadsheet to manage clients. Their proposed product grew from that recurring copy-and-paste work, not from a search for another broad software category. The common starting point is specific operational friction experienced close enough to describe. Thomas also deliberately targeted smaller businesses while competitors focused on customers with millions of devices. That customer boundary mattered as much as the deployment feature: the product served a smaller audience wanting a different tool, rather than attempting to match every incumbent capability. Existing spending is an invitation to investigate, not a reason to abandon a category. Abhishek discovered that users of their earlier chatbot builder mostly wanted conversational forms. Those customers were treating it as a Typeform alternative, and Typeform's price increase exposed an opening. Abhishek did not stop at cheaper pricing: they searched Twitter, Reddit, and the incumbent's own forum for requested features the incumbent was not providing. David and Daniel took a similar approach in AI app building. They inspected competitors' Trustpilot reviews, public roadmaps, and Discord communities, then listened when customers asked to turn websites into mobile apps. Abhishek's advice is to find what people are searching for before building, while David recommends starting with an industry or interest you genuinely know and asking what the established product would look like for people like you. Distribution belongs inside the opportunity decision. Fomin looks for problems people actively search for on YouTube, Google, and sometimes Reddit, then asks how those people will reach the website. Their preferred examples are deliberately ordinary: generating documents, checking information, or converting formats. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Abhishek gives a direct competitor-research sequence: choose a popular tool, search Twitter and Reddit for its name followed by 'alternative,' identify the missing feature or painful price, and approach those users with a basic landing page explaining the replacement. Their bonus step is migration. A customer can paste a Typeform URL into the new product's landing page and receive a converted form within seconds. That makes the proposed switch visible instead of leaving it as a promise. The first version was equally stripped down: name, email, star-rating fields, and CSV export, without Google Sheets integrations or an API. Abhishek says that version took two weeks. David's research adds Trustpilot, public roadmaps, and competitor Discord servers to the search surface; their customer support conversations then supplied a specific request for mobile apps. Screenshot One's founder adds a capacity question before committing: are there enough companies needing the product and willing to pay enough to satisfy the founder's revenue goal? Their example goal was $10,000 MRR. The shared mechanics are concrete: locate buyers already using the category, identify the reason they might switch, make the narrower offer understandable, and keep the initial feature set close to that reason. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The founders do not use the same finish line for validation. Gil argues that friendly feedback and hypothetical willingness to pay can send a builder into months of development with false confidence. Their preferred test is collecting money before building. Screenshot One's founder draws a sharper distinction between support and use: people can even pay for a nonexistent product because they are nice, yet that does not establish that the finished software helps them. Their ultimate validation is at least ten paying customers from outside the founder's network who actually use the product. Rob Walling describes an earlier conversational test for Drip: they asked seventeen other founders about the problem, received ten or eleven positive answers, and then began building. BigIdeasDB gives another threshold: speak with ten people, proceed when three name a price, launch the smallest working version, and charge from day one. Those approaches put different weight on conversation, payment, and observed use. Gil's pre-sale also included a delivery commitment and refund terms; Screenshot One's standard specifically excludes purchases made merely to support the founder. Treat these as different practitioner tests rather than replacing their distinctions with a single universal customer count or a promise that enthusiastic interviews settle the business. Free access and recurring billing split the playbooks just as clearly. Your Average Tech Bro says forever-free access produced weak conversion in their own apps and recommends a short trial followed by paid access. They also prefer one-time purchases where possible: on Perfect Interview, they recall roughly 0.5% conversion for a subscription against about 1.2% to 1.3% for a same-priced one-time offer. Abhishek runs the opposite model for the Typeform alternative, reporting more than 90% of features available free, around 35,000 registered users, roughly 500 paying customers, and conversion between about 1.5% and less than 2%. David reports no free users for Shipper, explaining that the expensive service directs its money toward a better product for paying customers. AI Tomorrow Desk describes My Ask AI with a free-forever plan for one assistant and paid plans offering more assistants, content, and branding removal. These are not interchangeable pricing prescriptions. The same conversation about narrow products contains a paid-only AI builder, a generous free form builder, a customer-service chatbot with a free entry tier, and a founder reporting better conversion from one-time purchases. The reported conversion figures belong to those particular products and offers; the founders themselves chose different ways to collect revenue. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive-looking mistake in these accounts is often building the wrong thing for too long. Your Average Tech Bro recalls six or seven failed apps across the first two years, followed by a change toward proven categories and deliberate marketing. They describe the engineering trap directly: adding features feels productive even when there are no users and no revenue. Screenshot One's founder spent five months preparing the first launch because they wanted everything perfect, then called that a mistake. Their later approach aims for one month or less, starts with one small feature, lets friends click through it for breakage, and reaches prospective customers before building out the surrounding machinery. David's Shipper story gives the other side of early shipping. The initial version broke frequently; the first two months were the toughest, and confidence in the product arrived around month three. By months five and six, David says the team was shipping features competitors lacked. These founders do not describe a polished first release as the prerequisite for finding buyers. They describe wasted time in perfectionism, a rough product that needed improvement, and the need to put marketing and customer contact beside the engineering work rather than indefinitely behind it. [QBajJCMxByw @ 00:00:22] [QBajJCMxByw @ 00:02:11] [TCGXT7ySco8 @ 00:04:19] [Nnpz1wsTjBI @ 00:05:04]

A small product does not automatically produce cheap delivery. Screenshot One's founder initially chose $7 monthly because $5 felt too cheap, then discovered margins were almost zero. They reduced the free plan and raised prices. Later, with around two million screenshots rendered, they report server costs of $3,000 to $4,000 monthly, total expenses around $4,500, and profit margins between 40% and 60%. Gil's Subscribr has a different cost center: around $3,500 monthly in AI compute, about $2,000 in advertising, and another $1,500 for items including scraping, hosting, and email. Gil also says the first ten pre-sale buyers might not even have been profitable at their introductory offer, although the broader pre-sale funded the build. Shawn describes a much lighter operating profile for Sync to Sheets, where the largest monthly bill was under $90 for email infrastructure. Tightening that product's free-plan limits reportedly lifted revenue from $5,000 to more than $8,000. These accounts put concrete operating costs beside the attractive revenue headlines. The founders changed free limits, prices, or spending priorities when those economics demanded it; Gil explicitly favors profit over growth and warns about expensive agencies and distracting product directions eating into that profit. Customer fit and platform dependence can reverse an apparently working opportunity. Screenshot One's cancellation form produced vague answers, so the founder inspected each departing customer's company and use case, then emailed a specific yes-or-no question about the suspected reason. Those conversations revealed customers expecting a no-code product. The founder changed the copy to emphasize developers and the need to write integration code, reporting monthly churn moving from about 11% toward 7%. AI Tomorrow Desk recounts a different reversal for Tony's Blackmagic. A Twitter profile progress bar became a more useful product after adding a Chrome sidebar with analytics and CRM features, but Twitter API pricing changes later forced a sale. The account puts Blackmagic at $14,000 MRR when it sold for $128,000 because API access had become too expensive. Gil's pre-sale carried another explicit exposure: if the product was not delivered successfully, buyers could ask for their money back, and the founder had committed to a 60-day build. Each case names a real pressure point rather than a generic warning: the wrong customer expectation, a changed platform bill, or cash collected against an undelivered product. Finding the narrow problem includes understanding the particular promises and dependencies attached to solving it.
