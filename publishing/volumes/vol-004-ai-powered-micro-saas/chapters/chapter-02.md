# Chapter 02: Choose a Vertical and Examine Its Defensibility

*Vertical versus horizontal SaaS, niche AI solutions, specialized data, agents replacing business functions, wrapper risks, and competing views on AI disruption*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Rob Walling favors vertical SaaS for founders pursuing focused, capital-efficient businesses; Jordan Crawford and Abdalla Emam put buyer knowledge ahead of easily copied features. Jake Heller shows the machinery behind specialization: legal databases, document integrations, OCR handling, chained prompts, and extensive tests. Jamie Cuffe targets insurance work already outsourced under documented procedures and reports 50–75% customer cost savings. Bessemer reports vertical AI contracts reaching around 80% of existing software contract values among overlapping customers. The disagreement is substantial: Sean and BergTube describe seat-based software giving way to outcomes, while Practical Founders argues for software plus AI plus data. MicroConf challenges switching-cost defenses; Sahill Poddar emphasizes relationships, capital, and services beyond software. Wrapper speed opens the door; workflow execution, customer understanding, and operating depth determine what follows.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Rob Walling defines vertical SaaS as software built for a specific industry: dental practice management, real-estate CRM, legal case tracking, or contractor project management. The attraction is depth rather than breadth. These products tend to be integral to running the customer’s business, not optional conveniences, and Walling reports lower churn among the vertical companies across their investments. The narrower market can still support the founder’s intended business; Walling contrasts venture-scale ambitions with a founder seeking a much smaller recurring revenue company and an eventual sale. The argument is not that every vertical beats every horizontal product, but that less sophisticated, less funded competition can make the bootstrap route easier [FlJaIqI0N10 @ 00:05:51]. Sean makes the same industry-specific choice through Clockless, a billing automation product for small law firms, describing domain language and an exact customer problem as the opening against broad platforms [GK9LZkd6aHM @ 00:02:05]. Jordan Crawford puts the commercial advantage in narrow expertise connected to buying behavior. Customers’ reasons for purchasing often remain in their heads or emerge only during calls; Crawford says models do not automatically possess that knowledge. A tightly defined segment lets a company turn those insights into targeted workflows, focused messaging, and a better buying experience instead of researching many disconnected personas [RINTsdOTWfc @ 00:01:31].

Abdalla Emam separates using a model API from having no durable advantage. Building on infrastructure is ordinary; the dangerous position is a product whose value disappears when the underlying model adds a feature. Emam identifies proprietary data, workflow ownership, and vertical specialization as concrete forms of defensibility. Data becomes valuable when it comes from actual cases, decisions, and operating workflows. Workflow ownership means handling activities such as insurance claims, caregiver scheduling, customer support pipelines, or internal documentation deeply enough that replacement becomes painful [NwYdSzPW6yg @ 00:02:47]. New Machina describes vertical agents through the same domain depth: specialized language, data, workflows, and connections to existing industry systems. An agent does work through natural-language interaction or autonomous execution rather than merely presenting menus and forms; relevant integrations include healthcare records and sales CRMs [orVropqNnCA @ 00:01:19]. Practical Founders makes the incumbent version of this argument with concrete-pumping software. Understanding how the trade operates, how work is priced, and how customers interact with the surrounding ecosystem is not something a generalized model automatically reproduces. The investment thesis combines software, AI, proprietary workflow information, customer knowledge, and relationships. Automation adds value to that existing base rather than requiring the business to discard its system of record [Avxi60Ny5SM @ 00:34:12].

Y Combinator’s vertical-agent thesis expands the target beyond the software budget. Traditional SaaS still requires people to enter data, operate workflows, and complete the surrounding work. The proposed agent business combines software and work previously performed by people, reaching payroll expenditure as well as application spending. The panel argues that this could make vertical-agent companies much larger than their SaaS equivalents; it is their market thesis, not a description of every existing agent business [ASABxNenD_U @ 00:20:27]. Bessemer supplies a specific commercial comparison: Litify runs the core workflow of personal-injury law firms, while EvenUp delivers demand-letter work using AI behind the scenes. Customers compare that service with expensive labor and the value of its output, rather than simply treating it as another software subscription. Bessemer also reports customers taking on more work, not merely cutting headcount [icnddcX1NFA @ 00:04:40]. Jamie Cuffe applies the same budget logic at Pace by starting with insurance processes already outsourced at scale. Those customers already have operating procedures, accuracy checks, and a spending line to examine. Pace prioritizes high-volume work with substantial potential ROI, commonly submission intake or claims intake, before moving further through policy administration or claims operations. The initial target is an existing operating activity with an established handoff, not a request for the customer to invent an AI use case [Uqr2U24uxOs @ 00:11:30].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Crawford’s selection exercise starts by imagining the product expanded into every candidate vertical, then asking which customer would receive vastly more value over the following years. Crawford recommends Claude for inspiration and potentially ChatGPT for research, looking for less competition, more customer value, and a way to deliver a permissionless value proposition. Exploration should happen serially rather than through ten simultaneous attempts: pick a segment, pursue it, and use the contrast between attempts to identify where buying is easier. That focus also means declining some customers who could benefit, in order to serve the stronger segment [RINTsdOTWfc @ 00:06:09]. Sean makes the next move explicit: have a real buyer waiting and use a conversation to avoid months of building the wrong thing [GK9LZkd6aHM @ 00:03:07]. Y Combinator’s examples show how close observation produces that starting point. Sweetspot’s founders noticed a friend repeatedly refreshing a government website for opportunities to bid. Another founder went to work with their dentist parent and observed the repetitive claims-processing workload, then began writing software for that clinic. The panel’s instruction is to seek boring, repetitive administrative work connected to experience or an existing relationship. The opportunity begins with watching an actual task, not choosing an industry label and assuming its workflows [ASABxNenD_U @ 00:40:37].

Simon Høiberg starts from a production problem their own team experiences: generic stock footage is undesirable, while shooting custom B-roll consumes time. The demonstrated product accepts uploaded images, creates a character, and generates clips featuring that character. Lovable supplies the interface, Supabase supplies the backend through Lovable Cloud, and Replicate runs the models. Høiberg uploads images as a ZIP, starts a fine-tuning job, generates a character image, and passes that image to a video model as the starting frame. This is a specialized output assembled from models rather than another general chat interface [QIA9Ry42Lbs @ 00:05:13]. Jake Heller’s legal workflow uses a different chain but an equally explicit definition of the end result. Start with the research memo the lawyer wants, then work backward through search queries, retrieved cases, reading, notes, an outline, and cited writing. Heller describes a dozen or two dozen individual prompts, with tests for each stage and gold-standard expected answers [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:21:14]. The surrounding application also matters: legal document-management connections, proprietary annotations, and OCR handling for handwriting, tilted scans, and four printed pages squeezed onto one sheet. Heller describes dozens of requirements before the language model even receives the information, followed by careful formatting, prompting, and evaluation [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:25:40].

Cuffe’s deployment sequence begins with the insurance carrier’s operations leadership and a real task, not dummy demonstration data. Pace defines success criteria upfront, works onsite, and takes in existing procedures that may span 50–100 pages and around 60 steps. The team converts those instructions into agent operating procedures expressed in a document-like interface. Agents use tools for difficult document retrieval, rules, and writing back into industry systems; Pace uses APIs where available and web agents where they are not [Uqr2U24uxOs @ 00:35:32]. Sahill Poddar describes an adjacent data-to-action chain in financing: use language models to resolve entities in bank transactions, categorize debt, expenses, revenue, and missed payments, then feed the transformed time series into a model that projects cash flows. The work strengthens underwriting rather than stopping at a display of financial insights [y6eOdHpH5W8 @ 00:37:32]. Bessemer reports that most of its AI-enabled services businesses operate at gross margins above 60%, with model costs only a fraction of the cost base. The value sits in workflow, know-how, go-to-market execution, customer success, and last-mile operations. Its explanation is specific: adding models and people alone is insufficient; differentiated delivery requires custom functionality, processes, and expertise [icnddcX1NFA @ 00:10:21].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Sean argues for an AI-native rebuild: intelligence should be the product’s foundation rather than a chatbot added to conventional software. Clockless is the example because reading meetings, extracting billable time, and calculating otherwise lost revenue constitute the product itself [GK9LZkd6aHM @ 00:03:21]. BergTube takes the replacement argument further, describing vertical agents that find, qualify, and book leads instead of merely tracking them. Its proposed shift is from tools humans operate to systems delivering outcomes, including payment when a call is booked or a deal closes [ -_NbEobmiAY @ 00:03:24]. Practical Founders rejects the blanket conclusion that B2B SaaS is dead. Its alternative is software plus AI plus data: preserve the useful system of record, automate more of the work, improve internal efficiency, and add capabilities customers can pay for. Existing companies can build new AI-enabled products without abandoning their operating base [Avxi60Ny5SM @ 00:29:39]. The CNBC guest also distinguishes threatened products from software generally. The interview describes a transition from systems of record to systems of action, but says AI is not eating all software. Thin products that are not embedded in deep business workflows face the sharper threat. The guest also describes proactive agent reliability as still developing, rather than treating every demonstrated action as ready for mainstream business use [Dc-hThrOqF8 @ 00:03:59].

Walling regards embedded workflows and high switching costs as contributors to lower churn. Expansion revenue can then produce net negative churn: revenue grows in a month even without adding customers, provided pricing supports expansion [FlJaIqI0N10 @ 00:03:28]. The DNSimple founder speaking at MicroConf challenges that protection directly. AI tools can help customers migrate between providers, and features become faster to reproduce. The speaker is less certain about integrations because connected systems keep changing, but still questions treating integration work as a durable moat. The proposed defenses instead emphasize collaboration and network effects, regulated environments, operational excellence, and capital-intensive infrastructure [msH7lUkWZAY @ 00:02:27]. Emam retains workflow ownership as a defensibility category, specifically because replacing deeply embedded operating processes is painful. That is a different emphasis from MicroConf’s skepticism about switching barriers [NwYdSzPW6yg @ 00:03:04]. Poddar supplies another alternative: move beyond pure software. Financing has capital requirements, funding relationships, history, and scale that a software interface alone does not reproduce. Poddar contrasts having billions in lending capacity with having tens of millions and describes raising a major debt facility as work that takes months. Their defense rests partly on what the business can fund and execute outside the application, not just how the application looks [y6eOdHpH5W8 @ 00:43:55].

Heller’s CoCounsel engineering emphasizes decomposing a professional task into tightly understood stages, each with prompts and extensive tests. A research workflow can contain a dozen or two dozen prompts, and the team knows what a good intermediate answer should look like [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:23:22]. Cuffe pushes against constraining AI inside small workflow boxes surrounded by deterministic code. Pace aims for agents that follow an operating procedure end to end, retaining the context needed to make decisions across the process. Cuffe argues that fragmented AI nodes miss part of what current agents can do; the longer-term direction includes learning from gradable end-to-end outputs [Uqr2U24uxOs @ 00:09:17]. Bessemer’s commercial alternatives are similarly distinct: a copilot improves an existing worker, an agent performs work associated with internal headcount, and an AI-enabled service addresses outsourced spending. These are separate delivery models rather than one mandatory product shape [icnddcX1NFA @ 00:06:50]. Pricing also remains contested. Against declarations that seats must disappear, the MicroConf speaker recommends experimenting with outcome pricing while noting that subscriptions and other charging methods can coexist. Token costs affect the economics, but the task is to find where each pricing mechanism fits rather than assume that every product needs one universal replacement model [msH7lUkWZAY @ 00:09:45].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Heller spent years trying to get lawyers to annotate case law and contribute content. The model borrowed inspiration from user-generated communities, but Heller calls it a total failure: lawyers billed by the hour, were short on time, and would not donate enough work. Casetext pivoted toward natural-language processing and automated approaches instead [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:06:43]. Even better technology was not automatically enough to move the market. Heller recalls established lawyers seeing incremental improvements as a reason to change a profitable practice they preferred to leave alone. The reaction changed when customers perceived the technology shift as fundamental rather than marginal [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:08:38]. On Verticals, Luke recounts signing an early customer worth almost a million dollars in annual contract value, promising the requested enterprise product, and failing to deliver what was promised. Rebuilding trust took years. The lesson Luke draws is not to oversell; the team had not understood how long the enterprise requirements would take [y6eOdHpH5W8 @ 00:16:51]. Poddar answers with Paraffin’s own selling discipline: offer the minimal valuable product, identify the single strongest value proposition, and return to the drawing board if that alone is not worth buying. Additional capabilities can then delight the customer instead of becoming unpaid promises [y6eOdHpH5W8 @ 00:17:57].

Høiberg’s B-roll demonstration exposes a concrete operational hole: authentication was omitted while a mock user simplified the prototype. Publishing that version would let anyone generate clips against Høiberg’s Replicate account. Høiberg explicitly tells builders to resolve the security issues before publishing, and also explains that the smooth demonstration leaves out the back-and-forth normally required with Lovable [QIA9Ry42Lbs @ 00:11:33]. Heller’s reliability problems sit deeper inside the workflow. Changing a prompt to fix one set of tests can break another set, which is why the team treated prompting as test-driven development rather than an exercise in attractive demonstrations [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:24:42]. Customer trust is unforgiving in the legal setting Heller served: small mistakes generated immediate complaints, and a poor first encounter could cause a busy lawyer to postpone trying AI again. The first week therefore had to work well enough for customers to invest in the product [eBVi_sLaYsc @ 00:30:03]. MicroConf’s DNSimple founder makes operating quality a central defense for the same broad reason. As code creation gets easier, the speaker expects pressure to shift toward running dependable systems; customers encountering fragile one-off applications still value technical execution. The proposed advantage is operational excellence, not simply the quantity of generated features [msH7lUkWZAY @ 00:05:08].

Cuffe describes a large traditional outsourcing business with unchanged economics as Pace’s failure case. The ambition is to move from roughly 10% gross margins toward 80%, with product improvements continually lifting margins; Cuffe does not present that target as the company’s already achieved result. An expanding service business still needs a path to better delivery economics [Uqr2U24uxOs @ 00:06:28]. Bessemer’s observed companies provide a separate comparison: most of its AI-enabled services businesses were operating above 60% gross margins, supported by specialized workflows and operating expertise [icnddcX1NFA @ 00:10:34]. Valen’s discussion adds the performance-pricing exposure: when revenue depends on customer success, models, optimization, and trust affect the vendor’s margins. Outcome alignment is presented as operational complexity as well as a source of retention [CJf8KAHBVKA @ 00:02:49]. Poddar supplies an expansion check from actual platform screening. Paraffin examines merchant growth, acquisition, and churn before deciding whether lending makes economic sense. Poddar says platforms below roughly a billion dollars in payments generally should concentrate on their core business before adding lending, while acknowledging exceptions. Paraffin has also turned off large platforms because merchant churn was too high. Scale alone did not compensate for deterioration in the underlying customer base [y6eOdHpH5W8 @ 00:23:39].
