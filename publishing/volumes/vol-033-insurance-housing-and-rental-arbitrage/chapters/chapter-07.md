## Executive Summary

Midterm rental operators agree the play is a furnished, lease-based stay of roughly 28 nights to six months aimed at travel nurses, insurance-displaced families, corporate relocators and remote workers. Crossing the 30-day line moves the unit under landlord-tenant law and out of most STR rules, so leases, deposits and screening become the core product. The numbers cluster tightly: furnishing at $7 to $15 per square foot, rent at 1.5 to 2 times long-term rates or 30 to 65% above them, 80 to 92% occupancy, and furniture paid back in 6 to 12 months. Street mechanics include 28-night default minimums with last-minute step-downs, 72-hour lease deadlines, dedicated deposit accounts and banning Zelle-style payments. The splits: Sean Rakidzich says prioritizing monthly stays unforced is the wrong bias, Midterm Rental Consulting says Airbnb is mandatory, and Furnished Finder pitches its commission-free lead model.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on this roster draws the midterm line in roughly the same place: a furnished stay of about a month or longer that sits between the weekend Airbnb and the 12-month lease. Sarah Karakaian and Annette Grant set the floor at 28-plus nights and say 99.9% of these units are furnished [264jEA_cXOw @ 00:00:44]. Clint Coons's guest, Dr. Rachel Gainsbrugh, runs it from 39 days to about six months and flags that legal definitions shift state to state [2WVMiZV3TYI @ 00:05:06]. The tenant pool is consistent across speakers, and it is people with a concrete reason to be in town: travel nurses on 13-week contracts are the biggest bloc, followed by insurance-displaced families, corporate relocators, military trainees, first responders and remote workers. The Furnished Rental Strategist counts nurses, corporate, insurance, relocation and digital nomads and says flatly, "That's five different demand sources" [iMXOGF4Engc @ 00:03:50]. Bailey Kramer frames the unit as "a temporary house," not an experience product: no hot tubs or murals, just the place a night-shift nurse or a family that lost their home can actually live [De_DgYIghMo @ 00:00:44]. That positioning drives every downstream decision on furniture, pricing, screening and paperwork, and nobody on the panel argues for building a midterm unit around Airbnb aesthetics or party-weekend demand.

The second point of agreement is legal posture. Cross the 30-day mark and you are no longer running a hotel-style rental; you are a landlord. The Furnished Rental Strategist says 30-plus-day stays fall under landlord-tenant law rather than STR rules, which is exactly why Arizona operators facing HOA limits, caps and permit lotteries pivot here [iMXOGF4Engc @ 00:02:45]. Clint Coons states it bluntly from the asset-protection side: 30-day stays bypass short-term rental regulation [2WVMiZV3TYI @ 00:34:07]. The flip side is that tenant law now applies to you. Karakaian and Grant say "you must have a lease in place" for anything 28 nights or longer, covering rent, dates, eviction process, pets, energy caps and furniture damage, built from a state lease template [264jEA_cXOw @ 00:07:24]. Cynthia Meyer adds the traps that sit inside that same landlord-tenant framework: local limits on unrelated occupants, which can kill a plan to stack four travel nurses in a four-bedroom, plus HOA rules, zoning and fair housing screening criteria [icpm0fFsk6Y @ 00:06:26]. The consensus is not that midterm is unregulated. It is that the 30-day line swaps one rulebook for another, and the operators who win treat the lease as the core product document rather than an afterthought.

On money, the speakers converge on a middle-lane thesis: midterm beats a long-term lease on gross, trails a hot short-term rental per night, and wins back the gap through fewer vacancies and turnovers. Cynthia Meyer cites about one-third to 50% more gross than a long-term rental, with fewer turnovers [icpm0fFsk6Y @ 00:03:04]. Furnished Finder CEO Jeff Hurst tells hosts to underwrite with long-term rent as the worst case, expect furniture to pay back in 6 to 12 months, then collect 30 to 50% more yield, and says occupancy can reach the mid-90s [etWVAe5zZDc @ 00:07:09]. Mindful Rental Pros concede that "in most cases, the per night yield might drop," but say fewer vacancies offset it and midterm often beats long-term and can beat short-term over time [rIPWQCsi2z8 @ 00:06:00]. Midterm Rental Consulting backs the thesis with a live portfolio: 92% occupancy, less than one week of vacancy between rentals, and rents 35% above long-term comps on single-family homes and 45% above on condos since 2017 [GC5LW4scQwo @ 00:01:11]. The baseline prerequisites repeat across speakers too: a furnished unit near hospitals, employers or universities, utilities and fast Wi-Fi bundled in, and a landlord willing to run screening, inspections and leases instead of hands-off nightly automation.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Furnishing math is where the numbers get specific. Bailey Kramer's rule of thumb is $10 to $15 per square foot including assembly labor, so a 1,000-square-foot unit lands at $10,000 to $15,000, with a one-bedroom closer to $10,000; over 90% of their furniture comes from Wayfair, delivery lands in under a week, and they say to wait until closing before ordering [De_DgYIghMo @ 00:03:12]. Jeff Hurst puts the midterm build at roughly $7 per square foot against $45 to $50 for a competitive short-term rental [etWVAe5zZDc @ 00:41:06]. Jesse Vasquez buys mid-grade overall but spends on high-touch items, quoting Rob Abasolo's "don't buy nice, you buy thrice," skipping IKEA beds and Rachael Ray cookware, running Nectar Lux mattresses and all-white Costco Hotel Signature linens that can be bleached [VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:25:43]. The Furnished Rental Strategist's spec sheet: queen beds, quality mattresses, dining for four to six, a desk, a smart TV, two sheet sets per bed and two towels per guest, utilities and internet bundled at a 400 Mbps minimum on a mesh network [xbiJ4HzellI @ 00:03:34]. Kramer's setup clock runs 1 to 1.5 days for a one-bedroom, about two days for a two-bedroom and 3 to 4 days for a three-bedroom.

Pricing starts from comps and a refusal to discount for length of stay. Karakaian and Grant argue flexibility is a premium and give a Columbus two-bedroom, one-bath benchmark: about $1,800 as a long-term lease, $3,500 to $4,000 as a short-term rental and $2,800 to $3,200 as a midterm, with comps pulled from Furnished Finder, Craigslist and Zillow and a search for "corporate rentals" to surface higher-priced listings [264jEA_cXOw @ 00:06:10]. Katie Lion of Furnished Finder sets base rent at "one and a half to two times long-term rates" and a deposit of one-third to one-half of a month [JG03P3JsuHc @ 00:25:43]. Bailey Kramer says minimum-stay settings are about 80% of what matters, more than price: a 28-night default, then last-minute step-downs to a two-night minimum inside 7 days, three nights inside 14 and four nights inside 21, plus a 90-night minimum on anything booked 90-plus days out [Fz00u2_mgqE @ 00:02:18]. Sean Rakidzich runs a nightly rate with separate 3- and 4-day discounts, adds 14- and 28-day discounts, and sets the 28-night discount from observed Airbnb monthly comps, used for slow season only [pjQc-j6P4_0 @ 00:10:51]. Kramer's rules get adjusted weekly, never set-and-forget, and orphan gaps of 2 to 14 nights drop to a two-night minimum.

The paperwork and payment stack is just as prescriptive. Jesse Vasquez keeps leases to one or two pages, writes a $25-per-day late fee after the 5th, sets deposits anywhere from half to a full month, screens individuals for a 650-plus credit score, never hands over keys before first month's rent, and refuses Zelle, Venmo and Cash App after getting burned by chargebacks in 2017 [VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:08:09]. Midterm Rental Consulting runs email, conversation, then lease on a modified Colorado template, e-signed, with a hard rule: "you have 72 hours to sign this lease," and the same 72-hour clock on the deposit [r-8W10XEuGg @ 00:05:06]. The same channel parks every deposit in a dedicated per-property savings account, separate from the operating checking account, and documents every deduction [PLf1d4djjZw @ 00:04:40]. On lead flow, Furnished Finder costs $99 per year per property by that channel's figures, while Airbnb takes 3%; yet Airbnb produced 7 of their last 10 rentals, Furnished Finder one, and Zillow Rental Manager delivered an 18-month tenant at 40% above long-term rent with zero host fee and an optional $30 monthly boost [GC5LW4scQwo @ 00:05:14]. Their operating rule: be on at least two platforms, and one of them must be Airbnb.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Furnish to the temporary-house spec
   - Budget the build, wait for closing, then batch-deliver and assemble.
   - Budget $10 to $15 per square foot, order only after closing since most items arrive in under a week, batch deliveries with a vacation hold, prioritize blackout curtains, a comfortable bed, white noise and a desk, and get a dumpster [Bailey Kramer — De_DgYIghMo @ 00:03:59].
2. Price off comps and set minimum-stay rules
   - Anchor rent to comps, then program minimums before touching price.
   - Set a 28-night default, step down to 2, 3 and 4-night minimums inside 7, 14 and 21 days, require 90 nights on bookings 90-plus days out, and revisit minimums and prices weekly [Bailey Kramer — Fz00u2_mgqE @ 00:02:18].
3. List on at least two platforms including Airbnb
   - Run Airbnb plus at least one lead platform such as Zillow or Furnished Finder.
   - Airbnb at a 3% fee produced 7 of the last 10 rentals; pair it with Zillow Rental Manager (free to hosts, optional $30 monthly boost) or Furnished Finder at $99 per year per property [Midterm Rental Consulting — GC5LW4scQwo @ 00:06:24].
4. Sign, collect, then inspect monthly
   - Lock the lease and first month's rent before keys, then inspect every month.
   - Collect first month's rent before handing over keys, avoid Zelle, Venmo and Cash App, and send someone every month between the 25th and 30th with 48-hour notice; skipped inspections let a treehouse get damaged during an 8-month insurance stay [Jesse Vasquez — VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:14:44].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Sean Rakidzich runs 155 arbitrage doors and opens with the bluntest line on the board: "Midterm rentals don't exist." [pjQc-j6P4_0 @ 00:02:09] To Rakidzich the real dividing line is whether a lease gets signed, so one unit serves nightly and monthly guests alike, with roughly 30% of units on monthly stays at any moment to lighten the housekeeping load. Going midterm-only is reserved for markets where regulation forces it, and chasing monthly stays without that pressure is called "the wrong bias" [pjQc-j6P4_0 @ 00:13:29]. Jeff Hurst, CEO of Furnished Finder, flips the frame and tells hosts that "Continuing to STR is probably the worst," pitching a hybrid where operators block 60 to 120 days for monthly tenants instead of selling random $600 weekends [etWVAe5zZDc @ 00:22:36]. Mindful Rental Pros sit in the middle: run STR at peak and MTR in shoulder seasons, because short-term calendars can flood with bookings and then crash with seasonality, and nobody has to pick one model forever [rIPWQCsi2z8 @ 00:09:08]. The real fork is regulatory and seasonal, not ideological: arbitrage operators like Rakidzich keep both levers live and spend scarce permitted bookings in peak season, while owners stuck in capped or banned markets commit harder to thirty-plus-day leases and treat monthly tenants as the whole business.

Channel strategy splits hard, even on fees. Midterm Rental Consulting sourced 7 of their last 10 tenants through Airbnb at a 3% host fee, calls the algorithms obnoxious but worth it, and sets a hard rule: be on at least two platforms, and one of them must be Airbnb [GC5LW4scQwo @ 00:06:24]. The same consultant pays $99 per year per property for Furnished Finder, skips auto-renew, and describes it as closer to Facebook Marketplace than a booking engine [r-8W10XEuGg @ 00:02:15]. Jeff Hurst quotes a $199 annual lead platform with no commissions and pegs Airbnb's take at 15.5% against Furnished Finder's 1 to 2%, so the fee argument depends on whose math an operator trusts [etWVAe5zZDc @ 00:18:13]. Rakidzich separately pushes back on any playbook that leads with Furnished Finder or apartments.com instead of Airbnb. Jesse Vasquez builds a third lane, prioritizing Zillow Rentals because insurers search it, and openly doubts CHBO after one booking there in roughly three years, telling viewers to take it with a grain of salt [VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:21:33]. The Clint Coons interview reads CHBO the opposite way, arguing a single booking more than covers the plan, so the platform stack an operator builds depends on which tenant avatar they chase.

Pricing doctrine is the widest gap on the board. The Airbnb Hosting and Short Term Rental Tips hosts refuse length-of-stay discounts outright, arguing that flexibility is convenience and convenience is a premium [264jEA_cXOw @ 00:04:57]. Rakidzich does the opposite, stacking 3- and 4-day discounts plus a 28-night discount set from observed Airbnb monthly comps, switched on only in slow season [pjQc-j6P4_0 @ 00:10:51]. Bailey Kramer says price barely matters next to minimum-stay rules, which they rate at 80% of the game: a 28-night default, then 2-, 3-, and 4-night minimums inside 7, 14, and 21 days, plus short minimums to fill orphan gaps [Fz00u2_mgqE @ 00:01:48]. Kramer also insists the rules get adjusted weekly, never set and forgotten. The size of the midterm premium is just as contested. Katie Lion of Furnished Finder sets base rent at 1.5 to 2 times long-term rates with deposits of one-third to one-half month, while Jesse Vasquez takes half to a full month and Cynthia Meyer budgets only one-third to 50% more gross than a long-term lease [JG03P3JsuHc @ 00:25:43]. Midterm Rental Consulting lands 35 to 45% above long-term comps, and Hurst quotes 30 to 50% more yield once furniture pays back, while modeling long-term rent as the worst case.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Jesse Vasquez learned the lease lesson the expensive way: starting in 2015 with generic leases that could not be enforced, then getting burned in 2017 by a peer-to-peer payment chargeback, which is why Venmo, Zelle, and Cash App are now off the table and keys never move before first month's rent [VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:04:07]. The sharper loss came from skipped inspections, when an eight-month insurance stay damaged a treehouse property; the fix is a monthly walk-through between the 25th and 30th with 48-hour notice, because you want to send somebody every single month [VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:16:04]. Midterm Rental Consulting rejects the Furnished Finder damage waiver because its terms make claims too hard, assumes deposit mishandling drives about 90% of non-eviction landlord court cases, and parks every deposit in a dedicated per-property savings account with every deduction documented [PLf1d4djjZw @ 00:01:20]. The Airbnb Hosting hosts add the calendar trap: never block dates until the lease is signed, because tire kickers are everywhere and anyone selling an easy button is lying, and with average stays around three months, expect three to four lease-ups a year [264jEA_cXOw @ 00:19:55]. Contracts can end early or extend, so the calendar is never truly locked in, even after signing.

Bailey Kramer, who ran 30 short-term rentals, draws the line operators blur: an MTR is a temporary house, not an epic weekend experience, and people skimp on the bed, while blackout curtains, a white noise machine for night-shift nurses, and a real desk are treated as non-negotiable [De_DgYIghMo @ 00:07:22]. Kramer also warns against ordering furniture before closing because delays happen, insists on a dumpster after once hauling flattened boxes in a Tesla, and flags that a 0% Amex Business Blue Plus card turns crazy expensive if the balance is not cleared, so cash stays on hand [De_DgYIghMo @ 00:09:12]. The Furnished Rental Strategist hits the same nerve from the décor side, warning that too much Airbnb aesthetics can backfire in the MTR world and that the unit should feel like a furnished apartment, not a staged magazine home [xbiJ4HzellI @ 00:04:17]. Jesse Vasquez supplies the replacement-cost trap: cheap gear gets bought three times, IKEA beds and Rachael Ray cookware are out, and a Roomba loses to an old-school Hoover, summed up as simple scales, fancy fails [VtuboLMzJj0 @ 00:35:05]. Clint Coons's guest adds a revenue trap: launching non-pet-friendly left money on the table until flipping the switch raised revenue 30%.

The economics checks are where optimistic pro formas get corrected fast. Cynthia Meyer expects about two vacant months a year, budgets at 80 to 85% occupancy, and holds a three-month cash reserve, while also flagging occupancy limits on unrelated tenants, such as four travel nurses in a four-bedroom [icpm0fFsk6Y @ 00:07:54]. The Furnished Rental Strategist's Chandler math shows how thin the cushion runs: $3,500 rent minus $300 utilities and $80 internet nets $3,120 before the mortgage, with $12,000 of furniture taking about a year to pay back, and the strategist calls it not a totally passive play [xbiJ4HzellI @ 00:10:50]. Hurst draws the hard boundaries, saying the model does not work for 5-bedroom units or in Aspen and that hybrids should stop selling random weekends that break midterm blocks [etWVAe5zZDc @ 00:08:08]. Midterm Rental Consulting delivers the pricing reality check: renters do not care about your mortgage, so the consultant prices in basement, regular, and reach tiers, assumes 80 to 85% occupancy, and admits that the 92% they personally run comes from years of knowing when to adjust [PLf1d4djjZw @ 00:13:30]. Bailey Kramer applies the same discipline to minimum-stay settings, adjusting them weekly instead of setting and forgetting, because orphan-gap rules are worth hundreds, if not thousands, per property.
