# Chapter 12: Insuring the Rental Operation

*Homeowners versus landlord insurance, lessor's risk only (LRO) coverage, general liability for rented premises, renters liability, insuring co-living houses, flexible rental policies, security deposits, damage waivers, and deposit alternatives*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Every practitioner here lands on the same verdict: once a tenant, guest, or room renter moves in, the homeowner's policy is a dead letter, and the carrier's first claim question is who was living there. The fix is a dwelling fire policy, pushed to DP3 with replacement cost, loss of rent, vandalism, and water coverage, stacked with $500,000 to $1 million of liability and an umbrella. Hannigan prices the switch at about $1,200 a year against an $895 Michigan home policy; Steadily and BiggerPockets peg the landlord premium around 25 percent higher. Scaled operators move to scheduled or monthly reporting policies, with American Modern bundling up to 30 homes on one bill. Splits show up on self-insuring a dozen doors, skipping deposits on Airbnb but charging them on Vrbo, and surety versus true-insurance deposit replacements.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The loudest point of agreement is blunt: the day a tenant moves in, the homeowner's policy stops matching the house, and the carrier will use that mismatch against you. Justin and Aaron of The Phoenix Insurance quote the contract mechanics directly, saying the homeowner form pays losses when the primary title owner is occupying the home, which is exactly the language a dwelling fire policy strips out [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:01:02]. Real Property Management Palisade frames the trap as silent: an owner moves out, leaves the policy untouched, and the failure mode is not a higher premium but a denied claim after a fire, burst pipe, or liability suit, because the carrier's first question is who was living in the home [aUKpl6xobwU @ 00:00:20]. Steadily adds that an insurer can cancel a homeowner policy at any time once the owner is not residing there, and can deny compensation on a claim [8qVhmrP1DZM @ 00:00:32]. Sylvia White at Blue Fox Properties says claims get denied every single day for this reason, and most landlords only learn it after a major loss [7sscnFcWkp4 @ 00:00:47]. Across agents, property managers, and investors, nobody treats the homeowner form as a gray-area workaround; every speaker treats it as uninsured exposure wearing a policy number.

The replacement product carries several names, and the consensus is to learn them before calling an agent. The Phoenix Insurance says the industry term is dwelling fire, sold in three tiers: DP1 as the bare-bones form, DP2 as a step up, and DP3 as the best level, adding replacement cost, loss of rent, and in some cases foundation and water damage add-ons [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:05:10]. Blue Fox Properties lists four pillars a landlord form covers: the structure against fire, storms, vandalism, and burst pipes; landlord liability for a broken step or loose handrail; loss of rent while a fire or water loss is repaired; and limited personal property for the appliances, window coverings, and light fixtures you supply [7sscnFcWkp4 @ 00:02:37]. Equally unanimous is what the policy will not touch. Jesse Vasquez of BiggerPockets says a landlord form covers owner items only when they maintain the property, like a lawnmower or snow blower in a locked shed, and that Vasquez's own lease requires tenants to carry renters insurance [bfsA8QKK2ZU @ 00:04:48]. Bryan Gutowsky pushes the same lease requirement, noting it shifts some exposure off the owner but never all of it [UUWJRIHQaqk @ 00:04:03].

Practitioners also agree on why the landlord form costs more, and on why shaving that premium is a losing trade. Ryan Hannigan of Hannigan Insurance Agency gives the street price: roughly $100 a month, or $1,200 a year, for a landlord policy against a typical $895 Michigan home policy, then warns that across 17 years of watching owners try to fool the carrier, it always backfires [aGvo7Dq3gTM @ 00:02:46]. Vasquez cites the Insurance Information Institute figure that a landlord policy runs about 25 percent more than a homeowner policy on the exact same property, driven by tenants generating more claims than owners [bfsA8QKK2ZU @ 00:05:30]. The Phoenix Insurance hosts push the honesty rule into short-term territory: stays under six months count as short-term rentals, not every dwelling fire form covers them, and misrepresenting the use means you may collect nothing on the claim, with carriers no longer forgiving it next time [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:03:38]. The CoLiving Insider extends the same rule to rent-by-the-room houses, calling it the single most important instruction: tell the insurance company exactly how the house is used, because most standard carriers will not write room rentals and a cheap mismatched policy may be worth nothing [RyLyOzBiWEs @ 00:02:09].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Once the form is right, the mechanics shift to building limits that actually pay at claim time. Sylvia White at Blue Fox Properties runs a seven-item checklist: replacement cost so repairs pay at today's prices, extended or guaranteed dwelling coverage as a buffer when construction costs spike, ordinance and law for code upgrades common in older homes, water damage and sewer backup endorsements, loss of rent sized to the market's current rental rate, at least $500,000 of liability, and accidental tenant damage endorsements where available [7sscnFcWkp4 @ 00:03:25]. Bryan Gutowsky insists the dwelling limit track replacement cost, meaning the full ground-up rebuild figure, not the market value the house would sell for [UUWJRIHQaqk @ 00:02:54]. Personal property is where owners over-buy or under-buy. The Phoenix Insurance points out that a homeowner form sets contents around 60 percent of dwelling, so a $200,000 house carries $120,000 of contents that a rental rarely needs, yet appliances can still walk out with an evicted tenant and fully furnished units need enough to replace every piece [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:07:21]. For commercial lessor's risk buildings, Michelle Belden of CID Insurance checks submissions against roughly $100 to $250 per square foot and flags an 80 percent coinsurance clause that penalizes underinsured buildings [OEkTTQjwjDw @ 00:16:27].

Liability is the layer every agent pushes hardest, and the recommended numbers converge tightly. Gutowsky tells landlords to max the liability on either form at $500,000 or $1 million, depending on what the carrier offers, and to price an umbrella once multiple rentals stack up exposure [UUWJRIHQaqk @ 00:01:58]. The Phoenix Insurance flags a structural trap: some carriers will not write liability on a dwelling fire policy at all and instead extend it from the owner's homeowner policy, so operators must confirm liability actually sits on the rental, while dwelling-focused companies will write $1 million of liability and keep rental claims off the primary homeowner policy [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:08:17]. Entity hygiene matters too, and it is cheap to get right. Gutowsky says that when the deed sits in an LLC, the LLC must be listed as a named insured, which most carriers do at no charge [UUWJRIHQaqk @ 00:03:22]. On the tenant side, Mark of Think Insurance says many agents go cheap at $100,000 of renters liability, while the most common limit at that agency is $500,000 [zqIy396b0ac @ 00:01:28]. That gives an operator a concrete lease number to demand from every tenant instead of a vague insurance clause buried in boilerplate.

Scaling operators change the policy architecture entirely. The Phoenix Insurance says that once real estate is a business rather than a hobby, a scheduled or monthly reporting policy puts every property on one policy with one monthly bill, letting flippers and landlords add and drop inventory as deals close [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:09:31]. American Modern pitches the same consolidation: up to 30 homes on one policy with one bill and one renewal date, duplexes and three- and four-family homes accepted, protection classes 9 and 10 written, and an endorsement for Airbnb-type properties that it says few carriers offer [fR49bewBEuo @ 00:00:44]. Short-stay operators layer deposit mechanics on top. Jacob and Elissa run about a $1,000 security deposit on a Northwest midterm rental grossing $4,000 to $10,000 a month, skip deposits on Airbnb, and float a $50 to $100 non-refundable damage waiver covering up to $500 off-platform as a self-insurance play [ZoPbBZvfW94 @ 00:08:16]. Nate Bunty describes long-term deposit replacements: a surety that pays the $1,500 to the manager and bills the tenant, or a true insurance product sold as a monthly fee that totals less than a deposit over the lease, underwritten on the manager's screening rather than each tenant [0Z4NHI1xnKY @ 00:03:18].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Declare the Rental Use
   - Call the agent, cancel the homeowner policy on the rental, and rewrite it as a landlord policy.
   - Hannigan cancels the homeowner policy and writes a brand-new landlord policy once a tenant moves in [Hannigan Insurance Agency — aGvo7Dq3gTM @ 00:02:14]; for room rentals, get the agent's acknowledgment of rent-by-the-room use to unrelated adults in writing [The CoLiving Insider — RyLyOzBiWEs @ 00:10:10].
2. Specify DP3 at Replacement Cost
   - Request the DP3 tier with replacement cost, loss of rent, vandalism, and water coverage.
   - Get the third-level form with replacement cost and loss of rent [The Phoenix Insurance — S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:05:27], and insure the dwelling to full rebuild cost rather than market value [Bryan Gutowsky | Michigan Insurance — UUWJRIHQaqk @ 00:02:54].
3. Stack Liability and Fix the Named Insured
   - Confirm liability sits on the rental policy at $500,000 to $1 million, add an umbrella, and list the LLC.
   - Some carriers only extend liability from the homeowner policy, so verify it [The Phoenix Insurance — S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:08:17]; max liability, add an umbrella, and name the deeded LLC at no charge [Bryan Gutowsky | Michigan Insurance — UUWJRIHQaqk @ 00:03:22].
4. Push Exposure Onto Tenants and Guests
   - Require renters insurance in the lease and set deposits, waivers, or deposit alternatives by channel.
   - Require tenant renters insurance in every lease [BiggerPockets — bfsA8QKK2ZU @ 00:04:48]; charge deposits off-Airbnb or sell a $50 to $100 damage waiver [Jacob and Elissa — ZoPbBZvfW94 @ 00:08:16]; or replace deposits with a surety or monthly-fee insurance product [Nate Bunty — 0Z4NHI1xnKY @ 00:03:38].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over how much to pay and how much to buy. Steadily puts landlord coverage at about 25 percent more than a homeowner policy on the same property and teaches premium cutting: ask for discounts on a new roof or better windows, raise the deductible, and stack multi-policy and multi-location discounts [8qVhmrP1DZM @ 00:02:12]. Hannigan Insurance Agency frames the jump as minor, quoting 100 bucks a month, or 1,200 a year, for a landlord policy against a typical Michigan home policy of 895, and calls it not a big price change [aGvo7Dq3gTM @ 00:02:29]. The Phoenix Insurance pushes the other way: start from what you need, not what it costs, and buy the DP3 if you need the policy to actually pay at the time of loss. Their hosts openly hate the phrase investor friendly because it means cheap, and they tell small operators not to play with the big boys without the big back [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:06:52]. The same hosts carve out one exception: an owner with a dozen doors can spread risk like an insurance company, carry disaster coverage, and absorb smaller losses, while a one-property owner who skimps can watch that single asset turn unprofitable after one uncovered hit [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:05:56].

Practitioners also split on how to structure a growing book. The Phoenix Insurance runs a ladder: one property gets a single dwelling fire policy, four or five properties can still sit on a few separate dwelling fire policies, and a true business should move to a scheduled or monthly reporting policy that puts every property on one monthly bill, built for flippers whose inventory churns [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:09:48]. AmericanModernIns pitches its rental program the same way, bundling up to 30 homes onto one policy with one bill and one renewal date [fR49bewBEuo @ 00:00:44]. CID Insurance flags the limit of that shortcut on commercial buildings, warning that most of its carriers do not offer blanket limits and that even the ones that do still demand a statement of values breaking out square footage, units, and limits per building [OEkTTQjwjDw @ 00:17:49]. Liability placement is just as contested. The Phoenix Insurance warns that some carriers will not write liability on a dwelling fire policy and force it to be extended from the homeowner policy, while dwelling specialists will write a million dollars of liability so claims stay off your primary policy [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:08:17]. Blue Fox Properties sets the floor at 500,000 in liability with an umbrella on top [7sscnFcWkp4 @ 00:03:58].

Guest-damage protection is where the playbooks diverge hardest. Jacob and Elissa run platform-specific rules: no security deposit on Airbnb because those guests do not expect one and Airbnb mediation offers some protection, but roughly a 1,000 dollar deposit per booking on Vrbo and other OTAs for a midterm unit renting at 4,000 to 10,000 a month [ZoPbBZvfW94 @ 00:02:22]. For cheap short stays they scale it down, since a 1,000 deposit on a 100 dollar two-night booking makes no sense, and they offer an alternative: a non-refundable 50 or 100 dollar damage waiver that covers up to 500, which they call self-insurance [ZoPbBZvfW94 @ 00:08:16]. Nate Bunty pitches a different model on the long-term side, replacing deposits with either a surety that pays the landlord and then bills the tenant, or a true insurance product sold as a monthly fee that pays out with no tenant-level qualification and no end-of-lease fight over deductions [0Z4NHI1xnKY @ 00:02:37]. The CoLiving Insider cautions that platform programs such as PadSplit's Host Guarantee are not always the same as an insurance policy, so operators should ask what it covers, its limits, and whether it replaces or only adds to their own coverage [RyLyOzBiWEs @ 00:08:47].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most repeated failure is the silent one: the owner moves out, a tenant moves in, and the homeowner policy never changes. Real Property Management Palisade says this costs New Jersey owners five and six figures, and the failure mode is not a higher premium but a denied claim, because the carrier's first question after a fire, burst pipe, or liability suit is who was living in the home [aUKpl6xobwU @ 00:00:20]. The Phoenix Insurance says they cannot count how many owners have rented for years on the homeowner policy bought at closing, which violates contract language that pays losses only when the primary title owner occupies the home [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:01:02]. BiggerPockets warns that an injured tenant on the wrong policy can leave the owner on the hook for hundreds of thousands of dollars [bfsA8QKK2ZU @ 00:03:40]. Hannigan Insurance Agency, drawing on 17 years in the business, says people who try to fool the insurance company always get bitten, and the fix is to cancel the home policy and write a fresh landlord policy when a tenant moves in [aGvo7Dq3gTM @ 00:02:46]. The Phoenix Insurance applies the same logic to short-term rentals: buy the cheaper long-term form while running Airbnb and you stand a chance of getting no money on the claim [S15pjuTJ618 @ 00:03:56].

Co-living operators face the harshest version of the misrepresentation trap. The CoLiving Insider walks through Lopez versus Allstate: the owner applied describing a one-family home of 1,600 square feet, but the house had six rented units with lockable doors and shared bathrooms in roughly 3,000 square feet. After a loss estimated near 371,000 dollars, Allstate rescinded the policy back to the beginning and refunded the premiums; the owner went to court and lost, then appealed and lost again [RyLyOzBiWEs @ 00:02:42]. The same channel reports a national sober living network tracing many claim denials to paperwork listing homes as single-family rentals, and flags fine print on a new rent-by-the-room product: a 25,000 dollar deductible for resident damage in shared spaces, theft excluded on the broadest form, and dog bites excluded [RyLyOzBiWEs @ 00:07:42]. Blue Fox Properties documents a denial vector that has nothing to do with the policy form: missing smoke alarms are one of the fastest ways to get a claim denied, dual cylinder deadbolts can sink liability claims when tenants cannot exit, and missing screens can be cited as a contributing factor in liability claims [7sscnFcWkp4 @ 00:04:30]. Real Property Management Palisade adds that vacancy provisions between tenants can hold surprises [aUKpl6xobwU @ 00:01:49].

On commercial and lessor's risk buildings, the traps are underwriting math. CID Insurance underwriter Michelle Belden checks building limit divided by square footage, normally seeing 100 to 250 dollars, and treats a figure like 33 dollars as a red flag requiring a building evaluation report, because most policies carry an 80 percent co-insurance clause that penalizes an underinsured building at loss time [OEkTTQjwjDw @ 00:16:11]. The same underwriter reports that buildings without full wiring, plumbing, heating, and roof updates in the last 30 to 35 years get cut to basic actual cash value, some markets want 70 percent occupancy, and every CID lessor's risk market requires owners to collect tenant certificates of insurance or there is no quote at all [OEkTTQjwjDw @ 00:14:15]. Elemental Risk Management explains why: when an uninsured tenant's hair salon customer gets hurt, the attorney can pivot to the landlord, so the channel advises getting a certificate every year at renewal [yqr3C1o6bxQ @ 00:01:50]. Dopazo Insurance shows the tenant's own coverage is thinner than landlords assume: general liability excludes real property in the tenant's care, custody, or control, and damage to premises rented covers only fire, usually at 50,000 or 100,000, which is why the landlord still needs a commercial property policy [-vWCekxkuSs @ 00:02:23].
