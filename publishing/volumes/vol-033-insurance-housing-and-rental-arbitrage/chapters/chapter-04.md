# Chapter 04: Building a Corporate Housing Business

*Starting and scaling a corporate housing company, word-for-word outreach pitches, landing 90-day contracts, building the business client-first ('backwards'), listing on CHBO, how a 95%-occupancy operator works without Airbnb, and corporate housing versus vacation rentals*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Operators running corporate housing from two units to 290+ agree the money sits in business-to-business demand: guests whose employer or insurer pays, staying 30 days to six months, at roughly double market rent or 30-40% above unfurnished leases. Angela Healy underwrites only units she expects to rent 80% of the time; Tim Touchette holds about 85-92%; Gabriel Wacker runs about 95%. Street mechanics center on the three-edges location rule (hospitals, highways, hotels), Wi-Fi, blackout curtains, desks and full kitchens, a one-page PDF, domain email, and a short question-led pitch to relocation managers, HR and adjusters. The splits: Healy and Touchette share vacancy with owners under management agreements, while Dr Chau and Wacker arbitrage leases; Noelle Randall starts on Airbnb first, Dr Chau abandons vacation rentals, and Jorge Garcia lines up demand before signing any lease.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who defines the business starts with who pays. Tim Touchette, running 290+ units in DC, defines corporate housing as 'people who aren't paying the rent themselves,' a definition he uses when people ask him what the business actually is [ZJwE-gL3Y20 @ 00:14:49]. Angela Healy of Avenue West sells to Fortune 100 and Fortune 5000 companies, Microsoft, tech, oil and gas, and healthcare, usually on roughly 90-day assignments, and pitches against 300-400 square foot hotel rooms; her line is that direct corporate clients 'always pay more' for the same real estate [f2vS4cnBnoI @ 00:03:20]. The Garcia playbook breaks demand into four B2B segments: relocation companies, insurance agencies housing families displaced by fire or flood, corporate project teams, and traveling nurses [iQn0PMSPJ5Q @ 00:02:17]. Dr Chau Ong lands on the same client stack, corporate travelers, medical professionals and insurance clients, reporting no evictions over 10 years from that guest profile, against the vacation partiers who 'trashed the place' during his earlier Airbnb run [JWjjZOTJhUs @ 00:02:11]. The consequence for an operator is that the buyer is a coordinator, adjuster or HR desk spending someone else's budget, not a traveler comparing nightly rates on a phone, and every pricing, setup and screening decision downstream flows from that buyer.

Stay length and occupancy numbers cluster tightly across operators who otherwise run very different models. Healy cites Corporate Housing Providers Association statistics putting the industry average stay at 86 days, against 99 days in Avenue West inventory, and says she will not bring a property into the program unless she expects to rent it more than 80% of the time [f2vS4cnBnoI @ 00:14:00]. Touchette reports an average stay between 3 and 4 months, targets roughly 90% occupancy, and measures 90-92% in some DC neighborhoods with about 85% citywide [ZJwE-gL3Y20 @ 00:34:26]. Gabriel Wacker of Vicinity, in the industry since 1998, says the company traditionally runs about 95% occupied and treats occupancy maximization as a day-to-day discipline [DNkNK6FNKog @ 00:20:45]. Dr Chau frames the same gap against vacation rentals: 30-40% occupancy for vacation units versus 70-80% for corporate units, priced by the month instead of the night, and workable in any city instead of only destination markets [v4LiJvYw8rc @ 00:03:10]. The operating takeaway is consistent in practice: one check-in means fewer cleanings, key exchanges and guest messages, a booked unit tends to stay booked for roughly a full quarter, and Healy's 80% underwriting floor sits comfortably below what the larger operators actually post.

The third point of agreement is that this is a relationship business, not a listing business. Sheree Hill of Avenue West Atlanta draws the line plainly: Airbnb runs on pictures and reviews, corporate runs on relationships, and the product must be the entire unit, since rooms and basements suit Airbnb but not corporate guests [nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:12:49]. Wacker closes with 'Build those relationships,' and defines a professional operator by standardized practices, inventory and operations, warning that bad operators use the corporate housing label loosely [DNkNK6FNKog @ 00:27:45]. Garcia positions the operator as a 'partner, not a vendor,' following up after every stay, sending monthly availability lists, remembering birthdays and mailing thank-you notes, with contracts arriving years later [Ewazlh6zOLM @ 00:03:11]. Jesse Vasquez, answering a host with two years and zero Furnished Finder bookings, gives the same verdict from the other direction: be proactive, not platform-reliant, and go find the operators and companies directly instead of waiting on a listing to convert [ypZmx1qTnpY @ 00:02:47]. The baseline prerequisites that fall out of this are simple: whole units, a business identity instead of a personal one, standardized setups, and a pipeline of named contacts at relocation, HR, insurance and travel-nurse desks who already know your name before a need appears.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Dr Chau Ong's FAST method sets the sequence: Find a location, Ask to sublet, Set up to stand out, Tap corporate clients [JWjjZOTJhUs @ 00:02:45]. Location runs on his 'three edges' rule, hospitals, highways and hotels, with the unit sitting within roughly 5-10 minutes of them; he credits the filter with about an 80% success rate [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:16:54]. The landlord pitch is a package, not a plea: cleaning every couple of months, maintenance under $200 a month covered by the operator, a 3-year lease, and the 'ace card' of above-market rent [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:19:58]. Sheree Hill adds the paperwork discipline from the franchise side: a renter cannot run corporate units in a personal name and must set up as a business, should take a short lease of around six months, time it to a February-to-September peak season so lease timing matters, and should disclose the corporate housing intent to the landlord up front, with leasing first and buying later as the path into ownership [nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:09:37]. On unit size, Dr Chau's portfolio leans 3-4 bedroom single-family homes, while Sheree reads 1BR at $3,000-4,000 and 2BR at $3,800 into the $5,000s as the beginner lanes, flagging 3BR as a risk factor.

The setup spec is narrow and functional. Garcia's baseline list, framed as non-negotiable rather than upgrades: bulletproof Wi-Fi with a speed-test screenshot in the listing, blackout curtains for night-shift nurses, a real desk with an ergonomic chair, a full kitchen, a washer and dryer, and at least two full linen sets; accent walls and 'Instagrammable' decor do not matter and 'might even hurt you' [iQn0PMSPJ5Q @ 00:05:25]. Dr Chau specs 10-12 inch memory-foam mattresses and noise fans, caps furnishing at $7-9k, and runs the startup math at $2,500 rent plus $2,500 deposit plus $7k furniture for $12k all-in, recouped in about six months at $2k a month [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:25:58]. Vasquez budgets $10 per square foot at IKEA level up to $15 at Crate and Barrel level, says to overspend on beds, couches and dining tables, and funds it with an SBA loan or, the easier route, a credit card at 0% or 3-5% for the first year, paid off within 6-12 months [ypZmx1qTnpY @ 00:08:39]. On the owner-managed side, Healy's owners pay the fix-up and furnishing, and a makeover in Raleigh produced 4x the income, while a 1980s St. Charles home got new flooring, baths, kitchen, windows and an enclosed Zoom office [f2vS4cnBnoI @ 00:12:34].

Pricing and sales run on scripts. Dr Chau prices at double market rent, adjusted roughly $500 for utilities, underwrites 80% occupancy and adds $500-2,000 in peak season; in negotiation, 'whoever give out the first number... loses,' so he asks for a price range, never a budget, sees typical ranges of $150-300 a night, and notes insurance pays admin and pet fees [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:32:52]. The Roadmap Lodging pitch to relocation coordinators, HR managers and adjusters opens with 'I know you get a lot of housing emails, so I'll keep it brief,' asks about the toughest part of relocating someone, stops dead if it is a live call, and closes with 'Would you be opposed if I sent over a one-pager,' followed by a soft nudge after 3-4 days of silence [68tMFzAJyS0 @ 00:00:41]. Garcia's cadence is 5 outreach emails a week with the one-page PDF, a $6-a-month domain email instead of Gmail, a simple website and a Google Business Profile, noting one unit booked at $6K a month is already healthy [iQn0PMSPJ5Q @ 00:11:34]. Sheree closes the loop on collections: verify the employer, run background checks for evictions and fraud, and bill 15th-to-15th on a credit card with the employer paying [nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:15:56].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pick the location with the three-edges filter
   - Shortlist addresses within roughly 5-10 minutes of hospitals, highways and hotels before contacting any landlord.
   - Follow the FAST sequence of Find a location, Ask to sublet, Set up to stand out, Tap corporate clients, and use hospitals, highways and hotels as the location screen, which Dr Chau credits with about an 80% hit rate [Dr Chau Ong — JWjjZOTJhUs @ 00:03:04]
2. Secure the unit as a business, with disclosure
   - Set up a business entity, sign a short lease of around six months, and disclose the corporate housing intent to the landlord.
   - Renters 'cannot just rent this out in your name'; use a short lease timed to the February-to-September peak and tell the landlord the plan up front, or offer cleaning, sub-$200 maintenance coverage and above-market rent on a 3-year term [LaShaun Shanae — nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:09:55]
3. Build the baseline product and credibility kit
   - Install Wi-Fi, blackout curtains, desk and ergonomic chair, full kitchen, washer/dryer and two linen sets, then produce the one-page PDF, website, domain email and Google Business Profile.
   - The one-pager exists because HR managers must print or forward it and Airbnb links may hit corporate firewalls; a roughly $6-a-month domain email signals you will still be there in 90 days [Jorge Garcia | Roadmap Lodging — iQn0PMSPJ5Q @ 00:08:44]
4. Run question-led outreach with a timed follow-up
   - Email or call relocation coordinators, HR managers and insurance adjusters with a short opener, a pain-point question, and a one-pager close.
   - Ask about the toughest part of relocating someone, stop and listen on live calls, offer to send a one-pager, and after 3-4 days with no reply send a soft nudge offering details or a 10-minute call [Jorge Garcia | Roadmap Lodging — 68tMFzAJyS0 @ 00:01:23]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The deepest split is the order of operations. Tim Touchette of Stay A While runs a supply-side shop in DC: he markets his own inventory while most competitors sit on the demand side and source a unit only after a client asks [ZJwE-gL3Y20 @ 00:21:53]. Jorge Garcia of Roadmap Lodging flipped the sequence entirely. He calls the sign-furnish-and-hope routine a gamble, says his own first unit sat empty, and claims demand first eliminates 90% of the risk: call relocation managers, adjusters, HR directors and travel-nurse coordinators, ask about the hardest part of housing, send sample units, then backfill the property [Ewazlh6zOLM @ 00:01:21]. Dr Chau Ong goes the other way with FAST, which opens with Find location and Ask to sublet before Tap corporate clients ever happens, betting on the three edges of hospitals, highways and hotels and following the whales [JWjjZOTJhUs @ 00:02:45]. Gabriel Wacker at Vicinity splits the difference with dynamic inventory, building and tearing down 50 to 100 unit intern blocks for two to three months as contracts land [DNkNK6FNKog @ 00:08:29], so the operator choosing a lane is really choosing who eats the vacancy before the first booking.

Airbnb is the second fault line, and four camps treat it four ways. Noelle Randall tells students to run Airbnb first because it builds the reviews, the photos and the hard cost knowledge a corporate listing later trades on [cJJUHWNqI-I @ 00:05:24]. Dr Chau Ong walked away from that lane: he rates one vacation rental as the effort of ten midterm units and pushes operators toward monthly corporate stays instead [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:50:07]. Jesse Vasquez sits in the middle. He calls Airbnb the biggest marketing platform on the planet, lets companies book there for the million-dollar coverage, then works every guest with three questions: how long is the assignment, are you going to other markets, are you coming back to this market, so the next stay lands direct [ypZmx1qTnpY @ 00:01:12]. The Roadmap Lodging deep dive takes the hardest line, calling total reliance on Airbnb building your entire house on someone else's land [iQn0PMSPJ5Q @ 00:11:34]. The operator read: Randall and Vasquez treat the platform as a feeder to harvest, while Dr Chau and the Garcia camp treat it as the exposure the corporate business exists to escape, and the playbook you choose dictates where your first lead comes from.

Unit selection and deal structure split the field again. Sheree Hill of Avenue West Atlanta insists the guest gets the entire unit, prices one-bedrooms at $3,000 to $4,000 and two-bedrooms at $3,800 into the $5,000s, and flags three-bedrooms as a risk factor for beginners even at $4,000 to $5,000-plus in Midtown or Dunwoody [nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:25:58]. Dr Chau Ong builds the opposite book: mostly three- and four-bedroom single-family houses, with his students in California and Florida clearing about $4,000 a month per house [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:13:47]. Angela Healy refuses the sublease model outright. Avenue West signs a management agreement, owner and operator share vacancy, and the owner funds fix-up and furnishing, which is the inverse of an arbitrage operator carrying rent on an empty unit [f2vS4cnBnoI @ 00:06:33]. Jesse Vasquez prices the midterm lane differently from the multiplier crowd: about 2x long-term rent but roughly 15% less than Airbnb, so a $10,000 Airbnb property lands near $8,500 to $9,000 [ypZmx1qTnpY @ 00:07:21]. Hold that conservative band against the bigger multiples other operators quote before signing a lease, because the spread between camps is wide enough to flip a deal from cash flow to carry.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is the wrong guest. Dr Chau Ong's vacation-rental partiers trashed the place, which he calls a nightmare and the reason he now targets corporate travelers, medical professionals and insurance clients [JWjjZOTJhUs @ 00:01:38]. In a separate breakdown he says he never had one eviction from corporate guests in six years but did evict a vacationer, while elsewhere he cites ten years and seven to eight years with zero evictions, so the record is strong but the tenure figure moves [v4LiJvYw8rc @ 00:02:34]. Sheree Hill shows that a corporate label does not screen anyone by itself: she verifies the employer, runs background checks for evictions and fraud, notes that Atlanta is number one in the fraud, and caught a scammer using different names with five eviction cases on file [nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:19:04]. Jesse Vasquez adds the squatter angle. Companies book through Airbnb for the million-dollar coverage, but if a guest squats, Airbnb is not going to go there to kick them out, so he still pushes for a lease signed by the company or occupant to get local legal protection [ypZmx1qTnpY @ 00:00:13]. Payment rails matter too: he screens and collects through Turbo Tenant and says flatly not to use Zelle.

Vacancy is where arbitrage capital dies. Jorge Garcia's lease-first unit sat empty while he waited for demand he had not yet built, the experience behind his backwards model [Ewazlh6zOLM @ 00:01:05]. Angela Healy warns that competitors who sublet apartments collapse in downturns, which is exactly why Avenue West uses management agreements where owner and operator share vacancy rather than one side carrying fixed rent [f2vS4cnBnoI @ 00:20:48]. Tim Touchette lived the stress test. During COVID a friend's ten-unit nightly building in Dupont Circle hit 100% vacancy, while his own corporate book was down about 7 and 1/2% [ZJwE-gL3Y20 @ 00:45:01]. The slower bleed is contract length. Companies now cap commitments at three months, and that shift cost him condo buildings that imposed six-month minimums, so any building rule longer than your client's commitment window is dead inventory [ZJwE-gL3Y20 @ 00:50:10]. The economics check is simple: if your structure forces you to pay rent on a dark unit, your downside is the entire lease, not a soft month, and the only two hedges these operators report are shared-vacancy owner agreements and demand secured before the keys.

The last set of traps is operational speed and cash recovery. Sheree Hill says a broken oven cannot wait three to four days for a corporate guest, home warranties don't move fast, and the fix is a repair retainer the owner keeps on file [nZd4KYLOZDw @ 00:24:05]. The Roadmap Lodging breakdown names the product misses that cost corporate bookings: spotty internet is a work stoppage for an engineer or consultant, accent walls and Instagrammable decor might even hurt you, and a kitchen table instead of a desk with an ergonomic chair fails a 90-day stay that HR managers will notice [iQn0PMSPJ5Q @ 00:05:39]. On capital, Dr Chau Ong's startup math runs $2,500 rent, a $2,500 deposit and $7,000 of furniture for $12,000 out of pocket, recouped in six months at $2,000 a month, with furnishing capped at $7,000 to $9,000 [yo5DDACarw0 @ 00:25:58]. Jesse Vasquez funds through a 0% or 3 to 5% first-year credit card or an SBA loan and wants furniture paid off inside six to twelve months [ypZmx1qTnpY @ 00:06:24]. Check your own screening math too: he preaches tenant income of 2 to 3x rent, yet his worked example sits near 1x.
