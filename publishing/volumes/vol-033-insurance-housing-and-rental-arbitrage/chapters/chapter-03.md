# Chapter 03: The Payer's Side: ALE Coverage, Adjusters, and Relocation Benefits

*Additional living expense and loss-of-use coverage, how adjusters handle ALE claims, public adjusters, policyholder tactics for maximizing benefits, and corporate and military relocation programs that place employees in temporary housing*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Payer-side practitioners agree ALE only moves when a covered loss makes the home uninhabitable, and it only pays the increase over normal living costs. United Policyholders shows rent rising from $1,500 to $2,000 paying $500; Kela Trishaan reimburses $50 when a $150 weekly food baseline hits $200. Caps run as 20 to 30 percent of dwelling, such as $90,000 on a $300,000 home, or as Actual Loss Sustained with a usual 12-month clock, while California sets a 24-month floor extendable to 36. Adjusters switch families from hotels to comparable long-term housing after one to two weeks, which is where 30-plus-day corporate housing plugs in. Speakers split on flood as a covered peril, on whether to use furnished-rental vendors, and on how public adjusters should structure their 10 to 20 percent fees.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every payer-side speaker starts from the same trigger: the home has to be uninhabitable because of a covered loss before a single ALE dollar moves. Insurance Savvy frames it as direct physical loss from a listed peril such as fire, lightning or windstorm, and stresses that the coverage only pays costs incurred beyond usual expenses [Xnrpk3PKFg4 @ 00:01:55]. Bill Coffman words the same test as a home that is untenable in whole or in part, with coverage built to maintain the family's normal standard of living [idtxFNBKKIA @ 00:00:38]. The second universal rule is that ALE is a delta, not a stipend. The United Policyholders webinar runs the arithmetic: rent of $1,500 rising to $2,000 means the carrier pays $500, food going from $500 to $1,000 means the difference gets paid, and an insurer may offset a $150 utility bill if utilities are now bundled into the new rent [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:13:33]. Kela Trishaan applies the identical lens to meals, reimbursing $50 when a household that normally spends $150 a week spends $200, and stating flatly that the carrier is paying for the excess, not simply paying for meals [awTD63In04A @ 00:05:33]. For anyone housing displaced families, that delta is the budget the payer recognizes.

Speakers also converge on how the payer caps the meter. Insurance Savvy shows the percentage formula: a $300,000 dwelling limit at 30% leaves a $90,000 ALE ceiling, and some policies also cap duration at 12 or 24 months [Xnrpk3PKFg4 @ 00:02:33]. Mike Crowley of Crowley Insurance Agency confirms Coverage D is written either as a limit of 20 or 30 percent of dwelling depending on the company, or as Actual Loss Sustained, which carries no dollar cap but usually stops paying at 12 months [TkI2Qak3r34 @ 00:00:57]. United Policyholders widens the frame: limits come as dollars, time, both or neither, and a neighbor's policy can read completely differently from yours [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:05:54]. Under the ISO form, coverage runs only for the shortest time required to repair or settle elsewhere. In California, the same webinar cites §2051.5 as setting a 24-month minimum, with an insurer required to grant extensions up to 36 months total when a good-faith insured hits permit, materials or contractor delays, issued in 6-month increments for good cause, and the speakers warn that the extension is not automatic [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:22:58]. Whoever reads the declarations page first controls how long, and at what rate, a displaced household stays housed.

The third point of agreement is that the payer only funds housing that is reasonable, necessary and comparable to what the family had before. Sylvia Brow lists reasonable necessity as one of four eligibility tests, demanding documentation plus accommodations comparable to previous conditions [_Odm8IhYr80 @ 00:02:32]. Crowley says that on large claims carriers pull families out of hotels and into a rented house or apartment, and there is no milking a five-star hotel [TkI2Qak3r34 @ 00:01:47]. Kela Trishaan sets the switch point precisely: hotels suit one to two weeks, and anything a month or longer belongs in long-term housing benchmarked to comparable local stock, with a San Diego three-bedroom, two-bath pegged near $5,000 a month rather than $10,000 [awTD63In04A @ 00:06:15]. That is exactly the gap corporate housing operators pitch into. AvenueWest describes stays of 30-plus days, move-in ready with utilities, Wi-Fi and cable bundled, explicitly used for disaster transitional housing, and notes that full kitchens mean less reimbursed dining [g7utkw_SRk4 @ 00:01:13]. For a supplier, the consensus defines the product: furnished, comparable to the lost home, priced near local market rent, and built for stays that outlast a hotel booking.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the adjuster desk, the mechanics start with a baseline. Bill Coffman says the adjuster builds normal expenses from groceries, eating-out frequency and utilities, then reimburses total expenses minus normal expenses, warning that a household that overestimates its normal spend gets shortchanged on its recovery [idtxFNBKKIA @ 00:01:27]. Kela Trishaan decides at the very start of the claim whether to open the ALE exposure, then pins utilities to a hard baseline: three prior months plus the month showing the increase, so a normal $100 bill that jumps to $200 yields only the difference [awTD63In04A @ 00:03:56]. Meals follow the same playbook. Trishaan's typical baseline runs groceries of $150 to $200 plus DoorDash of $50 to $100, and the adjuster asks which restaurants the family normally uses; one or two outlier receipts get tolerated, but once a trend shows up, it gets stopped [awTD63In04A @ 00:15:09]. Housing gets benchmarked against comparable local units, and Trishaan treats summer price spikes as a business decision [awTD63In04A @ 00:07:45]. The result is a ledger where every dollar of excess is measured against a documented normal, and the household that knows its own numbers before the adjuster asks walks away with the larger check.

Policyholder-side operators run a tighter paper game. The United Policyholders webinar lays out the moves: get a realtor's written fair rental value estimate to switch payment formulas, since FRV is a fixed payment that needs no receipts while ALE demands a lease and receipts [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:27:25]. When an adjuster cites a restriction, the script is 'show me where it says that,' backed by a monthly contact log, documented insurer delays such as 'delayed for four months,' and a 10-business-day reply window [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:46:49]. Written follow-ups open with 'this will confirm,' run in bullets and skip the venting. The webinar also notes that most insurers are paying ALE advances before the costs are even incurred, and that a displaced family can cash out for a tiny home or RV. Lydia of Driven Saver shows what granular tracking earns: a job 10 miles farther away meant 20 extra round-trip miles, logged in a notebook plus a monthly Excel sheet covering the top three destinations, and the carrier paid it for six months [uCQYAZ1ws_w @ 00:01:11]. The same household got an antenna and clothesline covered while a dryer was refused, and another claimant got rental curtains and bath mats approved [uCQYAZ1ws_w @ 00:02:44].

On the supply side, money moves through direct billing and stay length. Vince Perri's Public Adjuster Show promotes Black Diamond Services, which bills ALE for the insured so the client pays nothing out of pocket [ZQjrOy75-Ow @ 00:01:20]. Nurse Lexi booked Hello Landing for three months on a 30-day minimum, says the longer the stay the cheaper it gets, asked for photos of the exact unit and for deals, landed $250 off the first month, and found pricing roughly level with Airbnb [v2OZyLu0O58 @ 00:02:44]. Screening ran through a background check, employment verification and likely a soft credit pull, with approval the next day, and the same channel names Blue and CorporateHousing.com as alternatives. Mid Term Rental Properties maps the relocation money flow: some employers arrange and pay for corporate housing directly, others give a temporary housing allowance, and others reimburse approved expenses, with stays typically running 30 to 90 days and extensions worth confirming up front [7AC0D_Eap9s @ 00:01:30]. The same operator tells guests to compare total cost, including taxes, utilities, parking, meals and fees, because corporate housing may or may not beat a hotel depending on location, property, length of stay and what is included [zpCCClwx4r8 @ 00:01:38].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Read the policy and confirm the coverage format
   - Pull the declarations page and identify the trigger, the Coverage D format and every cap.
   - Read your own policy and follow its instructions over generic guidance [Bill Coffman — idtxFNBKKIA @ 00:00:04]; Coverage D is either 20 or 30 percent of dwelling or Actual Loss Sustained with a usual 12-month clock [Crowley Insurance Agency, Inc — TkI2Qak3r34 @ 00:00:57].
2. Lock the baseline before spending
   - Document normal utilities, groceries and delivery spend so only the excess is claimed.
   - Use three prior months of utilities plus the month with the increase, and a meal baseline such as $150 to $200 groceries plus $50 to $100 DoorDash [Kela Trishaan — awTD63In04A @ 00:03:56].
3. Place comparable housing and pick the payment lane
   - Move from hotel to comparable long-term housing after one to two weeks and choose vendor billing or monthly reimbursement.
   - The customer can use a carrier vendor or pay and get reimbursed monthly [Kela Trishaan — awTD63In04A @ 00:03:18]; Black Diamond Services bills ALE directly so the insured pays nothing out of pocket [Public Adjuster Show with Vince Perri — ZQjrOy75-Ow @ 00:01:20].
4. Paper the file and run the clock
   - Send bulleted 'this will confirm' letters, keep a monthly contact log and set reply deadlines.
   - Give the carrier a 10-business-day reply window, document every insurer delay, and file a CDI complaint when deadlines slip [United Policyholders — bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:46:49].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Moves That Separate a Fully Paid ALE File From a Shortchanged One**
> 
> Four field-tested plays from adjusters and policyholders who have run ALE claims to the end.
> - Never pad the normal-expense baseline: overestimating normal expenses shortchanges the recovery, so find out the answer before stating a number [Bill Coffman — idtxFNBKKIA @ 00:01:44].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fault line is the trigger itself. Insurance Savvy states flatly that a flood without separate flood insurance produces no ALE at all, because the coverage rides on direct physical loss from a listed peril such as fire, lightning or windstorm [Xnrpk3PKFg4 @ 00:00:56]. Sylvia Brow at Be A Claims Adjuster, coaching new adjusters, lists the covered perils as fire, flood or severe weather, folding in windstorm, hail, tornado and earthquake [_Odm8IhYr80 @ 00:01:20]. An operator housing displaced families cannot bank on either reading; the declarations page decides who pays. The second fault line is how the limit is written. Mike Crowley explains that Coverage D comes either as a percentage of dwelling, twenty or thirty percent depending on the company, or as Actual Loss Sustained with no dollar cap but a time cap that is usually twelve months [TkI2Qak3r34 @ 00:00:39]. Amy and Sandy Watts at United Policyholders go further: limits can be dollar, time, both or neither, and a neighbor's policy on the same street may read completely differently [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:05:54]. For a housing provider, that means quoting a 90-day furnished stay against a family's specific remaining runway, never against an assumed industry norm.

The second split is the payment formula and the housing format. United Policyholders contrasts fair rental value, a fixed payment that needs no receipts and that some insurers grant even when it is not in the policy, against ALE, which demands a lease and receipts [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:12:27]. For an operator, the read is simple: FRV checks flow without receipts, ALE flows only against a signed lease, so the paperwork model of the unit has to match whichever formula the family is on. Kela Trishaan, working the adjuster side, draws the format line at duration: hotels for one to two weeks, long-term housing for anything a month or longer [awTD63In04A @ 00:06:15]. On furnished rental vendors, the camps split hard. Lydia at Driven Saver warns against them because the displaced family pays for damage, from a refrigerator down to a thrown-away fork, and recommends borrowing items or sourcing them on Facebook instead [uCQYAZ1ws_w @ 00:04:10]. AvenueWest Managed Corporate Housing pitches the opposite model: one bill and one contact for the whole stay [g7utkw_SRk4 @ 00:01:31]. Lydia's alternative keeps cash in the family's pocket; AvenueWest's keeps the setup friction and vendor juggling off it.

The sharpest commercial disagreement sits on the public adjuster side. Vince Perri argues that a public adjuster's 10 to 20 percent fee is offset by settlements running 50 to 100 percent higher than what an unrepresented policyholder pulls [ZQjrOy75-Ow @ 00:09:02]. Inside the same show, Perri and David Dube split on how that fee is charged. Perri adds the PA fee into the estimate, the Cal Spoon approach at 10 percent in Texas and up to 20 percent in Florida, while Dube prefers keeping the fee separate and running a single general-contractor-plus-mitigation estimate with the PA taking 20 percent [UvQF37P1Rn8 @ 00:46:16]. On standard-of-living fights, the policyholder coaches hand out different scripts. Bill Coffman says that if the adjuster wants the household to reduce its normal standard of living, negotiate for added benefits elsewhere in the claim [idtxFNBKKIA @ 00:02:01]. United Policyholders treats living below standard, as Camp Fire families did when housing was scarce, as a bargaining chip, and tells families they can ask to move as housing loosens [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:14:45]. One coach trades the concession immediately; the other banks it and comes back for the upgrade once inventory opens.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive firsthand failure on the payer's side is the hotel stay that never ends. Kela Trishaan is working a file where a family has sat in a hotel for roughly three months, which Trishaan calls a no-no because it risks exhausting the ALE limit, and three to five dogs made comparable housing hard to find [awTD63In04A @ 00:09:25]. The same file broke a second way: the family took a pre-planned birthday trip out of town, the trip was denied, and because the hotel was still being paid while they were away, they arguably owe that money back [awTD63In04A @ 00:12:29]. Trishaan's lesson is to ask about trips at the very start, because it is better to have that conversation in the beginning than to argue about it on the back end. Mike Crowley sets the same ceiling from the agency side: carriers move big losses into a rented house or apartment, and there is no milking a five-star hotel [TkI2Qak3r34 @ 00:01:47]. The furnished alternative looks like Gng. Jackson's employer-provided unit: fully stocked, a new washer and dryer, kids' beds, welcome food, pet gifts and a gate code [hp6VgBcT7YQ @ 00:04:16].

The second trap is the baseline. Bill Coffman's formula is total expenses minus normal expenses, and the warning is blunt: if you overestimate your normal expenses, you get shortchanged on your recovery [idtxFNBKKIA @ 00:01:27]. Kela Trishaan shows how the adjuster builds that baseline: groceries of $150 to $200 plus DoorDash of $50 to $100, only the excess over normal reimbursed, and restaurant patterns checked against where the customer normally eats, with one or two outlier receipts tolerated but a trend stopped [awTD63In04A @ 00:13:53]. Lydia at Driven Saver got six months of extra mileage paid for a commute ten miles farther each way, twenty miles round trip, tracked in a notebook plus a monthly Excel log of the top three destinations [uCQYAZ1ws_w @ 00:01:11]. The same claim drew an uneven line on replacement items: an antenna and a clothesline were covered, but a dryer was refused, even as another claimant got rental curtains and bath mats approved [uCQYAZ1ws_w @ 00:02:44]. The operator takeaway: a furnished unit that already includes laundry removes the dryer argument entirely, and every item the family must still buy needs a receipt and a reason tied to the loss.

The third trap is assuming the clock extends itself. Amy and Sandy Watts at United Policyholders walk through California's 24-month minimum and the extension up to 36 months that an insurer shall grant for permit, materials or contractor delays, then warn that the extension is not automatic, so the family has to ask for it in writing [bPQqrGrXfiI @ 00:37:37]. Their discipline is a monthly contact log, letters that open with 'this will confirm', bullet points instead of venting, and a ten-business-day reply window before escalating. Corporate relocation payers enforce timing rules just as hard. Henrick Mathieu warns that a misaligned closing date or a new build running behind schedule is not considered beyond your control under the CAF relocation directives [N4x6hSMmcaM @ 00:05:34]. Operational friction bites too: Nurse Lexi's three-month Hello Landing stay hit a lockbox mix-up and package-pickup headaches [v2OZyLu0O58 @ 00:04:07], and Ashley Bartoletti at Plus Relocation's counter is to print the check-in details, lockbox and on-site contact included, before the employee arrives [fv6KqAzMsVI @ 00:00:36]. Bartoletti also flags that the employee usually signs, and that the credit card on file covers damages and incidentals.
