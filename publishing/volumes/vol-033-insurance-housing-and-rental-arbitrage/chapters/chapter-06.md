# Chapter 06: Scaling, Automating, and Stress-Testing Arbitrage

*Scaling to 60, 76, and 300+ units, automation stacks, remote management, teams and org charts, a Mexico City case study, master-lease failure modes, and why some operators quit arbitrage for co-hosting*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Operators who scaled past 20, 60, even 450 doors agree on the same spine: pitch landlords as corporate housing, underwrite at two to three times rent against 24 months of AirDNA data, and hire a virtual assistant the day the lease is signed. Richie Matthews went from 18 to 35 units in six months after delegating, running an $80-a-month Hospitable-plus-pricing stack and 900 VA protocols. Colin Wright refuses deals without free rent and negotiates one-to-two-month escape clauses. Humza Zafar peaked at 450 units, cleared his 0% APR cards before they reset, and moved to A-class because fixed costs are identical across classes. The fault lines: Wright skips sublease addendums while Brian Fontenot refuses to do anything under the radar, and Milan Sánchez Stiepovic and Jeremy Werden argue co-hosting carries far lower risk than holding leases.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The operators who scaled fastest never walked into a leasing office asking about Airbnb. Brian Fontenot built a list of buildings that already accepted corporate housing, targeted large Class A complexes through a realtor, and pitched himself to the leasing professional as a 'corporate housing provider' until a corporate lease was verified [D63ppvOFpvk @ 00:10:26]. Richie Matthews runs the same filter on Class B stock 10 to 15 years old: the first question is 'do you allow corporate leasing?' and nothing else happens until the answer is yes [O4cBKKUBS64 @ 00:09:25]. In Mexico City, Nathan Carillo says the words 'sublet' or 'Airbnb' get him hung up on immediately, so he opens with 'corporate rental, short or long term' and works 20 inquiries and three to four appointments a day [z5rgPmF_Dm4 @ 00:13:35]. Farhan Abbasi says landlords 'didn't like hearing' his other framing but 'loved hearing guaranteed,' and his best target is an owner who has carried a unit vacant for more than a month, because debt service on an empty unit hurts [Y2Nj2IYzw-Q @ 00:18:41]. The baseline prerequisite across all four: a landlord who sees a guaranteed, professional tenant that kills vacancy, not a side hustle trying to slip a listing past the lease.

Every scaled operator underwrites the spread before touching furniture, and they converge on a hard multiple. Richie Matthews targets markets where gross revenue runs two to three times rent, checked in AirDNA against 24 to 36 months of history, citing a Nashville 2/2 that grosses $7,500 against $2,500 rent [VtVf0h80q0w @ 00:07:50]. His absolute floor is a 2.0 multiple verified on Rentometer, with rent at no more than half of revenue and ideally a third; if 24 months of month-over-month data does not exist for the market, he walks [O4cBKKUBS64 @ 00:03:08]. Quentin West works the math backwards: a $2,000 minimum profit plus $2,000 rent, $600 utilities and $500 cleaning means the unit must clear $5,100 a month, and AirDNA has to show more than $60,000 a year before anyone checks Zillow [GFTAQzCp4aM @ 00:18:27]. Colin Wright runs AirDNA, BNB Calc and Zillow rents together but warns the data is scraped and not 100% accurate; his own units outperform it, and he tells operators to balance occupancy against rate rather than chase a full calendar [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:11:43]. The shared rule: the spread clears a written floor before a lease ever reaches signature, and nobody here signs on a hunch.

The third point of agreement is that scale comes from systems and people, not from the founder answering messages. Matthews says step one is hiring a virtual assistant the day the lease is signed, and after building his system in March 2021 and delegating, he went from 18 to 35 units in six months [VtVf0h80q0w @ 00:04:17]. Abbasi ran his early units off an Excel and Google Sheets master file that produced date errors, and the business only became scalable once he paired a channel manager with a VA [Y2Nj2IYzw-Q @ 00:14:44]. Fontenot mapped the staffing curve by unit count: Guesty's communication service covered four to five units but still left him with 2 a.m. lockout calls, he hired an admin and communications person at five to seven units, grew that team to three or four people at 10 to 15 units, and added a property manager at 20 [D63ppvOFpvk @ 00:22:08]. Humza Zafar, running hundreds of doors, now works about three hours a week because a COO in Philadelphia runs operations and Philippines-based VAs handle customer service six days a week [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:18:39]. Abbasi's minimum stack is calendar sync, dynamic pricing and outside help once a portfolio passes four or five units.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The unit math the practitioners publish is specific. Rafa Loza's 2BR conversion cost a $1,000 deposit plus $2,950 rent, so $3,950 to get the keys, then roughly $15,000 for beds, TVs, wallpaper and locks; he averages at least $1,500 a month in profit over 12 months, swinging from $400 to $4,000 or $5,000, and pays the unit back in year one [52k99Zbi6fk @ 00:02:28]. Colin Wright tells newcomers to start with a studio or one-bedroom at $7,000 to $10,000 to furnish and $10,000 to $15,000 all-in, netting $10,000 to $20,000 a year, while houses renting at $5,000 to $10,000 can throw a negative month [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:13:01]. Short Term Dojo's monthly model runs $4,500 revenue against a $1,500 lease, $75 supplies, $75 STR-specific insurance, a $50 tech stack plus utilities, totaling $2,000 in costs and netting $2,500, versus roughly $500 as a long-term rental [tfwUVSMpB2c @ 00:05:11]. At the top end, Humza Zafar reports $2,600 to $2,800 rent producing $6,000 to $7,000 a month, $4,000 to $5,000 rent producing $8,000 to $11,000, and an $8,000-rent mansion furnished for $50,000 grossing $15,000 to $20,000 a month [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:07:23]. Zafar's reasoning is that fixed costs like internet and cleaning are identical across classes, so bigger units leave more margin per door.

The automation layer is nearly identical across operators. Rafael Loza's top PMS is Hospitable, which syncs calendars across Airbnb, VRBO, Booking.com and a direct site to stop same-day double bookings and feeds PriceLabs; he tells hosts to stay away from Airbnb's native pricing tool because it 'will underprice everything' [J8lQc66Jf6c @ 00:01:07]. Matthews prices his stack at $40 for Hospitable plus $40 for dynamic pricing, $80 a month, and lists every unit on 20 platforms the day it goes live, including Booking, Expedia, Furnished Finder and ALE channels, while 70% of amateurs use two or fewer [O4cBKKUBS64 @ 00:05:44]. Fontenot runs Guesty, Hey Market for SMS, Zendesk and Slack, uses SiteMinder to reach Expedia, HomeAway, corporate-housing and travel-nurse portals, and screens every guest through Autohost; when COVID left Wheelhouse's event data stale, he switched to PriceClaro's manual pricing service and saw a '20 to 30' revenue increase [D63ppvOFpvk @ 00:24:02]. Colin Wright layers Ring doorbells, smart locks with a unique code per guest and per cleaner, and Minut sensors that log decibels, smoking and crowds as claim evidence, plus PriceLabs managed by a revenue manager at $50 to $60 per listing [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:30:24]. Wright adds that setting the base price correctly is critical.

Financing and labor are where the scaled operators squeeze the most. Zafar funded his run to roughly 100 units with 0% APR business credit cards for furniture plus month-free rent concessions, then paused at 200 units for a few months to clear the cards before the 15-month promotional window closed [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:03:45]. Wright says he gets free rent on every deal because he will not take one without it, offers landlords package terms like '10 units, here's my terms,' and negotiates an escape clause usually costing one to two months of rent, which he offsets by asking the next property for a two-month concession [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:58:03]. Wright sources his own VAs on onlinejobs.ph at $3 to $5 an hour. Matthews' first unit in February 2020 cost $4,800 total, $4,500 of furniture and a $300 deposit, with four weeks of free rent, and his VAs train for five days, run autonomously on day six, and work from 900 protocols with a six-minute maximum response time split-tested across 10,000 reservations; they never touch refunds or safety [O4cBKKUBS64 @ 00:16:04]. Quentin West pushes leverage further: one credit check across more than 45 signed leases, an LLC, and ten hours of script practice before calling landlords [GFTAQzCp4aM @ 00:41:26].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether arbitrage should be the scaling engine at all. Milan Sánchez Stiepovic runs Happy Host at 163 units and says 98% of them now sit under management, calling co-hosting '100% superior' after trying both models, yet he still tells beginners to do their first unit as arbitrage [nPFkgk7QMfA @ 00:18:20]. Jeremy Werden quit arbitrage outright: a $2,500-rent unit grossing $5,000 against $1,000 in costs nets $1,500, but a slide to $3,000 revenue leaves the operator $500 short, while a co-host earning 20% of that same $5,000 pockets $1,000 a month with minimal overhead and no lease exposure [kXJgiO6RYaU @ 00:02:07]. Humza Zafar argues the opposite for the US market, rejecting 80/20 revenue share because a roughly $15K startup with rent concessions keeps the master lease worth carrying; he concedes only that Dubai, with $40–50K paid upfront, changes the math [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:44:45]. John Bell refuses to pick a side at all. At roughly 50 units split about evenly between arbitrage and co-hosting, co-hosting income covers his leases through slow seasons and COVID, and his rule is that 'it always bleeds into the next method so you're going to want a good blend' [scmRAbKvEPk @ 00:06:14].

Pace is the second fault line. Richie Matthews teaches speed: he copied the Sonder playbook, went to 18 properties in 12 months and 46 in two years, and frames his VA-plus-protocol system as the thing that makes that pace possible because 'you can't scale chaos' [O4cBKKUBS64 @ 00:07:11]. The CoLiving Insider prescribes the brake pedal instead: hold every new house at break-even for 90 days before opening the next, put each house in its own LLC 'so one bad lease cannot take down the rest,' and never pay above market just to win a deal, pointing to Quarters raising $300M and still going bankrupt as a business that grew faster than its margins could support [zj0PhRK1anA @ 00:07:57]. Humza Zafar sits between them. He scaled from 5 to roughly 100 units on 0% APR cards and free-month concessions, but deliberately paused at 200 units for a few months to clear the cards before the 15-month promotional window closed [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:13:20]. Rafa Loza looks back and says he should have capped out at about $30K a month in cash flow and bought property, after a $2.7–3M revenue year left him paying $86K in taxes with nothing to depreciate [52k99Zbi6fk @ 00:07:31].

How openly to run the model, and whose name goes on the paper, splits the room just as hard. Brian Fontenot refuses to sign leases in a personal name, routes everything through a business entity, issues guests a short-term occupancy permit so the arrangement is not a sublet, and insists on doing nothing 'under the radar,' even giving property managers portal access to his Autohost guest screening [D63ppvOFpvk @ 00:13:28]. Colin Wright runs looser: he skips the sublease addendum, leans on verbal or written permission, says an LLC is not required, keeps some leases in his own name, and openly books the cost, roughly 15 units lost to new owners over three years [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:23:11]. Nathan Carillo in Mexico City hides the model's name entirely, never saying 'sublet' or 'Airbnb' on a first call because owners hang up, and pitches a 'corporate rental, short/long term' backed by a professional video, landlord testimonials, a presentation and a website [z5rgPmF_Dm4 @ 00:13:35]. Quentin West, on Beau Eckstein's show, takes the entity route to its scaling conclusion: one credit check across 45+ signed leases, an LLC, and ten hours of script practice before working landlords [GFTAQzCp4aM @ 00:41:26].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The landlord exit is the failure every operator eventually meets. Rafa Loza states it bluntly: 'you will only be a business as long as the landlord or Property Management allows you to be in the building,' and he steers operators toward mom-and-pop owners because big complexes deny you [52k99Zbi6fk @ 00:03:34]. He lived it, growing from zero to 14 units in nine months and reaching about 28, before COVID and a building sale cut him back to three; it took another year and a half to rebuild to 76 [52k99Zbi6fk @ 00:06:41]. The CoLiving Insider walks through an owner selling nine months into payback on a $27K setup and an owner defaulting on the mortgage, and puts every fix before signing: strike the sale clause, add a buyout and a right of first refusal, pull county records, and get an SNDA plus a notice clause [zj0PhRK1anA @ 00:00:48]. Farhan Abbasi prices the wind-down when a market closes on you: storage at $350 a month for three apartments over about eight months, furniture that cost $2–3K per apartment with near-zero resale, and a final answer that 'the simplest one was to unfortunately throw them away' [Y2Nj2IYzw-Q @ 01:00:07].

Scale itself eats margin, and the operators who ran the biggest portfolios report it most precisely. Humza Zafar's Dallas economy units rented for $800 and earned about $3,000 in 2021–22, furnished with $2,000 of IKEA, until competition killed that tier and forced him into A-class only [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:04:57]. He also reports per-unit net shrinking as the portfolio grows, from $1,500 down to $700–800 and from $800 down to $300. His Detroit case is the cleanest warning on cannibalization: one building went from 20 to 50 units, another 30 were added across the street, and net per unit collapsed from $1,200–1,500 to about $500, a roughly $100K loss; he now concentrates units only in Dubai or New York-type demand [1D3J3ID1ZZM @ 00:33:19]. Jeremy Werden names the same trap from the competitor's side, saturation and price wars whenever nearby operators launch units, and pairs it with the seasonal mistake of failing to bank summer profit to carry winter rent [kXJgiO6RYaU @ 00:04:29]. Colin Wright frames the problem as markets saturated with bad hosts rather than with hosts, adding that the $2K IKEA furniture package that used to win no longer works [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:15:17].

The operational failures are personal, expensive, and usually discovered by the operators who scaled first. Jeremy Werden warns that roughly year-long 0% APR cards fund the furniture until the promo ends and the operator is left sitting on six-figure debt, a spiral he wants viewers to steer clear of entirely [kXJgiO6RYaU @ 00:05:02]. Richie Matthews self-furnished from IKEA and Amazon and cleaned his own units for the first year while acquiring 18 properties in 12 months; his summary of that stretch is 'my first year was up all night' [VtVf0h80q0w @ 00:08:55]. Colin Wright names his single biggest mistake as running with no SOPs at all, which meant training every VA one-on-one as the team grew [NSyEBVGX02o @ 00:52:50]. Brian Fontenot found that even with Guesty's communication service covering support at 4–5 units he was still taking 2 a.m. lockout calls, which forced an admin and communications hire at 5–7 units, growth of that team from one person to three or four by 10–15 units, and a dedicated property manager once he reached 20 units [D63ppvOFpvk @ 00:22:08].
