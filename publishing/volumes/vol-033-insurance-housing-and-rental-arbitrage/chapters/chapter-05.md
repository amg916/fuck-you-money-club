# Chapter 05: Pitching Landlords and Launching Rental Arbitrage

*Lessons from pitching 500 landlords, scripts and objection handling, the one thing that makes landlords say yes, cold-calling for midterm arbitrage, margin math, realistic time commitment, and self-funding your first units without a business loan*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Landlord-pitching operators converge on one rule: never lead with “Airbnb.” Aurelio Licata, Jorge Contreras, 360 BNB Solutions and Rafa Loza all pitch a “corporate housing company,” answer the two landlord fears (on-time rent and property condition) with autopay two days early, written damage responsibility and frequent professional cleaning, and push for two-to-three-year leases signed under an LLC. Street math runs on a doubling target: Sean Rakidzich's rule is to charge $3,000 against $2,000 rent, Linda Kim sets nightly rates near 10% of monthly rent, and setup runs $5-15K per unit per Rafa Loza. Free-rent gaps, staggered start dates and total-cash negotiation stretch capital. Disagreements are sharp: clear regulation versus low-regulation markets, apartments versus single-family homes, self-managing versus full automation, and Quentin West abandoning arbitrage while Colin Wright insists it still works.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The loudest point of agreement across the landlord-pitching operators is a vocabulary ban: the word “Airbnb” kills the call. Aurelio Licata, who pitched more than 500 landlords over five years, made it lesson six, “don't say Airbnb, say corporate housing company,” because the platform name triggers fear of parties and damage [Q3hSpjquUTE @ 00:06:28]. Licata backs the company name with real demand channels: insurance groups, hospitals, Furnishfinder and Booking.com. Jorge Contreras reports the same reflex from the other side of the phone: open with “I'd love to Airbnb your house” and the landlord hears “party” and hangs up, so the house rules go up front instead, with no parties, quiet hours 9pm to 9am, and security cameras [_M0OdXelhoU @ 00:02:49]. 360 BNB Solutions scripts the replacement line word for word: “Mr landlord we are a corporate leasing company,” serving travel nurses, doctors, chiropractors, professionals and hospital patients, with no smokers, no pets and generally no little kids [prqw1liLbCM @ 00:02:03]. Rafa Loza frames the logic as channel versus business: Airbnb is one channel alongside VRBO, Booking.com, Furnishfinder and Expedia, and the operator is the business, “You run a restaurant that happens to be listed on Yelp” [894g1JF-IuM @ 00:12:37].

Once the vocabulary is fixed, the operators agree the pitch lives or dies on two landlord fears. 360 BNB Solutions boils it down bluntly: “most landlords care about two things keeping my property in great condition and are you going to make the payments on time,” and answers both with professional cleaning one to two times a week, all maintenance handled, a “for sale” condition a long-term tenant never delivers, and payment by direct deposit with no eviction risk [prqw1liLbCM @ 00:04:16]. Jorge Contreras attacks the payment fear mechanically: fixed advertised rent that never fluctuates, autopay set “two days early,” and 100% written responsibility for guest-caused damage while the owner keeps age-related wear like a 10-year-old AC [d8qhoiAWdLs @ 00:01:43]. Aurelio Licata reframes the whole conversation in lesson eleven: “you're not offering rent, you're offering relief,” meaning consistent rent, no property manager to hire, multi-year leases and monthly professional cleaning set against the landlord's vacancy and turnover pain [Q3hSpjquUTE @ 00:10:14]. Lara Stewart, a 28-year RN running three Houston units, strips it even simpler: “you're asking to pay them rent every month is what you're asking to do” [mOvanxNbxWw @ 00:11:19]. Licata's line covers the nerves: “landlords aren't grading your resume.”

The baseline prerequisites are just as consistent. Short Term Sage host John insists on signing as a business, because “if you sign it personally you are now the tenant that they expect,” while warning that big REITs may still demand “company name slash your name” and a personal guarantee; a Dun & Bradstreet DUNS number builds the business credit separate from Experian, Equifax and TransUnion [wt5AlXFsjDQ @ 00:09:54]. Colin Wright's setup list is entity, tax ID, business bank account, professional email and website, then verification of city, building, HOA and landlord rules plus permits, with permission in writing, because “I would never try to sneak Airbnb past a landlord” [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:06:45]. Lease length is the next shared rule: Jorge Contreras prefers two- to three-year terms to lock in rent since payback runs six to nine months, and “you don't want to have to restart every 12 months with a new listing” [_M0OdXelhoU @ 00:06:23]. Finally, LoCasa sets the scaling prerequisite: “Your first unit should be your laboratory,” testing pricing, cleaning, guest communication, maintenance and actual-versus-projected expenses, because “if unit number one isn't even profitable, adding more units doesn't solve the problem” [OSi_ARPIpaM @ 00:00:42].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The spread math starts with Sean Rakidzich's rule of thumb: “if you pay $2,000 a month in rent you should charge $3,000 a month.” In Houston, a 12-to-15-month lease ran $1,700, a four-month unfurnished term $2,200-2,300, and month-to-month, often not allowed, “a ballpark double of rent” at $3,000-3,400, because the building prices in its own down days; add roughly $300 a month for furniture and the operator's cost lands near $2,000 while charging the building's own monthly rate furnished, with internet and electricity included [lGwvDjwR4uU @ 00:01:33]. Brian Biernat, on Vicsmanor, works the same logic per door: a 3BR/2BA should gross at least $4,500 with costs held near $2,000, netting $2,500, or about $2,300 with high utilities, at $150-180 a night [obnSO0EuoMk @ 00:14:51]. Linda Kim's screening shortcuts are net at least the rent, a nightly rate around 10% of monthly rent ($3,000 rent, $300 a night), and a furniture budget of three to four times rent [5vV0oy-jJ7g @ 00:52:22]. Colin Wright puts a hard ceiling on the fantasy: $2,000 rent against $5,500 revenue yields roughly $1,500-2,000 operating profit, not the $3,500 a naive subtraction suggests [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:04:15].

Lease structure is where the operators claw back setup cash. Jorge Contreras asks for two weeks of free rent and staggers the dates: “if you're signing the lease on June 1st have it begin June 15,” keys handed over June 1, a prorated first month and full deposit, alongside an application, three months of bank statements and a credit report, with the credit pull allowed only once both sides are committed [_M0OdXelhoU @ 00:05:06]. 360 BNB Solutions runs a similar gap: deposit today (April 3), first payment May 1, so the weeks in between cover the e-lock and security camera installs, and offers a two-year lease “to show you we're serious” [prqw1liLbCM @ 00:02:49]. Colin Wright lists the full menu, covering free weeks, deposit, lease term, renewal, improvement credits and multi-unit pricing, and quantifies one lever: two months free on $2,000 rent is $4,000 saved [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:06:02]. LoCasa widens the lens to the total cash requirement rather than rent alone, negotiating security deposit, furnishing, lease start date, concessions and agreement structure, but warns to ask without strong-arming small investors; at scale, “saving $2,000 on the front end of one deal isn't just saving $2,000” [OSi_ARPIpaM @ 00:04:16].

Deal flow is a volume game with specific daily quotas. Rafa Loza, on Joshua Baldovino's channel, prescribes the bluntest version: “pull up all the apartments in your area you're just gonna start calling ... call at least one a day and get rejected 50 times,” which matches a record of 30-plus no's before a first yes and 20-plus more before the second [1JRlBob3GhE @ 00:22:40]. Richie Matthews budgets the time: 15-20 minutes a day for one to two weeks of market research covering cash flow, legality and saturation, then contacting every landlord on Zillow at 15-30 minutes a day for four to six weeks, with program members averaging six to eight weeks to close [fH0oThfTwlg @ 00:01:51]. Lara Stewart vetted amenities first (gated, well lit, resort-style pool, state-of-the-art fitness center), then cold-called 10-15 complexes and found about 10 of 15 open to corporate leases; the 2020-built complex allows up to 16 arbitrage leases [mOvanxNbxWw @ 00:07:48]. Colin Wright adds a targeting filter: listings sitting vacant 30-60 days on Zillow, plus AirDNA, BNB calc and comps for underwriting, as in Paul's deal at $1,000 rent against roughly $3,000 projected [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:04:31].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Underwrite the Market
   - Pull AirDNA data and cross-check comps manually before calling any landlord.
   - Use AirDNA for ADR, occupancy and seasonality, target roughly 70% occupancy or higher, favor a seasonality score near 99, and cross-check manually on Airbnb because Kim “wouldn't rely solely on AirDNA” [Linda Kim — 5vV0oy-jJ7g @ 00:05:38]
2. Filter Before Touring
   - Confirm corporate leases are allowed, then tour only turnkey units in person.
   - Ask whether corporate leases are allowed before any tour, tour in person like a partner, take only turnkey, livable properties, and put the LLC on the lease, never a personal name [Aurelio Licata — Q3hSpjquUTE @ 00:07:04]
3. Deliver the Script
   - Pitch the corporate housing offer with fixed rent, cleaning and repairs handled.
   - Pitch a corporate housing company for working professionals: a 12-month lease at the advertised rent ($3,750 per month in the example), operated with permission, a deep clean after every guest 4-8 times a month, “for sale” condition and minor repairs handled [Jorge Contreras | Airbnb Arbitrage — _M0OdXelhoU @ 00:00:16]
4. Paper the Deal
   - Attach a one-page lease amendment, then shop insurance after signing.
   - Strike the no-sublet paragraphs, grant the right to market online through a personal website, booking.com, Expedia, Airbnb and HomeAway, keep it to one page and have it legally reviewed, then shop traditional coverage with loss of income once the lease is signed [Short Term Sage - Airbnb, Vacation Rental Host — wt5AlXFsjDQ @ 00:12:26]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split on market selection is regulatory appetite. Aurelio Licata, who pitched 500-plus landlords on the way to 50-plus properties, says the biggest risks are the wrong market or property, not the pitch, and steers toward cities with clear permit and licensing regimes while avoiding 30-day minimums and unregulated markets, leaning on lease addendums for protection [Q3hSpjquUTE @ 00:14:05]. 360 BNB Solutions runs the opposite play: rebutting a commenter who claimed 99% of landlords refuse, the channel claims only four of 21,000 cities have banned it completely, says students land multiple deals a day, and hunts low-regulation, no-HOA markets [prqw1liLbCM @ 00:04:41]. On disclosure, the stealth option gets no support. Linda Kim does not recommend running under the radar and tells operators to ask the management company directly [5vV0oy-jJ7g @ 00:14:54]. Colin Wright, running 150-plus Airbnbs, goes further: they would never try to sneak Airbnb past a landlord and wants city, building, HOA and landlord permission in writing before launch [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:06:45]. The operator decision is binary: treat a city with clear permit rules as the safer bet, as Licata does, or chase low-regulation, no-HOA ground, as 360 BNB Solutions does; either way the landlord hears "corporate leasing company," never a stealth plan.

Property type is the second fault line, and it splits operators with real door counts. Richie Matthews calls single-family arbitrage a bad bet: $40,000 to $50,000 to furnish, heavy seasonality, owners who can evict a successful tenant, and cities targeting suburban short-term rentals, so Matthews runs apartments [fH0oThfTwlg @ 00:00:55]. Aurelio Licata likes the same math from a different angle, noting one complex can open 10 to 20 doors with a single point of contact, though Licata still says to diversify [Q3hSpjquUTE @ 00:14:53]. Adam of Integrity Corporate Housing, operating since 2014, prefers homes, citing apartment sales and HOA evictions, and leans on reliable landlords who grant first rights and handle repairs, reporting only two evictions in 15 years while targeting 90-plus-day stays that run 70 to 80% passive [AxwgX6EuFNc @ 00:37:21]. Linda Kim lands in the house camp too: single-family homes, the more bedrooms the better, starting with a three-bedroom, and steering away from dated units, apartments and HOAs entirely [5vV0oy-jJ7g @ 00:14:07]. The tradeoff the operators describe is scale and one relationship versus landlord reliability and bedroom count; Adam's own guest Mike Brown, at 31 doors while working full-time, broke in through a 40-to-50-unit complex owned by a national company, so the split runs even inside one conversation.

The deepest disagreement is whether to keep running arbitrage at all, and who runs it. Quentin West built 47 units across three states, then replaced the model with fractional hosting: take over pricing, guest communication and marketing for 15% or more of revenue, with no lease, cleaning or maintenance, backed by a measured average revenue lift of 18.68% [XSg1bi0bVwI @ 00:05:37]. West says flatly they would rather have 10 of those clients than the 47 units. Colin Wright rejects the "arbitrage is dead" line, but concedes phone-photo setups no longer work and that you absolutely can lose money [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:01:13]. On management, Linda Kim says to run about four units yourself before hiring help [5vV0oy-jJ7g @ 00:58:24], while Richie Matthews says never self-manage and never hire a property manager either, because 15 to 30% of gross is all of your profit; Matthews instead stacked automations, a $4-per-hour VA and software, taking 1.5 years to build a system that fully automated a 62-unit portfolio [fH0oThfTwlg @ 00:02:35]. The sales logic differs too: West's fractional pitch to self-managing owners is that the money is roughly a wash and getting their nights and weekends back is the sale, while Matthews justifies the VA in cash terms: the $800 cost equals one booking otherwise lost.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive firsthand failure belongs to Quentin West. Each of the 47 units cost $8,000 to $20,000 before the first guest arrived, covering first and last month, deposit, furniture and photos, and each took 12 to 18 months to turn a profit, funded with business credit cards, lines of credit and loans [XSg1bi0bVwI @ 00:01:24]. When COVID hit, West could not pay rent and needed two years to dig out [XSg1bi0bVwI @ 00:02:01], later summing it up as confusing cash flow with wealth. Colin Wright names the scaling version of the same trap as the biggest mistake: expanding before one property works, because a deal losing $1,000 a month copied 10 times loses $10,000 a month; one operator, Drew, turned a property profitable only by fixing pricing and getting the landlord to lower the rent [iwB0c3u7Gpo @ 00:09:53]. LoCasa frames the fix as discipline: the common mistake is wanting five units within a month, and if unit number one is not profitable, adding more units does not solve the problem, so the first unit becomes a laboratory for pricing, cleaning, guest messaging, maintenance and actual versus projected expenses [OSi_ARPIpaM @ 00:00:42]. Reserves stay in the bank rather than every dollar getting reinvested.

Signed-in-principle deals still collapse after the handshake. Richie Matthews had a first-day yes turn into a no because the landlord wanted to screen guests, and acquisitions in Matthews's program average six to eight weeks to close even with 15 to 30 minutes a day of calling every landlord on Zillow [fH0oThfTwlg @ 00:02:22]. The rent itself can become the trap. Adam, running 30 to 40% margins on a buy-low, rent-high model, watched one landlord spot $3,000 to $4,000 a month on Zillow against a $1,000 rent, which forced a renegotiation [AxwgX6EuFNc @ 00:35:03]; Adam's guest Mike Brown separately exited one lease over poor building management. Linda Kim protects the spread by walking away: when a landlord wanted $4,000 against the $3,700 Kim's numbers needed, Kim left, and the landlord came back a month later [5vV0oy-jJ7g @ 00:31:38]. Wording is the last trap. Rafa Loza pitches as corporate housing and leads with the customer base, but warns that the phrase gets misread as a 30-day minimum, inspects every unit for storage, doorbell cameras and keypad locks before committing, and says to read ordinances repeatedly and take city-staff answers with a grain of salt [1JRlBob3GhE @ 00:25:40].

Operational bleed shows up in the calendar and the furniture. Brian Biernat, who left a telecom job of 28 years after reaching 16 properties, hit slow Augusts and dead January-February stretches and fixed them by targeting 30-plus-day stays, insurance placements and contractor crews, with deposits covering the heavier wear [obnSO0EuoMk @ 00:19:22]. Biernat also predicts 20% of operators will exit if Airbnb is their only channel and warns against superhost FOMO; a VA-built direct booking site now drives 70 to 75% of bookings [obnSO0EuoMk @ 00:23:55]. Katie on Landlord Diaries, at roughly 14 arbitrage units in two years with no outside money, warns that Ikea furniture at 3x rent is not going to work and prefers replacing furniture over repairing it [mOvanxNbxWw @ 00:18:22]. Rafa Loza's failure examples come from picking the property before the guest: a maze-like building with no in-unit washer and dryer, and a three-bedroom in a business market that sat dead from October to April [894g1JF-IuM @ 00:42:40]. Rafa adds that remote fixes cost $100 to $200 each and quietly erode a $1,000 to $2,000 profit, which is why the starting market should be your own backyard, where you just drive over, meet the plumber and fix the problem.
