# Chapter 02: Liens vs. Deeds vs. Redeemable Deeds

*Interest Versus Ownership, Redemption Deeds and Hybrid States, Surplus Overages, State Strategy Comparisons, and Choosing a Model on a Small Budget*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one dividing line: a tax lien buys a claim plus statutory interest, a tax deed buys the property, and a redeemable deed hands over the deed subject to a final owner buy-back paid as a flat penalty. Lien redemption estimates run from 90% to 98%, so lien buyers price for yield while deed buyers price for discount, with Ted Thomas citing 10 to 20 cents on the dollar. Street numbers include $1,000 at 12% returning $120, an 18% lien redeemed in month one netting about 1.5%, Georgia's 20% penalty inside 12 months, and Texas windows as short as six months from the foreclosure date. The sharpest split is budget: Dustin Hahn pushes anyone under $20,000 into over-the-counter deeds, while Casey Deon steers sub-$100 budgets into liens and Sean Higgins calls liens and deeds the same process at different stages.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: Paper, Property, or a Deed With a Clock

Every practitioner opens with the same dividing line: a tax lien buys a claim, a tax deed buys the property. Cecil Jones strips it to one word for a live room, telling students that a deed is ownership and a lien is not, because the lien buyer is simply paying somebody else's property taxes and getting a government-backed return in exchange [Yq9tyhYQJGY @ 00:01:28]. Ted Thomas says the certificate buyer owns 'only a piece of paper' with no possession, earning 16%, 18%, or 24% while it stays outstanding, whereas deed counties confiscate the property, wipe out the mortgage and other liens, and auction it for back taxes plus fees because the county is 'in the tax business, not in the property business' [jWaS4Y2Z1Mg @ 00:00:49]. Dustin Hahn frames the operator role the same way: in a lien state 'you are the bank' lending the county money at a guaranteed rate, while in a deed state 'you are the buyer' and pretty much get the property day-of [akAZkzeH2PY @ 00:00:00]. Lien Ledger calls one product a loan and the other a project, warns that picking wrong means either earning almost nothing or inheriting a mess, and closes with the line most operators end up repeating: liens pay you to wait, deeds pay you to work [ul1onF24UbM @ 00:00:00].

The second consensus point is that the overwhelming majority of liens never turn into property. Dustin Hahn puts redemption at 95% and names the biggest redeemer: the mortgage bank, because on a $200,000 house carrying $2,000 a year in taxes, three unpaid years lets the lien holder foreclose, and if the bank does not redeem, the lien holder wipes the bank out [wemMjCnrX34 @ 00:01:09]. Casey Deon of Tax Sale Academy uses 90 to 95%, which is why the lien side gets described as a check in the mailbox once the research is done up front, with all the work front-loaded before the bid [oKQjnGAQlGs @ 00:02:28]. The Tax Sale Secrets hosts put lien redemption at 'more than 95%' [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:03:51], then draw the contrast that defines the third system: redemption deeds, which they estimate redeem around 50% of the time or less [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:11:13]. The Residential Buyer goes higher still, saying 98% of owners pay off the certificate with interest before the investor ever gets to file for foreclosure [2S4mkxCrOhM @ 00:01:22]. The shared operator takeaway: buy a lien expecting a yield, not a house, and treat the foreclosure as the rare home run rather than the plan.

The baseline prerequisite nobody skips is knowing the dirt under the paper, and knowing which system the target state runs. Ted Thomas holds up a Jacksonville, Florida newspaper listing 30,000 certificates with no pictures and mocks buyers who just point at one and buy, because a lot of certificates sit on worthless property; the fix is to go look at it, which on the lien side 'ends your work' [PSfIJ8cKE5I @ 00:04:41]. Casey Deon makes the same point from the collateral side: a lien not attached to valuable real estate 'does absolutely zero good,' so the research burden survives even on the passive product [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:03:41]. On system choice, the Tax Sale Secrets hosts stress that state law decides whether a jurisdiction is a lien, deed, or redemption-deed state, and that once an operator understands those three fundamentals, property tax enforcement anywhere in the country becomes readable [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:00:17]. Sean Higgins adds the scale: over 5,000 taxing jurisdictions, and every state plus Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and Guam runs some form of lien or deed sale, with the county's position superseding the bank and even the IRS [IH1JGgxRwIg @ 00:01:41]. Casey also tells students that where they live should not dictate what they buy [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:01:11].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Lien math is simple interest on a small check, and the timing of redemption decides the real yield. Casey Deon's walk-through: $1,000 of back taxes in a 12% state returns $120 if the owner redeems at the end of year one, quality liens typically cost $300 to $1,000 even though some sell for $50 or $100, and 12 to 18-ish percent returns are 'not uncommon' [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:02:42]. Lien Ledger runs a $3,000 lien at 12% simple for two years to $720 of interest, then flags the catch: redeem in month one and you earn one month of interest, and in competitive Florida counties bidders knock the 18% cap far lower, because 'competition eats the headline number' [ul1onF24UbM @ 00:04:18]. Cecil Jones makes the same point with Florida's 18%: a lien paid next month earns 18 divided by 12, about 1.5%, not the headline rate [Yq9tyhYQJGY @ 00:02:03]. On the ceiling, Ted Thomas quotes 16, 18, and 24 percent with Chicago paying up to 36% [x3XX9ysKcUA @ 00:04:16], and describes bidding $100,000 of back taxes on a huge shopping center because the certificate paid 16% against roughly 1% at Bank of America [x3XX9ysKcUA @ 00:05:53].

Deed economics run on discount, and redeemable-deed economics run on a flat penalty. Lien Ledger's deed model: buy a house for $20,000, add $10,000 in repairs and costs, sell for $60,000, and net $30,000, or 100% on cash, while noting that $20,000 buys either one house or about six $3,000 liens, a concentrated bet against a spread bet [ul1onF24UbM @ 00:04:42]. The Tax Sale Secrets hosts point to an Ohio auction where properties were back on the market within 30 days of acquisition, and recent deals showing about $100,000 of potential profit after estimated rehab [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:07:58]. Cecil Jones paid $1,500 for a Lake Jackson waterfront parcel in Georgia, received the deed on the spot, and explains that a redemption inside Georgia's 12-month window pays a flat 20% penalty even if the owner comes back the next day [Yq9tyhYQJGY @ 00:03:17]. Casey Deon sizes the upside at as much as 25% within six months, so a $100,000 purchase redeemed at month six returns $125,000, but warns redeemable deeds cost much closer to deed prices than lien prices [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:11:34]. The Tax Sale Secrets crew adds that when a Texas parcel opens at 10 and sells at 50, the owner owes 25% on the full high bid [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:12:00].

Execution starts with a phone call and ends with a title company. Dustin Hahn's first move is to call the county and, if staff say they don't do tax liens, ask what happens when people don't pay their property taxes; 'tax defaulted list' or 'sheriff's sale' tells you the system [akAZkzeH2PY @ 00:01:01]. Mika Coleman relies on the Tax Sale Resources platform to flag whether a state is a redeemable-deed or straight-deed state, and warns that redemption periods often run longer on homesteaded properties than non-homesteaded ones [tMIwxs2U5JM @ 00:01:18]. William of Premier Wealth Builders says most California deed auctions run online on sites such as Bid4Assets, Clark County, Nevada sold in person, and Virginia winners must go to court for approval; because a tax deed is not a warranty deed and carries a cloud on title, a generic First American office quoted three years before insuring, so the play is the specific title company that insures right away plus a good real estate attorney [RvfjpH1PWxE @ 00:05:57]. On funding, Ted Thomas reports a Michigan student named Bill bought 60 properties in 60 months on a credit card, and Ted put 30% down by card on a $300,000 New York property [jWaS4Y2Z1Mg @ 00:05:52].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Identify the county's enforcement system
   - Call the county treasurer or tax office and ask what happens when owners don't pay.
   - If staff say they don't do tax liens, ask what happens in the county when people don't pay their property taxes; 'tax defaulted list' or 'sheriff's sale' reveals the system [Dustin Hahn — akAZkzeH2PY @ 00:01:01]
2. Physically verify the collateral
   - Pull the published list and go look at every parcel before raising a hand.
   - The Jacksonville list carried 30,000 certificates with no pictures; buyers who just point and buy end up with worthless property, so go look at it and confirm it has value [Ted Thomas — PSfIJ8cKE5I @ 00:04:58]
3. Run the three pre-bid checks
   - Check value, check what else is owed, check the state's redemption rules.
   - Skip vacant lots and ruined buildings because cheap usually means cheap for a reason, and read each county's terms, deadlines, and deposits before bringing a dollar [Lien Ledger — ul1onF24UbM @ 00:07:18]
4. Price the exit, including foreclosure cost
   - Add the lien price to attorney foreclosure fees and compare against resale value.
   - A $1,500 lien plus a $2,500 attorney foreclosure puts $4,000 into a landlocked vacant lot that might be worth only three or four grand, leaving no room to make it work [Tax Sale Academy — FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:04:14]

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — How the pros tilt the odds before they bid**
> 
> Four edges practitioners use to steer redemption odds, penalty timing, and margin in their favor.
> - Buy liens further into the redemption period, or county-held leftovers, to start foreclosing quicker instead of waiting out the full clock [Tax Sale Secrets — 0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:02:55]
> - For pure yield, target occupied homes and businesses with a high likelihood of redeeming; to land property, target liens with a lower chance of redemption [Tax Sale Academy — oKQjnGAQlGs @ 00:08:22]

> - Size the cushion, the gap between what the house is worth and what is owed: a big cushion is safety for a lien buyer and profit for a deed buyer [Lien Ledger — ul1onF24UbM @ 00:06:33]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over which product a thin bankroll should touch first. Dustin Hahn tells anyone holding less than $20,000 to skip liens and start with over-the-counter tax deeds, because liens are 'not the fastest way to go' and Hahn's team likes speed [wemMjCnrX34 @ 00:02:51]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy runs the opposite playbook: with a budget under $100, buy liens as the entry point to get your feet wet, while conceding that deeds can still be had for a couple of grand or even less than a grand in some areas [oKQjnGAQlGs @ 00:09:46]. Ted Thomas backs the small-ticket lien route, citing certificates at $2,000 to $5,000 and even $500, while warning that most tax deeds do not sell for $5,000 [PSfIJ8cKE5I @ 00:01:56]. Tax Sale Secrets flips the framing again, calling liens a strategy 'for people that have money' who can park capital and collect a rate, and reserving tax deeds and redemption deeds for anyone trying to build wealth [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:02:16]. The same dollar figure lands on opposite sides depending on which desk you sit at: Hahn optimizes for velocity and quick buys, while Casey and Thomas optimize for cheap entry and low effort. Pick the camp that matches your clock, not the one with the loudest pitch.

Every speaker agrees most liens redeem, but nobody agrees on the number, and the gap matters for anyone underwriting a foreclosure play. Ted Thomas puts payoff at 97 percent of certificates and describes the interest landing in your checking account with a phone notice [PSfIJ8cKE5I @ 00:03:42]. The Residential Buyer quotes 98 percent [2S4mkxCrOhM @ 00:01:22], while Casey at Tax Sale Academy uses a 90 to 96 percent band that shifts with where and what you buy [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:03:04]. Lien Ledger brings city-level numbers that cut well below the headline: about 70 percent of sold properties redeemed in Washington DC with only about 1 percent going to foreclosure, and roughly 80 percent of certificates paid back in Baltimore [ul1onF24UbM @ 00:01:48]. Yield is just as contested. Thomas sells 16, 18 and 24 percent, but Lien Ledger warns that Florida's 18 percent cap gets bid down at auction until competition eats the headline number, and that a month-one payoff earns one month of interest. The practical split: Thomas frames the certificate as predictable, certain and secure money, while Lien Ledger calls it a slow state-backed loan that is boring on purpose, with a catch. Underwrite to the realized rate and the realized redemption timing, not the ceiling the state sets by law.

Redeemable deeds draw the widest spread of opinions. Tax Sale Secrets estimates that if 95 percent of liens redeem, probably 50 percent or less of redemption deeds do, and says chances run pretty low in Delaware's 60-day window or in Texas, where the six-month clock starts at foreclosure rather than the sale [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:11:13]. Cecil Jones prefers Georgia and Texas outright because the 20 percent Georgia penalty is owed even if the owner redeems the next day, which beats a lien paying 18 divided by 12 for a one-month hold [Yq9tyhYQJGY @ 00:03:41]. Mika Coleman calls the redeemable deed a win-win, either the property or a return like 25 percent on a six-month redemption, but flags that homesteaded properties often carry longer windows [tMIwxs2U5JM @ 00:00:58]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy agrees on the 25 percent upside yet gates it behind staying power, since you pay much closer to tax deed prices and tie up capital for six or 12 months [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:12:16]. The operator split is about liquidity: Jones and Coleman chase the fixed penalty, while Casey warns that anyone who needs every penny all the time should not wait a property out. Tax Sale Secrets also notes counties never use the term; the hosts coined the redemption deed label back in 2004 and 2005.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common lien loss is buying paper secured by dirt nobody wants. Ted Thomas holds up a Jacksonville, Florida newspaper with 30,000 certificates and no pictures, and describes buyers who just point at one and buy it; a lot of tax lien certificates sit on worthless property, so you have to go look at it [PSfIJ8cKE5I @ 00:04:58]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy runs the math on what happens next: pay $1,500 for a lien, pay an attorney roughly $2,500 to foreclose, and you have $4,000 in a landlocked vacant lot that might be worth three or four grand, while unresearched code violations can turn the deal into a negative return [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:04:14]. Lien Ledger names the same risk bluntly: if the house is a condemned shack or an empty lot, foreclosure gets you nothing worth having [ul1onF24UbM @ 00:05:06]. Tax Sale Secrets adds the hybrid-state version of the trap, where a lien that ends up at a county deed sale with no bidder is probably a property you never wanted anyway [0adTzNpuLtk @ 00:05:17]. The rule across all four: the rate is irrelevant when the collateral is junk, so value the land before you price the certificate, and walk from anything cheap for a reason.

Deed buyers get hurt after the gavel, not before it. The Residential Buyer warns that prior mortgages usually clear at foreclosure, but municipal liens and code violations ride through the sale to the new buyer, and since no one lets you into the home, you may be buying a literal meth lab [2S4mkxCrOhM @ 00:03:06]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy says the moment you win, it is yours good or bad; if somebody burns the house down that morning you need to know, and you inherit occupants and code violations immediately [oKQjnGAQlGs @ 00:09:12]. The same trainer reports new investors who haphazardly buy and only discover at resale that they own junk land, landlocked parcels, or lots too small to build on [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:09:44]. Premier Wealth Builders flags the exit bottleneck: a tax deed is not a warranty deed, the cloud on title has to be cleared, and the local First American Title office may refuse title insurance for three years unless you use a title company that will insure now [RvfjpH1PWxE @ 00:05:57]. Price every deed bid as acquisition plus surviving municipal liens, code fines, occupant removal and title clearing, then confirm access or drive-by condition before the auction, or the discount evaporates on the first invoice.

The economics breaks are quieter but just as costly. Cecil Jones warns that a Florida lien advertised at 18 percent and redeemed next month pays roughly 1.5 percent, which is why Jones routes lien-style money into Georgia redeemable deeds where the 20 percent penalty is owed even on a one-day redemption [Yq9tyhYQJGY @ 00:04:13]. Lien Ledger runs the deed side: buy at $20,000, add $10,000 in repairs and costs, sell at $60,000 and net $30,000, but if repairs double and the house sits for a year that profit shrinks fast, and on the lien side a $3,000 certificate at 12 percent simple returns $720 after two years of locked cash [ul1onF24UbM @ 00:04:42]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy tells the surplus story as a cautionary benchmark: a $3,000 overage drew about a dozen chasers quoting 25 to 60 percent fees, and the paperwork took five or six minutes to file solo [FgLt0Zd5dxo @ 00:15:31]. Premier Wealth Builders adds a jurisdiction trap: in Virginia a winning bid has to go to court for approval, while in California you own it right now [RvfjpH1PWxE @ 00:04:06]. Model hold time, court approval time, competition and fee drag before the bid goes in, not after the gavel.
