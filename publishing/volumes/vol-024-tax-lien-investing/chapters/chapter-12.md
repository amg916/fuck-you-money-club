## Executive Summary

Practitioners funding tax lien portfolios converge on three engines: self-directed retirement money, entity wrappers, and other people's money. Custodians at Advanta, Next Generation, NuView, and CamaPlan route bids, certified checks, and redemptions entirely through the IRA, with fees such as NuView's $95 per purchase batch and Next Generation's $325 per asset per year. Ted Thomas sets a $20-25K entry floor, while Stephen Morel says matching bank-level 15-18% yields takes six to seven figures because institutions absorb roughly 80% of delinquent liens. Practitioners split on checkbook control versus custodian-issued checks, whether to chase big counties, and whether over-the-counter liens are junk or maximum-rate bargains.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every custodian on the circuit describes the same plumbing: the retirement account, not the investor, is the buyer, payer, and payee. Jared of Next Generation has contracts titled 'Next Generation TS FBO client name plus IRA number' and insists that every due diligence cost and every dollar of income flow through the IRA [l9-Us7KWWPM @ 00:12:43]. Advanta's Reneka Lightbourne runs the same rule through a checkbook-control LLC: the operating agreement names the IRA as owner, Advanta signs it, a check lands in the LLC's own bank account, bids are paid from that account, and interest and profits flow back into it [c3YDDPR5wVo @ 00:06:23]. CamaPlan's co-founder bids in the name 'CamaPlan FBO name IRA number,' pulls a TIN for the IRA, and now pushes an entity with its own bank account because bidding sites demand one [U08uLxrDVE4 @ 00:36:29]. NuView frames itself as a custodian like Schwab or Fidelity that gives no advice and does not lock clients into stocks, bonds, and mutual funds, so liens and deeds can sit inside the account [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:03:22]. The operator takeaway is blunt: the capital stack starts with paperwork. If the bidder registration, the bank account, and the redemption payee do not all match the IRA or its LLC, the deal is structured wrong before the first bid.

On how much money it takes, the veterans agree the floor is higher than the guru pitch. Ted Thomas, 85 and in their 33rd year, says $500 is not enough and tells newcomers to start with at least $20-25K, keep their job, and get a coach [jDYlxXpw-e4 @ 00:36:45]. Stephen Morel of Juristdeed says roughly 80% of delinquent liens now go to institutional investors and that matching bank-level returns takes six to seven figures, with institutions pulling 15-18% year over year [UyiZp8ocowM @ 00:13:10]. Kessler, the PropertyOnion tax deed professor, reports banks putting $100M into Florida to earn $5M in 90 days and Capital One pushing about $150M a year into the state, which is why they call Florida auctions not even worth participating in anymore [vD0IitWLjZE @ 00:17:32]. Ross Budrakey's answer is the same: banks and funds own the big counties, a $1M fund in Fulton County paid above retail, so the small operator should not compete with those guys and should instead work smaller counties in the path of progress [VmzztAnZ71w @ 00:09:40]. Scaling, in this consensus, is a capital-allocation problem first. The small check wins where institutional money does not bother to show up, and the big check only competes on yield.

Structure consensus among the lawyers and custodians lands on separating the lien book from anything that carries owner liability. Clint Coons runs every lien, say 20 certificates, through one Wyoming LLC that is disregarded for federal tax purposes and files no return; redemption funds pay into the LLC and get distributed out, and Coons says there is no lawsuit risk because a lien holder does not own the property [bzu0eYCEXNg @ 00:02:10]. Stephan Hild of Provident Trust Group recommends a checkbook-control LLC specifically for tax lien investing [AcKbZeNuPr4 @ 00:56:15], and Advanta's host says the IRA can either send funds before the sale or the investor can run checkbook control through an IRA-owned LLC [RDk1Gksh9R4 @ 00:51:16]. Morel adds the out-of-state wrinkle: bidders working another state register an LLC there, and the work itself is the most non-passive passive investment in the passive investments [UyiZp8ocowM @ 00:35:01]. Coons then layers one Wyoming LLC per deed property under a Wyoming holding LLC parent for anything kept long term. The operator rule: one clean entity holds the paper, retirement dollars enter it only through the custodian or an IRA-owned LLC, and every property that can generate a lawsuit gets its own shell.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Sale-day cash logistics decide whether IRA money can even bid, and the custodians teach exact routines. NuView's online workflow: open the account, register as a county bidder, fund the bidding account with, say, $20,000, win $14,000 across five liens, return the $6,000 balance, then send purchase details so the temporary asset can be split into individual liens [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:13:23]. Online sales often want only a 10% deposit with the balance due in about 24 hours, but NuView prefers full funding upfront. For live sales, NuView issues certified checks, for example four $5,000 checks; on a $14,000 win the bidder hands over three and waits weeks for the $1,000 refund [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:29:28]. Next Generation does the same with cashier's checks payable to the township, and unused checks go back into the IRA [l9-Us7KWWPM @ 00:58:36]. Chantelle Owens adds the Georgia ground truth: pay at the tax commissioner's office within an hour or two after the auction, no personal checks, and most counties cannot take wires [RDk1Gksh9R4 @ 00:59:37]. The refund example is telling: a $50K check against a $49K bid leaves cash stranded at the county until it comes back. Break the stake into blocks so unused capital returns to the account intact.

Carrying costs are small but they compound against small liens, so practitioners quote exact numbers. Next Generation charges $50 at setup, $325 per asset per year, $100 per purchase, and $5 per check or $30 per wire; Jared calls the per-asset structure usually the best fee arrangement [l9-Us7KWWPM @ 00:16:07]. NuView flips the model: $95 for every batch of liens bought in one process and $300 a year for all liens held, which the speaker contrasts with Schwab and TD fees that are baked in [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:36:44]. CamaPlan runs $275 a year above $40K invested and $150-275 below it, and walks a $15K account through the math: $390 in first-year fees and $1,193 over five years against $2,700 a year, $13,500 total, in interest, netting about 50% more than a taxable account; a $250K account comes out about 22% ahead [U08uLxrDVE4 @ 00:49:01]. Provident quotes a $50 activation fee halved in the first year and warns that transfers out of Equity Trust take two to three weeks [AcKbZeNuPr4 @ 00:50:34]. The unit-economics check: a $325 per-asset fee on $1-2K liens eats a large slice of the yield, so per-asset custodians fit fewer, larger certificates while batch pricing fits volume buyers.

Once personal capital is deployed, the scaling playbooks shift to pooled and borrowed money. CamaPlan's pool example puts $250K into one entity: Bill's traditional IRA holds 50%, Bill's Roth 10%, Cheryl 15%, and Jim personally 25%; the pool throws off $2,400 interest and $2,000 principal monthly, Bill receives $1,200 plus $1,000, the pool earns 20% APR, and only Jim's personal share is taxed [U08uLxrDVE4 @ 00:19:42]. The passive alternative is lending to an operator at a fixed 10-12% while the operator earns 12-24% [U08uLxrDVE4 @ 00:22:45]. Ross Budrakey ran the street version: friends lend to the LLC at 10% on unsecured notes while the LLC earns Georgia's 20%, so a one-month redemption annualizes to 120%, and lenders are refunded if the bid strikes out [VmzztAnZ71w @ 01:24:13]. That OPM engine produced the Sherwood deal in Florida, bought at $60,300 with $25K of rehab and a $125K resale, for $30K profit in four months. Clint Coons keeps scaled holdings clean by never foreclosing inside the lien LLC; the certificate gets assigned to Wyoming LLC number two, which takes title, and flips run through a Wyoming LLC taxed as a C Corp to avoid dealer status and the loss of investor benefits [bzu0eYCEXNg @ 00:04:24].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Open and Fund the Self-Directed Account
   - Apply with a self-directed custodian, fund it, then invest.
   - Advanta's three steps are apply, fund by rollover, transfer, or contribution, then invest [Advanta IRA — c3YDDPR5wVo @ 00:09:01]; NuView says old-employer 401(k), 403(b), and 457 plans are 100% rollover-eligible, while current-employer plans depend on the plan administrator [NuView Trust — Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:42:18].
2. Pick the Entity Wrapper for Each Money Source
   - Put IRA dollars in an IRA-owned checkbook LLC and personal lien cash in a disregarded Wyoming LLC.
   - Advanta has the operating agreement name the IRA as owner and opens a separate LLC bank account for bids and returns [Advanta IRA — c3YDDPR5wVo @ 00:06:23]; Clint Coons uses a Wyoming LLC disregarded for federal tax purposes that files no return [Clint Coons Esq. | Real Estate Asset Protection — bzu0eYCEXNg @ 00:01:14].
3. Stage Certified Sale-Day Funds
   - Fully fund the bidding account or cut certified checks in blocks before the auction.
   - Nicole at Next Generation says bids must be paid in full with certified funds [Next Generation — l9-Us7KWWPM @ 00:59:19]; NuView prefers full funding upfront even when online sales take a 10% deposit with the balance due in about 24 hours [NuView Trust — Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:23:22].
4. Book, Split, and Recycle Redemptions
   - Split won liens into individual assets and route every redemption back into the entity.
   - NuView splits the temporary asset into individual liens once purchase details arrive [NuView Trust — Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:13:23]; Coons has redemption funds paid into the lien LLC and then distributed to the owner [Clint Coons Esq. | Real Estate Asset Protection — bzu0eYCEXNg @ 00:02:29].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is how money reaches the auction. NuView Trust lays out two live-auction routes: a checkbook-control LLC, or custodian-issued certified checks, such as four $5,000 checks against a $14,000 win, with the investor waiting weeks for the unused $1,000 to come back [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:29:28]. For online sales NuView wants the bidding account fully funded upfront even when the county only asks for a 10% deposit. Next Generation runs the same paper route, issuing cashier's checks payable to the township, routing unused checks back into the IRA, and insisting every due diligence cost and dollar of income flow through the plan [l9-Us7KWWPM @ 00:58:36]. The CamaPlan speaker pushes the other way: bidding sites now demand a bank account, so they recommend setting up an entity with its own account instead of bidding under the custodian's FBO title alone [U08uLxrDVE4 @ 00:36:29]. Provident Trust lands in the same camp and flatly recommends a checkbook-control LLC for tax lien work [AcKbZeNuPr4 @ 00:56:15]. The trade-off is speed versus custody. Custodian checks keep every dollar inside the administrator's paperwork but lock capital in transit for weeks; the LLC gives the operator a live account that can pay a winning bid the same day.

The deepest rift is whether a lien is a yield instrument or a back door to the property. Ted Thomas plays both sides: they claim 97% pay off on paper, yet point to unpaid cases that delivered $200,000 properties for $1,000 to $2,000, and they flag divorce, fire, abandonment and death as signs a lien will not redeem [jDYlxXpw-e4 @ 00:32:14]. NuView Trust shuts that door hard, telling IRA investors not to buy a lien to get the property, because the best properties pay off fastest and the sweet spot is a good rate paid off late [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:18:15]. Joanne Musa splits the difference by state selection: if the goal is acquiring real estate, move capital into tax-deed or redeemable-deed states rather than waiting out a lien [DHgFebJCtQU @ 00:21:29]. Chantelle Owens runs the acquisition playbook openly, reporting a personal redemption rate under one percent after a decade of buying only through tax sales in Georgia's redeemable-deed system [RDk1Gksh9R4 @ 00:59:37]. For portfolio construction this is a fork, not a nuance. A yield book is sized for volume, laddered reinvestment and fast redemption; an acquisition book is sized for fewer, larger positions with foreclosure, title and rehab capital held in reserve.

Practitioners also split on where a growing book should hunt. Ross Budrakey's rule is not to compete with banks and funds that dominate big counties, citing a $1 million fund in Fulton County paying above retail, and to target smaller counties in the path of progress instead [VmzztAnZ71w @ 00:09:40]. Kessler goes further on Florida, where banks bid everything down to a quarter of one percent and funds bid 0.025% on 50 liens per page, concluding it is not even worth participating anymore [vD0IitWLjZE @ 01:04:08]. The live-versus-online question cuts the opposite way depending on who is speaking. Joanne Musa prefers live sales precisely because online sales are more competitive [DHgFebJCtQU @ 00:13:05]. Ted Thomas agrees online auctions bid higher and in-person rooms push prices down, yet still calls online buyers the buyers of the future, since there are roughly 5,000 auctions across 3,000 counties, all of them online [jDYlxXpw-e4 @ 00:30:13]. The scaling playbook that falls out is geographic arbitrage: Kessler contrasts Maricopa at 1% to 2% with Apache County at 6% to 10%, and the operators who keep margins are the ones willing to travel to rooms and counties the institutions skip.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive reversals come from skipping the property check, and several operators admit taking the hit themselves. Joanne Musa, who otherwise calls lien drive-bys a waste of gas, says they got burnt last year precisely by skipping inspection, and warns online photos can be one to three years old [DHgFebJCtQU @ 00:50:46]. On the Advanta IRA stage, Mo Hodges describes a buyer who paid $9,100 believing they had a Florida villa, received a strip of land instead, and is now in litigation, the textbook buyer-beware loss [c3YDDPR5wVo @ 00:38:35]. Kessler's first deed was a gutted mobile home that only broke even, which is why they now run two drive-bys, one by day and one by night, before any deed bid [vD0IitWLjZE @ 00:38:30]. The Tax Sale Support team puts the stakes bluntly: more people have lost money skipping on-site evaluation than by any other means, and they schedule visits a few days before the sale so redemptions clear first [sH-Esa2i1zE @ 00:26:28]. The economics check is simple. A $100 realtor visit or $15 photo set is trivial against a $9,100 strip of land, so the inspection budget belongs inside the per-deal cost before the bid ceiling is ever set.

The second trap is underpricing time and legal drag. Stephen Morel bought secondary-market liens after the redemption period had run, then hit an heir in occupancy with no probate opened and serial bankruptcy filings; expected time to liquidity of 9 to 18 months stretched to about three years and ended in cash-for-keys with first month's rent and moving costs paid [UyiZp8ocowM @ 00:29:16]. Morel also warns that one or two missed noticing deadlines can derail an entire year's returns [UyiZp8ocowM @ 00:39:08]. Ted Thomas tells buyers to plan on $2,000 to $5,000 extra for a clear title action, which normally takes months and in one case took 18 [jDYlxXpw-e4 @ 00:41:33]. In Georgia, Mo Hodges puts foreclosure at about 90 days after the 365-day period, with the investor paying for everything that happens after day 365 [c3YDDPR5wVo @ 00:42:21]. For a scaling portfolio these are not edge cases; they are recurring carrying costs that compound across every position held. A lien book funded with IRA dollars cannot pay a surprise attorney from outside the plan, so the legal reserve, the noticing calendar and a realistic three-year worst-case hold all have to be modeled before capital is committed.

Custody fees and rate compression are the quiet killers of a small IRA lien book. Next Generation's best structure is $325 per asset per year, on top of $100 per purchase and $5 per check or $30 per wire [l9-Us7KWWPM @ 00:16:30]. On a $1,000 to $2,000 lien bid down to single digits, a per-asset fee can consume most of the interest. NuView Trust prices differently, at $95 per purchase batch and $300 a year for all held liens, which favors buying many liens in one process [Hqqt5H7mS3o @ 00:36:44]. The same speaker reports winning Florida rates of 15% to 17% in 2008 now running below 6%. CamaPlan's math shows fees can still pencil out: a $15,000 account pays $390 in first-year fees and $1,193 over five years against $2,700 a year in interest, netting about 50% more than a taxable account [U08uLxrDVE4 @ 00:49:01]. Platinum Investment Properties Group explains why rates keep falling: Florida went online, hedge funds now bid a quarter of a percent to capture the 5% penalty, and sales went from 100 buyers to 50,000 [AcKbZeNuPr4 @ 00:59:40]. The check is mechanical: divide annual custody cost by expected interest per position before scaling the count.
