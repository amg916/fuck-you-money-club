## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the statutory rate is only a ceiling and the auction format decides the real yield. Florida and Arizona bid interest down from 18% and 16%; New Jersey runs 18% to 0% and then flips into dollar premiums at 0% interest; Colorado and other overbid states fix the rate and auction the premium; Louisiana and Utah bid down ownership percentage instead. Institutional money and online platforms grind popular counties toward zero, yet a 0.25% Florida bid still collects a 5% penalty on fast redemption, Colorado pays about 13%, and Arizona proxy engines can award the full 16% to a lone bidder. Operators split on whether quarter-point fast-five plays and 1% ownership bids are smart bets or traps when liens sit unredeemed for years, and on whether to fight New Jersey institutions or pivot elsewhere.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner on this beat agrees on one thing first: the statutory rate printed in the law is a ceiling, and the auction format decides how much of it survives to your pocket. Casey Demmon of Tax Sale Academy lays out the bid-down engine plainly: the sale opens at the highest allowable rate set by statute, bidders call it down in steps, and whoever accepts the lowest rate takes the lien, with Florida opening at 18% and sliding through 16, 14, 12, 10, nine, eight [0XlgBwC8tog @ 00:00:49]. Julia M. Spencer frames it against a normal auction: the price is set and does not change, so competitors are not bidding the price up, they are bidding the interest down to the lowest number they can live with [NAHQ-7eWR_c @ 00:01:25]. Olivia at Smart Capital hammers the same point from the yield side, calling the 18%, 16%, and 24% headlines ceilings set by law rather than promises, because in a competitive county the 18% routinely gets bid down to a small fraction of the ceiling [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:07:14]. Realauction's New Jersey walkthrough shows the extreme version: interest bids run from 18% to 0%, and once bidding passes zero it flips into dollar premiums tied to 0% interest [hKsLdKqVOE0 @ 00:00:54].

The second consensus point: premium, or overbid, formats invert the auction while freezing the rate. Casey Demmon describes premium states as ones where the interest rate is set by state statute and never changes, and the lien goes to whoever pays the most above the face value of the back taxes, with the gap between what is owed and what is paid called the premium or overbid [0I_mhnrF0So @ 00:01:05]. Brian Seinsticker of Tax Sale Resources says that whatever the local label, high bid premium, true bid premium, bid up, it is the traditional auction most people already picture, live or online, outbidding the other bidders in dollars [6hRZuHmCM0M @ 00:01:10]. The Tax Sale Secrets hosts put Colorado in this camp: a 3-year redemption under a premium bid system where a $1,200 lien gets bid to 1,201, 1,202, 1,203, and paying $5 or $10 over is effectively how you bid down your interest [stFlmSmpP3Q @ 00:01:43]. Smart Capital's Olivia adds that some counties run round-robin or rotational systems instead, and insists you know which system your county uses before you show up, because the winning strategy is completely different under each [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:17:46]. Misread the format and you misread the yield before placing a single bid.

The third agreement is about who is on the other side of the paddle. Josh, on Dustin Hahn's Tax Lien School panel, says New Jersey makes their worst-states list because large investors scoop up everything and are willing to bid it down to 0%, which makes it very hard for anyone to be scrappy when institutions or hedge funds show up [mHvzzGVcYdI @ 00:01:36]. Julia M. Spencer reports the same pattern: institutional investors like banks bid the interest down to almost zero or half a percent because they do not care about keeping properties and have a very high amount of money to park in liens on valuable properties [NAHQ-7eWR_c @ 00:02:17]. The SKW Central trainer quotes an article on Pima County, Arizona, where banks bought nearly 6,000 tax liens totaling at least $15.8 million after the 2008 bailout, earning up to 16% with a three-year foreclosure trigger, and concludes that if they recognize the value, so should individual investors [e-CuINE8x-g @ 00:00:48]. Smart Capital's Olivia names the mechanism behind the compression: online platforms like Real Auction opened the market to out-of-state money, which increased competition and pushed winning rates down in popular counties [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:16:39]. Big money and easy access both grind the rate toward the floor.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The mechanics start inside the bidding software. Realauction's New Jersey walkthrough runs the click path: Place Bids on the left, the batch summary screen, click a batch number, then work the bid menu where each ADV number opens item details and the face amount sits in the center; a page-fill tool drops one rate across every item, but you still must click Bid or Submit Bids, and it only covers the page you are looking at, not later pages in the batch [hKsLdKqVOE0 @ 00:02:10]. The Arizona module adds the setting that silently changes your yield: in a proxy bid county, if you bid 5% and nobody else bids, the engine wins you the 16% maximum, while in a direct bid county 5% is what you get even as the only bidder [cleRXY7vybQ @ 00:02:20]. Smart Capital's Olivia layers on session discipline: big online sales run in batches with hundreds of liens each over several days, bidders in every batch make worse decisions in the last one, and experienced bidders pre-rank targets and set automated maximums so the plan executes when attention fades [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:18:18]. They also flag Florida's cash clock: 10% at the auction and the balance within 48 hours, or you can lose the deposit and the certificate [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:17:12].

Real yield math splits into interest and penalty. Dustin Hahn explains interest states accrue monthly, so a 12% certificate pays 1% a month, and a lien redeemed one month after sale returns 1.33% at Arizona's 16% or 1.5% in Florida at the full rate, adding that much again for every month it sits [Ip2V3ixjk4k @ 00:03:52]. Penalty states charge the full amount on day one: Hahn's Texas example has the owner owing 25% on top of what you paid whether they redeem on day one or day 179, and Georgia charges 20% the day after the sale plus another 10% past the one-year mark [Ip2V3ixjk4k @ 00:02:28]. Brian Seinsticker connects the two inside Florida: the 'fast five' model works because a 5% penalty applies even if the lien redeems the next day, so a bidder at a quarter percent technically still earns 5% [XbThUArmcIE @ 00:01:24]. Seinsticker frames it as a way of deploying capital for a quick 5% and moving it somewhere else, not a strategy for earning interest over a multi-year period. Casey Demmon reports seeing liens sell at 1% or 1.5% for exactly that reason, since many states add a penalty of maybe five or six percent on top [0XlgBwC8tog @ 00:01:38]. The bid rate and the realized rate are two different numbers.

For pricing, Casey Demmon refuses a one-size premium formula for individuals, noting institutions with tens of millions in liens can run one because everything averages out; instead, call the county for redemption stats, get a figure like 1.8 years or two years, and model a fake $1,000 lien with a given premium against the return you need [cnd7EM15dyk @ 00:00:40]. The stacking angle changes that math: in some states, Casey Demmon says, the first-year holder gets first option on years two, three, and four, so a bidder can take year one at 1% and buy the later liens at 15% or 16% [0XlgBwC8tog @ 00:01:55]. Dustin Hahn confirms the New Jersey version, where you bid the rate down but subsequent liens added on top earn the full interest rate amount, calling it very powerful if done right [Ip2V3ixjk4k @ 00:04:58]. Smart Capital's Olivia runs a straight interest play for scale: an $8,000 lien won at a 12% bid-down rate with a two-year redemption collects roughly $1,900, about 24% total over two years, with a $200,000 house as collateral the whole time [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:21:03]. Every model starts from expected hold time, not from the auction-day rate.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Identify the format and the engine
   - Confirm bid-down, premium, ownership, or rotational format and whether the county runs proxy or direct bidding.
   - Open the county or municipality bidding rules to see whether a proxy auction will win you the maximum rate or a direct auction will lock in exactly what you typed [Realauction — cleRXY7vybQ @ 00:02:43].
2. Get the county's redemption timeline
   - Call the county for redemption stats and build a personal premium or rate formula off that hold period.
   - Use the county's figure, such as 1.8 years or two years, then run a fake $1,000 lien with a given premium against the return you need, adjusting as you go [Tax Sale Academy — cnd7EM15dyk @ 00:00:40].
3. Separate penalty from interest
   - Know whether the state pays a flat penalty on early redemption before deciding how low to bid.
   - In Florida a quarter-point bid still earns the 5% penalty if the lien redeems the next day, which is the whole basis of the fast-five model [Tax Sale Resources — XbThUArmcIE @ 00:01:41].
4. Set the walk-away cap and load the platform
   - Pre-rank targets, write down a maximum, and set automated maximums where the platform allows.
   - Start from a conservative value, multiply by a 60 to 70% safety factor, subtract surviving liens, quiet title costs, and holding costs, and stop when bidding passes that number [Smart Capital — TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:18:51].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fault line is whether a bid cap should come from a fixed formula at all. Olivia at Smart Capital teaches a hard walk-away number set before the gavel drops: conservative value times 0.65, minus every surviving lien, minus quiet title costs, minus holding costs, with no exceptions once bidding passes it. On a $200,000 Florida house carrying an $8,000 lien, that math lands at $115,000 of maximum total exposure [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:19:24]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy refuses to hand out a one-size formula for individuals, conceding only that institutions sitting on tens of millions of dollars in liens can run one because they play the long average and everything evens out [cnd7EM15dyk @ 00:00:15]. Casey's alternative is a personal formula: call the county for redemption stats, take the typical 1.8 or 2 years as the starting number, model a fake $1,000 lien at a given premium, and solve for the return required, folding in suit and quiet title costs whenever foreclosure is on the table [cnd7EM15dyk @ 00:00:40]. Brian Seinsticker of Tax Sale Resources adds that high bid premium, true bid premium and bid up are all names for the same traditional bid-up auction, so the formula fight is really about how far to chase a familiar price war [6hRZuHmCM0M @ 00:01:10].

The second split is whether a near-zero rate is a smart play or a slow bleed. Brian Seinsticker explains the Florida 'fast five': bidders drive the rate to a quarter point because a 5% penalty pays even if the lien redeems the next day, which turns the certificate into a way to deploy capital, grab a quick 5%, and move the money somewhere else [XbThUArmcIE @ 00:01:24]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy reports liens selling at 1% or 1.5% for the same reason, because many states stack a penalty of five or six percent on top, and lays out a second angle: take the first-year lien at 1% to hold the first option on years two, three and four, then buy those later liens at 15% or 16% [0XlgBwC8tog @ 00:01:38]. Dustin Hahn's crew runs identical logic in New Jersey, where the initial lien gets bid down but subsequent liens bought on top carry the full interest rate, a move Dustin calls very powerful when done right [Ip2V3ixjk4k @ 00:04:58]. Julia M. Spencer reads the floor differently: banks and other institutions bid toward zero or half a percent because they do not care about keeping properties and have huge sums to park against high-value collateral [NAHQ-7eWR_c @ 00:02:17].

The third split is about which arena to enter in the first place. Josh, on Dustin Hahn's panel, ranks New Jersey among the worst states because hedge funds and large institutions sweep the sale and bid it to zero, so a scrappy buyer should stick to smaller-population areas or pivot to Pennsylvania's over-the-counter tax deeds or to New York [I9YP4P0-OM0 @ 00:00:44]. The Tax Sale Secrets hosts argue the opposite end of the size curve in Colorado: a $128,000 lien on a Best Western valued near $5.7 million, and roughly $250,000 in liens tied to one hotel operator, are buys for a millionaire because few bidders can bring that cash, and even rate-minded big fish might leave them close to 10% [stFlmSmpP3Q @ 00:10:01]. On ownership bid-down sales, Brian Seinsticker says the ownership percentage is irrelevant to redemption earnings in Louisiana, where 95 to 97 percent of liens redeem, so the odd format alone is no reason to stay away [3thEKo1DOwU @ 00:03:36]. Casey treats the same format as a co-ownership fight waiting to happen: a 30% winner who forecloses must negotiate a buyout with the 70% delinquent owner or sue to force a partition sale, usually with a hired attorney [YMBhKuuupDc @ 00:03:07].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap the practitioners report is a cheap rate or a fat premium colliding with the wrong redemption clock. Brian Seinsticker warns that the quarter-point Florida play burns the buyer who lands a lien that never redeems: hold it two years and the effective return falls to about two and a half percent a year, a figure Brian says does not even keep pace with inflation [XbThUArmcIE @ 00:02:11]. Casey at Tax Sale Academy walks through the premium-side version using a real certificate with a $293.57 face value and a $28.82 overbid: in states where the overbid is not reimbursed or earns no interest, paying too much and getting redeemed before enough months accrue to cover the premium means losing money outright [0I_mhnrF0So @ 00:02:27]. Olivia at Smart Capital lists the same timing wound from the opposite direction, budgeting for a two-year hold only to watch the owner redeem in month three at a pro-rated minimum return while the capital sat idle [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:28:49]. The Realauction New Jersey walkthrough shows why premium pain lands so hard there: once bidding moves past rate and into dollars above face, every premium bid associates with 0% interest on redemption [hKsLdKqVOE0 @ 00:02:28]. The economics check is identical across all four: the redemption timeline, not the headline rate, decides whether the bid made money.

The second failure pattern is showing up where the capital in the room is far bigger than yours. Josh, on the Tax Lien School panel, says most New Jersey sales get scooped up by large investors willing to bid to 0%, leaving more competition there than even in big California cities [mHvzzGVcYdI @ 00:01:53]. The host adds that desirable California counties such as Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo and Ventura see little action at all, because a bank carrying a million-dollar mortgage will simply pay several thousand in back taxes rather than risk getting eliminated [mHvzzGVcYdI @ 00:00:15]. The SKW Central trainer reads an article reporting that bailed-out banks bought nearly 6,000 Pima County liens totaling at least $15.8 million, chasing up to 16% with a three-year path to foreclosure, and turns the initial anger into a signal that the asset class is worth pursuing [e-CuINE8x-g @ 00:00:48]. Olivia at Smart Capital explains why the crowd keeps growing: online platforms opened the market to out-of-state investors, which increased competition and pushed winning rates down in popular counties, the trade-off of convenience [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:16:55]. The fix the practitioners report is geographic rather than tactical: smaller counties, over-the-counter leftovers, and states where the heavy money is not parked.

The third cluster is pure execution failure, where the bid or the paperwork breaks rather than the deal itself. The Realauction New Jersey tutorial flags that the fill tool types a number only across the page being viewed, not every item in a multi-page batch, and that bids still need the bid key or the submit bids click to register at all [hKsLdKqVOE0 @ 00:02:10]. Olivia at Smart Capital names fatigue as a real cost in multi-day batch auctions, noting that investors who bid in every batch make worse decisions in the last one, so experienced bidders pre-rank targets and set automated maximums wherever the platform allows it [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:18:18]. Olivia also calls missed notices the single most common way investors lose a valid claim: a wrong address or a filing one day late has led courts to throw out entire foreclosures, taking the legal fees, the time, and sometimes the investment position with them [TL6AIsoe5m0 @ 00:25:09]. Brian Seinsticker supplies the endgame trap for ownership bid-down states: bid ownership down to 1% and a foreclosure leaves the investor sharing the property with a 99% previous owner who is unhappy about it, a setup Brian expects to breed confusion and disputes [3thEKo1DOwU @ 00:02:48]. Every one of these losses hit after the hard work of finding the lien was already done.
