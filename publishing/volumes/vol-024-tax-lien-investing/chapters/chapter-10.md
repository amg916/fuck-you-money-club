## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree a junior federal tax lien can follow a foreclosed property past the auction: the IRS holds a 120-day redemption right, or longer if state law allows, to buy the property back from the winning bidder and resell it. The payout is the bid plus 6% annual interest plus necessary preservation expenses, minus rent collected or rental value of occupancy, and Natalia Willette warns bidders have 15 days after the IRS redemption letter to submit proof. Cosmetic rehab inside the window is lost if the IRS redeems. On the debtor side, the NFTL attaches to all present and future property, vanished from credit reports in 2018 but still kills deals at title search, and practitioners split on whether bureaus still pick it up and how freely the IRS grants withdrawals beyond the $25,000 direct debit path.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who works the auction side agrees on the core threat: a junior federal tax lien sitting in the chain of title does not simply evaporate when the gavel drops. Global Estate Hub frames it as a fully legal trap that catches seasoned investors and flippers off guard, where the government can show up a month after the sale, hand the winning bidder a check, and take the house back [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:00:46]. The stated motive is pure collection math: if a property sells for pennies on the dollar, the IRS loses its recovery, so it uses its statutory power to buy the property back from the bidder and resell it at a higher fair market value, effectively running its own flipping side hustle [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:02:07]. Florida real estate attorney Natalia Willette of LCO Law confirms the same 120-day right of redemption applies across mortgage foreclosure auctions, tax deed auctions, and similar proceedings, and once the IRS exercises it the bidder is no longer the owner [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:00:00]. Willette adds a structural point bidders miss: the lien is only extinguished when the foreclosure actually goes all the way to sale; if the matter settles or ends in a deed in lieu, the federal lien survives intact [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:01:00].

On what a federal tax lien actually grips, the tax pros line up tightly. Logan Allec, a CPA who owns Choice Tax Relief, explains that the lien exists the moment an assessed bill goes unpaid, a so-called secret lien that attaches automatically to everything the taxpayer owns now and acquires later, including business property and the money customers owe the business [joIEfxnWZzE @ 00:02:01]. Anton of Stop IRS Problems quotes the same sweeping reach, all assets now owned and future acquired, recorded with the county recorder in the taxpayer's last known county [MLaWCy3MlMQ @ 00:01:13]. The Notice of Federal Tax Lien is just the public announcement of that claim, and Allec stresses it is a claim, not a seizure: a lien is a claim to property, while a levy is the actual taking of a bank account or paycheck [joIEfxnWZzE @ 00:04:57]. The former revenue officer behind Former IRS Agent Representation adds the operator reality check: technically the lien hits furniture, cars, and personal property, but in practice it only really affects real property, because that is what surfaces when someone runs a property search [Y2mRqIi8jqY @ 00:04:01]. For an auction bidder, that is the whole point: the lien lives on title, and title is exactly where it gets found.

The third consensus point is the one that blindsides people: the NFTL dropped off consumer credit reports, but it never left public record. Allec documents that Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion voluntarily stopped reporting tax liens as of April 2018 after errors linked liens to the wrong people with matching names, and stresses it was a voluntary bureau decision, not a law, so it could theoretically be reversed [_kLMdX9sR6Y @ 00:06:50]. Clarity Tax Relief puts the operator consequence bluntly: the FICO score looks clean and the refinance still dies at the title search, because lenders, title companies, and business creditors pull public records as a matter of course [oPKD4zP1XUQ @ 00:02:14]. Clarity also sizes the pipeline: IRS Data Book numbers show 214,099 NFTL filings in FY2025, up from 196,996 in FY2024 and 179,019 in FY2023, an 8.7% one-year jump and 19.6% over two, even as the agency lost roughly 27% of its workforce, because filing is automated and tied to account balance [oPKD4zP1XUQ @ 00:00:24]. Allec notes the IRS generally does not file when the unpaid balance is below $10,000, though someone at the agency typically makes a case-specific filing determination before the notice goes out [joIEfxnWZzE @ 00:03:34].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The redemption clock runs on hard dates. Global Estate Hub lays out the sequence: for a non-judicial foreclosure to touch a federal tax lien, the foreclosing entity, usually the bank, must deliver written notice to the IRS at least 25 days before the sale; without that notice the lien survives completely intact, and with it the lien is discharged and the clock starts on day zero, the sale date, running 120 days or the period allowed under state law, whichever is longer, so some markets run past four months [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:03:17]. Willette pins the authority to 28 US Code Section 2410, the provision under which the IRS subjects itself to junior lienholder status in mortgage and tax deed foreclosures and sets the notice it requires [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:00:40]. The core payout formula matches across both accounts: the IRS refunds the exact winning bid, adds 6% per year statutory interest from the sale date, and adds necessary expenses to secure and maintain the property, then subtracts any rent collected and, if the bidder lived there, the reasonable rental value of that occupancy [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:04:05]. Willette adds that senior liens a bidder pays off during the window, such as another recorded mortgage, may also be reimbursable, subject to the notice requirement [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:01:56].

What counts as a reimbursable expense is where bidders bleed money. Global Estate Hub lists casualty insurance, changing the locks, paying property taxes, and fixing a burst pipe as reimbursable, while new kitchen cabinets, granite countertops, and hardwood floors are not; start the cosmetic flip before day 120 and a redemption wipes out every single cent of that spend [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:05:08]. Willette draws the line even tighter: utilities, cleaning, cosmetic repairs, and current-year property taxes do not qualify, but outstanding property taxes that were already due and senior to the IRS lien do, as does a roof repair needed to preserve the condition of the structure [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:02:16]. Then comes the hard deadline: once the IRS sends a letter, which Willette identifies as a 5597, stating it will redeem, the bidder has 15 days from receipt to send back full expense documentation plus proof of release on any superior mortgages paid off, and Willette calls that 15-day period very key [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:02:57]. The silver lining Global Estate Hub flags is that capital parked in a redeemed house earns the guaranteed 6% statutory rate, which the channel jokes is probably the only time the IRS will happily pay an investor interest [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:05:53].

The exit from a dead-money waiting period is paperwork, not patience. Global Estate Hub warns that trying to resell on day 30 hits a brick wall: title companies mandate an exception on the policy during the 120-day window, and no standard buyer's mortgage lender will fund a loan over that exception [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:06:21]. The workaround is an application to the IRS Collection Advisory Group for a waiver of the redemption right, using documents such as Letter 5597 or a certificate of discharge; if the IRS runs the numbers and sees no equity to flip, the right is effectively valueless and the release is issued for free, while a decision to redeem triggers a certificate of redemption, the formula payout, and title transferring to the United States [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:07:07]. On the owner-side mechanics that shape how much equity the IRS sees, Allec describes Form 14135 discharge as the house-sale tool that strips the lien off one specific property, usually in exchange for payment from proceeds or when there is not enough meat on the bone, filed at least 45 days before closing [joIEfxnWZzE @ 00:10:36]. Clarity adds the verification layer for any lien status check: transcript code 582 marks a lien filed and code 583 marks a later release [oPKD4zP1XUQ @ 00:04:09].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split is whether a filed Notice of Federal Tax Lien still lands on a credit file. Logan Allec says Experian, Equifax and TransUnion voluntarily stopped reporting liens after a CFPB study found entries linked to the wrong people, and stresses that voluntarily means the bureaus could reverse course [_kLMdX9sR6Y @ 00:06:50]. IRS Notices Explained quotes Experian outright, tax liens are no longer a part of credit reports, and tells viewers the score itself is safe from this public record [B1e8xDSixVg @ 00:00:45]. Chad Silver of IRS FBAR Tax Attorney runs the opposite script, warning that a lien often drops a score 100 points and surfaces on background checks for employment and professional licenses [D-ia73OSPG0 @ 00:00:52]. Anton at Stop IRS Problems lands in the middle: the government no longer files liens with the bureaus, yet the bureaus still pick them up from county recorders through digital exchange, and some liens even get published in newspapers where a local school district might see them [MLaWCy3MlMQ @ 00:02:08]. Every camp agrees on one point that matters at the closing table: the notice is a recorded public document, so any lender or title examiner who searches county records will find it no matter what a bureau prints.

On getting the notice withdrawn without paying in full, the split is how rigid the $25,000 line is. Bob Jablonsky treats it as a hard checklist: $25,000 or less assessed including penalties and interest, a direct debit installment agreement that pays out within 60 months or before the ten-year statute, full filing compliance, then three consecutive direct debit payments before Form 12277 goes in [EpqtO6uiV_E @ 00:02:37]. Logan Allec runs the same playbook, telling clients to push the balance to $25,000 or below first, but reports beating the threshold: the firm has argued the notice was inhibiting a taxpayer's ability to earn income, mostly for real estate operators, and got the IRS to withdraw or never file even on balances above $50,000 [joIEfxnWZzE @ 00:13:42]. The Former IRS Agent Representation host, a former revenue officer, blurs the vocabulary, saying that getting under $25,000 and making three direct payments means they will release the lien, and adds a pre-filing move: when the 1058 letter arrives, tell the IRS not to file because something is pending, and many times they withhold the filing [Y2mRqIi8jqY @ 00:05:14]. Chad Silver is looser, saying a series of on-time direct debit payments may get the lien withdrawn, but one missed payment can collapse the agreement [D-ia73OSPG0 @ 00:02:27].

Inside the 120-day IRS redemption window, the two foreclosure-auction specialists disagree on what the government actually refunds. Global Estate Hub LLC lists casualty insurance, changing the locks, paying property taxes and fixing a burst pipe as reimbursable necessary expenses on top of the bid and 6% interest [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:05:08]. Florida real estate attorney Natalia Willette at LCO Law is narrower: utilities, cleaning, cosmetic repairs and even current-year property taxes are out, while outstanding property taxes already due with lien position superior to the IRS, a roof repair, and payoffs of senior mortgages are in [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:02:16]. They also read Letter 5597 in opposite directions. Global Estate Hub frames 5597 as part of a waiver package filed with the IRS Collection Advisory Group, where a valueless redemption right gets released for free when there is not enough equity to flip [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:07:07]. Willette treats a 5597 as the notice that the IRS is coming to redeem, triggering a 15-day deadline to send back every receipt and release of lien [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:02:57]. The length of the window splits too: Global Estate Hub says 120 days or the state-law period, whichever is longer, while Willette quotes a flat 120 days under 28 US Code section 2410.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

For auction buyers, the costliest mistake is treating the gavel as the finish line. Global Estate Hub LLC says that if you launch the cosmetic flip, granite countertops, hardwood floors, new cabinets, before the 120-day window closes and the IRS redeems, every cent of those upgrades is gone [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:05:23]. The same channel flags a second wall: on day 30 a title company will mandate an exception for the redemption right, and no standard buyer's mortgage lender will fund over it, so a quick resale is dead until the clock runs or a waiver lands [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:06:21]. Check the notice trail before bidding too, because if the foreclosing bank never delivered written notice to the IRS at least 25 days before a non-judicial sale, the federal lien survives intact [q4Kqh7Vleyg @ 00:03:01]. Natalia Willette adds that the lien only gets extinguished if the foreclosure actually goes to sale; a settlement or deed in lieu leaves it standing, and once the redemption letter arrives an investor who misses the 15-day window to submit expense proof and senior-lien releases is gambling with reimbursement [GoBskUnMJ5k @ 00:01:19]. The economics check is blunt: capital parks for four months earning the statutory 6% if the IRS redeems.

On the taxpayer side, the failure pattern practitioners keep repeating is silence. Chad Silver's client Richard ignored the lien notice for lack of money; six months later the IRS levied $59,000 from the bank account and started garnishing 30% of wages [D-ia73OSPG0 @ 00:01:07]. Read The Corner shows how one day kills leverage: a Form 12153 postmarked on Day 31 misses the window, and the fallback equivalent hearing, requested within one year, carries no Tax Court review and no required pause in collection [FEsG-qPFvMc @ 00:09:40]. Dan Britt, after 35 years of practice, says one or two taxpayers every year get the bad news that they lost their best opportunity to settle or resolve the debt [eZJCPENFp3o @ 00:03:26]. Logan Allec warns that chasing hardship status as an exit backfires: currently not collectible status will not withdraw an existing notice, and if no notice exists yet there is an almost guaranteed chance the IRS files one, because nothing else would stop someone owing $100,000 from selling a house with $200,000 of equity and pocketing roughly $150,000 after costs [Fl9ZtNoyzj8 @ 00:04:14]. Chad Silver also notes fewer than one in three offers in compromise get accepted, so a sloppy offer just burns time while the lien sits.

The real estate closing table is where sequencing errors quietly cost operators their deals. Clarity Tax Relief says the trap that blindsides homeowners is a clean FICO score paired with a refinance that dies at the title search, and that a subordination request filed late or framed badly can blow the closing outright [oPKD4zP1XUQ @ 00:06:29]. IRS Notices Explained puts the hard number on it: the certificate application goes in at least 45 days before the transaction, with a current title report that is mandatory for subordination, a proposed closing statement, and exact property descriptions down to a 15-character aircraft serial number [B1e8xDSixVg @ 00:05:53]. Logan Allec adds that the payoff demand usually exceeds the amount printed on the original notice because penalties and interest keep stacking, so budget off the payoff figure, not the lien face [joIEfxnWZzE @ 00:07:16]. Even the finish line leaks. Darrin Mish says liens are supposed to release automatically after payment, an accepted offer, or statute expiration, but from personal experience that does not always happen [1Ovbn9aeSCU @ 00:00:58]. Clarity's rule is to confirm, never assume, that the Certificate of Release actually posted with the county recorder, since nobody calls to tell you the record cleared.
