*Finding County Leftover Lists, Maryland OTC Liens, Top OTC Deed States, No-Redemption Deed States, and Verifying Struck-Off Parcels Before Paying*

## Executive Summary

Over-the-counter tax liens and deeds are the leftovers: parcels that went to a county tax sale, drew no winning bid, and now sit on a list the treasurer will sell directly, usually for back taxes with no bidding. Ted Thomas, Dustin Hahn, Saen Higgins and the Tax Sale Support hosts agree the edge is asking the county what happens to properties that did not sell, because each state brands the list differently: scavenger, strike off, lands available, forfeited lands, repository. Reported buys run from $36 lots to a $5K Arkansas funeral home, with Florida OTC liens paying the full 18% and Maryland liens paying 20%. The split is over quality: Tax Sale Support calls the garbage label a misperception, while John Lane and Tax Deed Wolf say most leftovers carry major problems or are overpriced.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner defines the play the same way: a parcel goes to the county tax sale, nobody takes it, and it lands in inventory the treasurer will sell straight across the counter. Ted Thomas says unsold auction properties go onto an unsold list held at the treasurer's office, and that a Los Angeles sale might offer 2,000 properties and move only 1,500, leaving a deep pool for anyone who asks [g0bsPf3Jwo4 @ 00:02:24]. Saen Higgins frames it as a property that went to sale, did not sell, and went into inventory, adding that roughly half the states sell liens and half sell deeds, and that nothing survives a deed [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:02:30]. Steve and Shade at Tax Sale Support widen the definition to anything left after a sale that can be bought directly from the county, and they pin down the core pricing hook: buyers pay back taxes only and bid against no one, with no travel and no bidding up a $5,000 opener [5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:10:27]. The baseline prerequisite, per Dustin Hahn, is confirming whether you sit in a lien state or a deed state before calling anyone, because the leftover product changes completely depending on that answer [uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:01:43].

The second consensus point is linguistic: the list exists, but the clerk may not recognize your name for it. Thomas catalogs roughly a dozen labels by region, including the Illinois scavenger list, the Texas strike off list, the Florida lands available list and the Carolinas forfeited lands list, and tells buyers to simply ask for properties that did not sell [g0bsPf3Jwo4 @ 00:03:24]. Hahn hears the same complaint constantly, county workers swearing they have no over-the-counter tax liens, and fixes it with one reframed question: what happens to the leftover properties that don't sell at the tax lien or tax deed sale, naming the specific county and state [2M5EFWdf-is @ 00:00:46]. Higgins drops the OTC jargon entirely, using the script 'if you did have a sale and one didn't sell, where would I find that,' because there are 5,000-plus jurisdictions down to townships [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:03:08]. Tax Sale Support adds county owned, tax forfeited, land bank, struck off, upset bid and the Pennsylvania repository to the vocabulary, and the hosts say their state guides decode each term, so a buyer working a new county is translating, not guessing, when the clerk answers with a local label [5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:21:21].

Across every camp, the non-negotiable is seeing the parcel and checking title before money moves, because the county sells as-is. Ted Thomas calls it caveat emptor, with no warranty on condition or title, and insists you still walk the property and check the title for defects [g0bsPf3Jwo4 @ 00:03:50]. On the lien side, Thomas names the ghetto, the dump, and the chicken or pig farm as the kind of parcel that will neither redeem nor sell, so inspection comes first [rmXwIKKIDiw @ 00:05:39]. Dustin Hahn openly concedes that most of any leftover list is going to be crap and will not dispute it, telling buyers to accept that going in [uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:03:46]. Saen Higgins has watched buyers land a garbage dumpster, a strip four feet wide and a hundred yards long, and a median between subdivisions, and the rule is blunt: whatever the case is, you got to do your research [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:05:19]. The consensus is not that leftovers are gold or garbage, but that the list is a raw feed: price is low, competition is thin, and the screening burden shifts entirely onto the buyer, who gets no county warranty and no realtor holding their hand.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Pulling the list is a phone job, not a search-engine job. Dustin Hahn's call to the treasurer has three questions: when is the sale, what happens to properties that don't sell, and where does the inventory list live, whether that is an email, a website or a fax [uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:02:38]. The Over The Counter Tax Deeds channel boils it down to two questions, where is the list of unsold properties and how do I buy them, and as a fallback it Googles tax lien list, tax deed list or tax delinquent list near me, clicks contact us and calls the number [RxbJj9wFJXI @ 00:01:26]. Tax Deed Wolf starts with the county clerk of court and falls back to the contact page, and lists land banks, lands available, surplus, scavenger, upset bid and sheriff sale as names the inventory may hide under [sY8o7KeLptY @ 00:00:47]. Hahn's sharpest filter is friction itself: older, smaller counties and hard-to-get information beat easy ones, and hidden or paid lists, at 15 cents a page or roughly $30 total, are better across a 3,300-county field [YVYW5E5cTUI @ 00:16:14]. Friction keeps casual buyers out, which is the whole point.

Screening is a sort-and-strike exercise run against hard numbers. Tax Sale Support's Polk County, Florida demo opened a 592-page list holding about 29,500 liens, sorted by face amount from $2 to $18,000, and starred only the promising rows while scanning roughly 50 at a time; on a $93 lien on vacant residential land assessed at $4,100, a $1,173 prior-year roll-up meant Shade would probably just keep moving on [5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:55:48]. Hahn's title research sorts by property type and amount owed, confirms the price covers taxes to date, confirms chain-of-title parties were notified, and kills anything with IRS liens or buried gas tanks [uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:04:31]. Higgins checks whether the IRS was notified and the 120-day window, then checks improvements like sewer and power, because Higgins's own $1,500 lot needed $20,000 to bring power from the end of the street [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:07:11]. In Georgia, Hahn and guests trust the parcel ID over the street address, since a $2,800 minimum bid on a roughly $1M property does not make sense, and they note counties may still list improvement value on demolished houses [YVYW5E5cTUI @ 00:03:22]. Every one of these checks happens before a dollar goes to the county.

Closing is where OTC beats the auction floor: there is no clock and no bidding war, just paperwork. Higgins's version is the simplest, give the county the parcel number and the money and receive the deed, then make the first move clearing title through an attorney [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:07:59]. Thomas describes the deed-side cadence as making an offer and waiting a few days; if nobody makes a better offer, the county sells to you with no auction [g0bsPf3Jwo4 @ 00:09:35]. Alabama runs a quote-and-accept queue: Tax Deed Wolf pulls from a state list of more than 20,000 parcels dating to the 1990s, the county quotes a price such as $550, and the buyer has a 10-day window before the parcel moves to the next person in line [sY8o7KeLptY @ 00:03:15]. Maryland liens add a timing twist Hahn flags hard: the six-month redemption runs from the auction date, not your purchase date, so a June sale bought in September is already halfway home, but the lien expires after two years, and waiting until year two to foreclose is too late [Ayjol9fgpNg @ 00:02:15]. The takeaway across all four: the price is set, so the edge lives entirely in timing and paperwork.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pin the State Type and Pull the List
   - Confirm lien or deed state, then call the treasurer with the three leftover-inventory questions.
   - Ask the sale date, what happens to unsold properties, and where the inventory list lives by email, website or fax [Dustin Hahn — uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:02:38].
2. Sort and Star the Raw Feed
   - Sort the list by face amount and star only the promising rows on a fast first pass.
   - Junk and toxic parcels exist, so the hosts star promising ones while scanning about 50 rows at a time [Tax Sale Support — 5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:16:18].
3. Run Title and Desk Diligence
   - Verify taxes to date, notice to chain-of-title parties, IRS exposure and physical hazards.
   - Sort by type and amount owed, confirm chain-of-title parties were notified, and avoid IRS liens and buried gas tanks [Dustin Hahn — uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:04:31].
4. Pay, Take the Deed, Clear Title
   - Hand over the parcel number and funds, receive the deed, then clear title through an attorney.
   - Clearing title is the first step after buying, and wholesale buyers will ask for clear title plus an inspection report [Saen Higgins — nbHUraglUQo @ 00:08:18].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the OTC game is whether the leftover list is a landfill or a vault. Tax Deed Wolf is blunt: if a deed failed to sell, “chances are it's crap or it's overpriced,” and those parcels are typically not the best properties to get [sY8o7KeLptY @ 00:02:13]. John Lane of Tax Sale Lists, who reports acquiring 77,000+ properties over 20 years, attacks the guru story that bidders simply ran out of money. If that were true, OTC liens would cluster at the tail end of the sale, and Lane says, “I have never found that to be the case,” warning that most OTC liens carry major problems [ymnkPFHPY40 @ 00:01:04]. Ted Thomas pushes the opposite read: unsold parcels are “not necessarily bad properties,” because snow on auction day or bidders short on cash can strand good inventory [g0bsPf3Jwo4 @ 00:01:39]. Steve and Shade of Tax Sale Support call the garbage belief a misperception outright, pointing out that live auctions draw thin crowds, counties hold more property than investor money, and sales fall through or winning bidders walk away [5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:13:59]. The operator takeaway: both camps scan the same list, but the skeptics expect to reject nearly everything while the believers mine it for the outliers that slipped past the room.

The second fault line is how much physical diligence a cheap parcel deserves. Ted Thomas names buying unseen as mistake number one and insists, “You need boots on the ground,” paired with a defined exit plan before any money moves [rmXwIKKIDiw @ 00:13:06]. Saen Higgins splits the difference: 95% of the work happens from home, yet Higgins disagrees with pure remote buying because you make “a lot more money” when you actually show up [HjwUaTY_CAk @ 00:08:27]. Steve and Shade at Tax Sale Support run a tiered rule instead, working county records, tax records, maps, Google Maps, and sold plus for-sale comps on everything, but skipping the on-site visit entirely on deeds under $1,000 [5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:50:20]. Dustin Hahn offers the outsourced middle path: Google Earth first, a drive-by if you are close, otherwise pay wegowlook.com $20 to $50 for photos or hire a Craigslist gig driver to walk the lot [uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:06:06]. The real disagreement is the price threshold where a site visit stops paying for itself. Thomas treats it as mandatory at every price point, while the Tax Sale Support crew lets sub-$1,000 deeds ride on desk research alone, provided both value and use check out before payment.

Practitioners also split on whether OTC liens or OTC deeds are the right instrument. Ted Thomas sells liens as a passive play: no bidding, the state maximum rate, and the claim that “95 to 97 percent of all the people will come in and pay the tax” [XLUuBoGUOj8 @ 00:08:01]. Tax Sale Support agrees most liens end in interest earned rather than property, puts the chance of acquiring through a lien at only 5% climbing toward 20%, and tells operators who want dirt to buy deeds instead [5t3VbtPlElc @ 00:32:00]. Tax Deed Wolf flips the quality argument by instrument: leftover deeds are distrusted, but lien leftovers are different because auctions can run three weeks and plenty of liens slip through untouched [sY8o7KeLptY @ 00:02:30]. Dustin Hahn adds a timing wrinkle with Maryland OTC liens: the 6-month redemption clock runs from the auction, so many leftovers can be foreclosed immediately, and if they redeem the downside is 20% interest. The catch is that the liens expire after 2 years, summed up as “Quicker foreclosure turnaround time, but shorter lifespan” [Ayjol9fgpNg @ 00:03:18]. Pick the instrument by goal: yield seekers ride redemptions at the maximum rate, while acquirers buy deeds outright or target short-clock lien states like Maryland.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive OTC losses are physical, not paperwork. Saen Higgins bought a $1,500 lot and only then realized “the power's at the end of the street,” a $20,000 bill to bring electricity to a parcel that looked like a steal [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:07:11]. Higgins has also watched buyers land a garbage dumpster site, a strip 4 feet wide by 100 yards long, and a median between two subdivisions, parcels that sit on the county list precisely because nobody else wanted them [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:05:19]. Ted Thomas tells operators to inspect before paying because the list hides ghetto blocks, dumps, and chicken or pig farms, and a worthless property will neither redeem nor sell, leaving the buyer holding a lien nobody pays off and dirt nobody buys [rmXwIKKIDiw @ 00:05:39]. John Lane narrows the realistic target: “You may find decent vacant land on an otc list but it is unlikely you will find any decent improved parcels,” so a cheap house on a leftover list deserves suspicion rather than excitement, and Lane closes with a flat “be very careful” [ymnkPFHPY40 @ 00:01:28]. The pattern across all three operators: the sticker price on the leftover list is the smallest number in the deal, while utilities, access, and resale demand decide whether the money ever comes back.

Title and identification errors are the second kill zone. Dustin Hahn's research checklist sorts the list by type and amount owed, confirms the price covers taxes to date, and checks that every party in the chain of title was notified, while flagging IRS liens and buried gas tanks as walk-away items [uiH28IFKgC4 @ 00:04:31]. Saen Higgins drills into the federal angle specifically: check whether the IRS was notified and whether the 120-day window applies before treating a deed as clean [nbHUraglUQo @ 00:06:09]. Ted Thomas reminds buyers that OTC deeds are sold caveat emptor, with no warranty on condition or title, which is exactly why Thomas tells them to walk the property and check the title for defects themselves or hire someone to do it [g0bsPf3Jwo4 @ 00:04:35]. The Dustin Hahn panel adds a data trap: trust the parcel ID, never the street address, because a $2,800 minimum bid on a roughly $1M property doesn't make sense, and counties may still carry improvement value on houses that were already demolished, as the panel saw in Philadelphia [YVYW5E5cTUI @ 00:03:22]. Higgins's first move after any purchase is the same every time: clear title through an attorney, because wholesale buyers will ask for clear title and an inspection report before they pay.

The economics failures come from bad roll-ups, over-buying, and blown clocks. Tax Sale Support walks away from upside-down structures: a $100K property carrying $70K in taxes is out, and in the Polk County demo a $93 lien on vacant land that sold in 1997 for $30,000 looked tempting until the roll-up showed $1,173 in prior-year taxes, at which point Shade “would probably just keep moving on” [5t3VbtPlElc @ 01:02:25]. Ted Thomas tells the story of a student who bought 40 properties before selling a single one, and caps new investors at one or two liens and only one deed under the rule “buy and learn to sell” [XLUuBoGUOj8 @ 00:09:47]. Dustin Hahn's Maryland warning is about the calendar: the 6-month redemption runs from the June auction rather than your September purchase, and the liens expire after 2 years, so waiting until year two to foreclose is too late [Ayjol9fgpNg @ 00:02:30]. Saen Higgins confesses an admin blunder from about 14 years ago: online sales run through third parties require two separate registrations, one with the county and one with the platform, a mistake Higgins made firsthand [HjwUaTY_CAk @ 00:09:24]. Each of these failures is cheap to prevent upfront and expensive to discover after the money clears.
