# Chapter 08: Foreclosure and Quiet Title

*Tax Lien Foreclosure Steps, Judicial vs. Nonjudicial Processes, Suit to Quiet Title, Tax Foreclosure Certification, DIY Quiet Title, Timelines and Costs, and Why 'Pay the Taxes, Get the House' Is False*

## Executive Summary

Tax lien foreclosure practitioners agree the non-redeeming lien is the payday: the lienholder steps into the county's first-position strength, notices every interest holder, and wipes out mortgages and junior liens once the window closes. They equally agree the resulting county deed is a bare quitclaim whose clouded title no insurer will cover until it is cured, and that experienced counsel should drive both the foreclosure and the cure. Street numbers run from $500 to $4,000 for foreclosure counsel, a $1,400 flat-fee Michigan quiet title in roughly 120 days, $3,000 to $5,000 over three to six months per Ted Thomas, and $1,950 to $3,000 certification audits turning in three to six weeks. Practitioners split on suit versus certification, pro se filing, IRS lien survival, and installment sales that let time quiet the title.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on this foreclosure track agrees on the same engine: the tax lien sits in first position, and pushing it through foreclosure strips the encumbrances that sit behind it. Saen Higgins frames the lienholder as stepping into the county's shoes, saying that position carries the power to wipe out Bank of America, Wells Fargo, the mechanic's lien, Aunt Sally and even the IRS, and that if nobody steps up inside the prescribed window the property is theirs free and clear [EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:06:28]. Dustin Hahn calls the non-redeeming lien the home run of the business precisely because the tax lien is the priority lien and foreclosure wipes out the mortgage [tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:05:05]. Tax Sale Secrets ranks judicial tax foreclosure as the most powerful foreclosure a property can go through, telling buyers they are always better off taking a parcel that has just run through that process than one that has not [rQco9s4XodM @ 00:07:57]. The baseline economics are the same across the room: most owners redeem, so the deed is the exception, and Hahn models it as roughly one property out of every 20 certificates when 95 percent redeem [tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:04:12].

The second point of agreement is that the deed coming out of a tax foreclosure is a bare quitclaim and the title behind it is clouded. Ted Thomas says whatever the county calls it, whether a county deed, tax deed, treasurer's deed or commissioner's deed, it is always a quitclaim, the county takes no responsibility for condition or title defects, and a quitclaim alone is worthless to a commercial title company [O8MWhOJE6Oc @ 00:04:12]. Darren Findlay of Quiet Title Michigan puts it in chain-of-title terms: the county hands over a quitclaim sale deed, so if there is a defect, a tenant or an undischarged interest, the buyer receives it [7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:14:38]. Findlay adds that a title issue is inherent in every tax sale parcel because the title industry has watched former owners sue counties over notices and win, so insurers treat these properties as higher risk [7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:22:28]. Casey Dimmon of Tax Sale Academy lands in the same place, explaining that high-volume counties overlook steps and some sales get challenged in court, so title insurers simply refuse to insure; the options are selling as-is at a discount, which Dimmon has done many times, or removing the cloud [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:03:45].

Third, the practitioners agree the paperwork belongs with a specialist and that the courtroom fight is usually a formality. Saen Higgins says don't do this on your own over and over, telling lienholders to let the county run the foreclosure where it is required to and otherwise hire a real estate attorney in that particular jurisdiction who understands the local rules [EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:05:39]. Ted Thomas says a buyer can legally run a quiet title solo but refuses to, insisting on an attorney who has already done quiet titles, because an inexperienced one gets paid to learn how on your file [TRZbdE4y2sc @ 00:07:14]. On the other side of the docket, Darren Findlay reports that the vast majority of named defendants never respond or simply consent to the relief requested, which keeps most quiet title actions procedural in nature [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:06:08]. Casey Dimmon confirms that challengers rarely show up because litigating is expensive and time-consuming for a defendant, so the overwhelming majority of suits end in a default judgment after the statutory response window closes, and the same title company that refused coverage is then more than happy to insure [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:06:15].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Executing the lien foreclosure starts with one question: who drives it. Tax Sale Secrets splits states into self-executing systems, where the lienholder fills out a form or gives the county the go-ahead and the county sends every notice, and non-self-executing systems, where the burden sits on the lienholder and the move is to hire a real estate attorney and bake that cost into the deal [rQco9s4XodM @ 00:16:31]. They flag Florida as self-executing with a twist: the county runs the parcel through a tax deed sale, pays lienholders from the proceeds, and only hands over the property if nobody bids [rQco9s4XodM @ 00:18:02]. Saen Higgins describes the notice blast going to everyone with a financial interest, a response window of roughly 60 days in some counties and 100 in others, and attorney fees of about $500 on simple files up to $3,000 to $4,000 on complicated ones, which the owner must also pay if they redeem mid-process [EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:07:50]. Dustin Hahn budgets one to two thousand dollars for the foreclosure attorney, roughly another thousand for the follow-on quiet title, plus a year or two of subsequent taxes [tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:05:34].

The quiet title suit itself follows a fixed sequence that Darren Findlay of Quiet Title Michigan lays out step by step: run an independent title search even if a title company already did one, stack the deeds into a chain, then cast a wide net of defendants because missing one fish means the judgment fails to clear title and the process starts over [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:03:19]. The complaint is filed in the circuit court where the property sits, fees are paid, and service of process begins, using personal service where possible, certified mail, or a court order for alternate means such as publishing in the legal news and mailing last known addresses for missing owners, defunct entities and probate estates [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:04:56]. Findlay calls service the most time-consuming step, then files the default, re-serves it, obtains the default judgment, waits out the appeal period and records the perfected judgment at the Register of Deeds [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:08:02]. Casey Dimmon times the same track: a good attorney files within a week or two, defendants get 30 or 45 days to respond, and backed-up courts can add eight months to a year before a judge even sees the file [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:13:14].

Pricing spreads wide. Findlay quotes about 120 days and a flat $1,400 plus costs on tax reversion or very procedural files, with more complicated matters priced differently [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:09:55]. Ted Thomas plans on three to six months and $3,000 to $5,000 for a normal quiet title [MzL0xEaYnUI @ 00:01:31]. Casey Dimmon cites $2,000 to $5,000 as common suit figures, then lays out the faster lane: a tax foreclosure certification, where a third-party firm audits every document in the foreclosure file against state statute and, if it checks out, tells its partner title insurer to issue a policy. Dimmon names Tax Title Services out of California at four to six weeks with a paid rush option under a month, Clear to Sell in Florida at around three weeks, fees of $1,950 to $3,000, and an uncertifiable rate the companies put under 5 percent [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:15:10]. In an earlier Tax Sale Academy episode, the quoted ranges were $600 to $5,000 and a couple of months to a full year for a suit, against 45 to 60 days and $1,500 to $3,000 for certification [2g2qJcuuey4 @ 00:02:22].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Confirm Who Drives the Foreclosure
   - Determine whether the state runs self-executing or non-self-executing foreclosure before buying any lien.
   - In self-executing states the lienholder fills out a form and the county sends all notices; in non-self-executing states the lienholder carries the process and should plan on hiring a real estate attorney and pricing that into the investment [Tax Sale Secrets — rQco9s4XodM @ 00:17:06].
2. Trigger the Foreclosure and Notice Blast
   - After redemption expires, call the county as lienholder of record and elect to foreclose.
   - Notices go to every party with a financial interest, from the owner to Bank of America to the IRS; if nobody pays inside the county's 60-day or 100-day style window, the property transfers free and clear of liens and encumbrances [Saen Higgins — EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:06:10].
3. Build the Chain and the Defendant List
   - Run an independent title search and name every undischarged interest in a circuit court complaint.
   - Findlay's firm re-runs its own title search even when a title company supplies one, builds the chain person A to B to C, flags gaps and defects, and names every claimant before filing where the property sits [Quiet Title Michigan — kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:02:02].
4. Serve, Default, Record, Insure
   - Serve all defendants, enter defaults, obtain the judgment, let the appeal period run, and record it.
   - The perfected judgment is recorded at the Register of Deeds like any deed or mortgage [Quiet Title Michigan — kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:08:02], then taken to the same title company, which will issue title insurance against it [Tax Sale Academy — 7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:06:33].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Compressing Time and Cost from Lien to Insurable Title**
> 
> Four moves practitioners use to speed up foreclosure and shave the title-cure bill.
> - Buy foreclosable liens straight out of county inventory: certificates already past redemption let you start foreclosure immediately, which Higgins likens to taking 20 shots on goal now instead of waiting two years [Saen Higgins — EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:08:53].
> - Buy the first-year lien, not the second or third, so you hold the priority to foreclose; Hahn warns that a third-year lien buyer cannot foreclose [Dustin Hahn — tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:05:49].
> - Batch your files: Dimmon's cheapest quiet titles came from handing one attorney ten properties at once, which gave leverage to back the price down [Tax Sale Academy — vMMozg0xaSA @ 00:05:22].
> - Cast the defendant net wide, because one missed claimant leaves the title unresolved and forces the whole suit to start over to remove that one interest [Quiet Title Michigan — kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:03:19].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over who files the paper. Saen Higgins is absolutist. They call a do-it-yourself tax lien foreclosure 'just ridiculous' and compare it to swapping the batteries on a Tesla. Their play is to let the county run it where it is required to, or to retain a local real estate attorney for roughly $500 on simple files and up to $3,000 to $4,000 on complicated ones. A redeeming owner who comes in during the window pays those attorney fees [EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:07:38]. Dustin Hahn concedes the lienholder can foreclose solo, but still budgets one to two grand for an attorney plus roughly another thousand for the quiet title afterward [tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:05:19]. Casey Dimmon of Tax Sale Academy draws the legal line operators miss. Pro se filing only works when the deed sits in the investor's personal name. An LLC or corporation cannot be represented without a license to practice law, and that filing will likely be rejected or worse [vMMozg0xaSA @ 00:02:46]. Ted Thomas lands in the middle: yes, a quiet title can be done yourself, but their own answer is no. The proceeding turns adversarial when fences, easements, or encroachments surface. The move is an attorney who has already done quiet titles, not one who learns on your dime [TRZbdE4y2sc @ 00:04:24].

On how to cure the cloud, the playbooks fork. Casey Dimmon calls the suit to quiet title 'really the complete solution,' because the judgment alone gets the policy issued. The tax foreclosure certification is faster and usually cheaper, but it carries strings. The certifier's partner title insurer may be mandatory at closing. The tax-deed history may have to be disclosed. The certification can also expire and need paid re-certification if the asset sits for three, four, or five years [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:09:00]. In an earlier breakdown, the same operator is openly skeptical of the vendors' 'faster and cheaper' pitch. Quiet titles run $600 to $5,000 over a couple of months to a year, against certifications advertised at 45 to 60 days and $1,500 to $3,000. The instruction is to collect attorney quotes, compare them, and take the cheapest route [2g2qJcuuey4 @ 00:02:05]. Darren Findlay of Quiet Title Michigan rejects workarounds outright. Short of getting prior owners to deed off, which is very uncommon, the lawsuit is the only actual way to correct the title [7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:23:13]. Any strategy that merely lets a title agency close 'just kicks the can down the road,' because a title issue runs with the land and resurfaces with the next purchaser [7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:26:42].

The exit side splits too. Ted Thomas teaches advanced students to skip the lawsuit on certain deals. They sell on a contract of sale or installment note and keep legal title while the buyer makes payments for two or three years. Time then clears the title, and the title company says there have been no claims for three years and writes the policy. Brand-new investors are told to plan for the experienced attorney instead [MzL0xEaYnUI @ 00:07:50]. Casey Dimmon's cheaper alternative is to sell as-is at a discount without title insurance. They have done that 'many many times' over the years [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:04:03]. On the lien side, the foreclosure regime itself rewrites the playbook. Tax Sale Secrets separates self-executing states from non-self-executing states. In a self-executing state, the lienholder fills out a form and the county sends every notice. In a non-self-executing state, the lienholder carries the process and should plan on hiring a real estate attorney. Florida adds a layer: it runs the lien through a tax deed sale and pays lienholders from the proceeds, and hands over the property only if nobody bids [rQco9s4XodM @ 00:16:12]. Saen Higgins shortcuts the clock instead. They buy already-foreclosable liens out of county inventory, so 20 purchases become 20 immediate 'shots on goal' instead of a two-year redemption wait [EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:08:53].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Time is the bleed nobody prices in. Ted Thomas bought a tax-defaulted property online with a credit card for $140,000 and planned a $350,000 to $400,000 exit. Real estate dropped substantially the following winter, and they ultimately sold at $280,000. That sale came only after a retiring judge spent 13 months of diligent work getting the quiet title issued. Buyers could not get a mortgage until the title cleared. So Thomas ate 13 months of taxes, heat, cleaning, landscaping, snow removal, and security bills on an ownership period they did not want [O8MWhOJE6Oc @ 00:07:42]. Casey Dimmon flags where those months hide: an attorney who lets the file sit on the desk for a month or longer, 30 to 45 day defendant response windows, and court systems backed up eight months or even a year before a judge sees the case. The investor carries maintenance the whole time [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:13:14]. Ted Thomas's own baseline for a normal quiet title is three to six months and $3,000 to $5,000 [MzL0xEaYnUI @ 00:01:31]. Darren Findlay quotes about 120 days, and a $1,400 flat fee plus costs, only on procedural tax-reversion files. They warn that the judge's schedule and the defendant count set the real clock [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:09:18].

The second trap is a judgment that does not hold. Casey Dimmon warns that a pro se filer who blows a single stipulation can have the case thrown out by the judge. That burns hundreds of dollars in filing fees and hours of preparation. Worse, the filer can win a signed order whose language still fails the title insurance company's requirements, which forces the whole process to start over [vMMozg0xaSA @ 00:04:02]. Darren Findlay calls the defendant list a net. Miss one fish, meaning one undischarged interest holder, and the judgment of quiet title does not resolve the title. The process then starts all over again just to get rid of that one party [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:03:19]. Findlay says the office regularly gets called to fix judgments that other law firms obtained or started. The title company reviews every step behind the order. A judgment built on work that was not done correctly is suspect and can be overturned by a subsequent court [kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:08:20]. Even the certification route has a failure branch. Vendors report hitting uncertifiable tax sale files less than 5 percent of the time. When the defect cannot be solved, the investor needs a suit to quiet title after all, and the time spent on the certification attempt is gone [7BP12s9Jn2c @ 00:11:48].

The third cluster detonates before the gavel. Darren Findlay has watched clients buy what they thought was a structure plus the land at tax sale, only for the city or county to demolish it shortly afterward. Any dilapidated or unsafe-looking parcel gets checked against the demolition list before money goes down [7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:04:36]. Findlay also flags IRS liens. Those are governed by federal law, while the tax forfeiture runs under state law, and there is some question whether the county has the power to wipe them out. That pushes those files into a quiet title action [7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:25:11]. This directly undercuts Saen Higgins's street pitch that the foreclosing lienholder inherits the county's strength and extinguishes everything, 'including, yes, even the IRS' [EXkUbWWenl0 @ 00:06:28]. Dustin Hahn's trap is buying the wrong year and running the wrong math. Only a first-year lien carries the priority to foreclose, and a third-year purchase cannot be foreclosed. The viral 'pay the back taxes, own the house' hustle ignores two numbers. With roughly 95 percent redemption, an operator needs about 20 certificates to land one property. That operator still pays subsequent taxes, an attorney, and the quiet title [tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:05:34].

> **OPERATOR GATE — Clear These Four Gates Before Funding Any Foreclosure or Tax Deed Bid**
> 
> Each gate below traces to a delay, loss, or restart that a practitioner hit personally or watched clients hit. A file that fails any one of them gets repriced or passed.
> - Carry-cost gate: underwrite three to six months and $3,000 to $5,000 for a normal quiet title. Then stress-test a 13-month hold that includes taxes, heat, snow removal, and security, because buyers cannot get a mortgage until the title clears [Ted Thomas — O8MWhOJE6Oc @ 00:08:01].
> - Entity gate: decide on individual, LLC, or joint ownership before bidding. Re-deeding afterward can trigger property tax uncapping or gift conveyance issues [Quiet Title Michigan — 7yD_brKdgXk @ 00:13:04]. An LLC-owned parcel also cannot be quieted pro se [Tax Sale Academy — vMMozg0xaSA @ 00:02:46].
> - Defendant-net gate: build the full chain of title, name every undischarged interest, and serve each one properly, because one missed party forces a full restart [Quiet Title Michigan — kJRxvYeK_eU @ 00:03:19].
> - Lien-priority gate: confirm the certificate is the first-year lien that carries the right to foreclose. Model roughly 20 certificates per property acquired at about 95 percent redemption [Dustin Hahn — tXVBp8vbkT4 @ 00:05:34].
