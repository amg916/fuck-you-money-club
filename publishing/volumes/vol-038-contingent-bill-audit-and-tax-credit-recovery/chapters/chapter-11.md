## Executive Summary

Practitioners across healthcare, law, real estate, manufacturing audits and corporate expense recovery converge on one model: get paid from measured results against a defensible baseline. Expense Consulting audits telecom, waste and utilities at no cost to start and shares the savings; McDivitt fronts case costs with no monthly bills. In MSSP, ACOs bill fee-for-service and earn bonuses only after clearing a roughly 3% minimum savings corridor, with quality setting bonus size. Fredrikson caps physician gainsharing near 50% and uses 85/90/95% tiers rather than all-or-nothing triggers. Markovitz shows dispersed convener ACOs earning about $171 per beneficiary versus $95 for local ones, with strategic clinician selection the leading suspect. The friction: Aaron Hall warns contingency lawyers look out for themselves first, and IATF auditors hand out major nonconformances for undocumented, untested contingency plans.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner selling on contingency runs the same core bargain: the client fronts nothing, and the operator eats only from the result. Expense Consulting audits telecom, waste, supplies and utilities off-site, starts at no cost, and splits the savings it finds, then keeps auditing invoices on an ongoing basis [o2wyRpIYL0w @ 00:01:34]. McDivitt Law Firm frames the identical deal for litigation: no upfront money, no monthly bills, the lawyer fronts expert and medical-record costs, and calls and emails are never billed, because 'the lawyer's incentive is to just do work' [61zkacizavY @ 00:01:25]. Medicare's Shared Savings Program bolts the same logic onto physician practices. Advisory Board explains that providers keep billing fee-for-service exactly as normal, and a bonus is earned on top only when the ACO beats a spending target [hGtjN7H07Bo @ 00:00:48]. That target is built from historical cost, so a $10,000 historical cost could translate into a target of less than $9,800, and the ACO is on the hook for all of a patient's care, even from providers outside the ACO. Land above target and there is no bonus; miss by too much and Medicare can demand money back [hGtjN7H07Bo @ 00:01:41]. The upside is a check with few strings attached, but, as Advisory Board warns, the devil is in the details.

The second point of agreement is that the benchmark is the entire game, and whoever controls the baseline controls the payout. Reyann Davis of Value Health Innovations describes the MSSP benchmark as three years of history, trended forward and risk-adjusted, with participation running by TIN rather than by individual provider and a 5,000-beneficiary floor on traditional Medicare lives [EotsrYaUjUs @ 00:07:04]. Dr. Adam Markovitz goes further: these are voluntary programs built on benchmarks, 'and any benchmark can be gamed,' and every new anti-gaming rule adds complexity that only increases demand for conveners [Zzb5OKFwcbg @ 00:22:28]. On the gainsharing side, Fredrikson's lawyers distill the OIG advisory opinions into the same discipline: cap the payments, tie them to prior utilization, and disclose the arrangement, since a key element of a kickback is that it is secret [VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:12:12]. Quality is the second lock on the vault. Advisory Board states plainly that quality determines how big the bonus is, and an ACO that sacrifices quality to cut costs will not be rewarded for it [hGtjN7H07Bo @ 00:02:17]. The operator lesson across all four speakers is identical: savings only count when measured against a defensible historical baseline, with quality scores holding, and with the arrangement documented in the open.

Contingency in the planning sense draws the same consensus: the escape hatch has to be written, owned, and tested before the crisis hits. Jeb Smith calls a purchase contingency an if/then escape clause, and under the California purchase agreement it buys 17 days of due diligence while protecting earnest money in escrow, typically 3% of price, or $15,000 on a $500,000 home [HDpLh9uRXMo @ 00:04:05]. The moment the buyer signs a full release, that deposit is at risk and in theory becomes the seller's money. The IATF 16949 auditors apply identical logic to plants. simpleQuE ranks contingency planning a top-10 certification-body nonconformance and stresses the plan 'is not intended to be a method of prevention' [Sf10tIpwF74 @ 00:00:25]. Requirements include annual multidisciplinary review, periodic simulation testing with records, staff training, and customer approval of alternate sites. The IATF 16949 Auditing channel shows what happens when that discipline lapses: an audited site with only emails instead of a documented action plan, last reviewed 15 months earlier, took a major nonconformance because the customer had already been disrupted [4-TMYIb48FA @ 00:04:38]. Ownership belongs with top management, not a single individual [4-TMYIb48FA @ 00:05:39]. Every one of these speakers agrees that an unwritten, untested, single-owner contingency is effectively no contingency.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The MSSP payout math is where the money is made or lost. Davis lays out a minimum savings corridor of about 3%: an ACO that saves 2.5% gets 20% while CMS keeps 80%, and at 6% savings Levels A and B share 40% [EotsrYaUjUs @ 00:07:45]. Level A carries no risk, Level C is the first with risk, and ACOs can move up levels but never down. The corridor bites hard. COPE Health Solutions reports roughly 160 MSSP participants in 2018 generated savings yet got no check because of the minimum savings rate, which is a core pitch for Direct Contracting, where there is no MSR [PDcTn9Sq3mg @ 00:21:31]. Dr. Dan Mingle tracks the scale rules: a 5,000-beneficiary minimum still applies in benchmark year 3, and ACOs below 5,000 in benchmark years 1 or 2 are pushed into the Basic track with caps and exclusions [rPy0GAiwUUc @ 00:05:25]. Risk-score capture is the quiet multiplier. Davis notes 1.0 is average and 0.90 means 10% less expected spending, so an under-coded panel carries a lower expected-spending figure, and documentation must support the coding [EotsrYaUjUs @ 00:15:33]. Cash timing matters too: 2025 results land in August 2026, with payment in September to October 2026, so operators wait well over a year from the start of a performance year before any savings check clears.

Fredrikson's gainsharing playbook puts the sharpest numbers on the table. No physician share above 50% appears in the advisory opinions, though higher is not categorically barred; on implant savings, $10M saved with $5M to physicians is arguably fine, while at 75% the speakers flatly would not do it [VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:28:18]. Metrics must sit inside the physicians' control: a $100,000 bonus triggered at 90% on-time starts fails at about 87% because anesthesia causes 8% of delays and nursing 4%, so the fix is tiers at 85, 90 and 95%, 'a rheostat rather than a light switch' [VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:22:59]. Under CJR the physician cap is 50% of Medicare professional fees, so $100K saved against $30K in fees pays $15K, and equal splits are not allowed [VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:43:17]. Absolute targets such as ten minutes faster are fine; paying for doubled OB deliveries is suspect; each group gets capped at historical volume. John Ford shows where the savings actually come from in an ACO: work the ED frequent-flyer list, where one patient dropped from 73 visits a year to the low teens for about $20,000 saved [UEDbkKJ-9Xk @ 00:13:54]. Fredrikson's documentation rule is blunt: keep it off email, and keep device companies out entirely.

The aggressive edge comes from patient and network selection. Markovitz ran a 'reverse ACO Builder' test, inspired by Milliman's nationwide map of high- and low-cost physicians, and found dispersed convener ACOs spending about $400 below the regional benchmark versus about $100 for dispersed non-convener ACOs [Zzb5OKFwcbg @ 00:16:26]. Those dispersed convener ACOs earned roughly $171 per beneficiary per year in bonuses against about $95 for local ones, with no better quality scores, and 'these consultants are not coy about what they're doing.' Rick Forster of Privia builds a tiered 'virtual narrow network' from third-party and payer data plus clinical input, delivers it at the point of care as 'more informed referral,' monitors it, and never publishes it externally [UEDbkKJ-9Xk @ 00:17:15]. Rachel Corbett pulled CCLF claims files to see where patients already go, then benchmarked rates against health plans at about 90% of Medicare [UEDbkKJ-9Xk @ 00:59:34]. In the corporate bill-audit lane, Expense Consulting runs the simplest version of the model: an off-site audit of telecom, waste, supplies and utilities for billing errors and overcharges, vendor-neutral benchmarking and negotiation, no cost to start, and a share of whatever savings are recovered, with most clients seeing savings within weeks [o2wyRpIYL0w @ 00:00:35].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull the baseline
   - Rebuild the historical spend and current patient flow that the savings target is set on.
   - Use CCLF claims files to see where patients already go and benchmark rates against health plans at about 90% of Medicare, mirroring the trended, risk-adjusted historical benchmark [Garrett Schmitt — UEDbkKJ-9Xk @ 00:59:34]
2. Set the split and the metrics
   - Fix the physician share, volume caps, and controllable performance targets.
   - Cap physician share at 50%, tie payments to prior utilization, cap each group at historical volume such as 50 versus 25 cases, and keep every metric inside the physicians' control [Fredrikson — VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:34:48]
3. Paper it before care starts
   - Sign the sharing agreement and lock the savings methodology up front.
   - Under BPCI Advanced, sign sharing agreements before care is delivered, run them at least 1 year, and keep internal cost savings measurable, actual and verifiable under a uniform methodology [Fredrikson — VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:57:58]
4. Test the contingency plan
   - Run review, simulation, and training cycles with records on file.
   - Hold an annual multidisciplinary review, run real-world simulations with records, train staff, and get customer approval of alternate sites; desktop-only tests and one-person plans get flagged [simpleQuE - ISO Certification Consulting — Sf10tIpwF74 @ 00:01:08]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Contingency-fee alignment is the sharpest split. McDivitt Law Firm sells the structure as interest-aligned: hourly billing pays regardless of outcome, the client pays nothing upfront, gets no monthly bills, and the lawyer fronts case costs such as experts and medical records, so 'the lawyer's incentive is to just do work' [61zkacizavY @ 00:01:25]. Calls and emails are not billed either. Aaron Hall FAQs attacks that premise directly, warning that the attorney 'is looking out for herself or himself first' [VeB6ri8vEm8 @ 00:00:25] and that alignment does not automatically follow even at a 40% fee [VeB6ri8vEm8 @ 00:01:43]. Hall's car-accident math: a $10,000 offer landing after 3 hours of work pays the lawyer one-third, which creates a built-in incentive to settle early. Hall adds that some attorneys withdraw when no settlement materializes, that contingency arrangements are rare in bankruptcy, family, and criminal matters, uncommon in business disputes because of counterclaims, and that the client may end up paying more than hourly. Expense Consulting runs the same no-cost-to-start logic on corporate bills, auditing telecom, waste, supplies, and utilities off-site and sharing the savings found [o2wyRpIYL0w @ 00:01:34]. The operator read: contingency pricing removes upfront cash risk, but the percentage decides how hard and how long the provider keeps working.

Whether downside risk is required to win in shared savings splits the field. On Garrett Schmitt's ACO-success panel, Rachel Corbett said two-sided risk is not mandatory, Rick Forster of Privia wanted some 'skin in the game' but not a 100% backstop, and John Ford simply said no; moderator Dr. Katherine Schneider called the link between risk and success 'a little bit of a reverse causation' [UEDbkKJ-9Xk @ 00:52:15]. Nolan Feeny takes the opposite framing, calling risk sharing 'a key note feature of the program' [lKGpIaNHgRw @ 00:03:04] and noting Track 1, the no-downside option, was eliminated in 2019. Dr. Dan Mingle's numbers show the push working: ACOs in two-sided risk rose from 18% in 2018 to 71% in 2025, while the 2026 proposal lets a new ACO stay one-sided for all five years of its first period with no return afterwards [rPy0GAiwUUc @ 00:02:24]. Reyann Davis pitches the same lane, saying a new ACO 'can avoid risk for up to seven years' [EotsrYaUjUs @ 00:24:18], and warns you can move up levels but never down, with Level A carrying no risk and Level C the first rung with risk. The alternative playbook is clear: ride one-sided as long as the rules allow, build risk capture and care management, then step up deliberately.

The deepest split is over where shared-savings money actually comes from. Dr. Adam Markovitz on Health Affairs found dispersed convener ACOs earning about $171 per beneficiary per year against about $95 for local convener ACOs, with no better quality scores. Strategic selection is the suspected mechanism: Milliman's ACO Builder maps high- and low-cost physicians nationwide, and the 'reverse ACO Builder' test showed dispersed convener ACOs spending about $400 below the regional benchmark versus about $100 for dispersed non-convener ACOs [Zzb5OKFwcbg @ 00:16:26]. Markovitz's conclusion is blunt: 'any benchmark can be gamed,' every anti-gaming rule adds complexity that increases demand for conveners, and 'this is everyone just acting rationally' [Zzb5OKFwcbg @ 00:22:28]. The operating playbook from Garrett Schmitt's panel runs the other way: Forster's Privia team builds a tiered 'virtual narrow network' from payer and third-party data plus clinical input, delivers it at point of care as 'more informed referral,' and refuses to trust payer data blindly [UEDbkKJ-9Xk @ 00:18:12], while Ford works the ED frequent-flyer list, taking one patient from 73 visits a year to the low teens for about $20,000 saved. Reyann Davis adds a structural claim: independent ACOs historically perform better in the program [EotsrYaUjUs @ 00:22:26]. Operators choosing a lane are choosing between selection math and care-delivery math.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Contingency plans fail audit in predictable ways. In the IATF 16949 Auditing remote audit, a plant budgeted $1.2M profit and posted a $345,000 loss after automotive business fell almost 40%, two months ran at zero production, and delivery slid to 82%, 87%, and 92% against a 100% target, driven by a paint-line bottleneck and a COVID quarantine on second shift [4-TMYIb48FA @ 00:01:48]. The auditor found no documented action plan, only emails, and a plan last reviewed 15 months earlier; because the customer was disrupted, it was written up as a major NC [4-TMYIb48FA @ 00:04:38]. simpleQuE - ISO Certification Consulting calls contingency planning a top 10 certification-body nonconformance and logs two more major NCs: a prevention-only plan, and an 'ugly' one-page table that just said 'get back to running as soon as possible' [Sf10tIpwF74 @ 00:03:32]. Its hot buttons include plans that are really risk-management plans ('fire drills are not contingency planning'), process owners never involved, real disasters with no lessons applied, and weak or unevidenced simulations. IATF 16949 Auditing adds a moving-target problem: Sanctioned Interpretation 3 has changed the requirement several times since 2016, adding cyber simulation and training, and auditors must know every relevant interpretation [tVupXEamHUk @ 00:07:40].

The shared-savings check is slower, smaller, and more conditional than the pitch. Advisory Board warns that an ACO above target gets no bonus, missing it by too much means Medicare could demand money back, and sacrificing quality to cut costs is not rewarded because quality sets the bonus size; in short, 'the devil's in the details' [hGtjN7H07Bo @ 00:02:33]. COPE Health Solutions counted about 160 MSSP participants in 2018 that generated savings yet got no check because of the minimum savings rate [PDcTn9Sq3mg @ 00:21:31]. Reyann Davis puts the corridor at about 3% and the timing on the calendar: 2025 results arrive August 2026 with payment in September or October 2026, so an ACO funds a full year of work before cash lands; the $250,000 Advance Investment Payment plus 8 quarterly payments is deducted from any shared savings [EotsrYaUjUs @ 00:20:11]. Dr. Dan Mingle flags that AIP payback is due within 90 days and that the carry-over of CEHRT exceptions 'may be an empty promise' now that all participants must use CEHRT and make a promoting interoperability submission from 1/1/2025 [BVWc_qDwmOo @ 00:09:23]. Mingle also notes CMS now strips significant, anomalous and highly suspect billing codes out of the financial calculations. Run the funds-flow policy question Davis recommends before signing with any ACO.

Shared-savings and gainsharing deals break on design details, not intent. Fredrikson's worked example puts a $100,000 bonus on 90% on-time starts and watches it fail at about 87% because anesthesia (8%) and nursing (4%) delays sit outside the physicians' control, so the fix is tiers at 85/90/95%, 'rheostat rather than light switch' [VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:22:59]. The same panel treats the Methodist/Medtronic emails as proof that wording alone can taint a legal deal: 'just don't use email much,' and keep device companies out [VMuMGmjYplc @ 00:26:08]. Stark is 'the ugliest' of the four laws because it is strict liability, and on BPCI Advanced the participation agreement runs past 100 pages, so vague savings math gets burned on legal fees. Jeb Smith reports the parallel trap in purchase contingencies: the deposit, 3% or $15,000 on a $500,000 home, is safe until the buyer signs a full release, at which point it 'in theory becomes the seller's money' [HDpLh9uRXMo @ 00:21:54]. Waiving everything to beat four or five competing offers puts that deposit at risk even if something comes up later. Aaron Hall FAQs adds the provider-side exit: some contingency attorneys withdraw when no settlement comes, which is generally permissible [VeB6ri8vEm8 @ 00:03:09].
