# Chapter 01: Small-Parcel Auditing and Guaranteed-Service Refund Recovery

*Late-package GSR claims, automatic vs. requested refunds, denied-refund handling, audit workflows, qualifying a good auditor, common misconceptions, free audit tools like Audisee, and percentage-of-savings onboarding*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

FedEx and UPS guarantee time-definite delivery, and practitioners from Refund Retriever, Empact Parcel, and Compete Consulting Group agree a package delivered even a minute late earns a full refund the carriers will never flag. The shared playbook: take online billing credentials, auto-download every invoice, audit 40 to 44 points per shipment, file inside the 15-day window, and bill only on contingency, from Cerasis's 30 percent model to Refund Retriever's 50 percent starting split. Scarbrough Global and Refund Retriever both say the money sits in follow-up, with resubmission loops and live phone agents beating automatic denials, including FedEx rejecting GSR claims on already-paid invoices. Practitioners split on refund honesty, speed benchmarks, and minimums: Share a Refund bills only refunds it acted on and reads check refunds as a slow audit, while Cerasis argues tiny shippers cannot justify manual auditing.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator in this lane builds on the same premise: FedEx and UPS sell a time-definite promise, and when they break it the shipment is free. Brian Gibbs of Refund Retriever states that both carriers guarantee all ground and express packages to arrive by a certain date or time or the charge comes back [vOX4irOA_Tk @ 00:02:44], and his team defines a guaranteed service refund as available when a package lands even 60 seconds late [JeLyjRF9HEk @ 00:00:00]. The second shared belief is that the carrier will never flag the money for you. Empact Parcel runs the same one-minute trigger, frames the pitch bluntly as late packages being free, and warns that the carriers will not tell you which shipments are due a refund [frD9iyAvyds @ 00:00:19], while Compete Consulting Group compares carrier-provided monitoring tools to asking the fox to guard the henhouse and claims two billion dollars went unclaimed in a single year [RCPFFZVF7qs @ 00:00:35]. That combination, a hair-trigger refund right plus a counterparty with zero incentive to surface it, is the whole business. Nobody disputes that the refund exists; the fight is entirely about who detects the late scan, files first, and keeps pressing until the credit actually posts to the account.

The practitioners also agree that a human with a highlighter cannot win this game at volume. Watchdog Advisory describes monthly bills running to hundreds of pages of shipping invoices that are difficult if not impossible to review for errors [5eTaFaKQdHY @ 00:00:00], and Compete Consulting Group says carrier invoices can run hundreds of pages each week, enough to bury an entire team of accountants working shipment by shipment [RCPFFZVF7qs @ 00:01:06]. RAF Logistics points to more than 130 different add-on fees the carriers can now charge [eEERM3QJqyI @ 00:00:17], and Refund Retriever adds that surcharges alone can account for up to 35 percent of a shipper's parcel spend, with dimensional weight, residential delivery, peak season, and overweight fees piling on as prices rise every year [vOX4irOA_Tk @ 00:01:33]. The consensus response is full automation keyed off the carrier's own billing portal. Every vendor here sells the same onboarding promise: hand over online billing access, do nothing else, and watch credits land. Nobody pitches a model where the shipper keeps working the invoices; RAF says there is nothing you need to send weekly, Compete promises no work for you once you sign up, and Watchdog calls its setup three minutes with no work on the client's part.

On money, the consensus is pure contingency. Watchdog Advisory charges no setup costs or fees and takes a portion of savings, so no savings means no fee [5eTaFaKQdHY @ 00:01:47]; Refund Retriever bills a percentage of actual recovery with no cancellation, setup, or monthly fee [vOX4irOA_Tk @ 00:04:55]; Empact Parcel stays profitable on a split of refund amounts. That alignment matters because the second consensus point is that filing is the easy part. Scarbrough Global says customers could find the late packages and surcharges and even make the requests themselves, but the real fortune is in the follow-up, because carriers often deny requests for no reason, which their data shows, likely to discourage human filers [NKDaSW7CKiQ @ 00:01:27]. Scarbrough ties that persistence to its billing: the product is paid only on success, so squeezing out every refund is how it makes money. The third shared prerequisite is speed. Share a Refund describes a limited window right after an invoice posts in which claims can be filed, after which each refund is lost forever [PheSsrwvW9c @ 00:00:23], and Refund Retriever and Compete both pin the late-package window at 15 days. Credentials, automation, contingency, relentless follow-up, and a fast clock are the baseline every one of these operators treats as non-negotiable.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The workflow Nick of Share a Refund lays out runs in a fixed sequence: invoice retrieval, shipment audit, claim submission, claim verification, reporting, and billing [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:00:37]. Retrieval goes one of two ways. Large multinationals and Fortune 500 shippers with existing EDI integrations open FTP data pools, while the more common setup is handing the auditor a username and password for the carrier accounts so invoices are pulled directly from the carrier website [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:00:55]. Refund Retriever's signup is exactly that second path: contact details in step one, then FedEx and UPS online billing credentials plus billing address in step two, with a warning to confirm the login actually has invoice access; the company compares its always-on credential monitoring to mint.com [P3sA9eO0D-k @ 00:00:52]. Once invoices are in hand, the audit has to test every cost component on every shipment, both whether the charge should apply at all and whether its amount reflects the negotiated discount. Share a Refund checks 40 points per shipment, using the residential surcharge as its showcase: verify the ship-to really is residential, then verify the discount on that surcharge was applied [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:02:08]. Compete Consulting Group runs a 44-point audit on each shipment, and Watchdog sweeps late deliveries, invalid surcharges, and dimensional and weight charges.

Share a Refund insists each claim be filed fast, under the correct claim type, and for the exact amount of the mistake: if the residential discount was skipped, request the net difference; if a residential surcharge hit a commercial delivery, request the entire component [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:03:16]. Its claims verification service then captures every response type, reconciles each credit to its dispute, and runs a decision agent on rejections, choosing between filing an invalid address correction dispute, resubmitting the service failure, or dropping the shipment [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:04:36]. Scarbrough Global runs hundreds of checks before submitting only charges it is very confident about, then resubmits, resubmits, resubmits, and finally has an internal team call a live human being [NKDaSW7CKiQ @ 00:02:25]. Refund Retriever added a denial filter feeding in-house phone dispute agents, then proved it when a client emailed a list of denied FedEx disputes. In the FedEx invoice history, the team found the denial reason was an existing payment on file, because the client paid the invoice the moment it posted, which made FedEx auto-deny every GSR request. Phone agents called FedEx customer service inside the 15-day window and won refunds on all five late packages, recovering $98.42 on a $4,253.53 invoice, a 2.3 percent savings that would otherwise have been zero [JeLyjRF9HEk @ 00:01:42].

The unit economics are simple percentages. Refund Retriever starts at 50 percent of whatever it saves, so $50 of late-delivery refunds in a month produces a $25 fee, invoiced with the tracking number and the invoice where the credit sits, and Brian Gibbs says the rate drops and is negotiable for heavy shippers, citing a $25 million a year UPS account as one he would work with [vOX4irOA_Tk @ 00:05:11]. Cerasis models a 30 percent auditor fee: $1,000 in total savings nets the shipper $700, scaling to as much as $70,000 across an enterprise [GnXmnbxCZ-A @ 00:05:14]. On the recovery side, Gibbs found roughly 10 percent of the packages from his own eBay reselling business arriving late before he automated the process [1JZzLbF5PrI @ 00:00:50], Compete puts daily late deliveries at four to six percent, Empact pitches an immediate 1 to 5 percent cut in parcel spend, and Cerasis says proper auditing can recoup up to 5 percent. Watchdog claims its clients average roughly 30 percent savings when audit recovery is combined with parcel intelligence and carrier contract engineering, on technology used by more than 3,300 companies [5eTaFaKQdHY @ 00:01:12]. For a free first look, Lojistic's Audisee web tool takes uploaded UPS or FedEx invoices and instantly returns findings, per-tracking-number details, charts, and stats, though it will not audit both carriers in one batch.

1. Connect carrier billing access
   - Hand the auditor FedEx and UPS online billing credentials, or open an EDI/FTP data pool for enterprise accounts.

2. Audit every cost component
   - Run each shipment through a multi-point check covering late delivery, surcharge validity, and discount application.
   - Validate both the application of each cost component and its amount against negotiated discounts, then prioritize findings so the maximum refund is pursued on each shipment [Share a Refund — eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:01:31]
3. File fast with the right claim type and amount

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Fee structure is the sharpest split between shops. Brian at Refund Retriever says the firm starts at 50% of whatever it saves, so a $50 month of late-delivery refunds produces a $25 fee, and a shipper doing $25 million a year in UPS can negotiate that rate down because it is not set in stone [vOX4irOA_Tk @ 00:05:11]. Cerasis models the outsourced deal at a 30% cut instead: $1,000 in total savings still leaves the shipper a $700 net refund, scaling to as much as $70,000 across an enterprise [GnXmnbxCZ-A @ 00:05:14]. Nick at Share a Refund attacks the billing base rather than the percentage, charging that competitors bill on all refunds delivered, even ones they took no action on, while Share a Refund only splits refunds on shipments where its system acted, which the firm says yields a lower service fee [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:07:18]. Refund Retriever matches that stance for one credit class: when FedEx drops a systematic GSR refund onto an invoice unprompted, the firm marks it and never bills the client for it [8Bw_30ivpb4 @ 00:00:19]. Headline percentage and billable base are separate levers, and a cheaper-looking split can cost more if the auditor invoices credits it never chased.

Practitioners split hard on whether small shippers belong in the game at all. Nick at Share a Refund says auditors that refuse anyone spending under a threshold such as a million dollars a year are exposing limited bandwidth, thin automation, and throughput caps, and asks whether those shops could audit every shipment once peak season hits [PheSsrwvW9c @ 00:05:50]. Refund Retriever takes the opposite posture, onboarding companies from 50 shipments a week to 50 shipments a minute because its invoice downloads are run by programs rather than staff logging in [vOX4irOA_Tk @ 00:04:05]. Cerasis splits the difference with math: for a shipper using parcel only now and again, a 50-invoice audit that finds a 10% markup on 10% of invoices at $22 per shipment saves roughly one dollar, so paying an employee two hours to find it costs more than leaving the invoices alone [GnXmnbxCZ-A @ 00:03:04]. The same Cerasis host then argues that once the process is automated, audits cost no more than the software, which is why companies outsource and why the low-volume objection collapses [GnXmnbxCZ-A @ 00:04:39]. The working rule from all three: low volume kills manual in-house auditing, while automated contingency shops will still chase small accounts.

When carriers reject a claim, the playbooks diverge. Scarbrough Global's team says carriers often deny requests for no reason, which their data shows, and they believe it is done to discourage humans; their answer is brute persistence, resubmitting the same charge again and again and then putting an internal team on the phone with a live human [NKDaSW7CKiQ @ 00:01:27] [NKDaSW7CKiQ @ 00:02:25]. Scarbrough frames the follow-up, not the detection, as where the real fortune sits. Refund Retriever runs a filter that catches denied FedEx late-package disputes and routes them to in-house phone dispute agents who call FedEx customer service, conceding it is time consuming but that clients see more refunds each week [JeLyjRF9HEk @ 00:00:33]. Nick at Share a Refund treats rejection as a branching decision: a decision agent inside the claims verification service reads each rejected service-failure dispute and picks the next move, which might be filing on an invalid address correction instead, resubmitting the same service-failure dispute, or taking no further action at all [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:04:36]. Scarbrough bets on volume of retries backed by hundreds of checks, Refund Retriever on human phone escalation for a filtered list, and Share a Refund on per-shipment branching logic that also reconciles every credit back to its dispute for statistical feedback.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive self-inflicted wound is paying the carrier too fast. Refund Retriever pulled a client's FedEx invoice history after every submitted dispute came back denied and found the reason: an existing payment on file. The client had paid the invoice the moment it posted, which caused FedEx to automatically deny every GSR service-failure request even though the claims were inside the 15-day window [JeLyjRF9HEk @ 00:01:06] [JeLyjRF9HEk @ 00:01:25]. A phone dispute agent clawed back all five late packages, $98.42 on a $4,253.53 invoice, a 2.3% savings that would have been zero without the individual calls [JeLyjRF9HEk @ 00:01:42]. Nick at Share a Refund explains the mechanic: credits land on unpaid invoices during the gap between posting and payment, credits to open invoices are easier, and claims filed fast succeed more often; refunds arriving on the credit card on file or by mailed check signal disputes filed later than the ideal timeline [PheSsrwvW9c @ 00:02:03]. Nick also flags accounts payable working from a paper copy of the original invoice, instead of the updated credited amount, which overpays the carrier and forces refunds back as checks. Protect the payment gap until claims are filed, or auto-denials and slow refunds eat the recovery.

Brian Gibbs, President of Refund Retriever, built the company after getting burned around 2005: signing up with an existing recovery firm produced only more work, sending copies of invoices, and zero refunds [1JZzLbF5PrI @ 00:00:32]. Tracking packages by hand each week, Gibbs found about 10% of shipments from that eBay selling business arriving late, applied for every refund, and started getting money back before hiring a programmer to automate it [1JZzLbF5PrI @ 00:00:50]. The second trap is paying a vendor for credits the carrier gave away. Refund Retriever reports never seeing UPS refund a late parcel automatically, while FedEx rarely posts a systematic GSR failure credit on its own; if systematic SVC fail appears in the original reason column of the FedEx online billing invoice history, FedEx issued it automatically, and an auditor billing for it may be dishonest, though a refund applied within hours of the invoice publishing might be an honest dispute [8Bw_30ivpb4 @ 00:00:48]. Compete Consulting Group explains why shippers cannot simply rely on the carrier: you only have 15 days to claim, the carrier is not going to call to say a package arrived late, and trusting carrier web tools to flag every miss is like asking the fox to guard the henhouse [RCPFFZVF7qs @ 00:00:35].

The in-house math is where DIY auditors bleed. Cerasis calculates that if a single audit takes five minutes, a shipper spending $10,000 or less a year on small parcels would burn a full-time employee eight hours a day, five days a week, for nearly 21 weeks, and if 50% of audits require a claim, the time could double or triple while some carriers enforce strict filing limits [nGN0KikmdVk @ 00:02:50] [nGN0KikmdVk @ 00:03:05]. Even after finding errors, Cerasis warns that shippers must still contact carriers, prove their case, and request the refund, a process complex enough that an international refund can take weeks [GnXmnbxCZ-A @ 00:03:43]. Sloppy claims fail too. Nick at Share a Refund insists claims go in quickly and every claim carry the correct claim type and the right amount: a residential surcharge that missed its negotiated discount should be claimed at the net expected refund, while a residential surcharge slapped on a commercial delivery should be claimed in full [eTB0fyueR5A @ 00:03:16]. Speed, claim type, context, expected amount, and priority are all important considerations when filing. Share a Refund checks 40 different points per shipment to catch these, and Compete Consulting Group runs a 44-point audit on every shipment.
