# Chapter 08: Catch-Up Depreciation and the Section 179D Deduction

*Form 3115 look-back studies, 1031 exchange timing, bonus depreciation, engineering vs. limited-scope study types, 179D for A&E firms and contractors, Form 7205, IRA-expanded deduction amounts, and 179D allocations for not-for-profits*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on two retroactive money levers: catch-up depreciation through a Form 3115 accounting-method change that lands a negative 481A adjustment in the current year without amending returns, and the Section 179D deduction that Inflation Reduction Act changes pushed from $1.80 to as much as $5 per square foot. Tax Modern and The Wealthy Accountant walk the 3115 line by line, though they cite different DCN codes (7 versus 8). PorterKinney pegs bonus-eligible components at 20 to 30% of a building. Cherry Bekaert, Engineered Tax Services and George Ghazarian map the 179D stack: 25% savings over ASHRAE 90.1-2007, prevailing wage and apprenticeship for the 5x bonus, third-party engineer certification, and a signed allocation letter from tax-exempt owners. Disagreements center on study quality, passive-loss usability, recapture on sale, and Ghazarian's June 30, 2026 construction-start cutoff.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The operators agree that a missed cost segregation study is a recoverable deduction still waiting to be claimed, not a sunk loss. Tax Modern lays out the hard rule: once more than one return has been filed under the original method, amending is off the table and Form 3115 is mandatory, pulling the whole retroactive difference into the current year [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:02:55]. With only one return filed, the filer can amend that year instead, but the 3115 wins when the current year sits in a higher bracket than the prior one. PorterKinney sells this as the rare tax move where you pick the year: skip the study in a low-bracket purchase year, then five years later, when the business pushes into the 37% bracket, order the study and accelerate all the depreciation into that year with no amended return [G6l9WU9XBgI @ 00:08:25]. Keith Schrader of The Wealthy Accountant confirms the trigger: any property depreciated before the study needs the 3115 change-of-accounting-method application, with the study's cover letter and cost detail scanned and attached [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:00:38]. Cherry Bekaert's Bill Harson runs the same catch-up play on 179D, reaching back as far as 2006 through a 3115 instead of amended returns [0wGT1LCIPkY @ 00:15:15].

On 179D, the consensus is that the qualifying bar sits far lower than most building owners and designers assume. Cherry Bekaert's Ed Vel tells nonprofit owners that any new construction or renovation touching HVAC and hot water, interior lighting, or the building envelope belongs on the table, because the energy model compares the building against ASHRAE 2007, a 17-year-old standard, so a LEED project is not required [zU4xMPqHWY0 @ 00:05:51]. Bill Harson pushes it further: there is no need to prove lower utility bills, only that the upgrade beats the 2007 baseline, and on the question of whether it does, the answer always is yes, so the team pursues the deduction [0wGT1LCIPkY @ 00:13:00]. Engineered Tax Services frames the post-Inflation Reduction Act floor as a 25% cut in energy and power costs against ASHRAE 90.1-2007, worth 50 cents per square foot and climbing two cents per percentage point to $1 at 50% [1NYU-FDekUs @ 00:00:46]. Brady's Corner adds the street read from the old regime: because the comparison runs against an older energy standard, most newly constructed or renovated projects qualified, and an A&E firm's three university buildings totaling 250,000 square feet all hit the full $1.80 for a $450,000 deduction [t695zwyQZ7M @ 00:01:27].

The third point of agreement: when the building owner pays no tax, the deduction migrates to whoever designed the energy systems, and the paper trail is the whole game. Mitchell Tax Law explains that Congress added the allocation path because governments own a sizable share of commercial real estate; the owner allocates to the person primarily responsible for design, which can be an architect, engineer, contractor, environmental consultant or energy services provider, and the designer must hold the written, signed allocation before claiming, does not book it as income, and does not reduce future deductions by it [-ANQF-t6DyU @ 00:01:22]. George Ghazarian calls 179D probably the most overlooked deduction for contractors and puts it flatly: no allocation letter, no deduction, and the letter must be signed by the agency or tax-exempt owner, specify your designer role, and state which part of the project is allocated [Exr84oxoKzc @ 00:02:54]. Cherry Bekaert's Andre Khan lists the IRA expansion that opened private schools, charity hospitals, nonprofits, religious institutions and other non-taxpaying entities to allocation [zU4xMPqHWY0 @ 00:03:48]. Brady's Corner warns that government owners typically allocate to only one or two consultants per project, so the sooner the letter is secured, the better [t695zwyQZ7M @ 00:01:13].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Tax Modern's line-by-line build of the 3115 is the most granular walkthrough: tax year is just the current year (1/1/2025 to 12/31/2025), filer type is individual even for a single-member LLC, the DCN code entered is 7, audit protection is answered yes, and the conference-request box is checked yes because it gives more options [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:07:05]. The number that matters lands in Part IV, question 26: the cumulative 481A adjustment, entered as a negative figure because the study reduces taxable income [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:10:16]. On the return itself, that same amount is booked as a positive other expense named 481A adjustment so it flows to Schedule E line 19, and a second signed copy of the 3115, with the depreciation spreadsheet and Rev. Proc. 2022-14 statements, is mailed to the IRS separately [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:25:32]. Software is a real choke point: TurboTax online cannot produce the form, TurboTax desktop fills it only in forms mode with no attachments, so e-file first and paper-file via Form 8453. Keith Schrader keeps it lean: only pages 1, 2, 3 and 8 take entries, and Schedule E of the form carries five questions, including whether the filer ever lived in the property [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:01:47].

The catch-up math is mechanical once the study's cost summary is in hand. Schrader's duplex example: $400,000 purchase, $355,309 depreciable building, six years at 27.5-year straight line producing $77,522 of prior depreciation. The study carves out $85,600 of five-year and $6,893 of 15-year property, leaving $262,816 on the 27.5-year line, whose prior depreciation is restated to $57,342 [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:09:14]. Because the five-year class is fully expired, the remaining $66,922 ($85,600 minus $18,678 of reallocated prior depreciation) is keyed in as a manual current-year override; the 15-year bucket takes seven years of the table, 48.81%, or $3,433, less the $1,502 cited as already taken, for a $1,931 current deduction, and Schedule E depreciation jumps to roughly $78,000 [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:13:03]. For sizing deals before ordering a study, PorterKinney's rule of thumb is that 20 to 30% of a building lands in bonus-eligible classes, so a $1 million property throws off a $300,000 year-one deduction regardless of the down payment [G6l9WU9XBgI @ 00:01:56]. GavTax Advisory Services quotes the same band, 25% to 30%, or about $250,000 to $300,000 on a $1 million commercial building, tying that to the 100% bonus depreciation investors regained in 2025 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act [jNwzCbQub20 @ 00:03:39].

On the 179D side, Cherry Bekaert's Bill Harson describes the production line: collect drawings and pay applications, build the building as it stands in energy modeling software and run it for 365 hypothetical days, rebuild the same building to ASHRAE 90.1-2007 and run it again, then price the hypothetical cost savings, capped at 50% for the $5 per square foot maximum; a licensed professional engineer then inspects, confirms the building matches the model, and stamps the certification [0wGT1LCIPkY @ 00:19:08]. Cherry Bekaert's Ron Wayne Wright adds that Form 7205 is now mandatory and runs four sections: building and property data, the maximum-deduction calculation, the third-party certifier's contact information, and designer allocation details including every authorized signer [JZ5NlVOVnnw @ 00:12:27]. Engineered Tax Services pins down the bonus-rate labor math: contractors with at least four workers must hire at least one apprentice, with apprentice hours of 10% for 2022 starts, 12.5% for 2023 and 15% for 2024 or later, unlocking $2.50 at 25% savings and $5 at 50% [1NYU-FDekUs @ 00:01:54]. Eide Bailly's sequence is model, site survey of HVAC, envelope and lighting, then report and claim, which qualified a 200,000 square foot manufacturing addition at the full $1.80 per square foot for roughly $320,000 [OfVPZqJ7nxY @ 00:02:09].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull the Study and Prior Depreciation
   - Collect the study's cover letter, cost summary and cost detail, plus accumulated depreciation from the last filed return.
   - The cost detail and cost summary are the pages that get scanned and attached to the 3115 [The Wealthy Accountant — XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:00:38]; prior depreciation comes from the previous year return, excluding the current year being adjusted [Tax Modern — hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:20:15].
2. Compute the 481A Adjustment
   - Build old-versus-proposed schedules with the placed-in-service date and that year's bonus percentage, then take the difference.
   - The spreadsheet splits improvements into the structure plus 15-, 7- and 5-year property, applies bonus (80% in 2023), and the difference between schedules is the 481A figure that goes on Part IV question 26 [Tax Modern — hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:22:01].
3. Reload Assets and Override the Software
   - Reduce the building basis, add new class-life assets with restated prior depreciation, and hand-enter catch-up amounts.
   - Software cannot calculate the catch-up correctly, so current depreciation on the five- and 15-year assets is computed by hand and entered as an override [The Wealthy Accountant — XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:14:47]; check the software schedule matches the spreadsheet [Tax Modern — hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:24:40].
4. File, Mail and Paper the 179D Claim
   - Attach the 3115 to the return, mail a signed second copy, and for 179D designer claims secure the letter and certification before filing.
   - Mail a separately signed 3115 copy with the spreadsheet and statements, or use Form 8453 when software cannot attach them [Tax Modern — hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:27:32]; for 179D, get the signed allocation letter before filing because without it there is no deduction [George Ghazarian, CPA — Exr84oxoKzc @ 00:04:54].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Tax Modern draws the line at the filing count. With only one return filed on the old 27.5- or 39-year schedule, the operator can amend that year or run Form 3115 in the current year. Once more than one return is on file, amending is dead and 3115 is the only route [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:02:55]. Tax Modern's tiebreaker is bracket: if current-year income is higher, take the catch-up now rather than refunding the old year. PorterKinney turns that into a deliberate play. Their example owner skipped the study in a low-bracket purchase year, then five years later is pushing into the 37% bracket and accelerates all the missed depreciation into that year without an amended return [G6l9WU9XBgI @ 00:08:46]. The sharpest technical split is the designated change number. Tax Modern tells filers to put a seven in the DCN field for a cost segregation change [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:06:46]. Keith Schrader of The Wealthy Accountant says the code is eight because the move runs from one permissible form of depreciation to another permissible form [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:02:35]. Anyone cloning either filled-in walkthrough is copying two forms that disagree on this line. Schrader adds that copying his answers only works if each question is answered correctly for the actual property.

The front-loaded number depends on whose bonus math you run. Tax Modern's 3115 spreadsheet plugs in 80% bonus depreciation for a property placed in service in 2023 and lets it compute prior-year bonus on the 15- and 5-year buckets [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:19:21]. GavTax Advisory Services says the One Big Beautiful Bill Act restored 100% bonus in 2025. GavTax pegs a professional study at 25% to 30% of a $1 million commercial property, roughly $250,000 to $300,000 deductible in year one [jNwzCbQub20 @ 00:03:39]. PorterKinney lands on a similar 20% to 30% band and adds that the year-one deduction holds regardless of how much money went down on the building. On study depth, Engineered Tax Services ranks four tiers. A full engineering study is the gold standard. Below it sit a limited-scope cost seg light, statistical sampling that extrapolates a few apartment units to the whole complex, and square-foot rule-of-thumb allocations, which are the quickest, cheapest, and least precise [XTuAPOFbamo @ 00:01:15]. PorterKinney prices the real thing at $3,000 to $10,000 for a commercial building between $1 million and $10 million. They doubt the cheaper do-it-yourself online versions hold up, because a full-blown audit asks how the engineer broke out each asset class [G6l9WU9XBgI @ 00:04:50]. Engineered Tax Services frames the choice around property size, complexity, and the owner's tolerance for audit risk.

On 179D the practitioners do not even agree on the price per square foot, largely because they are quoting different law. Biz-Reps quotes $1.60 to $1.80 per square foot on a full 50% lighting reduction. Biz-Reps says filing is as simple as dropping the licensed contractor's report under other deductions [sY8onoIne8Q @ 00:06:08], and pitched the program as lasting only to the end of 2020. Engineered Tax Services lays out the post-IRA ladder: 50 cents at 25% savings over ASHRAE 90.1-2007, rising two cents per point to $1.00, then $2.50 to $5.00 once prevailing wage and apprenticeship are met [1NYU-FDekUs @ 00:02:26]. Ron Wainwright of Cherry Bekaert describes a far heavier lift. Form 7205 is a required filing, backed by an independent third-party energy report, and the IRS will compare it against its audit technique guideline in examination [JZ5NlVOVnnw @ 00:18:16]. The window splits too. Wainwright describes the $5 rate as permanent through a 2032 timetable. George Ghazarian, CPA says the OBBBA now requires construction beginning no later than June 30, 2026, and doubts Congress extends it [Exr84oxoKzc @ 00:03:35]. Ghazarian's floor is 54 cents rather than 50, and his ceiling runs to over $5 per square foot for the right project.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Tax Modern's walkthrough is a list of places the catch-up breaks. TurboTax Desktop will fill out Form 3115 in forms mode but will not attach the depreciation schedule or statements, so the return cannot actually carry the change. The fix is e-filing the return and then paper-mailing Form 8453 with the 3115, statements, and schedule [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:04:04]. The 481A amount must go on question 26 as a negative number. On Schedule E, though, it is entered as a positive other expense labeled 481A adjustment [hjpQzDqWetI @ 00:25:32]. Two copies go out, and only the separately mailed copy needs a signature, from both spouses on a joint return. Short-term rentals averaging under 30 days must run the structure over 39 years, not 27.5, and Tax Modern folds that correction into the same filing. Keith Schrader adds that software will not compute the 15-year catch-up correctly, so the operator calculates by hand and overrides the default. His math is $6,893 of 15-year basis times the 48.81% seven-year cumulative rate, which gives $3,433, less depreciation already allocated [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:15:01]. His own placard flips between $2,502 and $1,502 for that allocated figure, and he had already absorbed a two-dollar rounding error to reconcile the schedule [XhVfWjlDk3w @ 00:10:43].

Asset Wise Institute's warning is that a big study number can be dead on arrival. Their example is a high-income professional who exchanges into a $2 million multifamily building and buys a study. That investor may find the losses passive and partly suspended, carried forward instead of wiping out salary income [e9edFqL1urU @ 00:05:17]. Timing burns operators too. Close on December 20, renovate through January and February, and the building is not ready and available until March, so the expected year-of-closing depreciation never lands [e9edFqL1urU @ 00:07:30]. Asset Wise's fix is treating the first 90 days after closing as a tax setup period. GavTax Advisory Services lists three pitfalls. The first is misclassifying additions or structural work as short-life assets. The second is thin documentation without engineering reports, invoices, service dates, and categorized asset lists. The third is forgetting that the IRS may reclaim accelerated deductions as taxable income on sale [jNwzCbQub20 @ 00:04:17]. PorterKinney goes further on the exit. Selling for a gain undoes the depreciation benefit, the reclassified pieces become 1245 property with more unfavorable recapture, and a study rarely makes sense if the building will be sold within five years [G6l9WU9XBgI @ 00:07:27]. PorterKinney has also seen people copy another owner's cost segregation report and write their own name on it. That will not hold up in an IRS audit.

On 179D the failure points are paper and labor. George Ghazarian, CPA puts it flatly: the tax-exempt or government owner must sign an allocation letter specifying the designer's role and the allocated portion of the project, and without that letter the deduction is gone [Exr84oxoKzc @ 00:02:54]. Ghazarian's other fix is lining up the third-party engineer early instead of hunting one down at project end. Ron Wainwright of Cherry Bekaert says IRS examinations turned up allocation letters signed by people with no authority to transfer the deduction [JZ5NlVOVnnw @ 00:19:50]. He also says the energy savings cannot be self-certified; an independent third party has to do it. Wainwright's bigger trap is the $5 rate. An A&E firm should not assume a $5 transfer just because the building was placed in service in 2023. The firm needs representation, by job type and job level, that every prevailing wage was met [JZ5NlVOVnnw @ 00:21:35]. Andre Khan of Cherry Bekaert adds the back end. He says the 179D deduction carries a recapture that works like cost segregation if the building is sold or otherwise disposed of [0wGT1LCIPkY @ 00:13:54]. Wainwright also notes the IRA eliminated the old partial allowance and interim lighting analysis. That removed the ability to take deductions attributable to parking garages, and modeling now covers the entire building.
