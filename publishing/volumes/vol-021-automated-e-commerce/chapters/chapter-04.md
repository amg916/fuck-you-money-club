## Executive Summary

Forwarders, customs brokers, inventory-software operators and e-commerce finance modelers agree on one rule: the supplier invoice is only the starting point. Landed cost per unit is goods plus freight, insurance, duty, taxes, fees and delivery, divided by units received. CandiMentor's worked example turns a $10 supplier price into $12 per unit. Trade Counsel stacks 3.4%, 7.5% and 12.5% layers into 23.4% duty on Chinese plastic bins. ICandT shows a $100 China gearbox quote landing at $190.17. SKUio puts estimated duty into stock cost at receipt and corrects it once the broker's bill arrives. The practitioners split on three questions: FOB-valuation countries like the US versus CIF-valuation countries, DDP quotes versus export pricing, and whether to allocate shared freight by value or by weight. Racklify adds a parcel point: dimensional-weight pricing means you pay for the whole box, so fill it.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Forwarders, brokers and software operators all start from the same blunt point: the price on the supplier's invoice is not what the goods cost you. RRA Group calls the invoice only the starting point. It defines landed cost as the goods, freight, insurance, customs brokerage, duties and taxes, and delivery into your warehouse [007vdtJ5XrY @ 00:00:00]. Intel by CandiMentor draws the same line. Purchase price tells you what the supplier charges. Landed cost tells you what the product costs after it has traveled through the whole inbound supply chain. CandiMentor warns that logistics and import costs are where many estimates go wrong, because one shipment can pass through several carriers, brokers, ports and government charges [CvHvBnjwlJg @ 00:00:17]. Witners Shipping, which clears goods into Dubai, uses the same stack: product cost, international freight and insurance, customs duty, import VAT, then the handling, clearance and delivery charges your providers quote [8I51jDAoe-E @ 00:00:18]. US Customs Clearing lists the same four groups for American imports: product cost, freight and shipping fees, customs duties and taxes, and handling and documentation fees from brokers and forwarders [DLfvskykJiY @ 00:00:41]. The baseline for an operator is simple. If a cost touches the goods between the factory and your shelf, it belongs in the number.

The second point of agreement is that the tariff code drives everything else. Whoever is named as the importer carries the liability for getting it right. Trade Counsel explains that the importer of record is the company customs holds responsible for the duty. Every product has a 10-digit HTS code that sets the rate. A supplier may suggest a code, but if it is wrong you can owe the difference plus penalties [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:00:54]. John Tracy of the US Commercial Service, speaking at the New York SBDC, adds that harmonized codes are meant to match at the first six digits in every country, while each country sets the last four. Each destination also keeps its own rules for interpreting codes, so the code you use in the US at export may not be the one the foreign customs office applies [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:10:21]. ICandT stresses how fast this moves. Tariffs change almost every month, duties stack, some combine and some don't, and metal content and the HTS code can change the answer overnight [ebKN15qGOxo @ 00:01:21]. Witners Shipping makes the same point for the Gulf. The 5% common tariff is the default, but the HS code decides whether goods carry a different rate, such as 100% on tobacco or 50% on alcohol [8I51jDAoe-E @ 00:01:29].

Third, every operator turns the shipment total into a per-unit figure and treats the first number as an estimate that real bills will correct. Intel by CandiMentor divides total landed cost by the units received to get a realistic cost per sellable unit. Its best practice is to define the scope, write down your assumptions, use one method consistently, and check estimates against actual bills so the model improves over time [CvHvBnjwlJg @ 00:06:18]. RRA Group tells importers to mark every line as an estimate, note what is still unknown, update the sheet when real quotes arrive, and date the estimate because rates change. The landed cost per unit is then the figure to compare with the selling price [007vdtJ5XrY @ 00:01:18]. John Tracy, drawing on years in freight forwarding, labels any landed cost he gives a buyer as estimated. Rates fluctuate, and someone can miss a line, such as a 4-cents-per-kilo inspection fee when customs examines the cargo [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:20:54]. SKUio's jewelry demo shows why the timing matters. Brands price a piece on its factory cost, the duty shows up weeks later on the broker's bill, and until then every margin report looks better than the truth [DFZ8XDRkuCE @ 00:00:20].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The first mechanic is the valuation base. The same duty rate produces a different bill depending on what it is charged on. Witners Shipping walks through the Dubai sequence. Customs value is the transaction value plus transport, handling and insurance to the GCC border, which in practice is CIF value. The common tariff is 5% of CIF, and import VAT is 5% of CIF plus duty, not of the invoice alone [8I51jDAoe-E @ 00:01:29]. John Tracy runs a German export on the same logic: $10,000 of goods, $120 insurance and $2,500 freight give the CIF value, a 5% tariff comes to $631, and 19% VAT is charged on CIF plus duty before terminal handling and trucking to Munich [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:19:19]. Dr. Mercedes Sanchez Moore turns this around for imports. The US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand charge duty on the goods only, while most other countries use CIF. On $10,000 of goods with $3,500 of freight and insurance at 10% duty, the CIF method totals $14,850 against $14,500, a $350 gap [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:50:05]. Trade Counsel confirms how this works in the US. Ocean freight and insurance from China stay out of the dutiable value as long as they are shown separately, so $10,000 of bins pays $2,340 [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:02:19].

The second mechanic is the tariff stack, which practitioners calculate one layer at a time. Trade Counsel's plastic bins from China carry a 3.4% normal rate. On top sits a 7.5% Section 301 tariff in place since 2018 and 2019, then a newer Section 301 tariff, in force since July 2026, that adds 12.5%. Together they come to 23.4% [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:01:56]. CFOmarketIQ shows that the amount a rate applies to can change while the rate stays the same. A $100 part that is about 40% steel paid roughly $10 while the 25% tariff applied only to the steel content. From April it applied to the full customs value, so the duty became $25, fifteen dollars more per unit, and no supplier sent an email about it [nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:00:17]. ICandT builds the metal rule into its workbook. Section 232 applies at a metal share of 15% or more by weight, and parts made entirely of metal pay 50% on the full customs value. Base duty and China Section 301 tariffs are then added automatically based on country of origin [ebKN15qGOxo @ 00:04:25]. Shipping Solutions runs the stack in its calculator by entering Chapter 99 codes alongside HS number 6603203000. One Canadian code adds a 25% duty marked 'in addition to'. The Vietnam comparison shows zero default duty but a 10% special duty [4868R7AGDp0 @ 00:06:11].

The third mechanic covers the fees, the customs bond and the accounting that sit outside the duty rate. CFOmarketIQ puts the merchandise processing fee at about a third of a percent of value. Its cap per entry recently rose to a little over $670, and anything arriving by sea also pays a harbor maintenance fee. Duty is paid when the goods arrive, long before they sell, so a tariff change can squeeze your cash a quarter before it shows up in the P&L [nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:01:39]. Trade Counsel lists the setup. You need an importer number, which for most businesses is the federal tax ID. Shipments worth more than $2,500 need a customs bond, either a single-entry bond or a continuous bond starting at $50,000 of coverage. You also need an Importer Security Filing at least 24 hours before the goods are loaded in China, or customs can claim $5,000 per violation against the bond [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:03:17]. SKUio handles the books. Estimated duty goes into stock cost at receipt and is held in an Accrued Customs Duty account until the broker's bill clears it. Stock on hand and units already sold each take their share of any difference. Estimates older than 90 days are flagged, and any still unbilled are released 120 days after the last receipt [BAGqaHYyMlA @ 00:01:19].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the code and the tariff stack
   - List your top 20 imported items by spend, with country of origin and tariff code, and flag anything containing steel, aluminum or copper.
   - CFOmarketIQ's checklist starts with the top twenty items by spend, their country of origin and tariff code. It then flags metal content so you can recheck the duty on full value and reset standard costs where the duty moved [CFOmarketIQ — nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:03:32].
2. Build the worksheet in clearance order
   - Start with the invoice, add freight and insurance to reach CIF value, apply duty by HS code, add VAT on CIF plus duty, then add the clearance, port, terminal and delivery quotes. Divide the total by units.
   - Witners Shipping's five-step order runs from product cost through CIF value, duty, 5% VAT on CIF plus duty, and written quotes for the remaining charges. The total is then divided by the number of units to get a landed cost per unit [Witners Shipping — 8I51jDAoe-E @ 00:03:17].
3. Clear the paperwork before the ship loads
   - Have your broker, bond and Importer Security Filing ready 24 hours before loading. File the entry within 15 days of arrival and the entry summary within 10 working days of release.
   - Trade Counsel's timeline: 15 days from arrival to file the entry, then CBP Form 7501 filed and duty paid within 10 working days of release. The $800 duty-free allowance has been suspended since August 2025, and records must be kept for 5 years [Trade Counsel — -WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:04:13].
4. Book estimated duty at receipt and reconcile
   - Turn on Capitalise estimated customs duty, receive the shipment, enter the broker's bill split by estimated duty, and review the Duty Reconciliation report.
   - In SKU.io, choose your customs broker as the supplier, type the invoice number and enter the duty charged. The bill is split by each line's booked estimate so each variance lands fairly, and the shipment's status chip changes from Awaiting bill to Cleared [SKUio — BAGqaHYyMlA @ 00:02:32].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Plays That Move the Landed Number**
> 
> Brokers, finance modelers and fulfillment operators use these to keep the per-unit cost honest and the duty bill as small as it should be.
> - Make the supplier list the price of the goods separately from freight and insurance on the invoice and the bill of lading. If they send one bundled figure, freight can end up in the dutiable value when you import into FOB-valuation countries like the US [New York SBDC — LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:50:28].
> - Ask your broker which of your entries are still open. After the Supreme Court ruling, the trade court ordered refunds on entries that were not yet final. Once an entry is final, you have roughly six months to protest it [CFOmarketIQ — nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:02:43].
> - Build your model on the factory's most expensive volume tier. If the unit economics work at that price, they will work at the discounted tier too [John Galt Finance — 8RBtd8u_F-I @ 00:01:58].
> - Never ship a $2.99 item alone in a dimensional-weight box. Racklify's hand-soap six-pack sells for $12 to $15 and ships in one box at almost the same price as a single bottle [Racklify — NXZLkZ_Gfuk @ 00:04:14].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over Delivered Duty Paid. John Tracy of the New York SBDC, a former freight forwarder, says his shop told every client to avoid DDP at all costs: quote CIF to the nearest ocean port or airport, hand the buyer a landed-cost-to-door figure for planning only, and wash your hands of destination duty, taxes and delivery [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:24:54]. Dr. Mercedes Sanchez Moore, speaking on the import side of the same webinar, flips it: she still advises buyers to request a DDP price first because it gives a clean read on total cost, then work backward from the raw export price to find where the seller buffered freight and fees [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:43:45]. Intel by CandiMentor lands between them, noting a DDP invoice looks higher but is more complete, while FOB shifts the main international leg and import process onto the buyer [CvHvBnjwlJg @ 00:02:43]. Trade Counsel adds the liability angle operators miss: under FOB you are the importer of record that customs holds responsible for duty and every detail, while on DDP the seller usually takes that role [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:00:35]. The working rule: DDP as a price-discovery quote, FOB or CIF as the contract you actually sign.

The second split is the duty base itself. Sanchez Moore draws the line by country: the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand assess duty on the goods only, while Mexico, Egypt and most of the world charge on cost, insurance and freight; on $10,000 of goods with $3,500 freight and insurance at 10%, the CIF method lands $350 higher [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:49:00]. Trade Counsel confirms the US side, but with a condition: ocean freight and insurance from China stay out of the value only as long as they are shown separately on the paperwork [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:02:19]. Witners Shipping runs the opposite model for Dubai, where the 5% GCC common tariff hits the CIF value and import VAT of 5% then lands on CIF plus duty, not the invoice alone [8I51jDAoe-E @ 00:02:06]. Even the US metal rules flipped mid-year: CFOmarketIQ shows the same 25% rate on a $100 part that is about 40% steel jumping from roughly $10 of duty to $25 once it applied to full customs value instead of steel content, an extra $15 per unit nobody on the supplier side emailed about [nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:00:17]. Operator takeaway: know which base your destination uses before you trust anyone's duty math.

Practitioners also split on how to carry landed cost through the books, how to insure it, and who does the grunt work. Intel by CandiMentor allocates shared freight by value when consumption broadly follows value, but switches to weight or volume when large, heavy or bulky lines eat most of the carrier capacity [CvHvBnjwlJg @ 00:04:10]. SKUio skips both for duty and splits the broker's bill by each line's booked duty estimate, so a line that carried most of the duty absorbs most of the bill and each variance stays fair [BAGqaHYyMlA @ 00:02:32]. On insurance, Tracy ran inventory-in-transit as a rider on a chemical manufacturer's plant policy, which was the cheaper route but forced him to maintain spreadsheets of every movement, while other shippers preferred paying a per-shipment fee through their forwarder to offload the work [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 01:26:53]. On brokerage, Tracy recalls the one customer in roughly 15 years who pulled logistics in-house while importing from Asia, then quit after a single year and handed it back, and his verdict is that first-time importers absolutely need a customs broker for commercial transactions [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 01:05:17]. The DIY saving only shows up after the reps are in and the paperwork stops costing you tutorial time.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures come from duty the operator never modeled. CFOmarketIQ walks through the April change where nobody touched the 25% steel rate but moved it onto full customs value, and with no exception for goods already on the water, many CFOs only found out from the broker's invoice weeks later, or from a CEO asking why margin slipped [nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:00:50]. John Galt Finance names the endgame: add-on costs like duty, testing and inspection can run more than 10% and eat the whole margin, and when US tariffs hit the European Union market, a lot of companies went bankrupt because their models never assumed higher customs duties [8RBtd8u_F-I @ 00:02:36]. Trade Counsel stacks the China layers on a $10,000 order of plastic bins, 3.4% base plus 7.5% Section 301 plus a newer 12.5% Section 301 layer from July 2026, landing $2,340 in duty and about $2,400 with fees, and warns the $800 duty-free allowance has been suspended since August 2025 [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:04:36]. Stord flagged an earlier jolt: Section 321 de minimis eliminated for China and Hong Kong plus a universal 10% tariff as of April 2nd [eZY2MYqExrQ @ 00:00:16]. Every one of these hit operators whose spreadsheets still showed last quarter's rate.

Paperwork deadlines carry their own price tags, and they hit before a single unit sells. Trade Counsel warns the Importer Security Filing, naming seller, factory and HTS code, must reach customs at least 24 hours before goods are loaded in China, and a late, wrong or missing filing lets customs claim $5,000 per violation against your bond [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:03:34]. The same channel says anything made wholly or partly in Xinjiang is presumed forced-labor product, customs can detain it, and the burden of proving otherwise sits squarely on you as the importer [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:04:13]. Tracy's DDP horror story is the inspection hold: a non-resident importer of record gets called for exam, the cargo parks in a bonded warehouse, and the seller stays on the hook for very expensive storage charges until customs actually reviews and clears the documentation [LBisYGOUrx0 @ 00:22:48]. Intel by CandiMentor adds demurrage, the delay charge that appears when containers remain at a terminal too long, and treats it as a textbook avoidable charge that should be investigated rather than automatically booked as normal freight [CvHvBnjwlJg @ 00:04:45]. None of these charges appear on the supplier invoice, and as Trade Counsel puts it, get one step wrong and you find out at the port.

The economics checks are where small operators bleed quietly. Nama, a student in Marcus Lam's high-ticket dropshipping program, booked two sales in one day, about $7,000 and $4,000 in revenue, then admitted learning the hard way what margins look like after shipping [b7IMdD7nvhg @ 00:06:46]. The hosts framed it as the moment cost of goods, shipping and profit turn into real numbers, and why suppliers have to be picked on fulfillment and margin, not just availability. Racklify's Bill runs the low-ticket version: a $2.99 hand soap loses money shipped first-class, while a six-pack selling for $12 to $15 ships in one box at almost the same price as a single bottle [NXZLkZ_Gfuk @ 00:04:14]. CFOmarketIQ adds the cash trap: you pay duty when goods arrive, long before you sell them, so a tariff change can squeeze cash a quarter before it ever shows in the P&L, while standard costs lag reality by a quarter if nobody resets them [nUu4s78pX8E @ 00:02:28]. John Galt Finance's unit-economics waterfall is the last gate: a product selling at 250 ends the day with only 50, and the operator has to walk every rectangle to find where the other 200 went and optimize what can be optimized [8RBtd8u_F-I @ 00:09:01].
