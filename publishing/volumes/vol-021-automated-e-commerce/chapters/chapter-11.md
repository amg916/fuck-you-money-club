# Chapter 11: Google Shopping Feeds and Merchant Center

*Merchant Center setup for Shopify, titles and product categories, attribute rules, custom labels with Google Sheets, Merchant Center promotions, feed fixes for Performance Max ROAS, organic free listings, and high-ticket SEO niches*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Operators from Grow My Ads, Ecom Chris, KeyCommerce, Max Crakanthorp and Abdul Rauf Ali converge on one rule: in Shopping and Performance Max, the feed is the targeting, and price is the lever that rules all. They front-load titles with the converting search term, use most of the 150 characters, pick the deepest product category, keep price, sale price and availability identical to the site, and never change product IDs. Street numbers include a 10x click-and-impression jump from a Home Reserve sofa retitle, a 2-3x CTR lift from lifestyle imagery, 20-30% more sales from one winter-jacket retitle, and CPCs 2-3x higher on poorly optimized feeds. Custom labels built through attribute rules, Google Sheets or supplemental feeds segment by margin, price bucket and season. Practitioners split on brand-first versus keyword-first titles, description effort, Google's generative image tools and whether GTINs are essential.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator in this group starts from the same premise: in Shopping and Performance Max, the product feed is the targeting. Ecom Chris opens bluntly, saying that if Shopping or PMax feels random, it is usually not the bidding, it is the product data, and argues that money leaks out of the foundation rather than out of campaign structure, tROAS settings or hacks; poor input data brings low CTR and ROAS, inflated CPCs and misrepresentation suspensions [XQTEnn-3t0E @ 00:00:00]. Casey Gill, teaching inside the Jyll Saskin Gales course, says feed data replaces keyword research for Shopping and PMax, and works titles, then product types, then categories as separate passes, calling each fix a '1% factor' [Q-KrwvAVhR4 @ 00:01:25]. KeyCommerce adds pressure from the next channel: AI shopping assistants bypass the website and read feed data directly, so incomplete feeds simply get skipped [S1NhYxaPnic @ 00:00:28]. Abdul Rauf Ali, showing an account with $7.19M in conversion value at a 3.93 ROAS on roughly $1.8M spend, runs diagnostics in a fixed order of understand, match, click and close, and reads the search terms report before touching anything else [V0LAr3jchLs @ 00:01:25]. The shared conclusion is simple: fix the data first, then argue about bids.

Titles and price are the two levers ranked highest across the group. Austin of Grow My Ads says the first 30 to 70 characters of a title are most critical, yet sellers should use as much of the 150-character limit as possible, because Google may reorder words already in the title but does not add new ones; well-known brands such as Nike, Apple or Samsung go first, while unknown brands go at the end [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:04:15]. On price, Austin is unequivocal that 'price will rule all': a competitor 20% cheaper beats the best-built feed, and price baiting, checkout hacks or fake sale prices can get the account suspended [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:02:10]. Kai Cromwell reports that below-average prices lift impressions and CTR, recommends cheaper entry products with an upsell later, and warns that stale sale prices hurt visibility [W6w0BjjlBs4 @ 00:06:44]. Ecom Chris lands in the same place, calling Google a price comparison platform, flagging that shoppers hold back mid-month and at month end, and pricing within 5 to 10% of the average while insisting it is not a race to the bottom [XQTEnn-3t0E @ 00:15:41]. Titles earn the match; price decides the click.

The third point of agreement is strict consistency between the feed, the store and the business behind it. KeyCommerce's Sam tells operators to change price, availability and sale fields at the source, such as Shopify, because Google checks daily and a discrepancy brings quick disapproval; Sam also warns never to overwrite a product ID, since doing so creates a new product, loses its history, and the team has watched catalog-wide ID changes make performance plummet [UcU7Ishu1lY @ 00:04:52]. Max Crakanthorp always recommends adding a sale price because the green sale price boosts clicks, but a mismatch between feed and website gets the product suspended [H4yH9xs030Q @ 00:13:20]. In the setup course, Max adds that business information which does not match the website leads to misrepresentation bans, and that only an 'exceptional' rating earns the top quality store badge [FZOo2bNTrzo @ 00:08:23]. Wixpa closes the loop with a line operators should tape to the monitor: a perfect product feed does not by itself get products approved, because contact, refund, shipping, privacy and terms pages plus visible business details still have to exist on the site, and suspensions remain possible even after a clean app-based sync [RHJvGN_6sf0 @ 00:04:47].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Title work is where the hard numbers live. Ecom Chris says this is not SEO: build a 2 to 3 word primary keyword such as 'running shoe' plus secondary modifiers, and source terms in strict priority order, with Google Ads search terms first, then Merchant Center queries, autocomplete, competitor listings, and keyword tools last [d9kULGtUBD4 @ 00:10:53]. The payoff anecdote: retitling a winter jacket to match its converting search term produced 20 to 30% more sales in about a week at the same ROAS [d9kULGtUBD4 @ 00:12:01]. Austin at Grow My Ads reports the Home Reserve case, where renaming an 'Alley sofa' to a 'kid and pet friendly sectional sofa' with 'washable' in the title produced a 10x jump in clicks and impressions, and sales rose with it [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:10:45]. Grow My Ads then rolls that pattern across hundreds of SKUs without touching the store: an attribute rule prepends 'kid and pet friendly washable' and appends the Home Reserve brand, turning 'tuck standard sectional in malti soda' into a keyword-led title, which the team says drove very significant increases in performance [-w0GraOv2B0 @ 00:06:16]. Chris's filter for every modifier: if removing it would attract the wrong buyer, keep it, because search volume barely matters.

Custom labels are the segmentation engine, and every practitioner who covers them works the same five slots, labels 0 through 4. Tim Sutton shows a PMax client bucketed by price from 100 up to 10,000, with a '2,000+' label carrying its own tROAS and products with 100+ clicks and low ROAS excluded outright; Tim also warns manual edits do not scale to thousands of SKUs, so the labels live in a supplemental sheet filled down [APb0cOGKROE @ 00:05:06]. Grow My Ads builds labels with zero third-party tooling: in Merchant Center Next, go to data sources, the Content API source, attribute rules, add a rule on custom_label_3, and set it to 'Christmas' when the title contains Christmas, Santa or holiday; the preview shows the active value empty against the draft value, and the change goes live on the next fetch [-w0GraOv2B0 @ 00:03:09]. PPC Incubator's Ian maps a Google Sheet of brand tiers (top, high, mid, low) through feed rules, then splits listing groups first by 25% margin and then by low brand [79VrN9FhpT8 @ 00:01:21]. Abdul Rauf Ali tags top performers using a hygiene filter of ROAS above the 1.9 breakeven, at least 50 clicks, over a 6 to 12 month window [V0LAr3jchLs @ 00:08:40].

Promotions, images and tooling round out the mechanics. KeyCommerce sets promotions up through Settings, Add-ons, Promotions, Add; the promo code must already exist in the store, and because Google ends promos at midnight the day before, the end time goes in as 11:59 p.m.; review takes 1 to 24 hours, longer around Black Friday, and free shipping is the recommended first promotion [jmjy6G9akyM @ 00:01:32]. Max Crakanthorp runs a 10 to 20% test promo and notes first-order promotions are available with new-customer bidding [FZOo2bNTrzo @ 00:19:26]. On images, Austin reports lifestyle shots deliver a 2 to 3x CTR lift, uses Product Studio to remove backgrounds, raise resolution and generate scenes, got a good lifestyle image from a single Nano Banana Pro prompt, and keeps the white-background main image with lifestyle shots added as extras [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:13:00]. For feed software, Austin ranks Channable first, puts Feedonomics in the enterprise, hands-on tier for spend from a couple hundred thousand a year into the millions, and positions DataFeedWatch as the cheaper, more DIY option [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:29:24]. Whatever tool runs the catalog, edits belong inside that tool.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Audit diagnostics and segment the catalog
   - Open Needs attention, resync if disapprovals look like a sync issue, then bucket products by performance.
   - Check diagnostics first; a disapproval rate of about 0.3% is usually a sync issue, so resync, then sort products into top sellers, wasters, high-click/low-CVR and high-impression/low-CTR, leaving top sellers for last [Ecom Chris — d9kULGtUBD4 @ 00:02:53]
2. Rewrite titles for the top SKUs via supplemental feed
   - Sort by conversions, batch the top 30-50 SKUs, and push new titles through a supplemental feed.
   - Use Brand + model + type or type + attribute + use case + brand, with no caps, symbols or promo text, and batch the top 30-50 SKUs sorted by conversions through a supplemental feed [Abdul Rauf Ali — V0LAr3jchLs @ 00:04:26]
3. Build custom labels with attribute rules
   - Create a rule on an unused custom label, preview, save as draft, apply, and segment in Google Ads.
   - Data sources > Content API > Attribute rules > Add rule on custom_label_3; if title contains Christmas OR Santa OR holiday set 'Christmas', check the draft value in preview, apply, and it goes live on the next fetch [Grow My Ads — -w0GraOv2B0 @ 00:03:09]
4. Launch a test promotion and lock the review cadence
   - Run a 10-20% test promo with an 11:59 p.m. end time, then review diagnostics weekly and store quality monthly.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over what leads the title. Austin at Grow My Ads puts household-name brands such as Nike, Apple and Samsung at the front and pushes unknown brands to the end. He argues the first 30 to 70 characters carry the weight, and Google may reorder words already in the title but never adds any [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:06:04]. His Home Reserve retitle to 'kid and pet friendly sectional sofa... washable' is the proof he points to for the benefit-led formula: a 10x jump in clicks and impressions, with sales rising. Kai Cromwell goes keyword-first, putting a phrase like 'leather office chair' ahead of the brand because only about 70 of the 150 characters are visible [W6w0BjjlBs4 @ 00:03:26]. Ecom Chris lands in the middle. He puts the brand first only when it is strong like Nike, and otherwise frontloads the main keyword. He also stacks attributes through the feed rather than hardcoding them, and never stacks synonyms [d9kULGtUBD4 @ 00:21:41]. Abdul Rauf Ali runs two templates depending on the catalog: brand plus model plus type, or type plus attribute plus use case plus brand. He sorts by conversions and only rewrites that slice first [V0LAr3jchLs @ 00:02:25]. The operator call: pick the template that matches your brand equity, then fill the visible zone with real search-term language.

Description depth splits the room just as hard. Max Crakanthorp treats the description as copy written 'for Google' and filled roughly 4,700 of 5,000 characters in his live build. He drafts with an AI prompt and then strips out irrelevant keywords [H4yH9xs030Q @ 00:06:23]. Ecom Chris says the description matters less than the title. He wants 200 to 500 characters, written for humans and leading with value, and warns that AI slop risks disapproval or higher CPC [XQTEnn-3t0E @ 00:07:28]. Austin at Grow My Ads applies an 80/20 rule: fix titles first and only write descriptions for top products [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:22:25]. The same fault line runs through image generation. Austin got a usable lifestyle scene from a single Nano Banana Pro prompt and uses Product Studio for background removal, upscaling and scene generation. KeyCommerce, by contrast, reports that Google's own generative image tool 'hasn't been that useful' [S1NhYxaPnic @ 00:05:00]. Both camps hold the same red line. Austin calls altering the product's look 'unethical', and he keeps the white-background main image while adding lifestyle shots as additional images. Testing method differs too: Max split-tests lifestyle versus plain images, either month versus month or across products.

Tooling is the last big fork. Austin ranks Channable as the number one pick. He positions Feedonomics as the enterprise, hands-on option for accounts spending a 'couple hundred thousand a year to millions', and calls DataFeedWatch the cheaper DIY route [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:29:24]. Yet in a separate Grow My Ads tutorial, the same agency runs a Content API client with no feed tool at all. It writes Merchant Center Next attribute rules that tag any title containing Christmas, Santa or holiday into custom_label_3, and rewrites sofa titles across hundreds of SKUs [-w0GraOv2B0 @ 00:01:38]. Tim Sutton says manual label edits do not scale to thousands of SKUs and pushes a supplemental sheet with fill-down instead [APb0cOGKROE @ 00:02:48]. His PMax client example used price buckets from 100 to 10,000, with a '2,000+' label carrying its own tROAS. Ian at PPC Incubator lays out three ways to populate labels side by side [79VrN9FhpT8 @ 00:00:41]:
- a Google Sheet of brand tiers mapped through feed rules;
- a supplemental feed keyed on ID, carrying labels like 'best price + low click + high AOV';
- a developer-pushed main feed carrying 'margin 25/30%'.
The choice is economic, not ideological. Spend level decides whether a paid platform earns its fee. Whatever you choose, the system holding the source of truth must be the only place edits happen.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The fastest way to lose a feed is a mismatch between what Merchant Center says and what the store shows. Max Crakanthorp always adds a sale price because the green sale price boosts clicks. But a sale price that does not match the website gets the product suspended [H4yH9xs030Q @ 00:13:46]. KeyCommerce's Sam says price and availability must be changed at the source, such as Shopify, because Google checks daily and a discrepancy brings quick disapproval [UcU7Ishu1lY @ 00:04:19]. Austin at Grow My Ads names the aggressive versions of the same play as suspension triggers: price baiting, checkout hacks and fake sale prices [hY8ZBC3A4uQ @ 00:03:19]. His alternative to faking a discount is honest price positioning, because in his words price will rule all, and a competitor 20% cheaper beats the best feed. Wixpa adds the trap that catches new Shopify stores: a perfect product feed does not guarantee product approval. Contact, refund, shipping, privacy and terms pages are still required, along with visible business details, and suspensions remain possible even after a clean app setup [RHJvGN_6sf0 @ 00:04:47]. The rule across all four is simple. Edit prices only where the store gets them, mirror them exactly, and treat every sale price as a live claim that Google checks daily.

History destruction is the quiet killer. KeyCommerce warns never to change an ID or SKU, because doing so would create a new product for Google. They report performance dropping after IDs were changed [S1NhYxaPnic @ 00:06:21]. The same video flags a second trap. If a feed app such as Semrush or DataFeedWatch runs the catalog, manual Merchant Center edits get overridden on the next sync, so all work belongs in the app. Wixpa calls the product ID format the migration trap. A new account keeps the default format, but a store switching apps must keep its previous format or lose its history [RHJvGN_6sf0 @ 00:02:18]. Wixpa also notes that inventory sync runs automatically once the app is connected and that a 'Pending' status is normal. Sync failures also masquerade as feed problems. Ecom Chris checks diagnostics first and reads a disapproval rate around 0.3% as usually a sync issue, fixed by a resync rather than a rewrite [d9kULGtUBD4 @ 00:02:53]. Max Crakanthorp hit the shipping version of the same bottleneck. He tells operators to turn off the Shopify shipping sync when disapprovals occur, then work through issues sorted by click potential, checking diagnostics weekly and store quality monthly [FZOo2bNTrzo @ 00:11:04].

The economics checks come down to data volume and waste. Ecom Chris tests one change per SKU and runs it for one to two weeks. He says that at $40 to $50 a day there is not enough data, because you need a lot of budget and a lot of data to test properly. His read on results: CTR rising while CVR falls means the title went too broad [d9kULGtUBD4 @ 00:30:17]. In the companion video he prices the downside of skipping the work. Poorly optimized feeds pay CPC two to three times higher, which he tracks alongside CTR, bounce rate, CVR and ROAS [XQTEnn-3t0E @ 00:23:16]. Abdul Rauf Ali runs a hygiene filter over six to twelve months: ROAS above 1.9, his breakeven, with at least 50 clicks. He gives the top performers a custom label [V0LAr3jchLs @ 00:08:40]. Tim Sutton's PMax client goes further on the cut side, excluding products with 100+ clicks and low ROAS [APb0cOGKROE @ 00:05:22]. Ecom Chris also warns of 'death by overoptimization', applying 80/20 with supplemental feeds for overrides. The operator takeaway for small budgets: batch only proven winners, cut proven losers on a click threshold, and resist rewriting the whole catalog at once.
