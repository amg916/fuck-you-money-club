## 1. Days 1–7: Underwrite the Assortment Before Committing Inventory  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Day 1 establishes the operating boundary: one domestic market, one storefront, one contracted 3PL, and three to five initial SKUs from authorized suppliers. Candidate #28 has 18 mapped plays; use the following chapter-local execution labels to make that coverage actionable without treating them as separate businesses. P01 selects the niche; P02 verifies dealer authorization; P03 checks product safety; P04 calculates landed cost; P05 underwrites contribution; P06 builds product pages; P07 configures checkout and tax; P08 integrates the 3PL; P09 routes orders; P10 monitors inventory; P11 handles delivery exceptions; P12 tracks returns; P13 tests paid acquisition; P14 captures permissioned email; P15 runs retention; P16 reconciles settlements; P17 controls cash; and P18 makes portfolio decisions. Assign the commercial lead P01–P07, P13, P17, and P18, and the operations lead P08–P12, P14–P16. These are the two full-time seats. Three contractors provide storefront and tracking implementation, creative and lifecycle production, and bookkeeping with tax coordination. The 3PL is a contracted service, not an invisible internal employee. Keep one shared exception queue so contractor handoffs cannot become orphaned work. Record a primary owner and backup for every play before accepting supplier terms.

Days 2–4 turn supplier enthusiasm into documentary evidence. Obtain a signed authorization identifying permitted channels, territories, warranty handling, image rights, and termination terms; a distributor invoice alone does not establish every resale permission. Match product identifiers to applicable test reports and certificates under 15 U.S.C. 2063 and 16 CFR Part 1110 where certification is required. Avoid children's products, ingestibles, batteries, and medical claims in this initial assortment unless the team already has category-specific expertise and documentation. Record lot identifiers and a recall contact, and establish how the operator will assess reporting duties under 15 U.S.C. 2064(b). For imports, identify the importer of record, classification, origin, customs value, duties, and broker before approving landed cost; 19 U.S.C. 1484 places entry responsibilities on the importer. Confirm insurance coverage against the actual category, rather than assuming a general policy covers every product. On Day 4, obtain a written 3PL rate card covering receiving, storage, pick fees, packaging, dimensional weight, returns, and minimum charges. Model the most expensive common shipping zone, not merely the warehouse's neighboring ZIP codes. Reject suppliers whose lead times or evidence cannot support the published customer promise.

Days 5–7 establish a cash envelope before placing the opening purchase order. This illustrative sprint requires $60,000 of committed funding, within the candidate's $30,000–$250,000 startup range, and caps first-month cash commitments at $35,000: $16,000 inventory and inbound freight, $12,000 loaded payroll and contractor work, $3,000 advertising, $2,000 systems and setup, and $2,000 contingency. The noninventory envelope is therefore $19,000, inside the stated $4,000–$25,000 monthly operating range, although actual accounting classification follows the underlying expense. Keep the remaining $25,000 uncommitted; customer receipts do not automatically enlarge spending authority. Inventory purchases consume cash immediately but become cost of goods as units sell, subject to impairment. Obtain real staffing quotes: the labor allowance is an assumption, not evidence that every market can supply five people at that cost. If loaded staffing exceeds it, revise funding and scope before purchase, rather than hiding unpaid owner labor inside profit. Stage the inventory commitment with the supplier and include cancellation exposure in the cash ledger. An operator starting with only $30,000 should complete validation and negotiate smaller orders instead of attempting this entire funded configuration.

## 2. Days 8–14: Prove Checkout, Routing, and Exception Recovery  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Days 8–10 build a narrow transaction path rather than a decorative storefront. Use Shopify for catalog and checkout, the contracted 3PL's supported connector for fulfillment, and QuickBooks Online or Xero for the accounting destination; select one accounting system, not both. The implementation contractor creates a staging checklist while the commercial lead owns prices, product claims, shipping promises, and customer-facing policies. For every SKU, publish dimensions, compatibility limits, warranty route, and realistic dispatch timing. Under 16 CFR Part 435, maintain a reasonable basis for the stated shipping time; if shipment cannot occur as promised, follow the applicable delay-notice, cancellation, consent, and refund requirements. Do not let a template promise same-day shipment when the warehouse cutoff makes that impossible. P07 includes determining registration obligations from physical presence, inventory location, and each destination state's economic nexus rules. A tax calculation app does not perform every registration or filing duty. The bookkeeping contractor produces a jurisdiction worksheet and the commercial lead resolves required registrations before affected sales begin. Restrict collection of customer data to operational needs, configure role-based access, and document the privacy requests workflow applicable to the actual business.

Days 11–12 attack the integration's failure paths. Send one normal order, one duplicate event, one cancellation, one partial allocation, one address correction, and one refund through controlled tests. Shopify webhooks may arrive more than once; use the order identifier and fulfillment operation as an idempotency key so a retry cannot create a second shipment. Authenticate incoming events, minimize stored customer fields, and place failed messages into a visible retry queue with an assigned owner. Distinguish financial authorization, capture, warehouse acceptance, label creation, carrier acceptance, and delivery. A purchased label is not proof that the parcel entered the carrier network. The operations lead reconciles available inventory against allocated orders and warehouse counts twice daily during launch. Set the reorder point from expected daily demand multiplied by replenishment lead time, then add explicit safety stock; for two units daily, fourteen days of lead time, and ten buffer units, the alert triggers at thirty-eight units. Keep safety stock separate from sellable stock when publishing availability. Never enable uncontrolled backorders to conceal a connector delay. Record each test's expected state, observed state, timestamp, and recovery owner so another person can reproduce the result.

Days 13–14 prove the customer and accounting loops before paid traffic arrives. The operations lead creates return reason codes, photographs condition standards, and defines who decides restock, refurbish, supplier credit, or disposal. P12 must connect a return authorization to the original order, physical receipt, refund, and inventory adjustment; an email thread alone is not a returns ledger. Configure Klaviyo or an equivalent lifecycle system for separately identified transactional and commercial messages. Under 15 U.S.C. 7704, commercial email needs accurate routing information, appropriate identification, a valid postal address, and a functioning opt-out honored within the statutory period. Suppress unsubscribed recipients from promotional flows while retaining necessary order service communications with correctly limited content. The creative contractor prepares first-purchase education and replenishment messages only where product usage supports them. The bookkeeping contractor then reconciles three controlled orders across storefront, payment processor, warehouse, and accounting records, including fees and one refund. Require zero unexplained order-level differences before publishing acquisition campaigns. Test orders remain excluded from customer conversion and contribution reports. Finish the week with a recorded handoff showing how either full-time operator can pause ads, disable a SKU, and locate a delayed shipment.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Create a controlled order
   - Place a clearly tagged test purchase using the storefront's supported testing method.
   - Record order identifier, expected tax, shipping charge, inventory allocation, and payment state; exclude the transaction from live performance cohorts.
2. Replay the fulfillment event
   - Submit the same authorized test event twice through the integration test path.
   - Confirm one warehouse order and one inventory allocation; retain the idempotency record and both processing outcomes.
3. Exercise cancellation and recovery
   - Cancel a separate unshipped test order and simulate a delayed-carrier exception.
   - Verify warehouse cancellation acknowledgment, inventory restoration, assigned customer follow-up, and any required shipping-delay notice.
4. Close the transaction ledger
   - Trace a refund through processor reporting and the accounting destination.
   - Explain fees, timing differences, and inventory movements; obtain both full-time operators' sign-off before enabling paid acquisition.

## 3. Days 15–21: Buy Measured Evidence Without Buying Uncontrolled Losses  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Days 15–17 release a small acquisition experiment only after stock is available and acceptance tests pass. The commercial lead selects one paid channel, either Google Shopping for existing search demand or Meta for demonstrable visual differentiation, rather than splitting a small budget across both. Start at $100 daily for three days, with the full sprint's paid-media commitment capped at $3,000. The creative contractor supplies two materially different messages for the same qualified audience and landing page; changing audience, offer, and page simultaneously makes diagnosis ambiguous. Tag campaigns consistently and join acquisition source to order identifiers in the storefront export. Platform-reported return on ad spend remains a diagnostic, not recognized revenue or profit. Reconcile attributed purchases against paid customer orders and separate existing customers from new customers. Calculate acquisition cost as channel spend divided by attributable new customers, reporting attribution limitations explicitly. If there are no customers, report spend and zero conversions rather than fabricating a cost ratio. Pause a creative after $150 without a purchase, inspect the funnel, and resume only with a documented correction. This is a spend-control rule, not statistical proof that the product cannot sell.

Day 18 replaces attractive dashboard ratios with order-level contribution. In the illustrative unit model, $110 of merchandise charges less $10 of expected refunds produces $100 of net retail sales, excluding sales tax. Subtract $40 landed product cost, $12 outbound fulfillment and shipping, $3 payment fees, and $3 return handling to produce $42 of contribution before acquisition, called CM1. Subtract $25 acquisition cost to produce $17 CM2, or 17% of net sales. Return handling is a separate expense, not a second deduction of the refunded selling price. Replace estimates with actual cohort results as returns mature, including inventory recovery and nonrecoverable fees. CM2 still excludes fixed payroll, software, insurance, and other overhead; it is not owner profit. At the canonical Base revenue of $1,125,000, a hypothetical 17% blended CM2 would yield $191,250 before fixed expenses. Reaching the canonical $129,375 owner profit would then leave only $61,875 for those expenses, a tight constraint that must be tested against actual staffing. If fixed costs exceed it, the model needs better blended contribution, not relabeling of payroll. The Scaled 18% net margin likewise requires contribution above 18% whenever fixed costs remain positive.

Days 19–21 test whether automation reduces work without concealing service debt. Inspect every late dispatch, stock discrepancy, refund request, and delivery exception daily. Measure dispatch performance against the published promise using orders whose dispatch deadline has elapsed; exclude neither difficult destinations nor cancelled warehouse mistakes merely to improve the rate. Track order accuracy from verified picks and customer reports, and keep the denominator visible. P15 can test post-purchase education and a relevant accessory offer, but projected repeat purchases never rescue a losing first-order acquisition case. Measure repeat contribution only after the repeat order settles and its return exposure is provisioned. Limit the launch queue to five open noncritical exceptions per full-time operator and require an initial response within one business day. If unresolved work exceeds that capacity for two consecutive days, pause acquisition expansion and repair the dominant failure. Stop paid traffic immediately when cumulative observed CM2 loss, including refund provisions, reaches $750. This acquisition-loss limit sits inside, not above, the $3,000 media envelope. Neither limit replaces the $35,000 total cash ceiling, and favorable processor settlements do not authorize automatic replenishment spending.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Four Ways Experienced Operators Preserve the Experiment**
> 
> Protect the quality of the evidence before trying to improve the reported conversion rate.
> - Ask the 3PL for parcel-level billed dimensions; one oversized carton can destroy the margin that an average shipping quote suggests.
> - Keep a separate SKU ledger for returns awaiting inspection so damaged goods cannot silently reappear as available inventory.
> - Use an order-level acquisition export alongside platform attribution; duplicate purchase events often look like sudden marketing improvement.
> - Negotiate staged supplier releases with written cancellation terms before seeking a lower unit price tied to an oversized opening order.

## 4. Days 22–30 and Day 60/90: Separate Kill, Fix, and Scale Decisions  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Days 22–30 turn the launch into a repeatable decision packet. A mature order has passed the disclosed return window plus seven days, with a minimum observation period of thirty days after delivery; maintain additional reserves for later disputes and warranty exposure. The thresholds below are operating choices for this experiment, not scorecard forecasts. A fresh store will usually lack mature orders on Day 30, and the candidate's two-to-six-month first-deal horizon makes a pre-revenue checkpoint entirely plausible. Classify that condition as Fix: continue bounded validation or wait for evidence, without calling missing data product failure. For each checkpoint, examine the latest ninety days of mature orders and require at least 100 at Day 30, 200 at Day 60, or 300 at Day 90 before granting Scale. Require at least fifty mature orders within any SKU and acquisition segment proposed for expansion. Pool only operationally comparable orders, and disclose concentration in a single product or campaign. These counts are minimum evidence floors, not statistical certainty. Add a stress calculation with acquisition cost increased 20% and net sales reduced 5%, holding other unit costs constant; stressed CM2 must remain positive.

Apply decisions in a fixed precedence order. First, Kill means stop the affected SKU, supplier, campaign, or automation path immediately for missing required safety evidence, revoked authorization, an unresolved serious safety signal, or a committed-cash breach. Assess reporting and customer remediation separately; stopping ads does not resolve an existing product hazard. Next, once the evidence floor is met, kill an economic test when mature CM2 is zero or negative after two documented corrective experiments, each observed through cohort maturity. Before those experiments mature, Fix applies and spending remains paused or separately bounded. Scale requires mature CM2 of at least 8%, 12%, and 15% at Days 30, 60, and 90 respectively, on-time dispatch of at least 98%, verified order accuracy of at least 99%, unit return rate no greater than 8%, and positive stressed CM2. Use the same mature cohort for return rates and contribution; report counts beside percentages. All other outcomes are Fix with one owner, one specific hypothesis, and a seven-day implementation deadline. Reaching that deadline proves execution only; it does not accelerate the return window. Stop the whole venture only when the constraint is systemic or remaining capital cannot fund a credible bounded repair.

Scale releases controlled increments, not a new annual revenue claim. Require unrestricted cash to cover at least ninety days of committed fixed cash costs plus separately reserved taxes, refunds, and supplier payables. This reserve test is stricter than the initial $25,000 buffer and may block expansion until additional funding or retained contribution exists. With all tests passed, increase paid spend no more than 20% per seven days and order no more than six weeks of forecast demand, adjusted for actual lead time. Obtain a new dated cash budget for Days 31–60 and 61–90; the original sprint ceiling never silently renews. Keep the two-full-time, three-contractor pod until sustained workload and contribution fund another seat. The canonical Scaled configuration is three full-time people plus four contractors, seven people total, not an unsupported five-person staffing promise. Preserve the annual scorecard exactly: $250,000 net-of-refund retail revenue at 5% produces $12,500 owner profit; $1,125,000 at 11.5% produces $129,375; $2,000,000 at 18% produces $360,000. These are complete annual scenarios after goods, fulfillment, advertising, and operating costs, not thirty-day run rates. Archive the Day 30 packet with signed decisions, evidence links, unresolved assumptions, and the next checkpoint date.
