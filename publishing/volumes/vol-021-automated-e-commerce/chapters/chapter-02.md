# Chapter 02: Opening Supplier Accounts: High-Ticket Brands and Amazon Wholesale Distributors

*US supplier research and outreach, approval tactics and dealer applications, handling suppliers with no price lists, distributor vs. brand-direct buying, finding authorized distributors, live distributor calls, and getting past "we don't sell to Amazon sellers"*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one playbook: register a real business, find brands through search strings, retailer catalogs and distributor rosters, then win accounts with persistent, relationship-first outreach. Ecommerce Paradise reports 30-50% acceptance when applying to 10-20 brands at once, and a day-8 phone call converting at 3x the rate of a second email. Marcus Lam sets a $500 profit floor per sale against a $100-200 cost to get each sale, while Daniel Boufford pegs distributor markups at 20-50%. Pedro Bermudez and Sertac Altun filter for small, under-served brands: under 50 people, or under $200K a month. The fault lines are sharp. Thomas Sleeth promises brands he will never list on Amazon, while Brad Sherman tells them on purpose. David Scheuer's coach starts with distributors, while Pedro calls them crowded.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every high-ticket operator on the roster lands on the same baseline: buy from domestic, authorized brand suppliers, not overseas catalogs. Ecommerce Paradise contrasts China shipping of four to six weeks, no dealer or warranty support, and thin margins against US suppliers that ship in one to five days, with custom or back-ordered pieces stretching to two to six weeks. He pegs high-ticket goods at $500 to $10,000+ carrying 10 to 30 percent margins [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:03:23]. Marcus Lam says his old low-ticket stack of AliExpress, CJ, AutoDS and Facebook ads bled money to chargebacks and refunds. He says the authorized-dealer model works because the brand lets you list its products, you email the supplier once a sale lands, and they ship for you [r2ydHddjtIw @ 00:16:19]. Thomas Sleeth scopes his approvals the same way: domestic, reputable brand suppliers only, never Chinese ones [HMXZkRAYR5Y @ 00:00:29]. The money in a single ticket is the draw. Lam reports some of his best orders netting $3,000, $4,000, even $5,000 profit. He sets a floor of at least $500 profit per sale because getting a single sale costs him $100 to $200 [g_OL9gG7q_I @ 00:02:01]. That floor shapes every supplier decision that follows.

The second point of agreement is that suppliers screen for a real business before they open an account. Ecommerce Paradise forms the LLC in South Dakota rather than Wyoming, which he says makes seller permits hard to get. He then stacks an EIN and a multi-state seller permit, noting that economic nexus sits around $100k per state [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:14:13]. Pedro Bermudez says you need an LLC, a reseller permit and a domain email before reaching out, and he attaches the reseller certificate to the first message [nm9aWWMYnEg @ 00:06:29]. Marcus Lam lists a resale certificate and tax ID as the entry ticket for Wayfair-sourced brands, and refuses to leave a card on file for a prepaid account [r2ydHddjtIw @ 00:17:59]. William Rivera puts the cost floor in plain numbers: a Georgia LLC runs about $200, and the reseller permit is free in that state [KImeudrcJTU @ 00:03:48]. The high-ticket and Amazon wholesale camps follow the same sequence. Entity, tax registration, resale certificate and a professional domain front are in place before the first call, email or dealer application goes out. That way the supplier sees a functioning retailer on the other end of the conversation and has fewer reasons to stall or decline the request.

The third consensus point is that the supplier list is built from search strings and competitor footprints, not bought. Marcus Lam searches '[brand] become a dealer', 'distributor application' or 'wholesale program', or goes to trade shows [r2ydHddjtIw @ 00:16:19]. On the Amazon side, David Scheuer's coach Asher runs '[brand] distributor' and '[brand] wholesale', skipping the sponsored results [HqGwAnGaUf8 @ 00:04:43]. Thomas of Amazon Wholesale uses the same '[brand] wholesale' search and adds 'Distributors' when the first pass fails [48JkrcvKaVs @ 00:00:14]. Marvin works the brand's own footer, hunting for Resellers, Contact Us or FAQ pages and searching them for 'wholesale' or 'distributor'. Funko, for example, sells direct only to sellers with a brick-and-mortar store or an active selling site and a 50k calendar-year minimum. Otherwise it points buyers to Alliance Entertainment, Diamond Comic Distributors and E distribution [fHLUiiiopmw @ 00:03:09]. The shared rule is that the brand usually tells you who is authorized. A dealer page, a footer link, a wholesale program page or a published distributor roster is the starting point. Each name found becomes a lead to call, email or apply to directly, instead of guessing at the supply chain.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Marcus Lam's high-ticket sourcing loop starts on retailer catalogs. He pulls the brands BBQGuys carries, then searches '[brand] dealer program', using Napoleon as the example. On Wayfair he filters to $4,000+ items, reasoning that every Wayfair brand already drop-ships. When a brand has no dealer page, he drops its phone number into a sheet and calls, as he did with Just Right [g_OL9gG7q_I @ 00:04:24]. Trevor Zheng runs Google Shopping's brand filter on a category like fireplaces, logs every brand in a spreadsheet and checks the Contact Us page. He then uses Apollo's free plan or LinkedIn to reach a head of e-commerce or a VP [v64Z-O_YyTY @ 00:03:18]. His four filters are branded demand checked in Ubersuggest, MAP, a high price point, and 5 to 10+ sellers per listing. He confirms MAP by seeing whether sellers cluster at one price, such as $2,255 [v64Z-O_YyTY @ 00:09:37]. Ecommerce Paradise then vets the survivors. He wants a margin of at least 10 percent and ideally 30+, US warehouses and a MAP policy. His red flags are minimum orders of $5,000+, drop-ship fees over $15 per order when $5 to $10 is normal, and no other retailers visible online [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:09:24].

On the Amazon wholesale side, the numbers get more granular. Pedro Bermudez runs a 30-second filter on each brand: is it in retail, is the Amazon listing sloppy, does the site carry wholesale language, and is the company under 50 people, which he calls 'really your zone' [nm9aWWMYnEg @ 00:05:23]. His Helium 10 Black Box settings are 3+ sellers, $5k+ revenue, 10 to 200 reviews and exactly one image. Those settings surfaced a Loctite product moving 300+ units a month [nm9aWWMYnEg @ 00:10:32]. Sertac Altun inverts the search in Smart Scout. His filters are Sports & Outdoors, max $200K a month, an average of 3 sellers, a US dominant seller, and an Amazon in-stock rate under 50 percent, with private label and agency excluded. That returned 157 brands [B-HQodfgIG4 @ 00:04:24]. William Rivera goes local with Google Maps searches for '[Amazon category] distributors'. He expects about 80 percent of results to be a poor fit, and points to PJ Distributors, which carries Dove and Pringles, as a hit [KImeudrcJTU @ 00:02:06]. The common output is a short list of under-served brands and suppliers worth calling. It favors listings with room for another seller over a giant spreadsheet of every product on the platform.

Outreach is where the scripts get specific. Brad Sherman's seven-step call opens with name, company, location and 'opening a wholesale account', then confirms 'am I speaking with the right person' and uses tactical empathy. Next it frames the business as an 'e-commerce company... stock and ship' to head off the drop-shipping objection before it surfaces [G9hSjTySxP0 @ 00:10:57]. He then states 'we do sell on Amazon' and, without pausing, asks the buyer about their own e-commerce strategy. Ecommerce Paradise automates the chase in GMass: follow-up on day 1, then day 4, a phone call on day 8, LinkedIn on day 12 and a final email on day 18. He reports that the day-8 call converts at 3x the rate of a second email [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:12:28]. Marvin builds a list of about 50 suppliers, emails all of them, then phones after a few days to a week, since calls get him the best response rate [fHLUiiiopmw @ 00:18:51]. Once an account opens, Daniel Boufford places a break-even $2,000 to $5,000 order to prove he is real, because about two-thirds of new accounts never order. He then uses volume, such as a pallet, to win $1 off per unit [jYFh4ucNZuw @ 00:01:17].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fork is how operators define a deal worth chasing. Trevor of Ecommerce Paradise sets the floor as a percentage: a supplier must clear at least 10 percent margin, ideally 30 percent or more, and anything under 10 to 15 percent gets skipped [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:08:03]. Marcus Lam sets his floor in dollars instead, a hard $500 profit per sale, because getting that sale costs him $100 to $200, and he points to single orders that netted $3,000, $4,000, even $5,000 in profit [g_OL9gG7q_I @ 00:01:43]. Sertac Altun runs much thinner unit economics on Amazon wholesale brand direct: a $300 cost item clearing $114 per unit, where ten units a month adds roughly $1,100, and a 20 percent share of a $146K-per-month brand lands around $28K revenue and $3K to $5K profit [B-HQodfgIG4 @ 00:08:55]. Joe and Mike of Build Assets Online add a filter on top: if you are not making sales yet, skip quote-only suppliers with no price list and pick suppliers with price lists and MAP, because roughly one sale comes out of every twenty quotes and a solo operator should not take that on [ocjVuvj0QJg @ 00:04:14]. The split is clean: high-ticket dropshippers chase fat dollar margin per order, while wholesale sellers accept smaller per-unit profit and scale through units and share.

The loudest split is whether to say the word Amazon on first contact. Marvin tells students to call themselves a retailer, never an Amazon seller, and says naming a specific product in outreach would separate you from about 99 percent of the pack [fHLUiiiopmw @ 00:24:00]. Coach Asher on the I Am David Scheuer channel agrees on staying quiet: do not mention Amazon at the start of the conversation [HqGwAnGaUf8 @ 00:08:45]. Brad Sherman of OperationAMZ deliberately goes the other way. His seven-step call opens with name, company, location, and 'opening a wholesale account,' checks that he has the right person, and uses tactical empathy with 'I'm sure you get a lot of calls.' Step six is pure honesty, 'we do sell on Amazon,' followed without a pause by the diversion question 'Can you tell me more about your e-commerce strategy?' He calls the 'not taking Amazon sellers' line 'smoke and mirrors' [G9hSjTySxP0 @ 00:14:32]. Thomas Sleeth, working high-ticket dropshipping rather than FBA, removes the question entirely: he promises suppliers he will sell only on his own site, not Amazon or eBay, and stacks that with paying upfront and owning support, disputes, refunds, and chargebacks so the brand has even less reason to say no [HMXZkRAYR5Y @ 00:06:40].

On distributors versus brand direct, the speakers split three ways. Pedro Bermudez calls distributor accounts crowded, with '11 sellers, same product,' and pushes toward small brands instead, using company size as his filter: 'Under 50 people is really your zone.' His 30-second filter also asks whether the brand is in retail, whether its Amazon listing is sloppy, and whether its site uses wholesale language [nm9aWWMYnEg @ 00:00:24]. Daniel Boufford sits in the middle. Distributor accounts are 'actually a lower barrier to entry,' but distributors mark up 20 to 50 percent, so profit depends on negotiating price down; he still prefers brand direct long term for better pricing and authorization, even though brands may expect content, ads, and SEO work from you [jYFh4ucNZuw @ 00:03:58]. MAP is the second fault line. Trevor Zheng ranks a MAP policy among his four supplier criteria, verifies it by checking whether sellers cluster at one price such as $2,255, reports anyone undercutting it, and wants competition of 5 to 10-plus sellers per listing; he tells first-timers to call MAP brands first and move fast [v64Z-O_YyTY @ 00:09:37]. William Rivera scripts the MAP question into his calls too, but says most suppliers he reaches do not have a MAP policy at all [KImeudrcJTU @ 00:05:07].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The fastest way to lose money is to pay a supplier that does not exist. Marvin walked through a fake distributor whose facility photo actually belonged to a gym, whose Google Maps pin showed an unfinished building, and whose site had been live for about a month [fHLUiiiopmw @ 00:16:57]. His vetting order runs name brands you can confirm on Amazon, the About Us page, the address on Google Maps, social media, Trustpilot and BBB, whether the outfit is retail, liquidation, or returns, and whether it is US-based, with vetting continuing after the first orders. Pedro Bermudez flags a second trap aimed at beginners: pre-made lists of Amazon products are a scam, and if the brand already sells well on Amazon itself, walk away [nm9aWWMYnEg @ 00:06:03]. Third-Party Profits uses age as a legitimacy proxy, filtering for 20 to 50 and 50-plus years in business because older companies are more likely to be authorized, and treats retail partners like Rite Aid, Target, and Family Dollar as the mark of a qualified distributor [1lcG6ji-5ls @ 00:01:18]. Ecommerce Paradise adds dropship red flags: minimums of $5,000 or more, drop-ship fees above $15 per order when $5 to $10 is normal, and no other retailers visible online [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:09:24].

Distributor accounts carry their own economics trap. Daniel Boufford says about two-thirds of new accounts never place an order, so he places a break-even order of $2,000 to $5,000 to prove he is real, then uses volume to pull price down, such as a pallet buy for $1 off per unit. He also warns that Amazon may reject distributor invoices, which puts account health at risk, and that some brands only sell through distributors [jYFh4ucNZuw @ 00:01:46]. Strictly Commerce showed real distributor onboarding on a live call: a customer setup packet goes to credit, the minimum is about $3,500, Joe's catalog order came to roughly $7K, the catalog updates every Monday, expiration dates run six to nine months out, and credit approval takes 24 to 36 hours [fOjBryou0Do @ 00:01:45]. Marvin's fix for thin distributor lists is to run them through Scan Unlimited and negotiate specifically on items sitting near break-even [fHLUiiiopmw @ 00:04:05]. Brad Sherman warns against burning leverage on easy accounts: brands that say yes immediately are often unprofitable, so do not pitch exclusivity there, just get the price list, and save the exclusive push for the middle, unsure brands that become the best accounts [G9hSjTySxP0 @ 00:24:44].

On the dropshipping side, the failures come from the model and the paperwork. Marcus Lam's earlier low-ticket run with AliExpress, CJ, AutoDS, and Facebook ads lost money to chargebacks and refunds, which pushed him to authorized-dealer high-ticket products and Google ads instead of Facebook [r2ydHddjtIw @ 00:05:01]. Build Assets Online once hid the add-to-cart button and ran 'call for price' on quote-only items; they describe it as possibly breaking Google's rules and say it was not worth it. Google Shopping will not run without a price, so their workaround is a ballpark price or text ads pointing to a get-a-quote lead form [ocjVuvj0QJg @ 00:03:03]. Ecommerce Paradise reports that most suppliers need two to four touches before they respond and want to see a real site before signing you up. His cadence is GMass emails on day 1 and day 4, a phone call on day 8, LinkedIn on day 12, and a final email on day 18, with the day-8 call converting at three times the rate of a second email. He applies to 10 to 20 brands at once expecting 30 to 50 percent acceptance, and forms the LLC in South Dakota rather than Wyoming, which is hard for seller permits [hHXf1_WdANY @ 00:12:15].
