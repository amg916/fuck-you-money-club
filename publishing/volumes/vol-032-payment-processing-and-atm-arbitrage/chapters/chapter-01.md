# Chapter 01: How Card Processing Works: Players, Payment Flow, and Debit Regulation

*Banks, ISOs, ISVs, and agents; authorization and settlement flow; ISO 20022 straight-through processing; merchant account statuses; day-to-day merchant support; and the Durbin Amendment's debit interchange cap, repeal campaigns, and rule changes*

## Executive Summary

Merchant-services operators describe card acceptance as a fee-splitting machine: issuing banks collect interchange, Visa and Mastercard charge dues and assessments, processors and sponsoring banks move and settle funds, and ISOs hand agents the terminals, onboarding, and support so the agent only has to sell. Mario of Residual Builders runs the math: a $20,000-per-month cafe at 2.80% throws off $560 in fees, $280 goes to card costs, and an 80/20 split pays the agent $224 every month. Interchange-plus is the transparent default, while cash discounting and dual pricing reshaped the pitch after roughly 2016. On debit regulation, the Durbin cap of 21 cents plus 0.05% for banks over $10 billion split the room: James Shepherd and Transaction Expert say processors pocketed the windfall and small-ticket merchants got squeezed, while Cornerstone Advisors flags a further cut toward 14.4 cents.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus — Who Touches the Swipe and Who Gets Paid

Joe Wagner breaks the card stack into fixed positions like a football roster. Issuing banks such as Chase or Wells Fargo put the card in the customer's hand, check available funds on every swipe, and collect interchange as their cut for fronting the money; acquiring banks sit at the finish line and deposit funds into the merchant's account minus fees. Visa, Mastercard, Discover, and American Express do not issue cards or hold money, they run the network and charge dues and assessments [N58l9UMrKHY @ 00:01:28]. Mario at Residual Builders draws the same map with more layers: processors like Fiserv, Elavon, and Global Payments move the data and settle, and behind every processor sits a sponsoring bank that is a member of the card networks, approves merchants, and supplies regulatory oversight [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:05:14]. The flow Mario lays out runs in five beats: customer pays, request hits the processor, the networks query the issuer, the issuer approves or declines, and approved funds settle into the merchant account while fees peel off for every party [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:06:28]. KULTURRA adds the gateway hop that routes card details to the right network before the processor talks to the customer's bank [QS1RtajZ0Yc @ 00:00:43].

Every practitioner who sells for a living agrees the agent should not become the bank. Joe Wagner says ISOs manage merchant accounts and build tailored solutions without processing payments themselves, and unless you want to be responsible for moving money around and following strict regulations, selling through an ISO is how to be successful [6dCPtuCCcvw @ 00:00:45]. In the roles breakdown, Wagner lists exactly what the ISO hands an agent: the terminals, the software, the customer service, the onboarding, and in many shops its own tech support and pricing models, which is why anyone selling merchant services is most likely working with or inside an ISO [N58l9UMrKHY @ 00:01:47]. Mario explains why so many agents choose an ISO over signing directly with a processor: the ISO is far more likely to do hand-holding, training, and support, which frees the agent to focus on marketing as the front-line sales force of the ecosystem [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:03:23]. Mario also argues the ground is shifting under the generalist. Software companies, in Mario's words, are basically payment companies in disguise, and when processing is bundled with vertical software and modern hardware, payments become the organic, natural upsell instead of the whole pitch [lubvPaxm55o @ 00:13:30].

On debit regulation, the speakers line up on the baseline numbers even when they fight over the politics. All Things Money says the Durbin Amendment, passed in 2010 inside Dodd-Frank, capped debit interchange at 0.05% of the transaction plus 21 cents and an extra penny for fraud protection, applied only to banks with $10 billion or more in assets, and required issuing banks to use at least two unaffiliated networks so merchants can choose the most cost-effective path [F87ku5Df-mA @ 00:01:01]. CenturyPayments puts the before-and-after in plain terms: banks were charging retailers an average of 44 cents per debit transaction, collecting nearly $16 billion a year, and the cap that hit in October 2011 cut that to 21 cents [Jn1CXq7YPYs @ 00:00:50]. Kevin Von Holten at Cornerstone Advisors confirms the cliff from the issuer side, saying a bank crossing $10 billion watches blended debit interchange drop from roughly 38 or 39 cents to 21 cents [FwB1KshUr5A @ 00:00:19]. James Shepherd, an agent of North American Bank Card, frames the same math from the processor's ledger: the bank used to bill the processor about 1% plus 15 to 20 cents on a check card, and Durbin forced that down to 0.05% plus 21 cents [_5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:06:21].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Mario's cafe math is the cleanest unit-economics walk-through any of these operators gives. A coffee shop owned by John runs $20,000 a month in card sales at an illustrative 2.80% blended rate, which produces $560 in monthly fees [lubvPaxm55o @ 00:03:06]. The card brands take the first bite; at an assumed 1.40% average card cost that is $280, leaving $280 in gross profit to be split between the agent and the ISO or processor partner. On an 80/20 split the agent keeps $224 a month, $2,688 a year, and $26,880 over ten years for as long as John stays open [lubvPaxm55o @ 00:04:25]. Stack it and 10 clients pay $2,240 a month, 50 pay $11,200, and 100 pay $22,400. Mario also warns that every card carries a different cost, which is exactly why the pricing model matters. On interchange-plus, the agent shows the merchant the real cost of each of the 50 different card types on the statement and adds a disclosed margin, with Mario's own example being 0.60% on top of cost [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:08:32]. James Shepherd runs the same structure at a thinner 0.25% markup and sets new clients up on nothing else [_5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:09:27].

Bryan Manning of BancardSales sets the rate benchmarks by acceptance environment. Card-present deals, where the card is swiped, dipped, or tapped including Apple Pay and Google Pay, run about 1.25% to 2%, while card-not-present deals keyed into a virtual terminal or entered on an online order form run about 2% to 3.5% because of the added bank risk [nLfsn6tUZh4 @ 00:04:44]. Manning budgets countertop terminals such as the VeriFone VX520 or Ingenico ICT220 at roughly $150 to $400, and a Clover point-of-sale build at about $1,000 depending on units and features [nLfsn6tUZh4 @ 00:02:52]. Mario lays out the menu agents pitch against those costs: interchange-plus, the older tiered model that buckets swipes into qualified, mid-qualified, and non-qualified, cash discounting released around 2016, and dual pricing, which displays both the card and cash price and became hugely popular in restaurants and retail. Tiered was the historical standard until merchants and agents moved toward transparent cost-plus [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:09:25]. Shepherd puts real numbers on the tiered and flat-rate trap for debit: merchants typically pay 1.5% to 2% plus an 18 to 30 cent transaction fee, sometimes 35 cents, so a $100 check-card sale costs roughly $2 to $3 [_5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:04:02].

The Durbin math is where agents find their sharpest pitch. Shepherd walks a $100 check-card sale: the bank used to charge the processor about $1 plus 15 to 20 cents, the cap cut that to 0.05% plus 21 cents, or 26 cents, yet nothing stopped a flat-rate processor from still billing 2%, pocketing an extra 74 cents per transaction [_5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:07:29]. Shepherd's verdict on flat-rate processors is blunt: do not trust them to hand back that windfall. Transaction Expert, quoting a 22-cent per-item fee plus 0.05% against an old 1% to 1.2%, splits merchants by ticket size: under about $10, coffee, pizza, and hot dog shops saw the fixed fee jump from 10 cents to 22 cents, roughly doubling debit cost, while tickets above about $30 got cheaper, with large-ticket merchants due 20% to 30% savings [cL-wn0WX_PA @ 00:02:53]. Walmart and Target pay interchange directly, but merchants under a million dollars a month in volume go through a processor that Durbin never forced to pass anything along [cL-wn0WX_PA @ 00:04:13]. On the issuer side, Kevin Von Holten reports the Fed moving the 21-cent figure toward 14.4 cents, a 31% hit for banks already over $10 billion and 60% to 70% for banks about to cross [FwB1KshUr5A @ 00:01:15].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Map the player stack before the pitch
   - Identify the merchant's issuer exposure, card networks, processor, sponsoring bank, and the ISO you will board through.
   - Sponsoring banks sit behind every processor, approve merchants, and provide regulatory oversight, so know who will underwrite the deal [Residual Builders — r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:05:31]
2. Pull and dissect the current statement
   - Separate true card cost from processor markup and flag qualified, mid-qualified, and non-qualified tiers.
   - On tiered or flat rate, debit often runs 1.5% to 2% plus 18 to 30 cents while the processor's own bank cost is far lower [durbinamendment — _5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:03:46]
3. Pick the pricing model and code the account right
   - Choose interchange-plus, cash discounting, or dual pricing, then confirm the SIC or MCC code and card-present status.
   - A wrong SIC or MCC code or miscategorized processing can leave merchants paying higher rates than they qualify for [BancardSales — nLfsn6tUZh4 @ 00:06:37]
4. Board through the ISO and model the residual
   - Submit the application, track underwriting, and log the expected monthly residual against your split.
   - At $20,000 monthly volume and an 80/20 split, the agent's share of $280 gross profit is $224 per month for the life of the account [Residual Builders — lubvPaxm55o @ 00:04:25]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split on the debit side is over who actually pocketed the Durbin money. CenturyPaymentsInc frames it as a clean merchant win: banks were charging retailers an average of 44 cents per debit transaction, the cap dropped that to 21 cents in October 2011, and the merchant walks away with lower bank charges and room to cut prices [Jn1CXq7YPYs @ 00:01:11]. All Things Money flips that verdict, citing George Mason University research showing small businesses saw no reduction in interchange fees and consumers saw minimal savings, while large retailers negotiated lower merchant service charges and kept the spread [F87ku5Df-mA @ 00:02:45]. The Electronic Payments Coalition goes further, claiming more than three-fourths of merchants never changed prices after implementation and 22 percent actually raised them, a six to eight billion dollar yearly 'merchant markup' [MXBPDGL9Iss @ 00:01:02]. Transaction Expert splits the difference with a ticket-size rule: if the average ticket runs about $30 or more, the check card got cheaper, and a large-ticket shop should have seen costs fall by 20 to 30 percent, while below that line the fixed per-item fee did the damage [cL-wn0WX_PA @ 00:03:09]. The operator takeaway is that the same law reads as a windfall or a tax depending on ticket size and on who sits between the merchant and the issuing bank.

On pricing, the playbooks diverge sharply. James Shepherd of Multi-Merchant Processing runs a hard line: only interchange plus for every new client, passing through whatever the bank charges plus 0.25 percent, so when Durbin cut the issuer cost those merchants automatically captured 100 percent of the drop [_5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:09:27]. Mario at Residual Builders also ran interchange plus for many years, but pitched a fatter 0.60 percent margin on top of the card cost and sold it as the most transparent model on the statement, because the merchant sees each card type, its cost, and the agent margin [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:08:48]. Joe Wagner pushes a different angle, telling agents their responsibility is to educate merchants on dual pricing or cash discounting while making sure they carry the best technology [6dCPtuCCcvw @ 00:00:45]. Mario then undercuts the cash discount pitch itself: after roughly ten years in market, most owners either already pass fees to customers or know the program and refuse it, so the novelty is gone and the industry is leaving what Mario calls phase three [lubvPaxm55o @ 00:12:21]. The practical fork is clear: interchange plus wins the transparency and Durbin pass-through argument, fee-passing programs win the total-savings argument, and the newest operators treat both as table stakes rather than the hook.

The second fork is structural: where an agent plugs in and how wide they sell. Joe Wagner is blunt that unless you want to move the money and carry strict regulations yourself, selling through an ISO is how you succeed, and they tell reps not to overcomplicate the role [6dCPtuCCcvw @ 00:01:01]. Residual Builders agrees on the ISO path for most newcomers, noting that an ISO is far more likely to provide hand-holding, training, and support than a direct payment processor would, which is why many agents choose to work under one [r78HBt5LE9U @ 00:03:23]. Where Mario parts ways with the keep-it-simple camp is positioning: the days of the generalist, and of selling savings and support, are counted, and phase four belongs to vertical software where payment processing becomes the organic natural upsell [lubvPaxm55o @ 00:14:04]. Joe Wagner's own roles breakdown lands closer to Mario than the simplicity pitch suggests, calling ISVs that build booking apps and restaurant point of sale systems with integrated payments an increasingly powerful distribution partner for agents [N58l9UMrKHY @ 00:02:44]. The real split is not whether ISOs matter but whether a bare rate pitch still opens doors, or whether software now has to carry the conversation.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The sharpest trap on the merchant side is the small-ticket squeeze. Transaction Expert says coffee shops, pizza shops, and hot dog stands with tickets under $10 'really got screwed,' because the fixed per-item fee on a check card jumped from 10 cents to 22 cents, roughly doubling the cost of every small debit swipe [cL-wn0WX_PA @ 00:02:53]. The Electronic Payments Coalition makes the same point from the opposite camp: the corner shop now pays the same capped amount on a $5 sandwich that Walmart pays on a $1,000 TV [MXBPDGL9Iss @ 00:00:47]. The second trap is the processor windfall. James Shepherd walks a $100 check card sale through it: the bank's charge to the processor fell from about one dollar to 26 cents, but a merchant on tiered or flat rate still pays the contracted two percent, so the processor pockets an extra 74 cents and carries no legal duty to pass it down [_5A2a4Vu7A8 @ 00:07:29]. BestCardProcessing reports the same pattern after implementation, with small and mid-sized merchants who are not enrolled in a specific pinpoint rate program simply not receiving the intended savings, and some processing companies unable to even offer that program [UMy8ceQ1bm4 @ 00:12:16].

On the issuer side, the failure mode is the $10 billion cliff. Cornerstone Advisors puts the drop for a bank crossing the line at a blended 38 to 39 cents down to 21 cents, and says the Fed's move to 14.4 cents means a 31 percent hit for banks already over, but a 60 to 70 percent impact for anyone approaching the threshold [FwB1KshUr5A @ 00:01:15]. They sized it for a $9.9 billion client holding 269,000 debit cards: to cross $10 billion and keep revenue flat it would need 498,000 more active cards, 188 percent growth, which they call impossible, forcing 30 different mitigation strategies while clients scramble to stay under the mark [FwB1KshUr5A @ 00:02:46]. CenturyPaymentsInc warned at launch that with such a loss in revenue, banks would most likely look for ways to make up that money somewhere else [Jn1CXq7YPYs @ 00:01:27]. The Electronic Payments Coalition says that is exactly what happened, with community banks, credit unions, and consumers carrying the burden through eliminated debit card rewards and free checking accounts [MXBPDGL9Iss @ 00:01:37]. For anyone whose economics ride on debit volume, the lesson is that capped issuer revenue flows downhill into fees and cuts elsewhere in the chain.

The back-office traps are less dramatic but cost real money. NMI warns that a past-due merchant left unresolved goes delinquent and gets locked out of their account, and if six months pass without collecting gateway fees, the balance is debited from the reseller's own affiliate account [WxBDVPgeUYI @ 00:01:43]. Bryan Manning of BancardSales flags setup errors: the wrong SIC or MCC code, or a miscategorized processing method, can leave a merchant paying higher rates than they otherwise qualify for, on top of the reality that a merchant account is effectively a short-term loan paid out before the cardholder settles their bill [nLfsn6tUZh4 @ 00:06:37]. ECS Payments says the most common support call is a variance between the merchant's settlement report and the processor's, usually an unnoticed refund, while charges from software like QuickBooks show up four to five times a week as phantom debits [NXWIjxZTNpI @ 00:07:26]. And for anyone eyeing the ATM side of this volume, Mario at Residual Builders calls ATM placement close to merchant services but heavy, expensive to buy, constantly needing cash loaded, often demanding a location profit share, with some locations that simply perform badly [lubvPaxm55o @ 00:08:22].
