## Executive Summary

Practitioners across six channels agree that residual income is a markup business. Joe Wagner's tire-shop math turns a 3% charge over 2% interchange into a $10 margin split with the ISO, multiplied across every transaction in the book. Electronic Money Company refuses leases and six-month rate hikes to protect retention, while Rich Norton's Payrock team stacks $250 to $500 upfront bonuses plus $150 lease payouts.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The baseline economics every speaker teaches is markup over cost. Joe Wagner frames it as a tire shop: buy tires at $300, sell them at $350, keep $50. In merchant services the tires are interchange, the majority of the underlying cost of a card transaction, which shifts with card type and whether the card is swiped, keyed over the phone or taken on a website. Wagner's simplified ticket is a $1,000 sale with 2% interchange ($20), billed to the merchant at 3% ($30). The $10 difference is the gross margin, and it is split between agent and ISO according to the compensation agreement [ogeyMD07VYc @ 00:01:17]. Gingergaye of Electronic Money Company describes the same engine as a split of every transaction that runs through the merchant accounts, compounding into thousands of transactions as the portfolio grows [J2MM1Nbrie8 @ 00:00:41]. Ryan Anthony of Champion Payments calls it collecting pennies on the dollar from machines placed in businesses. That income is largely passive once set up but very active while the book is being built, and it requires no capital outlay [RF6l5ilPKQ0 @ 00:00:42]. Rich Norton adds the street-level picture: every card payment a small business takes means some representative somewhere just earned a small slice of that margin [bAuRUu51g3c @ 00:02:17].

The second point of agreement is duration: the residual is meant to outlive the selling. Joe Wagner calls it true residual income. A rep can turn the phone off and stop answering emails while service departments handle the merchant, and the check keeps arriving. Wagner reports accounts that have paid every month for almost 15 years because those clients stayed happy and active [6Gf0BTz5h5E @ 00:09:17]. James Shepard of CCSalesPro defines the standard as life vested, meaning the residual is tied only to the account. With North American Bank Card and Total Merchant Services, a rep who stops selling keeps collecting as long as the accounts remain active. Shepard calls processors that stop paying when selling stops a problem, because every rep eventually stops through retirement or because something happens [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:07:19]. Rich Norton says residuals on the Payrock team belong to the rep for lifetime and beyond. Representatives there have earned six-figure income for 15 years while traveling the world, and the stream can pass to family, relatives or ministries after death [bAuRUu51g3c @ 00:02:44]. David Anthony, who has worked 23 years alongside Ginger, makes the same inheritance pitch: the stream goes on and on and can be left to the rep's children [NlqhCjnpL6c @ 00:00:20].

The third consensus is that the partner and the paperwork matter more than the percentage. Gingergaye, 25 years in the business, says the processor a rep signs with matters more to the bottom line than any percentage split. Ginger has watched talented reps get burned while others quietly built six-figure residuals by choosing carefully early. In Ginger's view, the better question than what percentage a rep gets is how long the rep gets it and who controls it, proven by monthly reporting rather than vague promises [AdPAOJ52cvk @ 00:01:36]. Joe Wagner opens from the same angle. A company can promise 70%, 80%, even 100%, but 100% of something the rep does not control and is not protected on is not the deal it looks like. Compensation says how much a rep makes; the agreement says whether the rep keeps it for life [Em1hM53SuTA @ 00:00:00]. James Shepard makes cost transparency the entry ticket and calls a processor that refuses to share the Schedule A a huge red flag [ruAqtp4NcPA @ 00:03:06]. Retention is the shared lens. Gingergaye says reps lose clients to sneaky rate hikes, hidden fees and bad tech support. When merchants are happy they stick around, so the residuals stay too, and that loyalty feeds referrals [CGOJATo9AMI @ 00:00:37].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

James Shepard's income model runs on the free calculator at instantquotetool.com under the goals tab, which is built to work for any processor. The default assumptions are 12 sales per month in year one, 7 in year two and 5 from year three on. They also assume a $450 average upfront bonus based on half leases and half free terminals, a $70 gross margin per account, and a deliberately conservative 25% residual split for a sub-agent who gets heavy support and leads. Shepard resets the split to 50% as the current industry average [ruAqtp4NcPA @ 00:01:12]. On Shepard's own team with North American Bank Card and Total Merchant Services, the street rate is $15 to $25 in upfront money per prospecting hour. Only three activities count: meeting owners, obtaining statements and presenting cost analyses. Ten hours a week yields roughly $150 to $250, while 40 field hours plus 20 admin hours yields $600 to $1,000 a week [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:03:29]. Per sale, the average upfront is $275 with a $200 floor, paid within one to two business days of approval, plus bonuses for skipping free equipment or landing larger merchants. Residual averages about $30 per account per month and lags roughly two months behind the sale [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:06:26]. Shepard says top agents reach $3,000 to $6,000 a month after one year [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:02:41].

The Residual Builders magic-number drill reverse-engineers the book from the target down. Step one is the survival number, the monthly cost of rent or mortgage, groceries, car payments, utilities and subscriptions, illustrated at $5,000. Step two is the lifestyle number. The host knows reps who are semi-retired on $10,000 to $15,000 a month in residuals and only replace the few merchants who cancel [4z4EyZ1Rkbk @ 00:03:16]. The host pegs realistic residual at $300 to $400 per client, so a $10,000 target means 25 to 35 accounts. The host then adds 15 to 20 extra clients for safety; at $400 that means 35 plus 15, for a 50-merchant goal [4z4EyZ1Rkbk @ 00:04:50]. At a 15% close rate, treated as one in seven qualified conversations, 50 deals require 350 qualified leads. A qualified lead is a decision-maker who understands the offer and shows real intrigue, not an owner who says to send an email [4z4EyZ1Rkbk @ 00:06:45]. The 90-day sprint is 10 accounts, which means 70 leads, about 25 a month or four solid leads a week. Ryan Anthony runs a lighter version: take monthly expenses, double them, and that is the residual target. With accounts bringing $100 to $500 a month, a family with $7,000 in monthly bills needed 20 to 60 accounts [RF6l5ilPKQ0 @ 00:02:58].

Upfront structure is the other lever. Rich Norton says the Payrock program offers traditional processing, a reward pay surcharge program and a consumer choice program. It pays $250 upfront on traditional processing and $500 on the choice programs. Rentals or leases are used where they fit, with the leasing company adding $150 per signup on top. Writing two or three merchants a week can produce a couple thousand dollars a week, with installs landing in three days [bAuRUu51g3c @ 00:04:53]. Norton also warns that the first residual check might be $25, so upfront money carries year one. Gingergaye of Electronic Money Company runs the opposite stack. Dual pricing pays agents the highest residual, comparative pricing is taught so no sale walks out the door, and leases are refused because big upfront lease money comes with a very low residual percentage [Fc87Q5Igrbc @ 00:01:28]. Reps there set their own equipment prices, and free terminals, old or new, are deducted from residuals. Reps can also mark up rates, PCI fees, monthly fees and an optional annual fee, knowing that pricing too high lets another rep take the account and the residual [Fc87Q5Igrbc @ 00:02:57]. James Shepard adds that 70% to 80% splits usually come with no upfront bonus. An 80% split that does include upfront often means an inflated Schedule A, which gives the rep a big piece of a smaller pie [ruAqtp4NcPA @ 00:04:41].

1. Do the math on the target
   - Total your monthly expenses and double the figure to set your residual target.

   - Convert the target into a merchant count, a buffer, and a qualified-lead requirement.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest argument in the trade is whether the residual percentage is the number worth chasing. James Shepard of CCSalesPro sets the income calculator at a conservative 25% for a sub agent getting leads and support, calls roughly 50% the current industry average, and says 70% or 80% splits usually come with no up-front bonus [ruAqtp4NcPA @ 00:03:32]. Shepard will still take 30% or 40% if that processor hands over leads and coaching that produce far more sales. Shepard also warns that an 80% split stacked with an up-front bonus usually means the Schedule A cost was raised, a big piece of a smaller pie [ruAqtp4NcPA @ 00:04:41]. Joe Wagner pushes harder against headline numbers: a company can promise 70%, 80%, even 100%, but 100% of something you do not control and are not protected on is not the deal it looks like [Em1hM53SuTA @ 00:00:00]. Ginger Gaye of Electronic Money Company, 25 years in, says who you sign with as a processor matters more for your bottom line than any percentage split, because reps who quietly built six-figure residuals chose their processor carefully [AdPAOJ52cvk @ 00:00:20]. The working read: Shepard weighs the split against leads and Schedule A cost, Wagner weighs it against contract durability, and Gaye weighs it against how the processor treats merchants after the signature.

The second fault line is front money versus back-end residual. Rich Norton, speaking on CCSalesPro, runs Payrock programs paying 250 up front for traditional processing, 500 for the choice programs, plus 150 from the leasing company on top, and says reps writing two or three merchants a week make a couple thousand a week while residuals build [bAuRUu51g3c @ 00:04:53]. Shepard pegs the average at $275 up front per sale, paid within one to two business days of approval, against about $30 per month in residual per account [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:05:07]. Electronic Money Company takes the opposite line: it does not sell leases at all, calling lease-heavy processors quick money now and very little later because the residual percentage is very low, and it lets reps give away terminals only by deducting the cost from their own residuals [Fc87Q5Igrbc @ 00:01:10]. Joe Wagner splits the difference by treating the up-front commission as getting paid to acquire an asset that behaves like a rental property, with time as the only investment [6Gf0BTz5h5E @ 00:05:40]. Norton's camp says the up-front check is what keeps a new rep from delivering pizzas at night, while Electronic Money Company insists the real money is in the residual income.

Pricing and prospecting playbooks also diverge. Gaye says dual pricing pays agents the highest residual commission, yet some processors only allow dual pricing, which forces a rep to walk away from any merchant who refuses to pass fees to customers; Electronic Money Company also teaches comparative pricing so the sale is not lost [Fc87Q5Igrbc @ 00:00:39]. On acquisition, Gaye says cold calling trumps all other methods and many deals close at the initial presentation, with merchant approval taking only a couple of days [J2MM1Nbrie8 @ 00:01:43], while Ryan Anthony went feet on the street pulling doors across five industries, restaurants, retail, auto repair, trades and professional services, all owner-managed so the decision maker was behind the counter [RF6l5ilPKQ0 @ 00:06:04]. Anthony picked owner-managed businesses specifically to dodge the red tape and long sales cycles of chains like Chick-fil-A or McDonald's. Residual Builders pitches the reverse: a sustainable, predictable way to generate five solid leads per week without a huge time investment, hitting the pavement or cold calling endlessly, is what that channel calls running a modern payments business [4z4EyZ1Rkbk @ 00:09:18]. So the split is real: Gaye and Anthony win on doors and dials, while Residual Builders sells a lead machine built to replace them.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure on record is Joe Wagner's own: after signing with a company first, Wagner lost the entire book after three and a half years, overnight, over one sentence in a clause Wagner was not educated to look for [Em1hM53SuTA @ 00:04:20]. Wagner now hunts for production requirements, minimums or annual quotas, splits that change when production drops, and anyone saying you lose residuals because you did not produce a deal in the last 90 days, calling that a job rather than lifetime residual income [Em1hM53SuTA @ 00:01:38]. James Shepard lands on the same trap from the recruiting side: many reps sit with a processor where they stop getting paid when they stop selling, and eventually everyone stops selling, so the residual must be life vested and tied only to the account [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:07:19]. Rich Norton says the industry is full of war stories and horror stories of people losing residual income, and that the first leader Norton worked under said not to worry about residuals because there was no money in that at all [bAuRUu51g3c @ 00:02:44]. Wagner also separates losing the right to submit new applications from losing the residuals already built, and refuses a verbal we always take care of our agents without seeing it in writing.

Electronic Money Company's failure list centers on merchants walking out the door. Ginger Gaye says many processors raise rates or fees every six months with small-print notices at the bottom of the statement; reps may earn a little short term, but merchants eventually figure out they have been bamboozled and switch [AdPAOJ52cvk @ 00:04:15]. Gaye says the wrong processor erodes residuals slowly with confusing statements, behind-the-scenes pricing changes and shifting bonus programs, enough that the rep does not notice until it hurts [AdPAOJ52cvk @ 00:02:18]. Support failure lands on the rep too: when support fails you take the blame, and merchants stuck on long holds with the 1-800 ignore me number become lost accounts and lost income [HAEcBxhwNkI @ 00:02:01]. The quieter killer is the bypass: the merchant calls the 1-800 line, pricing gets lowered or products get added without the rep, the merchant gets confused and frustrated, and switches to another processor, taking the residual along [HAEcBxhwNkI @ 00:03:03]. Gaye also warns that pricing an account too high lets another salesperson take it away, and the residual leaves with them. Liquidated damages clauses, which Gaye calls scummy, are named as the tool processors use to trap merchants on high rates or lousy service.

The ramp is where new reps break. Rich Norton says the first year is the most challenging because residual income starts at zero and the first check might be $25 [bAuRUu51g3c @ 00:04:04]. Shepard says residual runs about two months behind because the merchant has to start processing, so a 10-account month adds roughly $300 and a 20-account month roughly $600 [VNId5AUIElE @ 00:06:26]; working only 10 hours a week of prospecting yields about $150 to $250 a week up front. Ryan Anthony closed two deals very part-time, forgot about them, then got a check of about $120 for the first month of residuals, and says most people trip up at choosing a vendor and filling the funnel because of fear they hype up in their minds [RF6l5ilPKQ0 @ 00:10:12]. Residual Builders adds the math check: at $400 per client, $10,000 a month needs 35 clients, so pad the goal by 15 to 20 to land at 50, and at a 15% close rate, roughly one in seven, that means 350 qualified leads, meaning decision makers who understand the offer, not owners who say send me an email [4z4EyZ1Rkbk @ 00:05:05].
