## 1. Establish the retained-revenue denominator before valuing a cohort  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Start with three ledgers: merchant or buyer transaction volume, contractual compensation before downstream participation, and revenue actually retained by the operator. Only the third ledger supplies the scorecard denominator. A merchant processing $75,000 monthly does not create $75,000 of ISO revenue; an ATM loaded with $5,000 does not generate $5,000 of sales. For this illustrative bridge, Conservative contractual commissions total $120,000 with retained_share of 75%, while Base commissions total $556,250 with retained_share of 80%, and Scaled commissions total $1,000,000 with retained_share of 80%. Partner allocations therefore remove $30,000, $111,250, and $200,000 before the canonical revenue lines of $90,000, $445,000, and $800,000. These assumed commission amounts explain the supplied endpoints; they are not additional scorecard observations. Apply ASC 606 principal-versus-agent analysis to each specified service rather than assuming every cash collection belongs on a gross revenue line. Document who controls the service before transfer, who sets compensation, and who bears performance obligations. If a contract requires different presentation, maintain a reconciliation to the scorecard's retained economic basis instead of changing its profit denominator. Keep underlying card volume available for yield analysis, but never add it to operator revenue.

Build the Base cohort from four independently auditable retained-income streams. First, 175 active merchants producing $150 monthly residual each contribute $315,000 annually. Second, 100 gateway subscriptions retaining $50 monthly contribute $60,000. Third, ten venue ATMs each complete 1,000 annual surcharge-bearing withdrawals; a $3.50 surcharge less $1.30 of contractual venue and processing participation retains $2.20 per withdrawal, or $22,000 annually. Fourth, the commercial VCC example below contributes $48,000. Together these equal exactly $445,000 before operating deductions. Gateway subscriptions may belong to the same merchants, so do not count them as another 100 acquired merchant relationships. Each stream already reflects its applicable upstream or partner allocation; never deduct the illustrative 20% aggregate participation again. Map Schedule A authorization fees, monthly minimums, PCI program charges, gateway costs, and residual eligibility to statement fields. IRIS CRM should carry merchant identifiers, effective pricing dates, residual ownership, and rep participation alongside the signed agreement. Compare predicted residuals with processor statements monthly, preserving both transaction counts and dollar bases. A pricing change that lifts merchant charges without lifting retained residuals is not a successful margin expansion for the ISO owner.

Keep the twelve-play portfolio reference separate from the mechanics actually quantified here: the supplied named play is b2b_commercial_vcc_interchange_rebate_arbitrage. Its proposed 140–195 basis-point commercial rebate range is a contract-dependent economic input, not an automatic entitlement to network interchange. Illustrate $10 million of enrolled annual AP invoices with 40% vendor card acceptance, leaving $4 million of qualifying VCC settlements. At 160 basis points, program compensation is $64,000; a 75% operator participation retains $48,000 before delivery expenses. At 140 and 195 basis points, that same accepted volume retains $42,000 and $58,500 respectively. Buyer invoices remain third-party volume, not operator sales. Automatically inject validated Level-3 invoice and line-item fields when supported by the issuer and processor; metadata alone cannot ensure a particular qualification or rebate. For card-averse suppliers, offer a separately accepted 1.5%–2% early-pay ACH discount against agreed ten-day payment terms, with the discount beneficiary explicitly contracted. A $10,000 invoice discount produces $150–$200 of supplier concession, not necessarily operator compensation. Focus on fragmented long-tail vendors whose acceptance can be documented, and revert declined invoices to authorized ACH without counting them toward VCC yield.

## 3. Finance payout delays without borrowing merchant or vault funds  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Model collection timing from the economic earning date, not the date a salesperson expects payment. Assume a 45-day effective DSO measured from earned residual revenue, incorporating statement preparation and the scheduled payout interval. Add a separate 15-day stress lock only when it is incremental to that 45-day assumption. Using twelve thirty-day months, daily revenue equals $250 Conservative, $1,236.11 Base, and $2,222.22 Scaled. The 45-day receivable balances are therefore $11,250, $55,625, and $100,000; the extra lock adds $3,750, $18,541.67, and $33,333.33. Sixty-day total receivables become $15,000, $74,166.67, and $133,333.33. These are accounting exposures, not automatically the cash required to survive the wait. A residual already accrued but subject to a contractual setoff may be less financeable than an ordinary trade invoice. Review assignment restrictions, termination rights, personal recourse, and sponsor cross-collateralization before pledging receivables. Keep an aging schedule by processor, merchant cohort, and compensation type. Commercial VCC rebates may follow quarterly qualification cycles while ATM surcharges settle more frequently, so a blended DSO is a planning shortcut that must reconcile to the actual dated receipt schedule before committing acquisition spending.

Calculate the operating bridge from cash outflows rather than financing the entire receivable balance by reflex. Excluding expected loss allowances, annual cash operating expenses are $57,000 Conservative, $233,625 Base, and $328,000 Scaled. Assuming even spending, sixty days requires $9,500, $38,937.50, and $54,666.67. Add three months of modeled scrub losses as an accessible liquidity buffer: $1,500, $5,562.50, and $8,000. The resulting minimum planning balances are $11,000, $44,500, and $62,666.67 before equipment purchases, taxes, debt service, unusual settlements, or vault currency. These buffers are cash earmarks, not additional profit deductions. If a processor requires a separate restricted reserve, add the incremental restriction unless the contract permits the same dollars to satisfy both needs. The $8,000–$45,000 startup range can support an asset-light entry, but its upper end barely covers the Base operating bridge and does not fund Scaled liquidity. Front-loaded CAC invalidates the even-spending shortcut: paying the full $60,000 Base acquisition budget immediately requires a dated cash forecast rather than another annual average. Release owner distributions only after the forecast includes payroll commitments, contractor invoices, processor offsets, and scheduled cash-loading requirements.

Treat ATM vault currency as a separate circulating asset with its own replenishment calendar. Ten machines carrying $4,000 each tie up $40,000; a 25% replenishment buffer adds $10,000, creating a $50,000 currency requirement outside the operating bridge. At an illustrative 12% annual funding rate, financing that amount costs $6,000 annually, before delivery, insurance, and shrinkage. The canonical scenarios assume no separate incremental financing charge; adding this facility to Base without offsetting savings reduces owner profit from $189,125 to $183,125. Provider-owned vault cash reduces operator currency ownership but replaces it with contracted service charges and reconciliation obligations. Never use merchant settlement balances as a substitute facility. Configure remote telemetry around cash remaining, withdrawal velocity, communications failure, and weekend replenishment lead time; surcharge revenue disappears when a popular machine runs empty. Under 12 CFR 1005.16, an ATM operator charging a fee must provide the required notice before the consumer is committed and allow continuation only after the consumer elects to proceed. Record terminal disclosure configuration and reconciliation exceptions together, because a profitable withdrawal forecast is unusable when fees cannot be collected under the applicable operating requirements.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Construct the dated receivable ledger
   - Import processor statements and rebate schedules into a cohort-level earning and collection calendar.
   - Record earning date, statement date, contractual payout date, actual receipt, contractual offsets, and the counterparty controlling each hold.
2. Separate operating and custodial balances
   - Reconcile operator cash, restricted reserves, merchant settlement references, and ATM vault currency independently.
   - Use separate ledger accounts and appropriate bank arrangements; do not treat merchant funds or physical currency inventory as distributable profit.
3. Forecast thirteen weeks of cash movements
   - Schedule contractor invoices, acquisition payments, equipment purchases, currency replenishment, and expected residual receipts by week.
   - Run the ordinary collection schedule and an additional fifteen-day payout lock, then identify the lowest unrestricted operating balance.
4. Set acquisition and distribution limits
   - Fund the next merchant cohort only after preserving the operating bridge and all separate restricted requirements.
   - Document available facilities, borrowing costs, concentration limits, and the action required when a processor misses its expected payout date.

## 4. Stress contribution, concentration, and surcharge mechanics before scaling  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Stress the same canonical revenue base instead of constructing a more flattering replacement forecast. A 10% revenue loss with cash cost savings equal to 20% of lost revenue reduces profit by 8% of original revenue. Conservative therefore falls to $19,800, Base to $153,525, and Scaled to $376,000. An independent two-percentage-point increase in scrub losses costs $1,800, $8,900, and $16,000, leaving $25,200, $180,225, and $424,000. A separate thirty-day collection extension creates additional receivables of $7,500, $37,083.33, and $66,666.67. Financing those balances continuously at 12% annually costs $900, $4,450, and $8,000; the expense does not equal the receivable principal. Combining all three static shocks against original revenue produces profits of $17,100, $140,175, and $352,000. This additive convention is deliberately explicit; a dynamic forecast should recalculate losses and receivables after attrition instead. Do not assume full labor savings when a merchant leaves, because contractor retainers and employee payroll may persist. Use concentration reports to identify whether one processor, venue chain, or AP buyer can create several shocks simultaneously through a termination, reserve increase, or delayed qualification decision.

Test price compression at the merchant and supplier level. A $20 monthly residual concession across the illustrative 175-merchant Base cohort removes $42,000 of annual contribution before any cost response, reducing owner profit to $147,125. Avoid matching a competitor's headline rate until interchange categories, authorization counts, gateway charges, and monthly minimums reconcile to a comparable retained yield. In the VCC cohort, acceptance falling from 40% to 25% of $10 million enrolled invoices lowers retained revenue from $48,000 to $30,000 at the same 160-basis-point rebate and 75% participation. Base owner profit then becomes $171,125 with unchanged costs. Level-3 data can support correct qualification, but cannot compel supplier acceptance or eliminate issuer program exclusions. ACH fallback should preserve payment execution and buyer retention without booking an unearned rebate. High-risk MID orchestration means sponsor-approved routing for accurately disclosed businesses, not hiding merchant identity, product categories, or chargebacks. If underwriting declines a category, stop that proposed routing and seek a sponsor explicitly willing to underwrite it. Monitor chargebacks and MATCH-related contract provisions before acquisition incentives turn rejected or unstable merchants into expensive onboarding work with no durable residual.

Translate surcharge requirements into pricing logic and test transactions, not decorative policy text. Visa and Mastercard rules distinguish credit-card surcharging from debit and prepaid acceptance; a debit card does not become surcharge-eligible merely because it is processed without a PIN. Regulation II, including 12 CFR 235.3 and 235.7, governs covered debit interchange and routing restrictions; it does not establish the commercial credit-card rebate assumed in the VCC example. New York General Business Law Section 518 imposes surcharge price-display and amount requirements, while other states require separate location-specific review. Configure genuine dual pricing consistently across advertised prices, checkout, receipts, and merchant agreements rather than relabeling an added card fee after the sale. Sponsor-bank underwriting requirements and the business definitions in 31 CFR 1010.100 require activity-specific analysis; a limited withdrawal ATM operation and a broader funds-transfer service should not receive identical assumptions. Before expansion, reconcile retained revenue, demonstrate positive stressed contribution, verify payout access, and fund separate currency needs. If any condition fails, pause that cohort's acquisition spending while preserving existing merchant support and ordinary payment execution. The profitable operator protects both unit contribution and the timing of usable cash.
