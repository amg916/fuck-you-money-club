## The Practitioner Consensus: Interchange Is the Real Margin Pool

Every practitioner on this beat starts from the same arithmetic: the processor markup is the small number, and interchange is the big one. Sean Jones of Revolution Payments puts interchange at 75 to 90 percent of total card fees and stresses that this money flows back to the issuing bank, not the processor, across more than 400 distinct rates that shift by card type, processing method, ticket size and industry [vVvGM8Rlor8 @ 00:00:15]. Jeremy Layton of VeraSave lands in the same range, saying 80-plus percent of a merchant's fees are interchange, which is exactly why his shop aims at interchange rather than processor margin [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:07:56]. Jones adds the uncomfortable reason most merchants never hear about Level 3: processors earn nothing from interchange, and plenty of reps lack the knowledge because no certification is required to enter the field [QBl05xxHst0 @ 00:01:27]. James Sheppard of CCSalesPro frames the operator payoff bluntly. Facing a merchant who wanted lower costs, the choice was either to cut residuals and lose margin by lowering the pricing, or to find a way to cut the interchange costs, which cost the ISO nothing [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:33:36]. The consensus play is to attack the interchange category each transaction lands in while leaving the markup intact, so the merchant saves real money and the residual survives the conversation.

The second point of agreement is who actually benefits. TouchSuite is explicit that only business, corporate, purchasing and government cards gain from enhanced data, and that consumer cards generally do not, so the opportunity has to be sized against business-card volume alone [60QTYm0QFZU @ 00:00:22]. Roger McNamara, appearing on CCSalesPro, sharpens the filter further: about 30 percent of B2B transactions still do not qualify, because cards carrying personal liability are excluded from the commercial programs [tG2nIK3Th4Q @ 00:10:18]. The baseline prerequisite is the right pipe. Jones warns that credit card terminals do not support Level 3 line-item detail, and that a merchant needs a Level 3-capable payment gateway, which a competent processor should be able to help select [QBl05xxHst0 @ 00:02:31]. The final shared rule is that data has to be real and verified, not merely present in the authorization. PayTrace states it flatly under Visa's Commercial Enhanced Data Program: if you are not verified, you do not get Level 3 regardless of what you are trying to send in [5z1bCzU6Luw @ 00:26:40]. That turns Level 3 eligibility into a status the merchant earns rather than a toggle the gateway flips. Across channels the model is identical: identify commercial volume, route it through a capable gateway, and feed it data that survives Visa's scrutiny.

On the debit side, the consensus is about pass-through, not optimization tricks. Merchant Doctor warns that some processors will not pass Durbin savings to merchants and tells owners to look for qualified, mid-qualified and non-qualified buckets on their statements, then demand true interchange-plus pricing [Hx-TboJVDyo @ 00:02:58]. Sloan Bush of Interchange Plus Solutions, speaking with Practical Ecommerce, makes the same case with numbers: e-commerce debit interchange sat at 1.6 percent plus 15 cents while merchants were paying roughly 2.25 percent plus 30 cents, and tiered processors pocketed the difference [LjfmC9EATic @ 00:06:24]. The legal ground is shifting but has not moved checkout yet. LawLens reports the 8th Circuit ruled unanimously for the truck stop Corner Post on October 26, 2026, knocking down the Fed's debit-card fee cap [M4iOZn_3wng @ 00:01:30], yet stresses that no new fee was set, the Fed has roughly 45 days to seek rehearing, and until the mandate issues, the 21 cent cap stands [M4iOZn_3wng @ 00:04:36]. The operator takeaway shared by all three is the same: debit savings only reach a merchant whose pricing is true interchange-plus, because a tiered statement can absorb a rate cut before the merchant ever sees it. Whatever the courts eventually set, the statement structure decides who keeps the money.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The street workflow starts with the statement, not the pitch deck. TouchSuite's method is to find every line labeled business, corporate, purchasing or commercial, flag the standard and non-qualified lines, then compare a qualified line against a downgraded one to measure the rate gap [60QTYm0QFZU @ 00:00:40]. CCSalesPro's Payment Insights segment tells reps to hunt for standard or EIRF entries, with the standard downgrade rate sitting around 2.95 percent [mqEQN-xgAAk @ 00:03:33]. The same segment shows how cheap the fix can be: an auto shop doing over $100,000 a month had 80 percent of its transactions downgraded because it batched weekly to dodge a 25 cent batch fee. Settlement dates that did not match authorization dates triggered the downgrades, costing about $1,000 a month to save roughly $2, and the cure was simply daily auto-batching [mqEQN-xgAAk @ 00:04:03]. Eric Jenks of PayTrace puts the timing rule at transactions not settled within 48 hours falling to EIRF, though in the same breath gives a figure of 20 hours, so tighter is safer [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:44:03]. Before any data integration, the operator move is to read the downgrade lines, check batch timing, and quote the gap in dollars the owner can see on their own statement.

The numbers practitioners quote cluster around a one-point spread on Visa commercial cards. PayTrace walks Visa rates from 2.5 to 3 percent down to 1.75 percent with full data, a saving of 75 to 120 basis points [5z1bCzU6Luw @ 00:20:53]. Jenks maps the Visa commercial standard rate of 2.95 percent to Level 2 at 2.05 percent, a 90-point spread worth about $800 in that example, and deliberately skipped modeling a Level 3 move because those Visa cards are not assured to reach it [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:48:15]. Jones cites Visa commercial interchange at 1.9 versus 2.7 percent, an 80 basis point difference before markup, even while headlining the same video with cuts of 1 percent and more [QBl05xxHst0 @ 00:01:27]. Jones's client case is the loudest proof point: an e-commerce merchant whose commercial transactions landed at 2.95, 2.65, 3 and 2.8 moved to 1.45 on large tickets and about 1.9 at Level 2, for savings Jones reports as $35,000 per month [EQEdEkxVIXU @ 00:01:14]. Jenks's example is the conservative template for a proposal: model the Level 2 move first, treat Level 3 as upside, and multiply the spread against commercial volume only, never the merchant's full processing book.

Visa's Commercial Enhanced Data Program rewired the mechanics. TouchSuite reports processors passing through a 0.05 percent participation fee on US commercial and small business transactions sent with Level 2 or Level 3 data, charged whether or not a lower rate applies, and says that since April 2026 sending data may cost more than sending none at all [mFDxx7C-_EA @ 00:00:02]. Verified merchants get Product 3 rates at settlement, while non-verified merchants pay the higher rate up front and see the difference credited back roughly 10 to 15 days later if the data passes [mFDxx7C-_EA @ 00:00:56]. Layton explains what Visa is screening for: its AI flags repeated or generic values in the enhanced data fields, so recycled filler is exactly what gets caught [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:17:35]. PayTrace's answer is a product list tool holding five fields per product, a one-time setup it pegs at about half an hour, after which genuine line-item data rides along with each transaction [5z1bCzU6Luw @ 00:30:53]. The working sequence is consistent across these operators: build a real product catalog, pass clean line items long enough to become verified, then watch settlement and credit-backs to confirm Product 3 rates are actually landing rather than assuming the gateway handled it.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether Level 3 is something a processor simply switches on. Sean Jones of Revolution Payments pitches an interchange audit that will 'automatically update all of your transactions' to Level 3, and says businesses are finding one to one-and-a-half percent added to bottom-line profit margins on their existing sales volume [vVvGM8Rlor8 @ 00:01:23]. Eric Jenks of PayTrace, working the same B2B lane on the CCSalesPro live event, refuses to model it that way. His Visa commercial example moves a 2.95% standard rate only to Level 2 at 2.05%, a 90-point spread worth about $800, because those Visa cards 'don't guarantee to go to level three' [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:48:15]. PayTrace's own CEDP session hardens that into a flat rule: if the merchant is not verified, it does not get Level 3 regardless of what data it tries to send in [5z1bCzU6Luw @ 00:26:40]. TouchSuite adds the cash-flow mechanics behind that rule. Non-Verified merchants pay the higher rate up front at settlement and only see the Product 3 difference credited back roughly 10 to 15 days later, and only if Visa's checks pass the line items [mFDxx7C-_EA @ 00:00:56]. Jones sells the automatic version; Jenks, PayTrace and TouchSuite quote the verified-only version, and the two numbers cannot sit in the same proposal.

Practitioners also disagree on how big the prize actually is. Revolution Payments headlines Level 3 as cutting interchange 'by 1% and more,' yet the same video's worked Visa commercial number is 1.9% against 2.7%, an 80 basis point gap before any markup, and Jones adds that processors earn nothing from interchange, which is why so few of them ever bring it up [QBl05xxHst0 @ 00:01:27]. His own client case swings far wider: before rates of 2.95, 2.65, 3% and 2.8 dropped to 1.45 on large tickets and about 1.9 at Level 2, for a claimed $35,000 per month in savings on an e-commerce merchant [EQEdEkxVIXU @ 00:01:14]. PayTrace frames the Visa win as 2.5 to 3% falling to 1.75%, a saving in the 75 to 120 basis point range [5z1bCzU6Luw @ 00:20:53]. TouchSuite's deliberately made-up example runs much smaller: $100,000 a month in card sales, 40% on business cards, a $1,250 average ticket keyed into a virtual terminal, an assumed 0.5% rate gap, and about $200 a month or $2,400 a year affected by enhanced data [60QTYm0QFZU @ 00:01:31]. Jones sells the ceiling, TouchSuite models the floor, and PayTrace publishes a range an agent can defend line by line in front of a CFO.

The third fault line is who gets paid for the savings and how hard to push the data. Jeremy Layton of VeraSave says the only way his firm makes money is bringing a savings to a merchant: it takes a portion of savings for a set period, charges nothing if nothing is found, and he admits the model has caused contention with processors [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:07:21]. James Sheppard argues from the agent's seat instead. He says he could either cut his residuals and lose margin by lowering pricing, or find a way to cut the interchange costs 'which didn't cost me anything' [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:33:36]. On data integrity the two CCSalesPro voices openly diverge. Sheppard says Visa's CEDP screening could be gamed within a couple of months, and he expected ERP transaction IDs to become mandatory to close that hole [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:20:31]. Layton counters with what he sees in the field: some gateways and acquirers are still inserting randomized fake data into transactions and taking a share of the resulting savings, and he warns of 'big problems' for them if they get caught [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:22:09]. Sheppard's read is that the screening is beatable; Layton's read is that whoever is beating it is carrying the risk on behalf of every merchant in the portfolio.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive mistakes are self-inflicted settlement habits. CCSalesPro's auto shop case is the cleanest example: a merchant doing over $100,000 a month batched weekly to dodge a 25-cent batch fee, the settlement date stopped matching the authorization date, 80% of its transactions downgraded, and the shop burned roughly $1,000 a month to save about $2 until daily auto-batching was switched on [mqEQN-xgAAk @ 00:04:03]. Eric Jenks puts a clock on the same trap, telling agents that transactions not settled within 48 hours fall to EIRF, although in the same answer he also says 20 hours, so the exact window he gives is inconsistent [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:44:03]. Roger McNamara flags a quieter trigger on the CCSalesPro Level 2 and 3 session: a missing sequential invoice number knocks a B2B ticket from roughly 2.05 up to about 2.25 [tG2nIK3Th4Q @ 00:12:28]. TouchSuite's statement checklist stacks up the rest of the usual suspects: zero or missing tax amounts, blank PO numbers, line items that do not sum to the charged total, batches that are not settled promptly, and gateway fields left optional, off by default or unmapped, all under the blunt reminder that 'sending data is not the same as qualifying' [60QTYm0QFZU @ 00:00:55].

Visa's CEDP rollout turned enhanced data from a free upgrade into a trap. Layton reports the program went live October 17, 2025 with Visa tight-lipped on details, and that Visa then paused reviews on November 11 in a rollout he calls 'utter chaos' [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:17:55]. What merchants actually hit was worse than a pause: verified status flipping within one to two weeks, and real line-item data getting flagged as fake [KlaksrooZhw @ 00:19:42]. TouchSuite lays out the cost side. Processors report a 0.05% participation fee on US commercial and small business transactions sent with Level 2 or Level 3 data whether or not a lower rate applies, and since Visa retired Level 2 programs for Business, Corporate and Purchasing cards in mid-April 2026, leaving only fleet fuel, sending Level 2 only can trigger the fee with no rate benefit at all [mFDxx7C-_EA @ 00:00:40]. Its hypothetical distributor, with $100,000 a month in Visa commercial volume, a $1,000 average ticket and zero card-present volume, pays about $2,790 a month on Level 2 only, roughly $600 a year more than sending no data, against about $1,980 a month once verified for Product 3 [mFDxx7C-_EA @ 00:01:34]. The old easy win is now the line item that bleeds.

The economics check that trips up most merchants is whether savings ever reach them. Merchant Doctor warns that some processors simply will not pass Durbin debit savings through, and that qualified, mid-qualified and non-qualified lines on a statement are the tell that a merchant needs to demand true interchange-plus [Hx-TboJVDyo @ 00:02:58]. Sloan Bush of Interchange Plus Solutions puts numbers on the skim: e-commerce debit interchange of 1.6% plus 15 cents, merchants on tiered plans still paying about 2 and a quarter percent plus 30 cents, and tiered processors pocketing the 1.6% savings [LjfmC9EATic @ 00:06:24]. The debit rule itself is a moving target. LawLens reports the 8th Circuit's unanimous October 26, 2026 ruling that knocked out how the Fed built the Regulation II cap, but no new fee was set, the Fed has about 45 days to seek rehearing, and until the mandate issues the 21-cent cap stands and checkout costs do not move [M4iOZn_3wng @ 00:04:36]. On the B2B side the bottleneck is the merchant's own back office: Jenks reports prospects refusing optimization outright because they are locked into a million-dollar ERP system that nobody wants to touch for a payments project [AKvayIEwefA @ 00:55:16].
