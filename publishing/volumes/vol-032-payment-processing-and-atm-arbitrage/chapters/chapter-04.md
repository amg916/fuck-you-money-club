# Chapter 04: Dual Pricing and Cash Discount: Mechanics and the Sales Pitch

*How cash discount and dual pricing work on POS systems; free-POS packages; 3% and 4% Edge programs; pros, cons, and worked examples; presenting cash discounting without confusing the merchant; field closing tactics; and cash discount residuals as the key variable*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Dual pricing and cash discounting sellers converge on one promise: post a cash price and a card price, let the customer choose, and push processing cost off the merchant's statement. The gas pump is the universal proof point, used by Joe Wagner, James Shepherd, FTx POS and Titan to normalize the spread, typically three to four percent. On the register, a Valor terminal turns a $100 cash price into a $104 card price; PaymentCloud prices a $17.66 cheeseburger at $18.37 on card. Shepherd warns that residuals live or die on average ticket: $25 to $70 is the sweet spot, a $10 ticket goes negative 20 basis points at 3 percent, and high-ticket corporate reward cards near 2.5 percent interchange squeeze margin. They split on whether shelves must carry both prices and on trial length, from 30 to 60 days.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus — Two Prices, the Gas Pump Frame, and a Zero-Fee Statement

Across the operators, the core mechanism is the same: the merchant posts a cash price and a card price, the customer picks, and the spread covers processing so the merchant stops absorbing fees. Chad The POS Guy frames it bluntly, saying the cash price is always lower and the whole point is that 'your customer pays for the fees, and not you' [pNxf7ZuEt5w @ 00:00:34]. Electronic Money Company sells the same outcome as a merchant statement showing no fees at month end, with card revenue arriving at full value and clients reporting 10 to 30% increases in profit margins [QxwC71KUT-g @ 00:02:36]. Joe Wagner's Easy Pay material pitches offsetting up to 100% of processing fees, naming the specific bleed points merchants hate: percentages, transaction fees, batch fees, statement fees, PCI fees and annual fees [81tAw0Al6N0 @ 00:00:34]. Titan Merchant Services adds the fairness angle, arguing that interchange subsidizes consumer reward programs, so every customer eats year-after-year price hikes driven by someone else's rewards card, while dual pricing hands cash payers a three to four percent break and brings that same three to four percent back to the business [lsC_M7zjTjY @ 00:01:26]. The shared promise is not a lower rate; it is removing the processing line from the merchant's cost structure entirely.

The single most repeated proof point is the fuel pump. Joe Wagner tells merchants that a gas station charging an extra five or ten cents per gallon on a card is the exact program they already accept as consumers, then jokes that nobody drives to another station over a dime [KRZuPsngOLw @ 00:02:18]. James Shepherd of CCSalesPro, after field-selling in rural Pennsylvania, calls the fuel station concept the thread that ties the whole pitch together: it works as the opener, the psychological preamble before objections, the rebuttal itself and the education the merchant later repeats to customers [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:07:13]. FTx POS points to the same pump sign reading cash price and credit price as the textbook example of dual pricing [YyYXmSsR5as @ 00:01:39]. On customer acceptance, the sellers are uniformly confident. Scott Shaw tells merchants who fear the added four percent will drive shoppers elsewhere that studies show cash discounting was not an issue at the point of sale 99.2 percent of the time and had zero impact on sales, and backs that with a 60-day trial plus a promise to switch back to traditional processing on request [hdPpKRl1Bhk @ 00:02:43]. The consensus: consumer familiarity, not technology, closes the merchant.

Operators also agree on the baseline prerequisites. First, the platform has to support it: Chad The POS Guy warns that Toast and Square cannot pass fees to the customer, so merchants on those systems are forced to eat the roughly 3 percent [pNxf7ZuEt5w @ 00:00:48]. Second, the terminal does the math, not the cashier. Joe Wagner's Easy Pay pitch says the free terminal automatically recognizes payment type, prints the correct cash or credit price on the receipt and tracks everything so there is no reconciliation work [9ImWZ__BWMs @ 00:01:03]. Third, signage and price display: FTx POS insists you must display the credit price and cannot show only the cheaper cash price and then charge more at checkout, because that is just a surcharge by another name [YyYXmSsR5as @ 00:01:56], while O3 Merchant Services' Tanya lists menus, signs, point of sale screens and itemized receipts as places prices must appear [y6h2-zKFtQ0 @ 00:02:04]. Fourth, the commercial wrapper: nearly every seller leads with free equipment and no long-term contract, asking the merchant for a 45 to 60 day test run rather than a lease. Joe Wagner's own line is that merchants stay because they are happy, not because they are locked in.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers — Register Math, Residual Math, and the Scripted Close

The register math is simple and the practitioners show it to the cent. On a Valor PayTech terminal, James Shepherd has the merchant key in only the cash price; the terminal applies the differential, usually four percent, and displays cash price 100, card price 104, with the receipt printing just card price 104 once the card option is chosen [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:03:30]. Scott Shaw's signage tells customers a four percent fee applies to all store sales with an immediate four percent cash discount, so a ten dollar item rings up at ten dollars cash or ten dollars and forty cents on a card [hdPpKRl1Bhk @ 00:01:19]. PaymentCloud's cafe example lists a bacon cheeseburger at $17.66 cash and $18.37 card, a spread of about 4.02 percent, with both prices on the POS screen and at the bottom of the receipt [xRGCXR9kPug @ 00:01:13]. On the merchant's side of the ledger, Chad The POS Guy runs $30,000 a month at about 3 percent: $900 out of pocket and $29,100 taken home under traditional processing, versus the full $30,000 under dual pricing [pNxf7ZuEt5w @ 00:01:07]. Shepherd stresses the back end is identical to a non-cash adjustment program; only display, receipt and signage change.

For the ISO side, James Shepherd calls average ticket the most important variable in cash discounting residuals, because the program is really flat rate pricing: the rep earns on the service fee collected from card payers, not on the discount [AnhJYbY3ptw @ 00:00:20]. The sweet spot is a $25 to $70 average ticket. Signature debit carries regulated interchange of 22 cents plus 5 basis points, roughly 30 to 32 cents all-in with processor fees, which is only about 1 percent on a $30 sale, so a 3 percent service fee leaves around 180 basis points of markup [AnhJYbY3ptw @ 00:02:07]. That debit math matters in practice: FTx POS reports that over 80 percent of its parent company's card transactions flag as debit [YyYXmSsR5as @ 00:00:58]. The edges bite. At a $10 ticket the 32 cents becomes 3.2 percent, so a 3 percent fee yields residuals of negative 20 basis points; at $5 it is 6.4 percent, underwater even at 3.99 percent, which is why Shepherd suggests a per-item fee like 59 cents on tiny tickets. At $150 tickets, jewelry and B2B merchants may run corporate reward cards near 2.5 percent interchange, so the percentage has to climb [AnhJYbY3ptw @ 00:04:26].

The sales sequence is equally scripted. Joe Wagner's four-step soft-blow method runs: make them feel the savings, normalize it through the gas pump, explain that the program works exactly that way, then pull out the application with 'here let me show you,' a line Wagner says will make some reps millions [KRZuPsngOLw @ 00:04:02]. Inside the longer five-step conversation of intro, short story, presentation, close and rehash, Wagner refuses to ask for the bill, instead offering an either-or ballpark like 'are you paying like 700 a month here, are you more like in the 400 range,' then annualizing a $500 answer into $6,000 a year and asking what the merchant would do with it [tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:09:26]. The application's first page shows annual, batch and transaction fees crossed out with black X's, which doubles as the bridge into the presentation and then into the close [tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:06:24]. James Shepherd's field opener skips the curiosity hook entirely: ask whether the merchant has seen a fuel station with cash and card prices, then explain that their current price becomes the cash price and the company 'just collects that tiny little difference' to offset processing [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:06:11]. Shepherd reports a really good response to that opener.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Intro That Kills Rebuttals Up Front
   - Open likable and short, pre-empting the 'I'm busy' and 'I get calls all day' brush-offs before the merchant can use them.
   - Wagner's opener is 'I know you're real busy and I probably get like two calls on this a day, let me just share what I've got and I'll be out of your way,' because people buy when they like and trust you [Joe Wagner | Merchant Services & Passive Income — tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:01:44].
2. Feel the Savings, Then Normalize at the Pump
   - Get a ballpark monthly fee figure, annualize it, then walk the merchant back to the last time they paid a higher card price for gas.
   - Ask about the gas station charging five or ten cents more per gallon on a card and joke that nobody drives elsewhere over a dime, so the merchant remembers being the consumer and not flinching [Joe Wagner | Merchant Services & Passive Income — KRZuPsngOLw @ 00:02:18].
3. Pull the App Out as a Trust Tool
   - Show the first page with annual, batch and transaction fees crossed out, pull the free terminal form, and write the core model on the paper.
   - Wagner writes 'month to month, no contract' on the application and offers an initial next to it, so the words match a document and the rep is already bridging into the close [Joe Wagner | Merchant Services & Passive Income — tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:18:07].
4. Bridge Close, Handshake and Rehash
   - Deliver a bridge close line, stick a hand out for the handshake, then confirm signage, install timing and the referral ask.
   - Line: 'The hardest part is what we're doing right here, it only takes another couple of minutes'; then signage ships, the pre-programmed terminal arrives in five to seven days, and the rep warns they will come back for referrals [Joe Wagner | Merchant Services & Passive Income — tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:25:39].

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fault line is what the product actually is. PaymentCloud draws a hard line: a cash discount knocks a discount off the regular price when the customer pays cash, while dual pricing runs two separate prices with the cash price sitting as the original sticker price [xRGCXR9kPug @ 00:01:54]. James Shepherd of CCSalesPro flattens that distinction on the money side, telling reps that this version of dual pricing has the exact same economic model as a non-cash adjustment or service fee program, so residuals and margin do not move at all; only the display, signage and receipt print change. Shepherd's reason for switching is card-brand pressure: Visa has come out strongly against adding a fee line item to the receipt, so the customer now picks a cash or card price before the card ever touches the terminal [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:02:08]. Scott Shaw still pitches the older structure openly, with signage announcing a four percent fee on all store sales and software that adds a four percent service fee at checkout, turning a $10 item into $10.40 on a card [hdPpKRl1Bhk @ 00:01:00]. Joe Wagner sidesteps the labels entirely and sells the whole thing as flat-rate processing with the junk fees crossed out [tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:03:12].

The second split is how much shelf work the merchant has to do. Tanya at O3 Merchant Services tells owners they must display prices everywhere, on menus, signs, point of sale screens and itemized receipts, before running the program [y6h2-zKFtQ0 @ 00:02:04]. Shepherd pushes back on blanket shelf rules: what the merchant puts on the shelf or menu is the merchant's decision, the terminal keys in only the cash price and calculates a card price usually four percent higher, and only certain states such as New York truly require both prices on the shelf or menu [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:04:09]. FTx POS lands in the middle with one bright line: you must display the credit price, and showing only the cheaper cash price then charging more at checkout is just another way of doing a surcharge [YyYXmSsR5as @ 00:01:56]. On surcharging itself, PaymentCloud says it carries many regulations and state rules while dual pricing does not [xRGCXR9kPug @ 00:02:28], and FTx goes further, saying dual pricing has no caps, works in all 50 states, and lets a merchant discount as deep as they want, whereas surcharging is capped at 3% or actual cost and blocked on every debit card, which FTx says covers over 80% of card transactions in its parent company stores.

The economics of the merchant-facing offer also diverge. Joe Wagner's Easy Pay model charges one flat monthly fee for the life of the account: $25 under $5,000 in volume, $50 between $5,000 and $10,000, and a flat $100 above $10,000, no matter whether the merchant runs $50,000 or $100,000 [81tAw0Al6N0 @ 00:01:38]. iSmart Payments' Edge pitch goes lower, claiming most businesses processing over $10,000 a month pay zero, with software that knows in real time what each non-cash transaction will cost and adjusts the purchase price to offset it [x7WUlpG34a8 @ 00:01:46]. Customer reaction is the other live argument. Scott Shaw's pitch claims studies show cash discounting was not an issue 99.2 percent of the time and had zero impact on sales [hdPpKRl1Bhk @ 00:02:43], while PaymentCloud lists customer confusion and extra staff explaining time as real costs and warns that too wide a cash-card gap can discourage sales if customers see the card price as unfair [xRGCXR9kPug @ 00:03:36]. Shaw backs the claim with a 60-day trial and a promise to switch merchants back to traditional processing while still paying less than they do now. PaymentCloud's answer is the opposite lever: strike a balance between the cash incentive and a reasonable card price, and treat customer communication as the key to making the program work.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The sharpest economics trap belongs to the rep, not the merchant. James Shepherd of CCSalesPro tells reps to reclassify cash discounting as flat-rate pricing, because what they actually earn is the service fee collected on card transactions, and flat rate behaves nothing like interchange plus. The sweet spot is a $25 to $70 average ticket, where signature debit at 22 cents plus 5 basis points lands around 30 to 32 cents all-in, roughly 1% of a $30 sale, leaving about 180 basis points of markup on a 3% fee [AnhJYbY3ptw @ 00:01:30]. Below that band the math flips. A $10 average ticket makes 32 cents equal 3.2%, so a 3% fee puts residuals at negative 20 basis points, and a $5 ticket costs 6.4%, still underwater at 3.99% [AnhJYbY3ptw @ 00:02:44]. The other edge is high ticket: a $150 average jewelry store or a B2B account swiping corporate reward cards with 2.5% interchange earns almost nothing on a 3% fee [AnhJYbY3ptw @ 00:04:12]. That is the check to run before copying a blanket offer like Titan Merchant Services' three to four percent cash discount pitched to businesses from all different industry types [lsC_M7zjTjY @ 00:01:26]. Shepherd's fixes are a per-item fee such as 59 cents on tiny tickets and a higher percentage on rewards-heavy high-ticket books.

On the street, the deal usually dies on wording, not math. Joe Wagner is blunt that walking in and announcing the merchant can drop their fees by charging their customers does not work, because the merchant has not yet been made to feel the savings or remember being the consumer [KRZuPsngOLw @ 00:03:12]. The second killer is the awkward pause. Reps who pitch without the application already on the table eventually run out of things to say, then reach for the app and trigger a rapid-fire list of reasons not to sign today; Wagner keeps the app out from the first bridge so the paperwork never arrives as a threat [tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:19:42]. The third is over-talking after the yes. Once the rep is asking for address and phone number, Wagner says deals are so fragile that one unnecessary detail can trigger ten more rebuttals, and Wagner fully expects reps who ignore this to call after walking out without the deal [tgxPcd70hpA @ 00:24:41]. Shepherd reports a personal reversal: the old pitch opened with eliminating fees to spark curiosity, then rural Pennsylvania showed those hoops could be skipped in favor of leading straight with the fuel station question, now threaded through the opening, the objection preamble and the rebuttal [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:05:20].

Structural traps sit underneath the pitch. Shepherd moved reps off the non-cash adjustment because that program was clearly under attack by the card brands, with Visa coming out strongly against a fee line item on the receipt [3bPRCy508Lc @ 00:02:08]. FTx POS flags the surcharge trap from the other side: over 80% of card transactions in its parent company stores would be flagged as debit and could not be surcharged at all, and surcharging is also not available for all Amex cards, capped at 3% or actual cost, live in only 47 states, with seven more states carrying pending legislation to ban it [YyYXmSsR5as @ 00:00:58]. Platform choice can block the whole program: Chad The POS Guy says Toast and Square cannot pass the fee to the customer, so a merchant doing $30,000 a month at about 3% eats $900 out of pocket and takes home roughly $29,100 [pNxf7ZuEt5w @ 00:00:48]. PaymentCloud adds the leftover-cost check: some processors still charge small transaction or service fees even under dual pricing, so every cost line needs review before the merchant assumes the bill goes to zero [xRGCXR9kPug @ 00:04:23]. PaymentCloud also lists setup with a supporting processor and compliance with the law and the PCI Data Security Standard among the disadvantages.
