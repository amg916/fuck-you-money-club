# Chapter 06: Due Diligence: Perc Tests, Septic, and Site Checks

*Perc test walkthroughs and Title 5 measurements, what a failed perc test means and alternative septic options, health department soil review, slope and water-table concerns, Florida wetlands and deal-killing checks, and 15-point due diligence checklists*

## Executive Summary

Land operators and septic pros agree that on any parcel without sewer, the soil decides whether a house can go there, so the perc or site evaluation has to run inside the due diligence window, never after closing. Apke Brothers screen overlays for slope, access, wetlands and flood in five to ten minutes, buy at 40 to 45% of market value, and cap standard diligence at 48 hours. The tests are timed water drops or soil-morphology reads, and the money spread is wide: $150 to $1,500 for a county perc, roughly $5,000 to $20,000 for conventional systems, and $15,000 to $60,000 for mounds, treatment units and drip. Practitioners split on risk: REtipster's Seth has skipped perc on sub-$1,000 lots and has never been burnt, while Mason Dixon Acres skipped it and hit car-sized rock.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who touched septic lands on the same baseline: if the parcel has no sewer line, buildability lives or dies in the dirt. Seth at REtipster frames the perc test as the issue that can make or break a land deal, because a lot that cannot drain wastewater cannot carry a house, and a lot that cannot carry a house loses its highest and best use [yp6qPMTkjgE @ 00:01:51]. Builder Roy Hunter, on site with Virginia agent Ben Quann, puts it bluntly: the purpose of the test is to make sure the property is buildable, and the way you prove buildable is whether a septic system fits [uvqvdLjMBL0 @ 00:01:47]. Acrewell Land Company adds that roughly 25% of US households rely on septic, and once you leave town your ability to build depends on it [AYim3zSksEs @ 00:01:03]. Groundwork, a civil engineer in water utilities, explains why the soil matters more than the hardware: the buried tank only separates solids from liquid, while the soil in the drain field does the real cleaning every day for the 15 to 40 years the system is supposed to last [dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:01:03]. The operator takeaway is consistent across all four: on rural paper, the soil report is the real valuation document.

The second point of agreement is sequencing: test while you can still walk. Florida Land Facts, a Sebring-area operator, says never close first and test later; run the soil evaluation inside the due diligence period and start early, because in a busy building season scheduling the evaluator and getting results back can take weeks [zWTn3NY8wqc @ 00:02:14]. Groundwork spells out why the clock matters: if the lot fails after closing, the seller pays nothing and the unbuildable lot is yours, while the three outs, paying for a different system, moving the field, or renegotiating and walking, are all cheaper before you close [dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:01:20]. The installer behind Wells, Septic & More closes the same loop from the contractor side, telling buyers to make sure a property perks before purchasing a home or land so they know a proper system can go in [F-UdA3MqSzw @ 00:10:22]. Mason Dixon Acres is the live counterexample: the couple skipped the test because the price was good and the timber had value, then pulled a rock the footprint of a small car out of the second pit and admitted that without septic the property is essentially rendered useless [8h9gMkksdD8 @ 00:02:22]. The test is cheap; the surprise is not.

Practitioners also agree that a failed perc is a cost problem before it is a death sentence. Jack Bosch calls the belief that a failed test means you cannot build one of the most misunderstood factors in real estate, because sand-filter, hydraulic, and self-contained toilet systems can still let you build at a higher price [nVJMPjUWdZw @ 00:07:25]. Liberty Acres says the same in one line: a failure usually means the soil cannot take a conventional drain field, not that the land can never hold a home, and the state and local health department decide which alternatives are allowed [Mio31c7ePlc @ 00:00:00]. The flip side of that consensus is that nobody trusts the neighbors. Acrewell warns that soil characteristics can change within 10 feet in any direction, so septic on both adjoining houses does not mean the vacant lot in the middle qualifies [AYim3zSksEs @ 00:06:00]. Seth at REtipster uses adjoining houses only as a vague signal when speed matters, stressing that neighbors pulling it off does not mean you will [yp6qPMTkjgE @ 00:04:51]. Net consensus: price the alternative system, check local approvals, and treat the house next door as a clue rather than proof.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Apke Brothers run a four-stage acquisition screen after the purchase agreement is signed. Stage one is a computer check in The Land Portal: toggle contour lines, flood zones, wetlands, and water features, mark problems on paper, and confirm road access and slope, which they call the two biggest items; the pink contour lines sit 20 feet apart, the 3D button exposes valleys where water collects, and a KML export drops the parcel into Google Earth street view [j3-xqUjgb7k @ 00:06:08]. A rookie takes an hour, a veteran five to ten minutes. Stage two is the price check: they average buying at 40 to 45% of market value, like to double their money, grade every deal A to F on price, property, and location, and renegotiate with the seller when land and price do not line up [j3-xqUjgb7k @ 00:07:40]. Stage three moves to the county for deed, zoning, setbacks, and any perc record at the health department. Stage four is boots on the ground, roughly $200 for a drone photographer, done while the file sits in title so they can still back out and list immediately [j3-xqUjgb7k @ 00:23:39]. Neil Clements adds a survey on every single purchase, calling a skipped $1,000 survey ludicrous [w08yc3MEaGE @ 00:10:38].

The test itself is a timed drop. MassDEP walks through the Title 5 version: measure the shelf so the test sits in the most restrictive layer, dig the hole at least 12 inches wide and 18 inches deep, hold water at the 12-inch mark for 15 minutes to swell the soil, then time the drop from 9 inches to 6 inches and divide by three to get minutes per inch [Q3kV6OiudSU @ 00:00:34]. Groundwork cites Alabama extension guidance of at least six holes 24 to 42 inches deep, soaked at least four hours, read every 5 to 30 minutes, with 60 minutes per inch or slower failing a standard field and the water table or bedrock needing to sit at least 4 feet below the trenches [dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:01:40]. In the field, Mason Dixon Acres ran a probe stage first, two 7-foot holes about 70 feet apart for the sewage enforcement officer to read the limiting zone, before six post holes get the actual water [8h9gMkksdD8 @ 00:00:20]. Roy Hunter's crew no longer pours water at all; they read soil morphology, color, texture, and consistency, looking for 36 inches of good soil under trenches at least 18 inches deep [uvqvdLjMBL0 @ 00:02:33].

The money side is where operators get blindsided. Seth at REtipster pays the county anywhere from $150 to $1,500 for a perc test, and on lots bought free and clear for well under $1,000 has skipped it entirely when the deal had to close that week, reporting they have never been burnt [yp6qPMTkjgE @ 00:02:04]. Groundwork's cost guide runs $150 to $500 for the soil evaluation, $1,000 to $5,000 for a designer, about $5,000 for a conventional system, $15,000 to $25,000 for a mound, and $15,000 to $30,000 or more for an aerobic treatment unit, warning that two identical-looking lots can differ by $10,000 or more [dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:05:35]. Ben Quann calls $20,000 the going rate for a conventional system in Virginia, estimates about $30,000 for the treatment-unit design on their buyer's lot, and puts drip systems at $45,000 to $60,000 [uvqvdLjMBL0 @ 00:06:08]. Jack Bosch frames the decision as a ratio: a roughly $10,000 normal system versus $30,000 to $50,000 for an alternative kills a $5,000 lot carrying a $30,000 mobile home, yet barely registers on a $250,000 lot under a million-dollar custom home [nVJMPjUWdZw @ 00:09:46].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Run the desktop overlay screen
   - Pull contours, flood zones, wetlands, water features, and road access on the parcel before spending money.
   - Apke Brothers flag every wetland, slope, and access issue on paper, cross-check with the 3D view and a KML export to Google Earth, and expect a veteran to finish in five to ten minutes [Apke Brothers — j3-xqUjgb7k @ 00:12:45].
2. Call the health department before the offer
   - Ask whether the lot was ever tested, approved, or turned down, whether a permit exists, and how old any soil report is.
   - Groundwork calls the county health department before making an offer [Groundwork — dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:07:05] and warns that in Wayne County a soil report is valid for two years, so a seller's old report can be stale [Groundwork — dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:04:29].
3. Lock the contingency and the calendar
   - Make the purchase contingent on a passing soil evaluation with enough time for wet-season testing.
   - Groundwork writes the soil contingency with room for wet weather testing [Groundwork — dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:06:49], and Bill Bower notes wet-season lots must test from mid-February to mid-April or wait for the next season [The Jen Holden Group of Compass — 3ppoErpmGfs @ 00:02:43].
4. Read the written result and reprice
   - Get the failure condition in writing, retest other spots, and fold the required system cost into the offer.
   - Liberty Acres asks which condition failed, whether another spot can be tested, and gets a written estimate for the system and upkeep [Liberty Acres — Mio31c7ePlc @ 00:01:08]; Florida Land Facts says that septic bill belongs in your offer price [Florida Land Facts — zWTn3NY8wqc @ 00:03:18].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether a land flipper pays for a perc test before closing at all. Seth of REtipster buys many vacant lots for well under $1,000 free and clear, and when a deal has to close that week, Seth says it may be worth rolling the dice rather than paying more for the test than for the property itself, adding that the approach has never burned the business to date [yp6qPMTkjgE @ 00:03:14]. Mason Dixon Acres ran the same logic as owner-builders: they got such a good price and saw enough money in logs that they skipped the perc test, then hit rock big enough that the inspector ruled the site unsuitable for any septic system, a result they say essentially renders the property useless [8h9gMkksdD8 @ 00:02:22]. Florida Land Facts takes the hard line the other way, telling buyers never to close first and test later, and to start the soil evaluation early because a busy building season can push evaluator scheduling and results out by weeks [zWTn3NY8wqc @ 00:02:14]. Groundwork frames it as sequence: if a lot fails, paying for a different system, moving the field, or renegotiating are all cheaper before closing, so the rule is test first, buy second [dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:06:32]. The practical divide comes down to the price paid versus how much of the resale value depends on a house going in.

Practitioners also clash on how fast diligence can move. The Apke Brothers run a four-step process on cash land buys and argue standard diligence should take 48 hours max, because sellers back out when a deal bought near 40% of market value sits too long [j3-xqUjgb7k @ 00:29:06]. Bill Bower, speaking with The Jen Holden Group of Compass, describes a calendar no investor can compress: in parts of Anne Arundel County, wet season perc lots must be tested from roughly mid-February to mid-April, and missing that window means waiting until the next season to design the system [3ppoErpmGfs @ 00:03:01]. Even the test itself is disputed. Roy Hunter, appearing on Living in Virginia With Ben Quann, says the dig-a-hole-and-time-the-water method was dropped probably 20 to 25 years ago, replaced by soil morphology that reads color, texture, and consistency, even though everybody still calls it a perc test [uvqvdLjMBL0 @ 00:02:21]. MassDEP still walks through a timed water test: hold the water at the 12-inch mark for 15 minutes, then time the drop from 9 inches to 6 inches and divide by three for minutes per inch [Q3kV6OiudSU @ 00:01:52]. An operator promising a seller a fast close has to know which regime the county runs, and which season it demands, before quoting any timeline.

What a failed perc actually means divides the field. Jack Bosch calls it one of the most misunderstood factors in real estate, insisting land that fails can still carry a house through sand filter, hydraulic, or self-contained toilet systems, just at a much higher price [nVJMPjUWdZw @ 00:07:25]. Wells, Septic & More reports a harsher local reality: most of the time in that area, a lot that does not perc will not be allowed to develop at all, and existing homes with failed systems get pushed onto holding tanks [F-UdA3MqSzw @ 00:02:56]. The neighbor shortcut splits operators too. When rushing a cheap deal, Seth of REtipster checks adjoining parcels for houses as a vague signal that the septic question has been solved nearby, while warning that neighbors passing does not mean the lot will [yp6qPMTkjgE @ 00:04:37]. Acrewell Land Company goes further, saying soil characteristics can change 10 feet in any direction, so septic systems on both sides prove nothing about the vacant lot in the middle. Acrewell's alternative is paying a soil scientist for a mapped evaluation across the whole property, which costs more than the county route but samples far more ground and gives the seller more pricing power and confidence with buyers [AYim3zSksEs @ 00:06:00].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is learning the soil after the money is gone. Mason Dixon Acres closed without a perc, then the first probe pit hit rock about three feet down for a 36-inch limiting zone, good enough only for a sand mound they describe as roughly twice the cost of conventional and up to five feet tall out of the ground [8h9gMkksdD8 @ 00:00:53]. The second pit, 70 feet away on the contour, produced a boulder roughly the footprint of a small car, the site was ruled out for any septic system, and the team ended up dragging a big excavator into dense woods to dig five more pits, filling one in immediately when it hit rock again [8h9gMkksdD8 @ 00:04:58]. Seth of REtipster saw the same pattern on a 12-acre parcel that failed the perc in several spots before one corner passed, which is why one bad hole should never end the search on a larger tract [yp6qPMTkjgE @ 00:05:17]. On the reviewer side, John Dixon, filmed at a health department review by Soils Inc. & VA Septic, deliberately digs where no consultant dug and explores deeper than the minimum to catch bedrock, ledge, or a perched water table that varies across the site [vTDxU1jKGSI @ 00:02:40].

Septic math routinely swallows the land price. Florida Land Facts points to Highlands County lots near Sebring trading around $8,000 an acre, then prices Florida conventional installs at $10,000 to $15,000 and engineered mounds at $15,000 to $25,000, meaning the septic can cost two or three times the dirt [zWTn3NY8wqc @ 00:02:56]. Jack Bosch runs the same check on an end buyer's budget: a $5,000 lot carrying a $30,000 mobile home cannot absorb a $30,000 to $50,000 alternative system, while a $250,000 custom-home lot under a million-dollar house can [nVJMPjUWdZw @ 00:09:46]. On Living in Virginia With Ben Quann, the team estimates about $30,000 for an alternative treatment unit against roughly $20,000 for conventional, with drip systems running $45,000 to $60,000, and warns a budget gets blown by having a lot perced for the wrong thing [uvqvdLjMBL0 @ 00:06:08]. Wells, Septic & More shows the worst branch: a holding tank, usually around 3,000 gallons giving roughly 10 days of water, at a couple hundred to nearly $1,000 a month in pumping, which drags resale value down [F-UdA3MqSzw @ 00:04:02]. Every one of those numbers belongs in the offer price before signing.

The quieter traps sit in paperwork and calendars. Neil Clements of REtipster orders a survey on every deal because roughly 25 to 30% of the time the boundaries come back different from what the GIS program showed, and a survey can expose an easement that makes the whole parcel unbuildable [w08yc3MEaGE @ 00:10:54]. Neil also leaned on title insurance on a $200,000 purchase where a wrong legal description meant buying the neighboring property, forcing a PI and 20 signatures gathered across five or six states to clear it [w08yc3MEaGE @ 00:12:08]. Bill Bower tells The Jen Holden Group of Compass about clients who never walked their whole site and had no idea a steep slope and floodplain sat out back until the tight survey contours exposed them [3ppoErpmGfs @ 00:04:35]. Groundwork flags the stale-paper trap: in Indiana's Wayne County a soil report is valid two years and the permit one, so an old report from the seller may be stale by the time you buy, and a dry August test can show a number March will not honor [dSAQlt9llvM @ 00:04:29]. The closing reminder from Neil is blunt: before the wire goes out you can always say no, because once you own it, there are no takebacks.
