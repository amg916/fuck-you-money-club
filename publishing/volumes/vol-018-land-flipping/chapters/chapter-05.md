# Chapter 05: Comping and Pricing Vacant Land

*Zillow, Redfin, and LandWatch comps, acreage price-per-acre adjustments, scraping LandWatch listings, pricing in non-disclosure states, why Zillow misleads on land values, avoiding overpaying, picking counties with Zillow data, and comping a subdivide*

## Executive Summary

Land operators converge on one rule: value vacant land off sold, land-only comps in a tight size band and a six-to-twelve-month window, then adjust for wetlands, septic, and frontage. Seth of REtipster turns Redfin or LandWatch exports into a price-per-acre average, and Price Boss and PRYCD automate the pull. Offer math is blunt: Cool Wholesaling pegs Fairmont retail at $5,000 to $6,000 an acre and caps an 8-acre buy at $35,000; Brent Bowers aims under 50 cents on the dollar. Acrewell's Alex Reese says asking prices run two to four times market value. In non-disclosure states, Sumner Advanced haircuts last list price by days on market, while the Apke Brothers leaned on a realtor's opinion to justify a seven-parcel subdivide. They split on tooling: Bowers calls PRYCD his easy button, while Seth says Zillow often omits acreage.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who runs land numbers starts in the same place: strip the search down to vacant land and lean on what actually closed. Brent Bowers clicks sold-only, deselects every home type, re-selects lots and land, and keeps the window to six months, stretching to nine only when nothing shows, because 'what someone is willing to pay for it is what the value actually is' [JNufnuENoDM @ 00:01:05]. Seth of REtipster makes the identical cut on Redfin, calling sold data 'almost always the better way to go' since it is not some seller's or realtor's opinion, and warning that reaching back two or three years makes the numbers less relevant [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:01:58]. The Cool Wholesaling crew filters Fairmont, North Carolina by sold and home type land, and reports that Jasper's team comps on Zillow almost exclusively and doesn't even have PropStream [3x8J7AdyCEw @ 00:00:01]. Damian of WE ARE LAND SPECIALISTS opens every Zillow pull with the price filter switched off and a twelve-month sold window, then widens to 24 months, or cross-checks county GIS last-sale data, when dirt barely trades in the pocket [JRa_lwdSNjo @ 00:10:48]. The shared logic: the older or more distant the sale, the less it tells you about today's buyer.

The second universal rule is apples to apples on size, because price per acre collapses as parcels get bigger. Valuing an 8-acre lot in Fairmont, the Cool Wholesaling crew set aside 37 acres at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 an acre and 50 acres for 139,000, saying the per-acre price 'gets smaller as you go up in size,' so those big tracts 'don't really apply' [3x8J7AdyCEw @ 00:01:22]. Seth of REtipster calls it a statistical constant: look at thousands of vacant lots in one area and the smaller ones command more per acre, so a five-acre subject comped off a 2.37-acre sale deserves a discount on the per-acre figure [9cX2NuhnYro @ 00:09:39]. Brent Bowers brackets hard, giving a 10-acre subject only 9-to-11-acre comps, and wants them within a couple of miles, calling five miles out a stretch [JNufnuENoDM @ 00:03:14]. Setting matters as much as acreage. Pricing a rural lot between Antioch and Shannon near Red Springs, North Carolina, the Cool Wholesaling team refused city comps, threw out subdivision lots as a builder charging premium, dropped a 6,500 sale that looked landlocked behind the main road, and landed on roughly 13,000 as max retail for a rural parcel in a not-high-end area [t8XDRE0ZjFc @ 00:02:57].

The third point of agreement: raw per-acre math is only the skeleton, and physical traits plus a local land agent finish the valuation. Once Upon a Brick warns that 'not every two acres is the same': flat versus sloped, wetland or not, road access, a pond, a skinny strip versus a rectangle with road frontage, and how close electric and water run all move the price [Tmf12E9Dg7A @ 00:06:15]. Alex Reese of Acrewell turns that into a six-step check called ASSESS (access, soil, slope, environmental, street view, satellite view) and shows a 3.3-acre lot with 2.8 acres outside mapped wetlands behaving like a bigger property than a 4.58-acre lot with only 1.1 acres outside [TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:22:24]. Outliers get cut without apology: Seth deletes any sale that does not line up with the rest, guessing it carried some issue or was a deal between friends or family members [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:06:17]. And almost everyone ends at the phone. Seth calls two or three agents who list multiple vacant lots, asks what price moves a five-acre parcel in 30 days, three months, or six months, and expects different numbers from different agents, the same reason one comp is never enough [9cX2NuhnYro @ 00:16:31].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The cleanest bulk method comes from Seth at REtipster, who credits Carl James and Todd Millin from the REtipster community. Filter Redfin to land, set a lot-size band, switch to sold, and hit the download button to pull every comp into a spreadsheet [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:03:07]. Delete everything except sale price and lot size, then add two columns: acres, calculated as lot square feet divided by 43,560, and dollars per acre, calculated as price divided by acres; drag both formulas down and finish with =AVERAGE over the column [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:04:15]. Where Redfin hides the download button, as in Costilla County, Colorado, Seth runs the free Chrome extension Instant Data Scraper, points its locate-next button at Redfin's page arrow so it crawls past the first 40 of 296 results, exports a CSV, and uses find-and-replace to strip the word acres out of the size column, which in one pass made 310 replacements [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:08:23]. Where Redfin does not operate at all, like Tallapoosa County, Alabama, the same scraper runs on LandWatch. Price Boss automates the LandWatch version: advanced search by state and county, price 0 to 50,000, property type land, sort by most recent so paid-to-the-top ads do not skew the pull, paste each page into the yellow bar, and hit compute [FOpRZxfMs4I @ 00:01:32].

Once the per-acre number is set, operators move straight to offer math. The Cool Wholesaling crew read Fairmont comps of 5 acres at 30,000 and 9 acres at 50,000 as retail of 5,000 to 6,000 an acre, took the conservative 5,000 because big parcels sell slower than single building lots, priced the 8-acre subject at 40,000 retail, and set the buy target at 30,000, 35,000 max, without even pulling up the parcel [3x8J7AdyCEw @ 00:03:12]. Seth reverse-engineers a single lot: 17,900 divided by 2.37 acres is $7,552 an acre, then he knocks it to 7,000, 6,500, or 6,000 for a larger five-acre subject [9cX2NuhnYro @ 00:09:00]. Brent Bowers caps comp research at 15 to 20 minutes per new area because land runs on averages and the offer is ideally under 50 cents on the dollar, leaving room for error [JNufnuENoDM @ 00:10:39]. Alex Reese adjusts comps line by line: against a $20,000 sale in the same Middleburg, Florida subdivision, an installed septic adds about $10,000, a well about $15,000, and site prep at least $5,000, putting the listed lot near $50,000, which is exactly what he would offer against a $75,000 ask [TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:07:34].

Non-disclosure states force operators to price off list data, and the street method is a days-on-market haircut. Sumner Advanced reads the last list price where a sold comp went under contract, never as the sale price: same-day contingent likely closed at list, a 117-day listing gets maybe 10 to 15 or 20 percent off, a 317-day listing 30 to 35 percent, and the six-month-to-a-year band can run toward 50 percent, while active listings older than a year are left out of the math [8pB5HoHJ4Bk @ 00:04:05]. The Apke Brothers ran exactly this play in Tate County, Mississippi: a 9.4-acre sale with a long listing history was guessed near $35,000, a 12-acre listed at 125,000 that went pending after five months was pegged near $100,000, and when comps still could not justify the price, a trusted local realtor's 50,000-to-70,000 opinion closed the gap [bIqgihxHoo8 @ 00:05:17]. When only for-sale listings exist, Seth discounts them 20 to 30 percent [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:02:42]. Jeff checks LandWatch descriptions for seller-finance terms, where one ad showed 1,997 cash versus 297 down and 97 a month for 48 months, and multiplies the monthly payment by the months to get the terms price before treating it as a comp [mM0tCRujCDM @ 00:01:35].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the Search Box
   - Filter Zillow or Redfin to sold, lots and land only, a bracketed acreage band, and a six-month window.
   - Bowers deselects every home type, reselects lots and land, matches HOA status to the subject, brackets a 10-acre parcel at 9 to 11 acres, and widens to nine months only when nothing appears [Brent Bowers — JNufnuENoDM @ 00:01:39].
2. Build the Per-Acre Sheet
   - Export or scrape price and lot size, convert to acres, compute dollars per acre, and average it.
   - Divide square feet by 43,560 for acres, divide sale price by acres, delete outliers that do not line up, and run =AVERAGE down the dollars-per-acre column [REtipster — rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:05:19].
3. Haircut List Data and Adjust for Improvements
   - Discount list-price comps by days on market, then add or subtract for septic, well, site prep, and wetlands.
   - Take up to 15 percent off inside 90 days, around 20 to 30 percent at three to six months, 35 percent or more past six months, and skip actives older than a year [Sumner Advanced — 8pB5HoHJ4Bk @ 00:04:05]; credit installed septic, well, and site prep the way Alex Reese added about $30,000 over a raw comp [Acrewell Land Company — TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:08:06].
4. Verify by Phone, Then Set the Offer
   - Get a quick-sale number from a land-specialist agent and back the offer out of conservative retail.
   - Find the land specialist on LandWatch and ask what the parcel sells for in 30 days [Brent Bowers — JNufnuENoDM @ 00:08:59], then work backward from conservative retail, as with 8 acres at $5,000 an acre for $40,000 retail and a $30,000 to $35,000 max buy [Cool Wholesaling+ Land Deals-Real Estate Investing — 3x8J7AdyCEw @ 00:03:12].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is over which free portal deserves to be the primary comp engine. Cool Wholesaling+ Land Deals argues Zillow now works really well for comping and says Jasper's team runs almost exclusively on it without even paying for PropStream [3x8J7AdyCEw @ 00:00:01]. REtipster's Seth backs Zillow for scouting a new county because it serves the most markets and has the best mapping and filtering functionality [E9KBudZB1Do @ 00:00:30], but for hard per-acre math the order flips: Zillow is a poor fit because it does not consistently put acreages in its listings, so the Redfin spreadsheet download comes first, with LandWatch as the backup [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:13:46]. LandSpeed Price Boss skips the house portals entirely, running a LandWatch advanced search by county with a 0 to 50,000 price band and land-only property type, sorting by most recent so the results are not skewed toward the cheapest ads, the priciest ads or the sellers who paid to float to the top of the page [FOpRZxfMs4I @ 00:01:32]. The real choice is about data shape. Zillow suits map-based eyeballing in thick markets where sold pins are plentiful. Redfin exports or scraped LandWatch pages suit operators who want a clean column of prices divided by a column of acres and averaged across dozens of rows.

Practitioners also disagree on how far back and how wide to cast the net. DannyG Real Estate starts at six months sold, as tight as possible, and only expands if nothing turns up, because starting narrow finds the best possible comp [UqPvFZ6TGGo @ 00:00:46]. Brent Bowers also anchors on six months because a lot can change in that window, stretching to nine months only when land comps are scarce, and insists on a tight apples-to-apples size band of 9 to 11 acres for a 10-acre subject [JNufnuENoDM @ 00:03:14]. WE ARE LAND SPECIALISTS runs the opposite default: kill the price filter, pull the last 12 months of lots and land, and if the pocket is thin, push Zillow or the county GIS sales layer out to 24 months [JRa_lwdSNjo @ 00:10:48]. Once Upon a Brick sets a far looser size band, telling a buyer comping a 2-acre parcel to search 1 to 10 acres and then average about five similar sales rather than trusting any single one [Tmf12E9Dg7A @ 00:02:01]. The tension is volume versus relevance. Narrow windows protect against stale prices in markets that are moving, but when the sold pins run dry, the tight operators end up expanding anyway, while the wide-net operators accept noisier per-acre numbers and lean on averaging to smooth the spread.

The sharpest numeric disagreement is how hard to haircut asking prices. REtipster applies a 20 to 30 percent discount when forced to use for-sale listings in thin markets [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:02:42]. Sumner Advanced builds a sliding scale off days on market in non-disclosure states: a listing that went contingent within about 90 days gets 0 to 15 percent, three to six months roughly 20 to 30 percent, six months to a year 35 percent or as much as 50 percent, and anything active longer than a year gets thrown out of the discount math entirely [8pB5HoHJ4Bk @ 00:04:05]. Acrewell Land Company goes much further, calling asking prices made-up numbers that can run two, three or 400 percent of market value, with sellers accepting offers 50 to 80 percent below ask, often without even countering [TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:03:07]. Brent Bowers frames it as a national rule of thumb that people ask a little more than they actually get, yet the example given has a 10,000 ask on five acres hopefully landing around 5,000 [JNufnuENoDM @ 00:04:31]. Which scale an operator adopts directly sets the retail anchor, and every offer built downstream inherits that error in either direction.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Acrewell Land Company's Alex Reese documents how badly list prices mislead. A 0.62-acre lot in Citrus Springs, Florida came to market at 75,000 and was cut to 42,000 within three months, a 44 percent drop, while sitting at 119 days on Zillow with 26 views, 18 saves and no buyer [TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:01:13]. A Middleburg lot at 5309 Chickory Circle went from an 85,000 ask to a 20,000 sale after two and a half years and three separate listings, never drawing a buyer until the price fell below 28,000 [TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:05:46]. The cause was physical: roughly 40 percent wetlands and flood zone, with the remaining ground in Kinault peat, a very poorly drained soil that may force an alternative septic system at much higher cost [TbH_4hSsYHU @ 00:06:46]. For Land and Country hits the same wall on a 47.53-acre Lebanon, Tennessee listing, where the stream corridor is mapped as floodway that cannot be built in at all, shrinking the usable building envelope toward the road frontage and handing the buyer leverage to push for a better price [7FfLOUMhyyA @ 00:04:11]. The trap is comping a lot by acreage and address alone. Two parcels in the same subdivision at the same size can sit tens of thousands apart once wet ground, drainage and buildable area are measured.

Non-disclosure states are where comp math breaks first. Sumner Advanced warns that the MLS in a state like Texas shows the last list price where a sold property went under contract, not what it closed at, so reading a 149 last list price as a 149 sale is the core mistake [8pB5HoHJ4Bk @ 00:00:52]. Apke Brothers ran straight into this on a 70-acre Tate County, Mississippi subdivide under contract at 135,000 plus a 5,000 survey: a nearby 9.4-acre parcel listed in late 2023 probably closed around 35,000, which did not make them feel good when they needed 40,000 to 50,000 per 10-acre lot to double their money [bIqgihxHoo8 @ 00:04:41]. With too few comps to justify the price, they called realtors and got a 50,000 to 70,000 opinion; eight months in, four parcels averaged 62,000 for about 240,000 back on roughly 140,000, with three left listed at 50,000 to 65,000 to move fast [bIqgihxHoo8 @ 00:06:27]. Sumner frames the cost of thin data as time: a non-disclosure county might take two mail campaigns to get pricing right versus one in a disclosure state, with offer feedback filling the gap [8pB5HoHJ4Bk @ 00:06:22]. The economics check: when the only sold signal is a guessed close below your per-parcel break-even, the deal rides on realtor opinion until real sales land.

The rest of the firsthand mistakes are process errors. Brent Bowers locked up a lot for 285 dollars with no idea how to comp it, got a 45-day estimate of about 10,000 from a local realtor, then took that same realtor's 5,000 offer and closed four days later, a fast win that still left roughly half of the realtor's own retail estimate on the table [JNufnuENoDM @ 00:07:44]. REtipster admits to scraping LandWatch without first restricting the search to sold comps, which mixed asking prices into the output, and repeatedly deletes outliers that would otherwise throw off the per-acre average [rYCHQ59jzhY @ 00:13:34]. Cool Wholesaling+ Land Deals comped an 8-acre Fairmont, North Carolina lot at 5,000 to 6,000 per acre, roughly 40,000 to 48,000 retail, and found the wholesale price at or above retail once ponds, drainage and a zoning change back to residential were factored in, forcing a 30,000 to 35,000 max [3x8J7AdyCEw @ 00:02:26]. WE ARE LAND SPECIALISTS throws out a pocket comp because it was a waterfront lot with a boat slip, a premium the subject cannot share [JRa_lwdSNjo @ 00:04:48]. Each error points the same way: unscreened comps inflate retail, and inflated retail erases the spread.
