## The Practitioner Consensus: Terms Sell the Dirt, Buyers Are the Asset

Land sells fastest and fattest on terms, and the practitioners who move volume say so without hedging. Seth Williams frames seller financing as the income route that brings a higher price plus interest while keeping the operator out of the landlord business. The buyer owns the parcel, so 'you're not a landlord' [JL60o3hoi_w @ 00:02:17]. Seth ties the whole model to a lending gap: banks avoid raw-land loans without a development plan. The seller who carries the paper therefore captures a higher price, a faster sale, interest, closing fees, and servicing fees on the same dirt [JL60o3hoi_w @ 00:12:45]. Abhi of Gokce Capital runs a seven-figure operation nearly 800 deals deep. Abhi sells roughly 80% on terms and 20% for cash, treats 2023 and 2024 as exceptions, and calls it 'very hard' to sustain a land business without monthly terms income [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:09:39]. The Seller Finance Consultants panel lands the same point from the lending side. No conforming loan exists over four to five acres, so large acreage moves only on cash or seller financing [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 01:48:25]. The operating consensus is blunt: the bankless buyer is the default buyer for vacant land. The note is the product being sold, and the parcel is the collateral that makes the note possible.

The second point of agreement is that the buyer list, not the parcel, carries the business. Abhi tracks acquisition costs that climbed from about $5 per Facebook lead to $800 to $1,200, with direct mail rising alongside [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:42:14]. Abhi concludes that lifetime value is the only lever an operator actually controls. A $500 acquisition cost against $5,000 of lifetime gross profit leaves $4,500 to work with [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:14:53]. Abhi's answer is a customer success program: monthly check-in calls, a birthday call, and permission-based contact that never leads with an offer. The reasoning is that in the age of AI, 'human touch is our competitive advantage' [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:49:44]. Daniil Kleyman's panel frames the same buyer pool from the pricing side. They say seller financing 'has been proven to sell properties for 15% more especially in the lower uh price point markets' [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 00:50:55]. Both camps converge on one rule. The people who cannot or will not use a bank are the market, and the premium they pay for access to terms is the margin. Once leads stop being cheap, the real work is keeping those borrowers paying, referring, and coming back. Abhi estimates repeat buyers at roughly a fifth of sales, a share nobody on the team formally tracks yet.

On compliance, the practitioners working vacant land reach the same practical baseline. The heaviest Dodd-Frank rules hang on an owner-occupied dwelling, and a bare parcel is not one. Seth Williams reads Regulation Z Subpart E as limited to a consumer's principal dwelling, defined as a one-to-four unit residential structure. Seth concludes that 'vacant land seems to be excluded' from its originator, balloon, and ability-to-repay restrictions, and says more than one attorney agreed [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:09:01]. The Seller Finance Consultants panel states flatly that vacant land and investor buyers are exempt, and that the real test is owner occupancy [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 01:15:07]. William Bronchick has been an attorney since 1990 and has closed more than 1,000 owner-finance deals. Bronchick confines Dodd-Frank to residential sales to owner-occupant buyers, mobile homes included. Investor sales, commercial deals, and short lease-options fall outside it [MNznbFmtOwI @ 00:07:19]. The shared trap is the buyer who moves onto the land. Bronchick warns that coverage can reach raw land and contracts for deed when the buyer occupies within one year [ZGvGq-_10yA @ 00:03:45]. The practical consequence for a land seller is simple. Whether a note sits inside or outside the dwelling rules depends on who ends up living on the ground and when, not on what the deed says.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Abhi's unit economics are the cleanest template for terms deals: buy around $10,000, sell around $20,000, and never go above $50,000 [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:55:43]. Loans average $14,000 to $19,000. The typical buyer puts 10% down and pays about $500 a month [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:48:02]. Instead of selling notes or borrowing from funders, Abhi pulls cash forward with an early-payoff cashback on four-year loans. Borrowers get 5% back for paying off within six months, or 2.5% within twelve. About 10% of borrowers take it, and Abhi calls it cheaper than borrowing [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:11:17]. A higher down payment earns a lower rate. Against competitors advertising '$1 down, $199 a month,' the script is that such a structure takes ten years to become a landowner. Seth Williams shows why big down payments matter on cheap dirt. On a parcel bought for $500 and sold for $8,000, the down payment alone recovers the cost, which changes how much risk the seller is willing to carry [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:22:58]. Abhi's sales floor runs on speed. Leads get a response within 60 seconds, phones stay open 9am to 9pm, and a typical sale is a seven-call close. Each call aims for a hard yes or a hard no, because a maybe counts as failure.

The paper itself gets engineered around the dwelling trigger. Seth Williams recommends loan documents that require payoff before the buyer improves the property. Seth repeats the clause for RESPA, which exempts vacant-land loans unless a structure or manufactured home goes on the land within two years using loan proceeds [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:17:36]. Bronchick's version is a one-year no-residence clause on raw land [ZGvGq-_10yA @ 00:26:56]. Advertising is where Seth treats the rules as live, because Regulation N reads 'real property or a dwelling.' An ad quoting 5% interest, $2,000 down, and $499 a month locks those terms at closing. The workaround is a vague line: 'seller financing may be available on this property, contact the owner for more information' [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:27:41]. On privacy, Seth keeps intake to name, address, and phone, which stays clear of nonpublic personal information. Collecting an SSN, driver's license, or passport pulls that information in, and sharing it then requires an opt-out disclosure. Pulling a credit report only needs the FTC's no-opt-out form, which Seth calls fairly easy to fill out. When a deal does touch an owner-occupant, Bronchick paid a licensed loan originator $500 and added the fee to the buyer's closing costs [MNznbFmtOwI @ 00:25:25]. The paper stays in the seller's hands, and the compliance cost lands on the buyer.

Drone media is where the parcel gets sold visually, and the shooters agree the asset is the acreage, not the house. Matt Wood was shooting 35 acres with a lake. Wood starts with Google Earth, FAA restrictions, and a survey, then sets D-Log, ISO 500, shutter around 120, ND16, Sunny white balance, AEB 5, 24fps, and 4K [ZU1aoe6pwQI @ 00:03:23]. Wood keeps two-thirds horizon in frame. The flight pattern is two orbits, the second 50 to 100 feet higher, followed by low-and-slow passes that combine three simultaneous movements. Wood reviews the footage before leaving the property [ZU1aoe6pwQI @ 00:08:05]. Rob of Next Move runs one battery through four jobs on the Mavic 4 Pro. The 28mm wide lens is the 'map,' shot with a high push and a quarter orbit. The 70mm is the portrait lens, and the 168mm shoots from a distance to stay off the neighbors. Rob finishes with roughly ten-second vertical 9:16 clips and sells the bundle as a premium 'triple lens listing pass' [HrG-12X_ds8 @ 00:03:10]. Wyman Gentry prices aerials as an add-on: $125 for five photos and $225 for ten. The shoot takes about five minutes and requires Part 107 certification. Gentry steers clients away from drone shots when the neighbors are junky or a freeway sits next door [ItWPpkhNHto @ 00:03:51].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether Dodd-Frank touches a vacant-land note at all. Seth Williams of REtipster breaks with the early land-investor line that the law simply does not apply, then lands on a narrow reading: the Subpart E balloon, originator, and ability-to-repay rules key off a consumer's principal dwelling, defined as a one-to-four unit residential structure, so 'vacant land seems to be excluded from all of these special rules listed out in subpart E' [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:09:01]. The Seller Finance Consultants broker panel agrees from the licensing side, stating flatly that vacant land and investor buyers sit outside the rules [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 01:15:07]. William Bronchick draws the line by occupancy rather than property type: if the buyer moves onto the property within one year, the rules can reach raw land and installment contracts for deed [ZGvGq-_10yA @ 00:03:45]. Joe Crump sits at the far end, telling viewers the act is mainly focused on securities and private money, that ethical disclosure already satisfies it, and not to worry, while still adding 'I'm not saying don't comply' [mtE3-cvmAN0 @ 00:00:41]. For a land operator, every comfortable reading above depends on the buyer never occupying the parcel while the note is open, which means the loan documents, not the operator's hopes, have to carry that restriction.

The second fault line is whether to engineer around the deal limits or pay a licensed originator on every owner-occupant sale. William Bronchick teaches the counting game: one free pass per year for a natural person, estate, or trust, three per year for any entity under the eight-part test, and roughly nine deals a year for an operator running three LLCs [MNznbFmtOwI @ 00:20:23]. Seller Finance Consultants calls that structure 'a blatant attempt to evade,' putting multiple entities, forbearances, lease options, and land-trust titling in the same bucket [SB_vfxPYuF8 @ 00:00:43]. Their broker panel also brands the three-loan exemption a 'worthless exemption' because it bans the balloon, and Daniil Kleyman explains the cost: the plan is to season a note a few months and sell it, and that resale needs a balloon [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 00:37:12]. Their fix is routing every deal through an MLO, since an MLO can create a balloon that a non-MLO seller cannot, while the buyer pays the fee, so the seller should 'take the free insurance' [SB_vfxPYuF8 @ 00:02:45]. The split is not about cost, since both camps push the originator fee onto the buyer; it is about whether the note must survive a resale.

Practitioners also split hard on what to do with the paper after closing. Abhi of Gokce Capital, whose sales run roughly 80% terms across nearly 800 deals, deliberately does not sell notes [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:13:32]. Instead, the team pulls cash forward with an early-payoff cashback on a four-year loan: 5% back if paid off within six months, 2.5% within twelve, taken by about 10% of borrowers, which Abhi treats as cheaper than borrowing [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:11:17]. The Seller Finance Consultants model runs the opposite direction: the San Diego case resold at $437,500 with 30% down on an 8% note amortized over 30 years and due in five, then sold that note after three payments at a 12-15% investor yield for a $49,277.63 profit [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 01:01:58]. The Seller Financing, Real Estate & Note Investing channel splits the difference, declaring the old flip of 10% down and dumping the 90% note at 94 cents dead, and backing a 12-36 month partial sale that keeps the tail, with paper seasoned six to twelve months first [X6Bv1fbSvBk @ 00:03:06]. The choice tracks the business: a land shop selling $10,000 parcels for $20,000 lives on monthly terms income, while a house flipper carrying a $218,000 note needs the capital back to fund the next acquisition.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The Dodd-Frank downside is concrete in the numbers practitioners quote. The Seller Finance Consultants panel puts it bluntly: 'the penalty is the recision of the note and interest forgiven,' meaning an operator can lose the 10-30% down payment plus every payment collected, with licenses also at risk [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 00:47:31]. They add a resale hit before any lawsuit: a badly written $200,000 note facing a 30% discount is a $60,000 loss [gSHz85xxIz4 @ 00:36:36]. William Bronchick stresses that Dodd-Frank is not regulatory, so the threat is a private civil action rather than arrest, but the clock runs longer than it looks: the statute of limitations is three years, yet a buyer can raise the violation as a foreclosure offset ten years later [MNznbFmtOwI @ 00:13:40]. In the webinar version, Bronchick frames the bet as losing less than 1% of the time versus complying 100% of the time, and says no reported cases have surfaced [ZGvGq-_10yA @ 00:28:37]. A Houston investor Bronchick describes sells about 40 houses a year at around $60,000 and would simply give the house back if challenged. The economics check is blunt: the exposure lands exactly when an operator needs the note most, during default and foreclosure.

Land operators carry traps of their own. Seth Williams warns that the vacant-land exclusion from the dwelling rules evaporates if the buyer builds, so the loan documents should require payoff before the buyer improves the property [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:09:52]. William Bronchick reaches the same place from the occupancy side with a one-year no-residence clause on raw land [ZGvGq-_10yA @ 00:26:56]. Advertising is the quieter trap: Regulation N reaches 'real property or a dwelling,' so a listing promising 5% interest, $2,000 down, and $499 a month locks those terms in, and changing them at the closing table is the violation; the escape is a vague line that seller financing may be available [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:26:44]. Data collection is the third decision point. Name, address, and phone carry no nonpublic personal information, but an SSN, driver's license, or passport does, and sharing it triggers opt-out paperwork; Seth weighs that friction against margin, and on a $500 buy resold for $8,000 where the down payment alone repays the cost, the risk is 'not a big deal' [m1GoTr9J5TM @ 00:22:58]. Bronchick's clause, Seth's payoff clause, and vague ad copy cost nothing to add and close the three easiest doors to a claim.

The operating failures are about cost and speed more than law. Abhi reports customer acquisition cost climbing from about $5 per Facebook lead to $800-$1,200, with direct mail rising too, which is why Gokce Capital leans on lifetime value instead of cheap leads [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 00:42:14]. Speed is the other leak: a weekend voicemail delay lost a sale when the buyer spent the money on a Walmart TV instead, which backs the team's 60-second lead-response rule and 9am-9pm phone coverage [HpkNhY7WclQ @ 01:06:48]. Media agencies report the same pattern in people and paperwork. ACRE Partner says the agency lost its best creatives six to eight times and now hands members hiring templates, interview scripts, and SOPs to rebuild faster [x1a5YxEWplQ @ 00:07:22]. It also warns that brokerage partnerships launched without paperwork or clear expectations cost tens of thousands of dollars [x1a5YxEWplQ @ 00:06:36]. The common thread is that the first collapse point in both businesses is response capacity, not lead supply: Abhi added three hires to cover 150-200 active loans, and ACRE pushes owners at $20-40K a month to hire before they can reach $50-100K. Underfunded follow-up and undocumented handoffs leak margin faster than any market shift.
