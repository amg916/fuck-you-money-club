## Executive Summary

Land flippers agree on the core trade: buy vacant parcels from apathetic, free-and-clear owners, often inherited or absentee, at a deep discount and resell to builders, investors, or retail buyers. REtipster's Seth Williams teaches buying at 5% to 30% of value, while Clayton Hepler offers 50% to 55% and Ron Apke now buys near 80% and manufactures margin by subdividing. The street mechanics are numeric: counties with at least 200 land sales a year, days on market under 90 to 120, parcels scrubbed of the 40% to 50% with wetlands, flood, slope, or bad access, and offers built from median price per acre minus agent, closing, and lender costs. Texting beats mail for Landon Bleau, roughly 9x versus 3x return on ad spend. Disagreements remain over mail versus texting and over assigning contracts versus taking title.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator in this lane runs the same core arbitrage: buy dirt from an owner who no longer cares about it, at a price far below what a retail buyer or builder will pay, then resell fast. Seth Williams of REtipster frames the classic spread as offering 5% to 30% of market value and reselling at 50% to 80%, a 'win-win-win' where sellers who refuse that range are 'probably not the right person' [Zu8LEqQ-L0w @ 00:01:15]. At REWBCON, Williams widens the buy band to 10% to 40%, arguing the property is worth more the day you buy it [pBstDNy9IuM @ 00:08:18]. The spread has compressed as the market matured. Clayton Hepler on the DealMachine Podcast offers 50% to 55% of expected sale price on flips and 65% to 70% on double closes, with a $25K minimum, aiming to double the money [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:13:00]. Ron Apke has pushed further into a project model, buying around 80% of market value and manufacturing the margin by subdividing or adding a mobile home before selling above market [Z_ZynszdCFo @ 00:02:33]. The consensus is not one fixed percentage; it is that the margin lives in the gap between an apathetic seller's price and a motivated buyer's, and the operator's job is to sit in that gap.

The practitioners agree on who sells and why the asset class still works. Williams points out that banks rarely finance vacant lots, so most owners hold free and clear with no lender forcing a price, and that only about 6% of US land is developed [pBstDNy9IuM @ 00:06:52]. On Launch Control, Williams describes the 'seasoned' owner: someone who bought a dream parcel 20 to 30 years ago, inherited it, or is tired of paying the taxes, and who 'just don't care about it anymore' [4xzZ7rKlL4E @ 00:06:50]. Thomas, who reports a $63,000 month, describes the same seller as an inherited or absentee owner paying roughly $600 a year in taxes, someone who trades price for speed and certainty the way a driver accepts $5k from a dealer instead of $8k selling privately [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:25:05]. Hidden Money flags the identical signals for list building: delinquent taxes, out-of-state owners, decades of ownership, and estates [hWcjM0400as @ 00:00:52]. Thomas adds that land sellers carry less emotion and sign simpler contracts than house sellers, and puts the competition gap bluntly: 'for every 99 household sellers, there's one land flipper,' and most of those flippers only use the builder exit.

None of these operators sells land as passive income. REtipster's short take is blunt: 'This is not a passive process at all,' because acquisitions demand zoning, title, survey, and environmental work, many sellers decline deep discounts, and even owned lots need mowing, drainage fixes, and trespasser control, though it stays '10 times easier' than houses [WmRK4kG81e4 @ 00:00:47]. Ron Apke tells operators to plan for five years, not six months, to distrust 30-day promises, and to treat the business as a plan executed with consistency, since 'if you turn off your marketing, your leads are going to get turned off as well' [Z_ZynszdCFo @ 00:06:09]. Hepler dates the shift: the 2020 to 2022 market 'forgave bad underwriting,' and now operations and capital stacks decide who survives, with weak operators selling below what they underwrote [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:04:15]. Thomas lists who should stay out: anyone who won't pick up the phone, needs money within two weeks, avoids volume, or wants to get rich quick, and calls the role being 'an operator of a machine' that must run at least five days a week [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 00:33:03]. The shared baseline is a disciplined, volume-driven acquisitions machine, not a side bet.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Market selection is the first gate, and the screens are numeric. Apke goes rural, two to three counties outside a metro, and wants real transactions, days on market under about 120, a healthy sell-through rate, limited inventory, and county population density under 500 [Z_ZynszdCFo @ 00:04:04]. Hepler runs the same play on Zillow: sell-through above 100% a year within an acreage band, 25 to 250 or 300 active listings, tight pricing, and days on market under 90 rated good, 90 to 120 middling, and over 120 slow; 30 listings with one sale is unhealthy [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:07:59]. Thomas ignores days on market entirely because agents post lazy land listings, and instead demands at least 200 land sales a year, fewer actives than solds, and a sell-through target of 60% at six months and about 120% at one year [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:56:40]. Pitt County, NC, at 172 active versus 272 sold with reasonable price per acre, is 'absolutely' a market to spend in, while Harris County at 2,700 active versus 800 sold and Wake County's savvy, builder-saturated owners get rejected; median price per acre typically runs $10k to $50k [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 02:01:22]. Thin data lies: one Georgia county hit its sell-through targets on just eight sales and was still cut.

List quality is where land splits from house wholesaling. Thomas pulls 0.2 to 100 acres, 10-plus years of ownership, no corporate owners, no landlocked parcels, a minimum of roughly 30 feet of road frontage, plus wetland and flood limits, inside a land-specific tool like Land Insights or Land Portal, warning that skip tracing at 'less than a penny' buys junk data [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 02:05:04]. Hepler expects 40% to 50% of raw parcels to be bad, wetlands, flood plain, slope, flag lots, and scrubs them first; benchmarks run 6,000 to 8,000 mailers, about six weeks of cold calls, or 30,000 to 40,000 texts per deal at roughly $25K average profit and 5 to 10x return on ad spend [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:09:38]. Landon Bleau sends about 50,000 texts a week and 10,000 to 20,000 handwritten blind-offer mailers a month; texting produces 60% to 70% of deals at roughly 9x return versus about 3x for mail [Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:38:55]. Thomas's own funnel: 20,000 texts a month yields 57 offers and one to three qualified leads a week, about 10% of which close within 30 days, run through Smarter Contact with a simple 'Are you open to an offer?' opener [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 04:32:27].

The offer math is explicit. Thomas comps by median price per acre within acreage bands: a 5.7-acre sale at $61k gives $10,700 an acre, times 8.8 acres is $94,500, discounted about 10% to an $85k resale; after a 7% agent fee, $2,500 closing, and a $950 transactional lender, a 70% offer of $59k leaves roughly a $16k spread, and the seller gets texted a range near 60% to 65% [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 02:41:01]. Exit choice follows the deal: assign to a builder when the spread is thin, like $100k in and $105k out on a Nashville lot, or double close same day through a transactional lender on MLS deals and fat spreads, since the lender fee kills a $2k to $3k margin [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:07:08]. Hepler's two-call close targets doubling the money, offering 50% to 55% of expected sale for a flip, so a $60K property gets a $31K offer, after checking acreage band, proximity, characteristics, price, utilities, survey, deeded access, and buildability [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:13:00]. Hidden Money's beginner version: offer $6K cash on $15K land, budget $500 to $1,000 for title, close in about two weeks, and list slightly below market at $12,999 [hWcjM0400as @ 00:02:46].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Screen the County
   - Pull active listings, sold listings, and median price per acre for each candidate county.
   - Require at least 200 land sales a year, fewer actives than solds, and sell-through near 60% at six months and about 120% at one year, with median price per acre roughly $10k to $50k [Thomas | Land Flipping — ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:56:40]
2. Pull and Scrub the List
   - Filter parcels inside a land-specific data tool, scrub the junk, then skip trace.
   - Use 0.2 to 100 acres, 10-plus years owned, no corporate owners, no landlocked parcels, about 30 feet of frontage, and wetland and flood limits; expect to scrub 40% to 50% of raw parcels [Thomas | Land Flipping — ZfyoS572Kbk @ 02:05:04] [DealMachine Podcast — HE45qWS8txg @ 00:09:38]
3. Run Volume Outreach
   - Send short opener texts at volume, tag every no, and comp only the hand-raisers.
   - Open with 'Are you open to an offer?', tag no replies for later follow-up, and treat it as a funnel; texting runs about 9x return on ad spend versus about 3x for mail [Thomas | Land Flipping — ZfyoS572Kbk @ 02:15:37] [REtipster — Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:38:55]
4. Comp, Offer, Contract, Exit
   - Price by median price per acre, back out costs, sign a protected contract, and let the deal pick the exit.
   - Comp in acreage bands, subtract agent, closing, and lender costs, offer 60% to 70%, sign with $100 to $500 earnest money held by title and fully refundable through the inspection period, then choose assignment or double close [Thomas | Land Flipping — ZfyoS572Kbk @ 03:03:27]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Practitioners split hardest on how deep to buy. Ron Apke has moved to a project model, buying at about 80% of market value and then manufacturing the margin by subdividing or adding a mobile home before selling above market [Z_ZynszdCFo @ 00:02:33]. Clayton Hepler runs a tighter flip formula: offer 50% to 55% of expected sale price for a straight flip and 65% to 70% when a double close covers the spread, with a $25K profit floor, so a $60K property gets a $31K offer [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:13:00]. Seth Williams sits at the opposite pole, teaching offers at 5% to 30% of market value and resale at 50% to 80%, and stating that sellers who refuse that range are probably not the right person [Zu8LEqQ-L0w @ 00:01:15]. Landon Bleau shows how the number drifts with the market: offers ran 40% to 45% of value in 2024 and now climb to 70% on subdividable land, while average gross profit per deal rose from $18K to $28K and the minimum purchase sits around $30K to $40K [Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:35:47]. The practical read: deep-discount offers fit small, fast cash flips, while high-percentage offers only work when the operator adds value through splits, entitlements, or a double-close spread.

The lead-channel fight is just as sharp. Thomas runs a text-first machine and says flatly, "I don't mess with any direct mail," pushing beginners to SMS because "money loves speed" and reporting roughly 120,000 texts across 61 lists in Smarter Contact [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:47:02]. Landon Bleau still mails 10,000 to 20,000 handwritten blind offers a month, but the scoreboard favors phones: about 9x return on ad spend from texting versus about 3x from mail, with texting producing 60% to 70% of deals [Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:38:55]. Olufemi Ajose builds the opposite case, crediting $1M in profit to direct mail and arguing that branded envelopes, a logo, QR code, headshot, and investment-firm copy aimed at lawyers, doctors, and portfolio holders unlock six-figure spreads; the rule is to mail weekly for 6 to 8 months instead of blasting 12,000 to 15,000 pieces once [tF_EbilAGj4 @ 00:15:19]. Seth Williams lands in the middle, calling direct mail the most competitive and expensive channel while listing texting, cold calling, ringless voicemail, and email as cheaper alternatives [4xzZ7rKlL4E @ 00:14:34]. The split tracks deal size: Ajose's mail hunts big-asset owners, while Thomas and Bleau chase volume and speed.

Exit structure and sourcing draw a third set of disagreements. Thomas treats assignment and same-day double close as interchangeable tools, using transactional funding for MLS deals or large spreads such as a $50K buy against a $120K sale, and insisting that the deal picks the exit [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:05:23]. Alex Reese prefers to take title outright, warning that assignments are time-intensive and get more fragile the longer they run [iC5tSNnegIw @ 01:14:34]. On listed land, Thomas calls lowballing on-market and FSBO sellers a low return on time, while Reese fires high volumes of informal text offers at overpriced listings and put an $85K listing under contract at $20K on land worth about $45K [iC5tSNnegIw @ 01:29:07]. Clayton Hepler dismisses the builder infill lane Thomas started in, calling roughly $2,500 per deal the worst way to run the business [HE45qWS8txg @ 00:07:26]. Valuation splits too: Thomas comps by median price per acre inside acreage bands, while Reese says price per acre is irrelevant for residential land, where location, neighborhood home values, zoning, topography, and soil set the number. Even the market screen differs, with Hepler wanting under 90 days on market and Thomas ignoring days on market because agents post lazy land listings.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The dirt itself kills more deals than any negotiation. Ron Apke names soil as the main fallout point: with no city sewer or water, a property needs well and septic, and about 5% of deals die when the land fails the percolation test [Z_ZynszdCFo @ 00:05:02]. Thomas says soils have "killed more of my deals than anything," after paying about $600 per test, and now pushes that verification onto the buyer during the inspection period [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 01:37:27]. Landon Bleau ran an 18% fallout rate in 2024 while skipping perc tests, surveys, and title commitments, a shortcut that only held while properties were going under contract within 48 hours [Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:23:09]. Zoning text is the quieter trap. Alex Reese's worst deal was seven non-conforming lots in Bladen County bought for about $36K, all-in about $40K, where a contiguity clause in the zoning ordinance allowed only 3 homes; the land sold for about $47K and netted roughly $300 [iC5tSNnegIw @ 00:55:20]. Wetlands, floodways, slope, and access that only looks real round out the list, and the operators still standing treat every cheap parcel as guilty until the soil, the ordinance, and the road frontage clear.

Capital and hold time form the second failure cluster. Landon Bleau self-funded purchases expecting four-week resales and watched them take four months, locking up cash until moving to 4 lenders with $800K to $1M in liquidity [Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:53:04]. Bleau also admits to doubling mail and texting while adding cold calling and PPC in a single month, having "optimized for gross profit, which was really stupid," and reports a $25K haircut when a $100K listing sold at $75K. Alex Reese warns that roughly $20K of monthly overhead in payroll and mail can lose $15K to $20K in slow months, and tells newcomers to expect break-even only after about a year [iC5tSNnegIw @ 00:09:45]. Seth Williams's Alabama deal, bought at $4,587 and sold at $20,000, still dragged for 15 months with panic price cuts along the way [pBstDNy9IuM @ 00:10:47]. Thomas self-funded a Nevada desert parcel about 45 minutes from Las Vegas instead of using a transactional lender, cleared about $8K, and judged the hassle not worth it [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 04:00:56]. The economics check is blunt: a paper margin means nothing when capital is frozen and overhead keeps billing every month.

Process failures quietly bleed the rest. Thomas ranks failing to follow up as the number one mistake, noting that someone who says no in April can turn around and sell in June, and warns that 72-hour expiring contracts backfire because owners paying around $300 a year in taxes feel no urgency [ZfyoS572Kbk @ 03:09:08]. Ron Apke's biggest margin regret was missing highest-and-best use: subdividing could have produced 4x instead of a double, though chasing every $10,000 margin stalls investors who should still favor quick projects [Z_ZynszdCFo @ 00:07:02]. Seller financing carries a hidden default bill; Seth Williams puts the odds at 40% or 50% that loosely approved buyers default, calls it a huge hassle, and dropped no-pulse approvals in favor of underwriting and large down payments [4xzZ7rKlL4E @ 00:27:38]. Hiring can be the most expensive mistake of all: Landon Bleau says a poorly trained US-based acquisition manager cost $200K to $300K, and year-one skip tracing alone ran about $60K [Uaqe67xWZN0 @ 00:58:07]. Closing carries its own trap, with seller-impersonation scams forcing extra verification on web leads. Thomas also reports that nine out of every 10 deals hit some issue at closing, which makes an investor-friendly closer that has handled same-day double closes a requirement rather than a luxury.
