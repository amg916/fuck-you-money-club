## Executive Summary

Commercial roofers and rank-and-rent builders agree that leads are expensive, perishable, and worthless without disciplined follow-up and source tracking. Chris Bohn still works 15-year-old leads and demands same-day response, while ProLine and Adam Bensman insist on matching the sales process to the lead source. Rank-and-rent operators converge on owning ranked exact-match-domain sites, routing calls through $10 tracking numbers with whisper messages, and sending free leads before pitching. Ippei's math values a lead at about $42 under a 7% commission, puts running costs at roughly $30 per site per month, and reports a $20,000 commission from one $600,000 commercial roofing job. The splits are real: Matt Wacek prefers network routing to escape cold outreach and rent ceilings, Saravanan Ganesh skips GBPs entirely, and John Jenkins argues branding beats lead generation for roofers.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Roofing-side operators converge on one hard truth: a lead is a perishable, expensive asset, and the money is made in follow-up, not acquisition. Chris Bohn of True Colors Industrial, working the American WeatherStar program out of Grand Rapids, wants a same-day response because a prospect left waiting a week forgets you and collects two or three other quotes [sY92btEz6VQ @ 00:00:56]. He then refuses to ever label a lead dead: his shop still works leads going back 15 years and keeps fielding callbacks six to eight years later [sY92btEz6VQ @ 00:15:42]. ProLine's James lands on the same position from the software side, warning that most roofers massively mess up lead generation by chasing only the hottest prospects while ignoring people six months to five years out, and bluntly adding that you can have the most leads in the world and be trash at sales [XH8rj3P6nIs @ 00:10:58]. Adam Bensman of The STRONG Roofer frames the fix as matching the sales process to the source, noting that referral leads close at higher rates than cold channels, so the follow-up playbook should not be identical across doors, ads, and introductions [eQNS1yN6pQM @ 00:08:43]. The baseline prerequisite, everyone agrees, is a disciplined system that tracks every lead source and revisits every name on a schedule.

On the rank-and-rent side, the core mechanic every builder repeats is that you own the ranked website and rent out its phone calls, which is why Ippei pitches the model over running an AI agency where clients can simply fire you [wU1OKeR3fvI @ 00:00:15]. The plumbing is identical across practitioners: a call tracking number sits on the site, and a whisper message tells the answering business where the call came from. Ippei buys local-area-code numbers at $10 a month with the whisper included, then sends two to three free leads before moving the owner onto commission and, after two or three months, onto a flat fee [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:20:37]. Digital Real Estate Secrets runs the same play earlier in the ranking curve, routing calls with a whisper once the site climbs from page two toward page one, without telling the business first, because you cannot sell somebody leads when you do not have them [cSXYPhrwhwo @ 00:02:32]. Matt Wacek describes the alternative swap method, either overlaying a client lightbox with software or swapping logos and numbers, and calls the recurring revenue the biggest pro because businesses rarely bite the hand that feeds them [neFMrCCbe5k @ 00:01:36]. Real lead flow first, pitch second, is the shared order of operations.

Niche and city filters line up tightly. Ippei wants phone-driven local services rather than location-bound businesses like dentists, sets a floor of 50K population, calls 100K to 300K regions the sweet spot, and tells beginners to target $1,000 to $3,000 average customer value [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:08:34]. James Ranks independently lands on cities of 100K to 250K and tells builders to avoid seasonal niches entirely [PACpsGjR7Es @ 00:10:35]. Wacek reinforces the seasonality warning from a churn angle: lawn care and Christmas lights renters drop off out of season, so either charge enough to cover the whole year or hedge with evergreen niches like plumbing, electrician, and house cleaning [neFMrCCbe5k @ 00:04:48]. The exact-match domain is the other near-universal weapon. Saravanan Ganesh runs EMD only with no GMB and says the approach works even in saturated plumbing and HVAC markets [GHgraINYcOU @ 00:00:01], while James Ranks treats a taken EMD as a reason to skip the keyword and Ippei tells students to weaponize the EMD with keywords in the title and H1 through H3 tags. Roofing itself gets flagged as a beginner trap: Ippei lists roofing alongside Miami lawn care as too competitive to pick, even while his commercial roofing sites produce the biggest checks in his portfolio.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Ippei's pricing menu is the most detailed unit-economics breakdown from any speaker. Commission deals run on call tracking wired by API into the client's CRM, and he calls a 7% cut pretty fair [JuyXW9x-QMs @ 00:04:06]. For pay per call, he prices from the client's own math: 10 leads times a 30% close rate times a $2,000 ticket equals $6,000 in revenue, 7% of that is $420, so each lead is worth about $42, and calls longer than 30 seconds get auto-billed [JuyXW9x-QMs @ 00:08:51]. Flat fees land at $750 to $1,000 a month, a hybrid runs $300 a month plus 5% so ten sites produce roughly $3K monthly, and an upsell charges a $1,000 one-time setup for each extra site inside a 30-mile radius. His lead calculator scales the same logic: a site producing 20 leads a month at $60 a lead is worth renting to the owner for $1,200 a month [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:21:23]. Digital Real Estate Secrets prices from the customer's job value instead, reasoning that wedding DJs earning $750 to $1,500 per wedding make a ten-call site worth at least $500, so he offers an exclusive, single-vendor auto-pay deal at $500 to $750 a month [cSXYPhrwhwo @ 00:06:11].

Build costs stay deliberately thin. Ippei puts each site at around $30 a month, so ten sites run $300 and a single $200 to $300 commission covers the whole portfolio, with leads arriving at two to three months and sites peaking between months three and six [wU1OKeR3fvI @ 00:12:14]. His scale path is 20 sites, then 30, funded by SEO retainers like a massage therapist paying $1,500 a month on a 12-month contract; at 30 sites, $300 per site yields $9,000 a month (about $8,000 net), $750 yields $22,500, and a $1,000 average approaches $30,000. He says each site takes about 10 hours to build with no GBP needed, and sells done-for-you builds at $500 a site or ten for $4,300 [Lr9XDVpYZ8M @ 00:04:39]. Labor comes from Philippine virtual assistants at $5 to $8 an hour, a figure he quotes as $3 to $5 elsewhere [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:25:57]. Saravanan Ganesh's ceiling is more modest: an EMD averaging one to two calls a week with a couple of backlinks, if that, earns roughly $500 a month, so ten sites reach $5,000 [GHgraINYcOU @ 00:11:35]. Against paid search, Ippei's pitch to owners is blunt: $10 to $20 a month for an organically ranked site versus about $20 for a single AdWords click.

Roofing contractors teach a different set of street mechanics. Bohn's commercial proposal leads with 4 to 20 roof photos showing split seams and popped fasteners, followed by a scope of work with a signature page, product info, and proof of insurance [sY92btEz6VQ @ 00:07:54]. At the presentation, scheduled within a week of the evaluation, the salesman signs the proposal, turns it around, and sets the pen down; when budgets stall, Bohn offers to split the roof across two years. ProLine's James runs omnipresent retargeting with 14 unique ads served every seven days by funnel stage, and tells roofers to geofence job sites with ads built on a street-view photo instead of a drone shot, backed by yard signs, door hangers, and referral asks at point of sale and after collection [XH8rj3P6nIs @ 00:07:11]. John Jenkins of The Roof Genius spends $200 to $400 on sponsored ads covering a 50-mile radius whenever a hurricane tracker points toward his area, and starts storm marketing in January for the June through October season [dqnresNFTyg @ 00:32:03]. Bensman caps experimentation with a $3,000 to $10,000 monthly test budget on LSA and PPC, adding one channel at a time instead of scattering $1K, $2K, and $3K across many [eQNS1yN6pQM @ 00:10:37].

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the rank-and-rent camp is whether a Google Business Profile matters at all. Saravanan Ganesh runs exact-match domains only, with 'no GMB,' and claims the play still works inside saturated plumbing and HVAC markets [GHgraINYcOU @ 00:00:01]. Ippei sits close to that camp in practice: only about 20% of his sites carry a GBP, yet his network pulls over 100 calls a day [JuyXW9x-QMs @ 00:02:25]. He still bolts profiles on later through video verification once a site earns traction [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:25:19]. James Ranks takes the opposite stance and stacks both assets, pairing a GBP with an EMD so the operator 'ranks twice' across the map pack and organic results [PACpsGjR7Es @ 00:12:17]. The practical read for an operator: Saravanan's model keeps setup cheap and fast because there is no verification hurdle, while James treats the map listing as a second shot at the same searcher and argues expired GMB domains carry weight because the AI overview pulls from the map. Ippei's middle path treats the profile as an upgrade rather than a launch requirement, pricing the sites that hold one at the top of his $1,000 to $2,000 rent band while most of his builds ship without one and still ring.

How to get paid is the second fault line. Matt Wacek's case against classic rent is blunt: revenue is capped at whatever the site rents for, his examples run $100 to $500 a month, and raising it means renegotiating with the client [neFMrCCbe5k @ 00:02:44]. His alternative routes calls into a pay-per-call network that answers, routes and tracks every call, so there is no cold outreach and no client to please [neFMrCCbe5k @ 00:05:43]. Ippei rejects the ceiling argument with commission deals: a $600,000 commercial roofing customer from one of his sites paid him $20,000 [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:00:14], and he calls a 7% cut 'pretty fair' once call tracking feeds the client's CRM by API [JuyXW9x-QMs @ 00:04:06]. Wacek himself concedes that selling direct to a business pays three to five times the network rate, at the cost of outreach and collections. Digital Real Estate Secrets accepts that trade and leans into it, structuring exclusive single-vendor auto-pay deals priced off job value, such as $500 to $750 a month for a wedding DJ site producing ten calls. The disagreement is about labor, not morality: Wacek optimizes for zero client contact, while Ippei and the DJ operator treat outreach and rerouting leverage as the profit center of the whole model.

On the contractor side, roofing practitioners split over which channel deserves the first dollar. Adam Bensman tells roofers to cut waste, then test LSA and PPC with $3,000 to $10,000 a month, and to delay SEO because AI is a risk and the channel takes about a year to ramp [eQNS1yN6pQM @ 00:10:49]. The Roofing Marketer pitches the reverse, promising 20 to 30 inbound website leads 'without spending a dime' on ads, backed by a New Jersey roofer ranking first in the Maps pack for 'best roofing company near me' [y3fjHcsS478 @ 00:00:00]. John Jenkins of The Roof Genius skips both as a starting point: social media first, one flyer posted in every neighborhood group, door knocking the next day, and Google Ads only 'if you've got the money' [dqnresNFTyg @ 00:31:09]. His host frames the whole debate as brand generation beating lead generation. ProLine's James lands between them, arguing for an omnipresent flywheel of job-site signs, geofenced ads, community sponsorships and lead-magnet Facebook ads, all measured through split tests where the winners become the control [XH8rj3P6nIs @ 00:05:35]. Bensman's own framework explains the clash: storm companies usually live on going to the customer, while retail companies usually wait for the customer to come to them, so the right first channel depends on which kind of shop you actually run.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The roofing failures practitioners report most often are financial leaks nobody measured. Adam Bensman describes one roofer spending $30,000 to $35,000 a month with no trackable results, and another burning about $600,000 a year with 'terrible' returns [eQNS1yN6pQM @ 00:00:12]. His cut list includes a weather-app display ad running $1,000 to $2,000 a month, which he calls gambling, and the habit of spreading $1,000, $2,000 and $3,000 across many channels at once. ProLine's James is harsher about measurement: if the CRM cannot track lead source, run [XH8rj3P6nIs @ 00:06:08]. He also warns that a pile of leads never guarantees revenue, because 'you can have the most leads in the absolute world and be trash at sales.' Chris Bohn of True Colors Industrial adds the speed trap on commercial work: wait a week to respond and the prospect forgets you and may already hold two or three other quotes [sY92btEz6VQ @ 00:01:34]. His warning case is a 100,000-square-foot 'hot lead' that never called back, which he works with capped voicemails, receptionist pages, email and surprise stop-bys for a couple of weeks rather than writing it off [sY92btEz6VQ @ 00:01:57].

On the build side, the failure modes are speed, niche choice and Google penalties. Ippei names the biggest mistake outright: 'not building sites fast enough' [wU1OKeR3fvI @ 00:01:36]. His niche autopsy lists foundation repair in a 30,000-person city as a mistake, roofing and Miami lawn care as too competitive, garage door as too GBP-dependent, and house cleaning as too low-ticket against TaskRabbit. He also warns that scaled content abuse can hit across multiple sites at once, that stock photos and bloated page counts hurt, and that location pages need unique content [0G_j4pX14MI @ 00:11:28]. Core updates land three to four times a year, and the wrong backlinks damage rankings. James Ranks adds research traps: ChatGPT's competition ratings are unreliable [PACpsGjR7Es @ 00:13:37], seasonal niches should be avoided, and if a keyword's exact-match domain is already taken, skip it, as with a Memphis parking lot striping EMD already held by another rank-and-renter. Saravanan Ganesh applies the same .com rule city by city, and his saturation warning is concrete: top plumbing listings in Long Beach carry 100 to 230 reviews while Dallas leaders show 1,425 or more [GHgraINYcOU @ 00:00:39]. He admits rank-and-rent leads are not easy to get going.

Once a site ranks, the economics can still break. Matt Wacek warns that any ranking or conversion drop triggers cancellations and possibly bad reviews from the renter [neFMrCCbe5k @ 00:04:05], and seasonal niches like lawn care and Christmas lights lose renters off-season, so rent must be set high enough to cover the whole year. Digital Real Estate Secrets hits the sequencing trap: 'you can't sell somebody leads when you don't have them,' and there is no push-button system, only persistence and consistency [cSXYPhrwhwo @ 00:02:03]. Ippei's ticket-size math kills weak niches fast: a mobile barber at $100 a cut leaves roughly $10 on a 10% commission [raunmaNfU1o @ 00:33:59], while his pay-per-call formula only prices a lead near $42 when a 30% close rate and $2,000 job value hold up. On the roofing contractor side, John Jenkins reports a distribution trap inside Facebook neighborhood groups: post outside an admin's designated day and you get kicked out and banned, so he charts each group's posting day [dqnresNFTyg @ 00:16:06]. His economics check is long-term: branding takes years, so he holds one flyer and never swaps it.
