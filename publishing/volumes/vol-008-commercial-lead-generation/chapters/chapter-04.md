## Executive Summary

Practitioners running Clay at agency scale agree that single-database lists leak coverage. AJ Cassata sees Apollo 'valids' verify at only 40%, and traditional databases supply emails for 40-50% of a list, while a stacked waterfall lifts that to 60-80%. The street mechanics are consistent: import from Apollo, AI Ark, LinkedIn or Google Maps, exclude a DNC table, run a cheapest-first email waterfall capped around four steps, validate, classify with forced yes/no AI outputs, gate every column with run conditions, test on 10 rows, then push to Smartlead or Instantly. Using your own API keys avoids Clay's markup. The disagreements center on waterfall depth (Cassata cuts at four, Matt Lucero runs 11 providers, Romain Osman skips the waterfall for Prospeo alone) and on whether Clay replaces ZoomInfo or only complements it.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator here starts from the same premise: no single database holds your whole market. AJ Cassata, co-founder of the outbound agency Revenue Boost, reports that of 5,000 Apollo leads, even the 'valid' emails often verify at only 40%. Cassata warns that mailing invalids triggers bounces, spam placement, and burned inboxes and domains [6l8CThrSJEY @ 00:03:00]. Mark Colgan puts it plainly for non-technical users: Apollo will not have every email, some of the ones it does have will not be valid, so you cross-check against other providers [N-15SMLSc3c @ 00:00:12]. Matt Lucero's agency sends hundreds of thousands of cold emails a month. In one demo Lucero worked an Apollo export that carried emails for only about half the rows before any enrichment [HqdvpIGiOz4 @ 00:03:36]. The fix they converge on is the waterfall: ping providers in sequence, stop at the first accurate hit, and pay only when something is found. Cassata puts the upside at moving from the 40-50% coverage that traditional databases deliver to 60-80% of a list, 'almost double' [12s-u4y1VF0 @ 00:04:38]. That gap is the whole business case. More reachable contacts per account means more of the target market gets touched before a list has to be recycled, and fewer bounces eat through the sending domains that every campaign depends on.

The second point of agreement is what Clay actually is. Clay's own channel pitches it as a data aggregator rather than a data provider. It plugs into 80-plus sources and bills per use, so you pay only for data successfully returned [v31hKg-WSCc @ 00:02:00]. Matt Lucero runs over 1M cold emails a month through Clay and reports just under 5,000 sales meetings. Lucero calls it a 'data orchestrator,' a canvas that stitches the GTM stack together, and adds that 'the best tools are usually specialist tools' [jEkDgA8hPmo @ 00:01:40]. Patrick Spychalski spells out the economics: Clay has no data warehouse of its own and resells providers' data for credits, which is why a waterfall charges only the provider that hits [YLXzWpknuJk @ 00:19:22]. AJ Cassata describes the role the same way: 'It does not replace outreach tools. It sits in the middle of everything we already use.' Apollo and D7 feed in, and Instantly and HeyReach sit on the other end [AtF_XaOE3As @ 00:11:48]. The shared operating model is Find, Enrich, Transform, Export. You import lists, layer enrichments, turn the output into scores, tags and copy, then push to a sequencer, CRM, or dialer. None of these practitioners treats Clay as the sender. It is the research and routing layer that sits in front of the sender.

Third, the qualification layer is where the money is made. AJ Cassata notes there is no database filter for B2B versus B2C, for commercial versus residential contractors, or for whether a company actually sells data. Instead, AI scans websites and third-party sources and returns a yes/no answer or a 1-10 score that routes top leads to the best reps [12s-u4y1VF0 @ 00:05:33]. Matt Lucero forces prompts to 'only output B2B or b2c' so the column can be filtered, and runs work in small chunks so mistakes don't burn credits [HqdvpIGiOz4 @ 00:23:16]. The guardrails are near-universal. Romain Osman, credited with over 1,000 B2B appointments from cold email and LinkedIn, puts it bluntly: add a condition every time, 'otherwise it's just going to run for all of your rows and so you're going to lose a lot of credits' [RodU66qhDeY @ 00:10:37]. GTM With Deepika's rule is 'for testing just run two to three rows,' and each funding and tech-stack waterfall only fires when the input fields are present [1AB3H7aWJ0Q @ 00:10:15]. The baseline every serious builder shares has five parts: a do-not-contact exclusion table, conditional run logic on every paid column, forced binary AI outputs, a test batch before the full run, and validation before anything touches a sending inbox.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start with the price sheet. Xavier Caffrey runs the OneWay agency and claims more than $100M in generated pipeline. Caffrey lays out the tiers this way. Free gives 100 credits. Starter costs $149 for 2,000 credits, 5,000 searches, phone data and your own API keys. Explorer costs $349 for 10,000 credits plus HTTP API, webhooks and exclusions, and Caffrey recommends it for most users. Pro costs $800 for 50,000 credits and CRM sync [kY5E4wl8wlA @ 00:04:22]. Caffrey says to think of credits as tokens: an email lookup runs 1-3 credits and AI research runs 5-10. Sales Feed, a self-described non-technical seller, judged Starter worth it: 'the 149 I think is about what I pay for Sales Navigator a month anyway' [Z9xzPDRrQHw @ 00:07:54]. On the AI side, Matt Lucero priced a GPT-4o Mini call at about $0.00126, 'a tenth of a penny.' Premium models cost 'a penny 2 pennies 5 pennies 10 pennies' per row, so the cheap model writes a reusable summary and Claude 3.5 Sonnet is used only for interpretation [HqdvpIGiOz4 @ 00:19:33]. Provider prices vary widely. GTM With Deepika's tech-stack waterfall ordered tools at 1, 2, 4 and 18 credits per row, cheapest first, and the waterfall only fired when domain and funding were already present [1AB3H7aWJ0Q @ 00:07:26].

The email waterfall is the core build. Mark Colgan's demonstrated order runs Findymail, then LeadMagic, then Prospeo, then Dropcontact, checking each provider in turn until all have been checked [N-15SMLSc3c @ 00:00:29]. AJ Cassata's minimum inputs are full name plus company domain, or the LinkedIn profile alone. Cassata finds that valid emails usually come from the first two or three columns, puts the cheapest provider first, and caps waterfalls at about four steps because returns diminish [6l8CThrSJEY @ 00:13:17]. Matt Lucero runs the opposite extreme: 11 providers with validation between each step and Prospeo and LeadMagic moved to the front, which returned 9 of 10 valid emails [jEkDgA8hPmo @ 00:21:30]. Romain Osman pitches an eight-step 'basic workflow' as covering 90% of campaigns, and it adds deliverability checks before any spend. First comes a DNC lookup on company domain. Next is an MX provider check through a free LeadMagic HTTP API that skips Microsoft 365 rows for low deliverability. Then come Debounce validation and Prospeo enrichment. Finally, a merge column sends risky, unknown and catch-all emails to Enrich.so for a second verdict [RodU66qhDeY @ 00:05:09]. Osman's 'master email' column merges Debounce safe-to-send, Prospeo valid, and Enrich.so valid results into one field that feeds Instantly or Smartlead.

Once the email is found, AI turns raw rows into routing and copy. Clay's own CRE dinner campaign chains several steps. A LinkedIn import feeds a company-description enrichment, Claygent finds the office address, and OpenAI writes a blurb on what the company does. A casualization step follows, because a full street address makes it 'obvious that a robot is sending that email.' Then come a Yelp lookup for dinner near HQ and an Instantly push with variables [tbe8LBJOSSc @ 00:06:05]. Xavier Caffrey runs a Claygent website-design template that rates each site. A 'Hey {first name}, saw that you guys...' opener only runs if the design rating is 7 or higher [UpSWlkeuUqs @ 00:10:43]. AJ Cassata generates a first line that opens with 'I found you from your work on...', maps it as an Instantly variable, and connects a personal ChatGPT or Claude key to cut cost [12s-u4y1VF0 @ 00:19:11]. Matt Lucero pushes to Smartlead only if the row is B2B, and toggles 'allow leads that exist in other campaigns' and 'community bounce list' on the way out [HqdvpIGiOz4 @ 00:25:17]. The pattern repeats across all of these builds. Every AI column outputs something filterable, every paid column carries a condition, and only qualified, validated rows reach the sequencer.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the List and Strip Do-Not-Contacts
   - Import leads, clean the domain, then run a multi-row lookup against a DNC table before any paid column.
   - Run 'look up multiple rows in other table' against a DNC table that holds clients and opt-outs, with target column company domain and filter operator 'equals'. Every downstream column then skips the row if a record is found [Romain Osman Lead Generation — RodU66qhDeY @ 00:02:04].
2. Qualify with a Forced True/False AI Column
   - Use the meta-prompter to write a B2B true/false check, set a max cost, and test 10 rows first.
   - Lucero used the meta-prompter to build a B2B true/false column, chose the model, set a max cost, added 'run only if' logic and tested 10 rows. In the full run, 33 of the 50 leads came back B2B [Matt Lucero — jEkDgA8hPmo @ 00:13:47].
3. Run a Capped, Cheapest-First Email Waterfall
   - Order providers by cost, cap at about four steps, and gate fallbacks on an empty work email.
   - Set the fallback run condition to 'only run if work email is empty'. Use Claygent to 'visit their website and find me an email', and search other job titles at companies that still have no valid address [AJ Cassata — 6l8CThrSJEY @ 00:16:35].
4. Normalize, Personalize and Push to the Sequencer
   - Normalize company names, generate variables, and push only qualified, validated rows to Instantly.
   - Find founders, CEOs and presidents, run the work-email waterfall, normalize the company name, then push to Instantly with the enriched fields mapped as variables [Clay — tbe8LBJOSSc @ 00:07:18].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split among operators is how long the email waterfall should run. AJ Cassata of Revenue Boost says valid emails usually surface in the first two or three provider columns, so the waterfall gets capped at about four steps because returns diminish fast after that [6l8CThrSJEY @ 00:13:17]. The Clay channel's own demo, by contrast, runs a work-email waterfall across six different data providers off nothing more than a name and a company domain [tbe8LBJOSSc @ 00:02:23]. Matt Lucero goes further still, chaining eleven providers with validation between each step and pulling Prospeo and LeadMagic to the front, which returned nine valid emails out of ten test rows [jEkDgA8hPmo @ 00:21:30]. Romain Osman sits at the opposite extreme: in a workflow built to cover most campaigns, Osman runs no waterfall at all because Prospeo's coverage is good enough on its own, with LeadMagic, TryKitt and ICPs held back as an optional chain [RodU66qhDeY @ 00:06:32]. Cassata's case for short chains is cost: anything not found after four or five providers is unlikely to appear later, so the tail providers get cancelled. Lucero's long chain leans on validation between every hop rather than on a short provider list.

Practitioners also split on whether Clay replaces the data stack or simply sits on top of it. Xavier Caffrey, who runs the OneWay agency, pitches Clay's 105-plus data sources as enough to replace Apollo, ZoomInfo and Lusha outright [kY5E4wl8wlA @ 00:01:07]. Patrick Spychalski pushes back: Clay complements ZoomInfo and is probably not a replacement, and Clay holds no data warehouse of its own, reselling providers' data for credits [YLXzWpknuJk @ 00:10:28]. Mark Colgan takes the specialist line, paying separately for LeadMagic as the main finder and validator, running it on non-Clay projects, and warning that you don't have to use Clay for everything [N-15SMLSc3c @ 00:01:14]. The economics follow the philosophy. Caffrey recommends testing providers inside Clay at a discount and then buying directly from the winner, and AJ Cassata uses Clay credits to trial tools such as Semrush before switching to direct API keys because credits carry a markup, arguing that used right, Clay can actually save you money [AtF_XaOE3As @ 00:16:38]. The Clay channel concedes the same point from its side: once your own API key is connected, that waterfall step is not charged. Matt Lucero, who calls Clay a data orchestrator, sums up the camp: the best tools are usually specialist tools.

How much AI belongs in the workflow is the third fault line. Romain Osman opens with a deliberately basic build, no AI personalization, and claims it covers ninety percent of campaigns, leaning on DNC lookups, MX checks, validation and a clean master email column instead [RodU66qhDeY @ 00:00:00]. AJ Cassata runs the opposite shop: AI is ninety percent of Revenue Boost's Clay usage, from picking an e-commerce store's dominant traffic channel to dropping its top-selling product into the subject line [AtF_XaOE3As @ 00:11:05]. Patrick Spychalski splits the difference, describing their own outbound copy as roughly half human and half AI [YLXzWpknuJk @ 00:04:31]. The Clay channel frames the tool as the opposite of a volume cannon, stating that Clay isn't a plug-and-play AI tool looking to 10x the volume of outreach to customer spam folders [v31hKg-WSCc @ 00:03:58]. Cassata's stated reason for heavy AI is that reply rates are lower now because outreach is saturated, and Cassata answers it with variables like other business types, role focus and an AI-picked case study. Osman's build answers with plumbing rather than prose, merging Debounce safe-to-send, Prospeo valid and Enrich.so valid results into one master email column before anything reaches Instantly or Smartlead, and using AI only to strip LLC, Ltd and Inc from company names.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap every practitioner reports is trusting a single database. AJ Cassata says that out of 5,000 Apollo leads, even the emails flagged as valid often verify at only forty percent, and that mailing invalids drives bounces, spam placement and burned inboxes and domains [6l8CThrSJEY @ 00:03:00]. Matt Lucero saw the same pattern in a live build, where an Apollo export carried emails for only about half the rows, and warns that skipping validation produces high bounce rates [HqdvpIGiOz4 @ 00:05:40]. Romain Osman adds an infrastructure filter most beginners miss: an MX check through a free LeadMagic HTTP call flags Microsoft 365 domains, which Osman skips for low deliverability [RodU66qhDeY @ 00:04:25]. Volume makes it worse. Cassata warns that the more you scale cold email, the more you are going to land in spam, which is why the local-restaurant opener in the Revenue Boost demo doubles as a deliverability play [AtF_XaOE3As @ 00:22:47]. Cassata also ties coverage to TAM math, describing improved coverage of a 100,000-contact market from a 40,000 baseline as more than doubling revenue potential, with better coverage also delaying lead recycling. That upside only lands if every added address clears validation before it touches a sending inbox.

Credit burn is the second recurring loss, and it hits from several directions. Cassata warns that a waterfall provider can find an email that then fails validation, and Clay still charges for the find, which is one reason to delete providers costing over ten credits a row and put the cheapest first [6l8CThrSJEY @ 00:13:30]. Romain Osman calls run conditions non-negotiable: without one, a column runs for all rows and you lose a lot of credits [RodU66qhDeY @ 00:10:37]. Xavier Caffrey flags a structural leak: running research per lead instead of once at the company level and looking it up from the people table costs two to three times as much, and leaving auto-update on lets Clay run the entire table by accident [kY5E4wl8wlA @ 00:22:10]. Hamza Sakib, The AI Ops Guy, found a quieter drain in Topic Intent: topics Clay auto-adds produce weak signals that lead to the wrong people and use up a lot of credits, so only high and medium intent stays in the run [nRh4F859Xjo @ 00:05:32]. Caffrey prices an email lookup at one to three credits and AI research at five to ten, and tells operators to think of credits as tokens.

AI research fails quietly, and practitioners show it on camera. Sales Feed tried to find software companies with Glassdoor complaints about HR, benefits or payroll for a Rippling-style pitch, and the search came back empty: no companies were found, and the reviews it did surface lacked the specific details requested [Z9xzPDRrQHw @ 00:03:57]. The fix, recorded later, was defining multiple outputs such as yes/no and turnover percentage and using the AI formula builder to force a true/false answer. Deepika's funding waterfall showed a different break: Claygent was the cheap path at about one credit per row, but it errored when social links were not mapped as inputs [1AB3H7aWJ0Q @ 00:06:05]. Matt Lucero says AI output isn't perfect, so the B2B classifier ran on ten rows before the full pull, and 33 of 50 leads came back B2B [jEkDgA8hPmo @ 00:17:57]. Then there is the learning curve. Patrick Spychalski calls Clay a hard tool and tells operators to expect it [YLXzWpknuJk @ 00:32:10], while Sales Feed, months into using Clay and partnered with it, still finds the APIs intimidating and advises delegating the build to ops, an agency or a freelancer because competitors will win otherwise.
