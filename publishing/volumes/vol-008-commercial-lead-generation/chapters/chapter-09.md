## Executive Summary

Home-service practitioners agree that the Google Maps three-pack is the highest-intent, lowest-cost lead source an operator can own. The top three results take 90%+ of calls, proximity limits each profile to a few miles, and fresh photo reviews beat stale review totals. Steve Hunsaker valued five months of free Maps traffic at about $28K. Surge Digital Marketing reports LSA leads at $30-40, against $80-150 for shared Angi leads, as LSA migrates into Google Ads with target-CPL bidding. Practitioners split sharply on multiple profiles: some call them legitimate, some call them 'a little bit black hat,' and one runs them on fabricated addresses. They also split on Meta versus LSA as the better paid channel.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who works Maps treats the map pack as the cheapest high-intent lead source a home-service operator can own. Steve Hunsaker calls Maps leads the highest-intent traffic available, at zero media cost. He pegs a single Maps-sourced commercial client at roughly $120K over three years [PlZ43ZKMk34 @ 00:01:25]. Brayden Bass puts the stakes bluntly: the top three Maps results take 90%+ of calls, and a shop with 12 reviews loses to one with 200 [QnqQKoCeYa8 @ 00:02:13]. Israel Saucedo calls it 'three-pack or bust.' He notes that 70-80% of local searches happen on mobile, that 50%+ of clicks go to organic and GBP results against 20-30% for ads, and that many searchers call straight off the listing [Rp1Up2fDgPg @ 00:04:01]. The constraint everyone works around is proximity. Andy Walker says a profile only ranks top three within 'a couple mile radius' of its pin. A 100-mile reach only happens in rural markets with no competition, because the Maps algorithm is proximity-based [FcZZMpjLVNo @ 00:00:27]. Saucedo adds that a brand-new pin still produces leads within one to three miles, and the radius grows as the ranking factors are worked. The operating conclusion is to win the three-pack around your own pin first, then spend effort stretching that radius. Paying to rent someone else's audience comes later, if at all.

Reviews are the second point of near-total agreement. The consensus is about velocity and recency rather than raw count. Hunsaker says to drip one to two reviews a day instead of dumping them in bursts. He reports that 8 of his top 10 'most relevant' reviews carry photos, which bring geotags, and that local guides are weighted more heavily [PlZ43ZKMk34 @ 00:05:05]. Local View makes the same point for Local Services Ads: 'recent five-star reviews can make way more impact than dozens of old ones.' They say to ask happy customers through the LSA dashboard itself [bzWbX2kyAhg @ 00:00:44]. Jeremy at PushLeads sets a working target of 15-20 reviews a month, each requested within 24 hours of the job. He credits Darren Shaw for the velocity point and cites 50+ reviews as giving roughly 30% stronger visibility [vWdPlgxdJUA @ 00:06:52]. Austin Parker puts it plainly: a steady review flow beats a large but stale review count. He wants the main profile in the top three, judged on distance, relevance and prominence, before any expansion [BVNOmeAp7mg @ 00:00:53]. For an operator, the review ask has to become a daily habit tied to job completion. A pile of old five-star reviews loses weight while a competitor feeding in fresh ones climbs past it.

The third consensus is economic. Renting someone else's lead engine is a losing trade, and any leads you do own must be answered fast. Brayden Bass runs the math on shared leads: a $60 lead sold to four companies effectively costs $240. In his words, 'you're not buying leads, you're buying a race' [QnqQKoCeYa8 @ 00:01:05]. Hook Agency says Angi leads get split five ways, so paying for them means 'paying them to build the engine' instead of building your own [WkliDsQi_qI @ 00:01:10]. TradeOps Consulting calls speed to lead 'the easiest win you'll ever get.' They cite a +391% conversion lift for responding within 60 seconds and say 78% of leads move on after five minutes. At minimum, they set up GoHighLevel auto-texts for missed calls and form fills, or put a human rep or answering service on the phones [dUeSNisWyGc @ 00:01:02]. Local View ties speed directly to LSA rank. Google tracks how fast you answer calls and messages, and slow or missed responses drop placement. Their fix is to return missed calls quickly and set an after-hours auto-reply [bzWbX2kyAhg @ 00:00:44]. The order of operations is to stop renting shared leads where the market allows it, own the profile, and make sure a person or an automation picks up inside the first minute.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The build-out starts with categories, services and the description, and the speakers give exact settings. PushLeads tells contractors to pick the most specific primary category plus three to five secondaries. The job takes two minutes, and the date should be logged because 'category updates typically affect rankings within two to four weeks.' Jeremy also cites 86% of impressions coming from category searches and secondaries adding about 15% visibility [vWdPlgxdJUA @ 00:02:45]. The same playbook says to fill all 750 description characters and lead with geography and project types to repel bad-fit jobs. It also says to drop zip codes more than one county outside the profitable range. Austin Parker allows one primary category and up to nine secondaries, and wants website pages that mirror the profile [BVNOmeAp7mg @ 00:00:53]. Surfside PPC's local scan of dentists cuts against category stuffing. The #1 practice had one category, while Aspen Dental had ten and sat at #5, so 'having a ton really doesn't matter,' though more services 'definitely helps' [Eg42cdAsCqw @ 00:07:22]. Hunsaker writes the products and services sections with Claude, Manas or ChatGPT. He has the model ask five clarifying questions before drafting, so the copy carries real trade and city keywords instead of generic filler [wdo4cRHVxXA @ 00:06:14].

The review engine is where the numbers get most specific. Hunsaker says a verbal 'I'll leave you a review' converts only 2-5% of the time. His crews hand over an on-site tap card that 'you can buy online for like 25 bucks' [wdo4cRHVxXA @ 00:09:51]. He pairs it with CRM automation through Jobber, Housecall Pro, ServiceTitan or GoHighLevel at $20-30 a month. His math: out of 100 jobs, about 40 customers are willing to review. The in-person ask alone captures 24, automation alone captures 16, and both together capture 40. Over a year that compounds into a couple hundred reviews, against 50-70 with one method [wdo4cRHVxXA @ 00:11:09]. Brayden Bass opens with a conversation before sending the link and times the ask to peak satisfaction. He asks everyone rather than only happy customers, because Google prohibits gating [QnqQKoCeYa8 @ 00:03:51]. Surfside PPC asks for before-and-after photos and videos and warns against compensating anyone for reviews. They also note that reviewers with a local review history help the score [Eg42cdAsCqw @ 00:04:37]. Hunsaker's other lever is retroactive: he asks past reviewers to edit photos into their existing reviews. He rates one photo review at roughly ten generic ones, a ratio he states without offering proof.

Paid placement and expansion run on hard settings. Surge Digital Marketing says LSA is moving into Google Ads as 'Performance Max with pay per lead goals,' and manual bids are being replaced by target CPL. As they put it, 'A maximum is a wall, a target is an average' [pZ9P9Hmof3g @ 00:04:29]. Their budget settings are a daily budget of weekly divided by seven and a monthly cap of daily times 30.4. Services with different job values, such as $500 and $15K, go into separate campaigns. Performance history should be screenshotted because it does not carry over. The GBP should be frozen during migration week, because name, address or category edits trigger a 24-48 hour review that can pause the campaign. Austin Parker maps expansion with a geo-grid heat map. It typically shows a 5-10 mile rank radius, or 1-2 miles in dense markets. He places about four profiles per metro, spaced 10-20 miles apart, and targets areas with 50-100+ monthly searches where competitors have fewer than 30 reviews [BVNOmeAp7mg @ 00:03:59]. Andy Walker rents real offices at $300-500 a month with signage and files a keyworded DBA for under $100. He insists on a different phone number for every profile, warning that a shared number gets the main profile suspended. Forwarding to a central line is fine [FcZZMpjLVNo @ 00:09:14].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Most self-inflicted losses come from touching the wrong field on a live listing. Steve Hunsaker warns never to edit the phone number or address once a profile is ranking, because the edit risks suspension on a page he says prints leads and money, and his August to December 2025 numbers show 168 calls and 1,192 website clicks riding on it [PlZ43ZKMk34 @ 00:13:28]. Andy Walker names the phone number as the critical tripwire on multi-location builds: every added profile must carry a different number, call forwarding is fine, but sharing the main line gets the main profile suspended, the one asset the whole quadrant play is built on [FcZZMpjLVNo @ 00:09:14]. ItsKeaton adds that bulk photo uploads risk a profile ban, a live hazard because his $300 to $500 foot-in-the-door retainer targets profiles with few photos, dark images, missing hours and low reviews, so the pull is to dump an entire gallery on day one [d2krWJWz2iM @ 00:05:07]. Surge Digital Marketing ties the same trap to paid spend: name, address or category edits trigger a 24 to 48 hour review that can pause the linked campaign, so the profile gets frozen during LSA migration week [pZ9P9Hmof3g @ 00:10:54].

LSA operators report traps that cost history and recourse rather than rankings. Surge Digital Marketing watched a client lose years of account history because a former agency held admin access, so step one is verifying which email owns the account; performance history also does not carry into the new Performance Max structure, which makes screenshots mandatory before the switch [pZ9P9Hmof3g @ 00:02:11]. Blended targets are the second trap: a target CPL is an average, not a wall, and per-service targets get blended, so services worth $500 and $15K must sit in separate campaigns [pZ9P9Hmof3g @ 00:04:14]. PushLeads notes Google killed the lead dispute credit system in July 2024, leaving 'zero recourse' for mismatched leads, which is why the primary category and description must repel bad-fit calls up front [vWdPlgxdJUA @ 00:03:04]. Local View lists the quieter killers: expired background checks or licenses can drop or pause the ad, overextending into areas the business cannot serve backfires, and out-of-area calls should be marked 'not relevant' so targeting retrains instead of drifting [bzWbX2kyAhg @ 00:02:54].

The headline math in Chapter Q9 needs a haircut before anyone builds a budget on it. Andy Walker's quadrant model turns $500 a month in rent into 30 leads, 10 closes at $1,000 and '$114,000' in annual profit, then says outright the number is not typical [FcZZMpjLVNo @ 00:06:53]. Steve Hunsaker's roughly $28K 'free advertising' figure for August to December 2025 rests on 168 calls valued at $100 each plus an assumed 10% conversion on 1,192 website clicks, and he expects minimal change in month one [PlZ43ZKMk34 @ 00:13:53]. His separate review math is the clearest bottleneck check: a verbal 'I'll review' converts at only 2 to 5%, so of 100 jobs with 40 willing reviewers, in-person asks alone capture 24, automation alone 16, and only both together reach all 40, a couple hundred reviews a year versus 50 to 70 [wdo4cRHVxXA @ 00:09:01]. TradeOps Consulting brings the same skepticism to paid search, where clicks run $30 to $150 in home services, 20 to 30% of search terms are junk until negatives go in, and broad match plus PMax stay banned from starting budgets of $30 to $50 a day [dUeSNisWyGc @ 00:11:06].
