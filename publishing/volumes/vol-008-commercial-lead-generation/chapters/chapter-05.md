# Chapter 05: Email Infrastructure and Deliverability

## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one operating model: never cold-email from the main brand domain, buy lookalike secondary domains, put two or three Google Workspace or Outlook mailboxes on each, publish SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and MX, and warm every inbox two to four weeks before launch. The numbers cluster tightly: 15 to 50 cold sends per inbox per day, warmup ramps starting at two to ten emails, 50/50 cold-to-warmup splits, and automatic campaign pauses at 4 to 5 percent bounce. Smartlead operators report 49 percent of Gmail inboxes burning overnight and reply rates crashing below 0.5 percent on static copy until spintax went in. Disagreements are real: Damien of MailReach tolerates up to 100 sends per address, Kyle Dickson rotates half the fleet into rest monthly, and Coldbot insists authentication and warmup are prerequisites that guarantee nothing.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator in this set treats the main brand domain as untouchable for cold outreach. Smartlead's agency operator frames it with coca-cola.com: blast from that domain and it is only a matter of time before it gets flagged, and then mail to friends, family, colleagues, investors, and partners starts landing in spam too [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:04:45]. TechSales opens with the same nightmare in story form: cold emails go out from the company domain, replies stop within a week, and finance reports that customer invoices are hitting spam [aa7B2gisZdU @ 00:00:01]. CRMOZ puts a number on the trigger, warning that 5,000 cold marketing emails from yourcompany.com plus a wave of spam reports gets the domain blacklisted and real clients stop receiving invoices; the fix is a lookalike secondary domain such as yourcompany.net [T9cn3UyRkR8 @ 00:04:01]. Ethan Fleming paid for this lesson directly: a Zapier automation sending lead data from their main email wrecked their domain reputation, their proposal hit a prospect's spam filter, and they lost a $2,000-a-month client [TB1PQrXx5s8 @ 00:08:53]. The shared rule is operational separation: buy close-to-brand sending domains, keep them clean of hyphens and numbers, and let the primary domain handle invoices, support, and partner mail only.

The second point of agreement is provider choice and spreading load thin. Smartlead's operator calls SMTP inboxes "absolute rubbish" after three years of daily outreach, says they were the first setups wiped out when Gmail and Microsoft tightened filters, and calls Google and Outlook the Nike and Adidas of infrastructure [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:01:20]. Damien of MailReach rates Google Workspace or Office 365 mailboxes an absolute game changer and names a custom SMTP server the worst possible setup for landing in the inbox [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:02:42]. On density, the numbers converge: two to three mailboxes per domain from Kyle Dickson, who warns that if the domain gets destroyed every inbox on it dies with it [drsocawArfo @ 00:04:41]. Coldbot frames the domain as the building and mailboxes as rooms, so one hundred addresses on one new domain is still concentrated risk, and argues that Google Workspace's technical ceiling is not a cold-outreach target; Instantly's documentation recommends around thirty campaign emails per day for regular mailboxes [fM-ywBq8yoU @ 00:03:43]. The working rule that falls out: when more volume is needed, add healthy inboxes and domains rather than turning one inbox into a fire hose.

Authentication and warmup are the table stakes nobody skips, and nobody oversells. Smartlead's team, claiming 2 million cold emails sent in a year and more than 300 campaigns reviewed, lists four non-negotiables before warmup even begins, with SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and MX records all set up and verified at the top of the list [DIUObYQih3s @ 00:02:27]. TechSales compresses the trio into three questions an operator can remember: SPF answers who can send, DKIM answers whether the message is really from you, and DMARC decides what to do if it does not check out [aa7B2gisZdU @ 00:05:26]. Kyle Dickson's version is the bluntest of all: warm every inbox two to three weeks minimum before a single cold send, keep warmup running forever, and never shut it off [drsocawArfo @ 00:00:15]. Coldbot supplies the necessary cold water: you can have SPF, DKIM, and DMARC and still get poor placement, because authentication proves identity but does not force Gmail or Outlook to like your sending behavior, and a warmed inbox can still be damaged by bad data, complaints, or sudden volume spikes [fM-ywBq8yoU @ 00:05:42]. The consensus position is that both are prerequisites, not guarantees, and the infrastructure has to be monitored after launch rather than treated as a one-time checklist.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The DNS work is mechanical once you know the traps. Stewart Gauld's Google Workspace walkthrough: in the Admin console go to Apps, Gmail, Authenticate email, generate a new DKIM record, keep the 2048-bit key length rather than 1024 because it is more secure, keep the google prefix, paste the host name and TXT value into DNS, then click start authentication and allow up to 48 hours to propagate [fsZaEce18FE @ 00:03:43]. For Microsoft 365, SalesBlink points to the Defender portal instead: open Email authentication settings, the DKIM tab, add the two CNAME records selector1 and selector2, then switch on DKIM signing, while the SPF value swaps Google for spf.protection.outlook.com [8sprCYGSnpI @ 00:01:33]. CRMOZ, drawing on their audits, says never create two SPF records, because it is like handing the bouncer two different guest lists, and that one extra space or typo kills delivery outright; they also want a separate DKIM for every sending tool [T9cn3UyRkR8 @ 00:01:24]. On DMARC, Frankee Muryan runs p=none for the first 6 to 12 months unless the brand is big enough to attract spoofing, roughly 300k monthly website visitors, and pastes the zipped daily reports into ChatGPT to read them [YWsrDI9jLXk @ 00:05:40].

Warmup settings are where the numbers get specific. Kyle Dickson sets Instantly's ramp at two warmup emails per day per inbox, climbing two, four, six, eight up to a cap of 30, with a warmup reply rate somewhere between 65 and 95 percent but never 100, because a perfect reply rate followed by a normal cold campaign looks suspicious; their hard ceiling is 50 sends per inbox per day, split 20 cold and 30 warm [drsocawArfo @ 00:04:20]. Smartlead's agency operator starts bulk warmup at five per day with a daily ramp of five and a 20 percent reply rate, then runs a 50/50 split once campaigns go live, for example 15 cold plus 15 warmup per day [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:10:25]. The other Smartlead presenter publishes a week-by-week curve: 5 to 10 warmup emails in week one, 20 to 30 in week two, 30 to 40 from week three on, warmup reply rate at 30 to 40 percent, and at least four weeks before launch if you can afford the wait [DIUObYQih3s @ 00:06:10]. For sizing, TechSales does the arithmetic: 500 people a day at 40 per mailbox is 13 mailboxes, which at two or three per domain is five domains [aa7B2gisZdU @ 00:02:28].

Content and campaign settings carry their own hard rules. Smartlead's operator calls HTML the single biggest deliverability killer, turns on plain text mode under delivery optimization, switches off open and click tracking because the pixel forces HTML and Apple devices skew the stats anyway, and sets high bounce rate auto-protection to 5 so the campaign pauses itself at a 5 percent bounce rate [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:14:23]. Kyle Dickson runs the same kill switch tighter, pausing at 4 percent, and claims images or links in the first email cut deliverability roughly in half, so any link or image goes in the last email of the sequence [drsocawArfo @ 00:11:25]. Both push spintax hard: Kyle wants variations on nearly every sentence for 50 or 60 possible combinations, while the Smartlead operator watched reply rates collapse below 0.5 percent on static copy against a normal floor of 2 percent before adding AI spintax through an OpenAI key [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:02:09]. On lists, Damien of MailReach says roughly 23 percent of catch-all addresses bounce, so cap catch-alls at 20 percent of every list instead of burning through valid addresses first and then going all in on catch-alls [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:07:24].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Buy and size the sending domains
   - Purchase close-to-brand .com lookalike domains and size mailbox count from monthly volume.
   - Use names like getexample.com or examplehq.com, avoid hyphens and numbers, point each domain to the main website, and run the math: 500 people a day at 40 per mailbox is 13 mailboxes across five domains at two or three mailboxes each [TechSales — aa7B2gisZdU @ 00:01:44].
2. Authenticate every domain
   - Publish one SPF record, a DKIM record from the mailbox provider, a DMARC record, and MX.
   - In Google Admin go to Apps, Gmail, Authenticate email, generate a 2048-bit DKIM key, paste the TXT host and value into DNS, add a _dmarc TXT record with your report email, then confirm with Google Admin Toolbox Check MX [Stewart Gauld — fsZaEce18FE @ 00:04:03].
3. Warm before the first cold send
   - Turn on warmup for every inbox and let it ramp for 14 to 21 days.
   - Ramp two warmup emails per day up to a 30 cap, set the warmup reply rate between 65 and 95 percent, then ramp cold sends the same way to 15 to 20 per day while keeping total sends at 50 or fewer per inbox [Kyle Dickson — drsocawArfo @ 00:01:32].
4. Launch with guardrails on
   - Enable plain text mode, disable open and click tracking, and set bounce auto-protection.
   - Inside campaign protection and limits, switch on high bounce rate auto-protection at 5 so the campaign pauses itself once 5 percent of sends bounce, and keep warmup running alongside live campaigns [Smartlead — GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:14:23].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Nobody agrees on a single daily ceiling, so the cap is a dial each operator sets. Smartlead's agency operator plugs 25 emails per domain per day into a free sending-volume calculator and insists on a 50/50 split once campaigns go live: 15 cold sends matched by 15 warm-up sends per inbox, so domains and mailboxes keep a healthier longevity [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:10:25]. Smartlead's 2-million-email team keeps each inbox under 30 to 40 daily sends, randomizes slightly, and ramps warm-up into that same 30 to 40 band [DIUObYQih3s @ 00:03:33]. Kyle Dickson caps total traffic at 50 per inbox, split as 20 cold and 30 warm, and ramps the cold side 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 over a week or two after the 14-to-21-day warm-up ends [drsocawArfo @ 00:04:20]. Damien of MailReach sits at the aggressive end: up to 100 cold emails per day per address, but only on Google Workspace or Office 365, dropping to 50 or fewer on any other provider because results come in lower [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:05:05]. The common thread is scaling horizontally. When one inbox hits its ceiling, every one of these operators adds mailboxes and domains rather than pushing the same account harder, because the risk of burning a single overloaded inbox multiplies with every extra send.

The sharpest split is over cheap SMTP. Smartlead's agency operator calls SMTP inboxes most of the time absolute rubbish after three years of daily outreach, says they were the first setups wiped out when Gmail and Microsoft tightened their filters, and tells operators to buy only Google or Outlook even though SMTP runs half the price [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:01:20]. Damien backs that hard line: a custom SMTP or custom mail server is the worst setup for landing in the inbox, because Workspace and Office 365 own most of the mailbox market and nothing enters a Google or Microsoft home better than another Google or Microsoft mailbox [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:03:26]. Smartlead's own SmartSenders launch argues the opposite hedge: which provider delivers best changes every other week, so a diverse email infrastructure is the biggest defensibility metric, and the shopping cart sells Google, Outlook, and SMTP through Mail Reef side by side [GYd8Y73SCGE @ 00:02:05]. Digitellix runs a different lane entirely, pairing an email app with Amazon SES, SendGrid, or Mailgun, warning that running your own SMTP server means owning every reputation problem, and keeping a shared IP rather than a dedicated one under roughly 100,000 emails a month [iM8vOv1pK8g @ 00:02:01].

Domain architecture splits along two lines. Ethan Fleming uses a mail. subdomain for nurtures and newsletters inside GoHighLevel, but tells most beginners to buy a burner domain such as contactyourbrand.com, so a reputation blow-up stays isolated from the Gmail and Outlook mail the business runs on its root domain [TB1PQrXx5s8 @ 00:08:34]. Digitellix goes the other way for small senders, mailing from a subdomain like news.yourdomain.com so the main domain's everyday mail stays safe, and authenticating the exact domain used in the from address [iM8vOv1pK8g @ 00:03:05]. The second fight is links versus unsubscribe. Kyle Dickson strips every image and link from the first email, saying either one can cut deliverability roughly in half, explains that open tracking is itself a one-by-one pixel image that Google and Outlook caught on to, and saves any link or image for the last email of the sequence [drsocawArfo @ 00:11:07]. Damien demands an easy-to-spot, working unsubscribe link in every email, warning that without one the spam complaint rate skyrockets and destroys sender reputation in no time, and that asking prospects to reply unsubscribe does not count [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:10:40]. On the opening email, those two rules are in direct conflict.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures are burned assets. Smartlead's agency operator woke up to over 49% of their Gmail inboxes completely dead overnight and had to drop hundreds of dollars on brand-new domains just to keep client campaigns running [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:00:00]. Ethan Fleming lost a $2,000-a-month client because a Zapier automation was sending lead data from their main email; the damaged reputation pushed a proposal into the prospect's spam filter, and those two spam-foldered emails cost the deal. Another Fleming client fired 10,000 cold emails through one automation and tanked not only the CRM domain but the main Gmail and Outlook sending as well, which a separate burner domain would have isolated [TB1PQrXx5s8 @ 00:08:53]. TechSales opens on the same pattern: the first cold emails go out from the company's own domain, replies stop a week later, then finance reports that customer invoices are landing in spam [aa7B2gisZdU @ 00:00:01]. CRMOZ adds the volume-spike trap, where a domain silent for months suddenly jumps to 10,000 sends on a random Tuesday, Google reads it as a hacked account and blocks the domain instantly, and the fix is starting at 10 to 15 daily sends to highly engaged users and raising the number slowly [T9cn3UyRkR8 @ 00:04:42].

Content failures show up as silent reply collapse. Smartlead's agency operator watched client reply rates fall below 0.5%, against a usual floor of 2%, purely because identical static copy went to every prospect; the fix was AI spintax inside Smartlead, connected through an OpenAI API key to GPT-4, so each recipient receives a different variation of the same message [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:02:09]. Kyle Dickson pushes spintax into nearly every sentence to reach 50 or 60 combinations, and warns that one image or one link in the first cold email will probably cut deliverability in half, with half as many emails actually going out [drsocawArfo @ 00:11:25]. Smartlead's 2-million-email team reports the slow-burn version of the same trap: images and links can post strong first-week numbers, then most mail slides into spam by week two or three, so after testing they stopped sending images, while conceding that anyone consistently booking more meetings with them should keep going [DIUObYQih3s @ 00:07:19]. Damien calls links one of the top causes of content-related spam, says a single link can destroy deliverability and that MailReach sees it every day, and lists attachments among the top deliverability killers because they are used for phishing and hacking [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:09:00].

List quality is where bounce math kills domains. Damien measures catch-all addresses bouncing around 23%, and calls the worst move sending to valid addresses first, then going all-in on catch-alls once the valids run out; the fix is building every list with only 20% catch-alls so the bounce rate stays controlled [8EaKo_iAiPE @ 00:06:42]. Kyle Dickson calls bounces the enemy of domain and inbox reputation, runs an automation that pauses campaigns at a 4% bounce rate, and warns that once bounces pile up the only options are buying new inboxes and domains or letting them warm up for months [drsocawArfo @ 00:06:33]. Smartlead's agency operator sets the high-bounce auto-protection toggle to 5% so a campaign pauses itself, because past that line long-term domain and mailbox health starts taking real damage [GZEdhosrYRc @ 00:14:23]. Coldbot's diagnostic for a sudden placement drop runs in layers: did volume change, was a new domain added, did bounce rate rise, did the list source change, did tracking links appear, did one mailbox behave differently? Run seed tests by provider and compare domains instead of trusting campaign-wide averages, because authentication and warm-up are prerequisites, not guarantees [fM-ywBq8yoU @ 00:06:02].
