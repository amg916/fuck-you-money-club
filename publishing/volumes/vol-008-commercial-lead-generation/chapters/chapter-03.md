## 1. Reconcile billed leads to the canonical owner-profit waterfall  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Build the ledger around a client-month cohort, not an inbox, campaign, or advertising account. Every delivered record needs a prospect identifier, trigger timestamp, delivery timestamp, contract version, price, acceptance decision, and eventual cash receipt. In HubSpot or Salesforce, distinguish delivered, provisionally accepted, contractually earned, credited, and collected states; a booked meeting alone proves none of the later states. The Base illustration comprises ten full-year equivalent clients paying $2,500 monthly retainers, producing $300,000, plus 1,280 provisional lead units priced at $125, producing $160,000 before credits. Rejecting or reversing 280 units removes $35,000, leaving 1,000 accepted units and $425,000 recognized operator revenue. The resulting 21.875% lead-unit rejection rate differs from the 7.61% gross-billing allowance because retainers occupy most billings. These are illustrative contract mechanics constructed to reconcile the scorecard, not historical conversion observations. Advertising paid by clients directly to Google or LinkedIn never enters this waterfall. Where the agency advances advertising money as an agent, track the reimbursement receivable and vendor payable separately rather than inflating service sales. Earned retainers also require actual service delivery; a prepaid invoice is cash before it is necessarily revenue.

Apply the same construction to the other cases without changing their canonical totals. Conservative combines $60,000 in earned retainers with 500 provisional units at $100; 100 rejected units remove $10,000 from $110,000 billings, leaving $100,000 revenue. Scaled combines $540,000 in earned retainers with 1,800 provisional units at $150; 400 rejected units remove $60,000 from $810,000 billings, leaving $750,000 revenue. The table then deducts every modeled operating cost once. Contractor delivery includes research, verification, appointment setting, routing review, and quality checks. Acquisition expense belongs to winning agency clients, not the client's media budget. Data costs buy prospect inputs; software covers CRM, permitted enrichment infrastructure, mailboxes, and monitoring. Gross contribution after delivery, data, and software is $54,000, $262,500, and $490,000 respectively. It is not owner profit because selling, management, collections, and overhead remain unpaid. Treat the expense allocation as an explicit planning assumption: the supplied scorecard fixes revenue, final margin, profit, and staffing, but does not supply an audited cost ledger. Replace individual allocations with actual invoices while preserving a visible reconciliation rather than silently changing the benchmark. Keep overhead and delivery categories mutually exclusive so payroll cannot disappear between them.

The canonical teams are one full-time person plus three contractors for Conservative and Base, then two full-time people plus four contractors for Scaled. Contractor counts describe relationships, not six full-time salaries. The Conservative $32,000 delivery budget therefore requires fractional assignments and founder execution, while Base supports $110,000 of purchased delivery capacity. In Scaled, the $75,000 overhead allocation includes an illustrative $60,000 loaded non-owner employee cost and $15,000 of administration; test that assumption against the actual hiring market. Modeled owner profit is the residual available to the owner after the listed expenses, not a second salary added on top. No separate market-rate founder replacement salary or owner-level tax calculation is embedded here. That distinction matters when comparing an owner-operated practice with an absentee investment. Reconcile the twelve monthly cohort totals to the annual income statement and then reconcile collections to bank deposits. At Base, $127,500 divided by $425,000 yields exactly 30.0%; booking client advertising as revenue would change that denominator without creating earnings. Chargebacks already represented in the $35,000 allowance must not reappear as a second expense when the credit memo settles. Record any excess loss separately and revise the cohort estimate.

## 3. Fund the DSO and payout-lock gap before increasing delivery  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Profit does not fund payroll until customers pay and processors release usable cash. Define DSO here as days from earned-service invoicing to customer payment; define payout lock separately as days between that payment and unrestricted bank availability. If your accounting DSO already measures final bank receipt, adding the lock again would overstate the gap. For an intentionally conservative steady-state screen, assume all annual operating deductions are cash costs spread evenly over 365 days, paid before the corresponding collections. Conservative uses 30 DSO plus seven lock days; Base uses 45 plus fourteen; Scaled uses 60 plus twenty-one. Dividing $80,000, $297,500, and $450,000 by 365 produces daily burn of approximately $219.18, $815.07, and $1,232.88. Multiplying by the respective 37, 59, and 81 days produces funding screens of $8,110, $48,089, and $99,863. These figures are not additional income-statement expenses or exact balance-sheet working capital. They estimate liquidity tied up during the cash-conversion delay. Retainer prepayments, vendor terms, noncash expenses, actual processor reserves, and weekly payroll timing belong in the detailed forecast. A processor holding a percentage reserve needs its own cash line rather than an invented extra DSO assumption.

The stated $5,000–$40,000 startup capital range can launch a smaller operation but cannot automatically finance the modeled Base or Scaled collection gap. Adding fourteen days of cash-cost contingency raises the screens to approximately $11,178, $59,500, and $117,123. Those figures explain why a profitable agency can stall when it adds clients with long payment terms. The supplied $2,000–$12,000 monthly opex range should be treated as an early operating-footprint indicator, not a cap on the all-in annual scenario deductions: Base expenses average $24,791.67 monthly once delivery, acquisition, and all other modeled costs are included. Require monthly retainers in advance where commercially feasible, set a fixed lead-dispute window, and release contractor batches against funded service periods. In the Base illustration, ten prepaid $2,500 retainers bring in $25,000 monthly before the acceptance-based lead component settles. Model that timing explicitly rather than subtracting an entire year's retainers from the float estimate. Deposits remain obligations until earned and may be refundable under the contract. Never finance one client's refundable advertising balance with another client's money. Segregate the ledger and cash controls needed to honor each client's obligations.

Build the thirteen-week cash forecast from dated transactions: opening unrestricted cash, retainer receipts, accepted-lead receipts, contractor runs, subscriptions, refunds, tax payments, and financing settlements. Keep accounting credit allowances separate from restricted processor cash and discretionary liquidity buffers. The Base $35,000 annual scrub allowance averages $2,916.67 monthly, but it reduces provisional revenue; it is not automatically another $2,916.67 monthly cash expense. Only a collected charge later refunded creates that refund outflow. Estimate refund timing from mature cohorts and use a clearly labeled contingency for immature cohorts, rather than claiming statistical precision from a handful of records. For client advertising advances, add the full unreimbursed principal to the forecast even though it is outside recognized service revenue. A $100,000 advance outstanding for thirty days at 12% annual simple interest costs about $986.30 using 365 days, before issuer fees; the $1,000 reward example is a rounded planning input. Pause new advances when reimbursement timing deteriorates. Approved prefunding or direct client billing removes that exposure more reliably than chasing a larger card limit. Reconcile forecast deposits to bank availability, not the processor dashboard's headline balance, before authorizing the next delivery cycle.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the cohort and acceptance contract
   - Export delivery records with timestamps, agreed prices, acceptance states, and credit deadlines.
   - Deduplicate by client and prospect identifier; preserve the qualification evidence used for each billed unit.
2. Post revenue and cost without double counting
   - Reconcile provisional charges, scrub credits, earned retainers, and the six expense categories.
   - Keep client advertising reimbursements outside service revenue and distinguish credit memos from cash refunds.
3. Date every expected cash movement
   - Populate thirteen weekly columns with bank-available collections and actual payment dates.
   - Include processor restrictions, refundable deposits, contractor runs, financing principal, and interest separately.
4. Fund the stressed trough before expanding
   - Compare minimum weekly unrestricted cash against the chosen contingency and documented financing capacity.
   - Shorten terms, obtain client prefunding, or reduce new delivery commitments when the forecast crosses the minimum.

## 4. Stress acceptance, CAC, churn, and lawful operating capacity  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Stress one variable at a time before combining shocks. A 25% increase in the modeled annual acquisition budgets costs another $4,500 Conservative, $15,000 Base, and $22,500 Scaled, reducing annual owner profit to $15,500, $112,500, and $277,500 if revenue and other costs remain fixed. An additional five percentage points of rejection applies to provisional lead billings, not total service revenue. Against $50,000, $160,000, and $270,000 of such billings, the incremental credits are $2,500, $8,000, and $13,500. Owner profit becomes $17,500, $119,500, and $286,500, while recognized revenue also declines by those amounts. With both shocks, profit becomes $13,000, $104,500, and $264,000. These are downside sensitivities, not replacements for the canonical scorecard. A fifteen-day collection delay instead consumes approximately $3,288, $12,226, and $18,493 of additional liquidity before any incremental interest expense. Do not subtract that principal from profit. If the delay requires borrowing, calculate the interest on the actual drawn amount and duration and then add that expense. The distinction between cash tied up and cash permanently lost is the central operating discipline in this chapter.

Measure agency CAC using closed, paying client cohorts, including founder sales time at an explicit internal rate when evaluating repeatability. The modeled cash budgets of $18,000 for six wins, $60,000 for twelve, and $90,000 for eighteen imply $3,000, $5,000, and $5,000 cash CAC. Those annual wins are not automatically concurrent full-year accounts; replacement clients and late-year starts explain why Base uses ten annual equivalents. Its monthly contribution per equivalent account is $262,500 divided by ten and twelve, or $2,187.50. A $5,000 CAC therefore has an illustrative 2.29-month contribution payback before collection delay, overhead, and financing. Avoid presenting that figure as lifetime value without retention evidence. Losing one Base account's $30,000 annual retainer while avoiding $8,000 of associated delivery cost reduces profit by $22,000 to $105,500; any lost lead fees require an additional adjustment. Track contribution by client, vertical, trigger, and contractor hour. A cheap data source that produces disputed appointments can destroy margin despite attractive cost per record. Pause the offending segment, preserve the profitable cohorts, and renegotiate qualification criteria before buying more traffic or adding mailboxes.

Operational capacity also depends on lawful contact and defensible evidence. CAN-SPAM, 15 U.S.C. 7704, applies to commercial email, including business outreach: use accurate headers, appropriate advertising identification, a valid postal address, and a functioning opt-out, and honor opt-outs within ten business days. Centralize suppression across every outbound inbox and subcontractor; additional domains must not become a way to resume contact with someone who opted out. For calls and texts, evaluate the technology, number type, purpose, and applicable consent requirements under 47 U.S.C. 227 and 47 CFR 64.1200, plus relevant state rules. B2B targeting is not a universal exception, particularly when business contacts use mobile numbers. Where California privacy law applies, assess notice, deletion, opt-out, and service-provider versus sale or sharing obligations before reselling enriched records. Store source rights and contact-permission evidence beside the lead, with retention tied to documented needs. CRM automation should stop disallowed routing and produce auditable exception queues, not merely increase throughput. Finally, refresh the weekly forecast whenever a major client changes acceptance terms, a data vendor removes resale rights, or a payment processor extends a reserve. Each event changes the sellable unit, the cost to fulfill it, or the date cash becomes usable.
