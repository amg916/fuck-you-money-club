# Chapter 01: Lead Agency Models, Pricing, and Scale

*Pay-per-lead versus pay-per-appointment and retainers, pricing calculations, appointment-setting costs, rank-and-rent comparisons, and practitioner growth accounts*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Operators who left retainers agree that the money in commercial lead generation comes from owned assets: one niche, one brand, one ad account, and leads resold to buyers who already pay for them. Billy Robinson, Kelly Joseph, Caleb Harris and Leadbase report monthly books of $221K to $400K built on that arbitrage. The mechanics are simple math. Generate a lead at $18 to $50, resell it at 1.5x to 3x, and keep the buyer's ROAS at 2x to 3x or better so they reorder. Speed-to-lead decides retention, so operators add AI SMS agents, three automated texts and two-minute call rules to delivery. They split over the billing unit. Ruben Frederic and Benjamin Bjørnholt push pay-per-appointment. Cameron England and Nick Wood call pure pay-per-lead broken. Michael Gardiner and Belkins keep retainers once proof exists.

## The Practitioner Consensus: Own the Lead, Pick the Niche, Fix Speed-to-Lead

Every operator running a large pay-per-lead book started inside a retainer agency they now describe as a trap. Billy Robinson had roughly ten staff on 50-hour weeks and says he was "working for scraps." Then a mortgage broker paying a 4,000 retainer plus 3,500 in ad spend for about 55 leads, a $136 cost per lead, accepted his offer to sell leads at $75 apiece instead [NStq42nosUk @ 00:00:55]. Ryan at Leadbase charged $1,500 to $3K a month on retainer, topped out around 20 to 25K monthly, and says those clients "bossed us around" [4npGl2BQhgg @ 00:03:29]. Both describe the same escape route: ownership. Ryan's line is blunt: "I own it all." That covers the leads, the data, the website and the Facebook account, so he is no longer renting a client's assets [4npGl2BQhgg @ 00:05:43]. Robinson's build sequence makes the same point step by step. Sell leads instead of retainers. Lock one niche and validate demand. Own the brand, site, pixel and ad account as sellable assets. Wire up distribution from day one, run B2B Facebook image ads to acquire buyers, and get paid upfront [NStq42nosUk @ 00:03:06]. The agency stops renting hours to one account holder and starts selling inventory it controls to every buyer the niche will support.

The second point of agreement is where the model works at all. Caleb Harris screens for two things: high customer value and existing marketing spend. His example is a $10K concrete job set against a $100 to $150 lead, and he warns operators not to burn three months choosing [TNZJNdxQvsM @ 00:05:03]. Cameron England argues pay-per-lead is wrong for more than 95% of agency owners. Even so, he concedes it fits blue-collar niches that sites like Angie's List have conditioned to pay for leads [yRgel0EBz2s @ 00:01:43]. Benjamin Bjørnholt sees the same buyer psychology from the appointment side. Remodeling and roofing contractors are used to performance pricing from Angi's and HomeAdvisor. Medical buyers carry lower tickets and are used to branding programs, so paid-in-full deals fit them better, with telemedicine at 6k PIF [d7ES0xh_MqI @ 00:10:21]. The My First Million hosts set a hard floor: the blueprint only works when a lead is worth $100 or more, and the ones below that line "seem to struggle" [Poc5H6aVTlw @ 00:03:24]. The consensus filter has three parts: a big ticket, buyers who already spend on marketing, and a category that already treats bought leads as a normal line item rather than a novelty.

The third agreement is that the lead itself rarely kills the account; the buyer's follow-up does. Billy Robinson's old mortgage-broker offer delivered 25 leads at about 100 each into an email or Google Sheet. The broker reached roughly 40%, converted about one, and never reordered. Robinson calls this "a speed-to-lead problem" paid for through churn [-_uHG2CwBjA @ 00:01:30]. Pavlo saw the same pattern selling Facebook-ad leads by spreadsheet. Owners call once during work hours, get no answer and never call again, so the account dies within two to four months. His clients with real sales teams stayed four to six months or longer [OiTif_DfgUE @ 00:01:34]. Kelly Joseph writes the rule into delivery: call by minute two, make three calls on day one, route round-robin with daily caps, and avoid live transfers [muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:31:28]. Ruben Frederic runs roughly 42 to 43 pay-per-lead roofing clients and puts the leverage in plain math. His typical contact rate on 14-day GoHighLevel workflows is one in five to one in seven, and lifting it from 15% to 30% doubles the business [B6M6968u10c @ 00:14:17]. All of them treat contact speed as part of the product they sell, not as the buyer's problem, because the monthly reorder depends on it.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Pricing is markup arithmetic run backward from the buyer's return. Hunter Ceroy reports $600K a month and starts from the rule that a client needs a 2 to 3x ROAS. He prices a bathroom remodel lead that costs $50 at $75, a 1.5x markup. A hundred leads bill $7,500, a 3% close on a $6K average order returns $18K, and the client lands at 2.4x [0Cfp-Fc0sag @ 00:02:25]. In life insurance he generates leads near $18 and sells them at $45 for 2.5x. A 6% conversion on a $3K policy then hands the buyer 4x [0Cfp-Fc0sag @ 00:05:43]. Kelly Joseph runs the same math on $1k of spend at a $25 CPL. Forty leads sold at $60 bring $2,400 in revenue and $1,400 profit, a 58% margin, while 25 billable calls at $110 produce $2,750 and $1,750 profit [muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:05:53]. Caleb Harris keeps the beginner version simpler still. Twenty leads at $100 is $2,000 collected upfront against $500 of ads, for $1,500 profit, and 100 leads from one buyer equals the revenue of ten $1K retainers [TNZJNdxQvsM @ 00:09:55]. Ceroy's discipline is to set the markup, then check it against the buyer's close rate and average order. He trims the multiple whenever the buyer's return slips under the 2x line.

The delivery stack is cheap and largely off the shelf. Billy Robinson runs each buyer on a GoHighLevel sub-account plus Appointment Wise, which costs 97 a month for three clients or 297 for unlimited. He lists GHL's native AI, CloseBot, Stammer AI and ZappyChat as alternatives. The bot asks five qualifying questions, and he says a female bot name such as "Sarah" converts better [-_uHG2CwBjA @ 00:02:36]. Adding that SMS agent to the leads let him raise the price from 100 to 150 per lead. For routing, Robinson's distribution software sends leads round robin or in any sequence he sets. His tool is Lead Bite, with Zapier as the fallback, and campaigns keep running until every client's quota is filled [fsKaAe_lO6M @ 00:00:58]. Kelly Joseph's funnel runs bridge page, then quiz, then TCPA consent, then thank-you page. His headlines combine audience, pain and speed [muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:18:56]. Jakub Jablonsky makes over half a million a month and uses the quiz funnel most. It routes each lead to the right buyer and sells rejects to aggregators to raise revenue per visitor. He fires the pixel only on the best prospects [SygbkYDB3iM @ 00:04:31]. Across all three operators, qualification and routing happen before a buyer ever sees the lead.

Buyers are acquired with the same ad machine that generates the leads. Billy Robinson runs image, SMS-screenshot and before-and-after ads with story copy aimed at one-man-band mortgage brokers. A quiz first disqualifies anyone who has never bought leads or has a budget under 3K. He reports B2B cost per lead of A$30 to 50, US$20 to 35 or £15 to 20, and one in three of those leads books a call [-_uHG2CwBjA @ 00:07:33]. His P&L at 20 clients runs to just under $40K in cost against $35K profit. The costs are media 25K, AI 500, GHL 300, closer 12,500 and developer 700. He claims the model works even at 100% churn [-_uHG2CwBjA @ 00:10:09]. Kelly Joseph gets 98% of his buyers from Meta ads, even with Canva creatives at $50 a day [muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:36:21]. Ruben Frederic skipped ads entirely for his appointment side. He bought data on Fiverr, loaded it into GoHighLevel and ran a text blast pitching $249 to $300 per appointment. The offer carried no ad spend, retainer or setup fee, the first appointment was free, and he closed eight clients in three days [B6M6968u10c @ 00:04:19]. All three run a filtered pipe: disqualify weak buyers early, show them the product working, and close fast before delivery starts.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Price-check the niche with real buyers
   - Email existing lead buyers and in-house marketing managers for a per-lead price before building anything.
   - A My First Million host emailed a lead gen site, which offered $100 per verified long-haul CDL driver lead. He then emailed trucking-company growth managers on LinkedIn, who said they pay $150. That set a $100 to $150 range before test ads produced leads at $250 [My First Million — Poc5H6aVTlw @ 00:00:59]. Then run the niche through three filters: plenty of buyers, a lead price of 2.5x to 3x cost, and ads that will not get banned [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:10:21].
2. Build the owned funnel and server-side tracking
   - Stand up a bridge page, quiz, TCPA consent step and thank-you page on your own brand and ad account.
   - Write headlines from audience plus pain plus speed, and place TCPA consent between the quiz and the thank-you page [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:18:56]. Wire Conversions API server-side via Stape.io and post sold leads and billable calls back to the platforms [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:26:39].
3. Launch, judge and scale traffic on a fixed clock
   - Run one broad CBO campaign with 3 to 5 adsets of 3 to 5 creatives each at $50 to $100 a day.
   - Judge results in 72-hour windows. Kill anything that has spent 1.5x to 2x the target CPL with zero leads [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:23:27]. Scale winners by 20 to 30% per day [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:24:33].
4. Collect upfront, route exclusively, enforce terms
   - Make new buyers prepay a pilot, then route leads round robin until each quota is filled.
   - New buyers prepay; big commercial buyers can pay per billable call [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:07:25]. Distribute round robin until every client's quota is met [Billy Robinson — fsKaAe_lO6M @ 00:00:58]. Move to net 7 after the pilot, and pause delivery when a buyer is 7 days late [Kelly Joseph — muQkzU3fXJY @ 00:40:30].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is what the agency actually sells to the client. Ruben Frederic runs both books at once, roughly 8 pay-per-appointment roofing clients against 42-43 pay-per-lead clients. Appointments bill at $250 on a $125 cost, while leads top out at $100 against a $50-75 market, and the final recommendation is a hybrid: appointments front-facing, leads as the downsell [B6M6968u10c @ 00:26:59]. Cameron England goes further, arguing pay-per-booking at $200 beats selling a $40 lead because raw leads are a commodity [yRgel0EBz2s @ 00:12:20]. Pavlo arrived at the same answer through churn. Spreadsheet and email lead delivery lost clients inside 2-4 months, while three automated follow-up texts stacked with before/afters, testimonials and a booking calendar pushed retention to 6-7 months and then past a year [OiTif_DfgUE @ 00:01:50]. Belkins attacks appointment pricing from the buyer's chair. Providers price their risk into every meeting, so ten meetings at $2,000 each is simply retainer money, and the provider plays it safe [RZgWzmwugYU @ 00:06:57]. Ruben Frederic also flags the ceiling on the appointment side: a push toward roughly 40 clients with poorly managed setters failed, and the model gets hard past 30 clients unless fulfillment is standardized.

The retainer question splits three ways. Billy Robinson calls it a trap: about 10 staff on 50-hour weeks working for scraps, until a mortgage broker paying a 4,000 retainer plus 3,500 in ad spend for roughly 55 leads, a $136 CPL, got flipped into buying leads at $75 [NStq42nosUk @ 00:00:55]. Leadbase ran the same retainer grind from 2015 to 2020 at $1,500-3K per client, peaking at 20-25K/mo while clients bossed the team around, yet still tells operators to keep about 5K/mo of retainer survival income while building an owned lead brand [4npGl2BQhgg @ 00:08:09]. Nick Wood ran the opposite direction. The original math was $100 per lead times 50 for 5 grand, but clients hit a spending ceiling and disputed invoices, so the last 2.5 years have been flat-fee deals only, with the warning that per-lead billing will only end in disaster [3YFMp1RGYG0 @ 00:05:44]. Michael Gardiner sits between the camps. Every managed client runs on retainer, credited with scaring off bad clients, scaling by tiers toward $10,000 a month and removing per-lead judgment, yet the same structure is hard to sell without proof and churns without results [JJHRXPMJfuo @ 00:14:12].

Who to sell to, and whether to give anything away, also divides the field just as sharply. Billy Robinson deliberately targets one-man-band mortgage brokers, roughly 25,000 of them in Australia, because large firms bring CRM conflicts, and 20 such clients equal 75k/mo [-_uHG2CwBjA @ 00:04:39]. Jakub Jablonsky flips that: avoid the 3-5k/mo small buyer, chase midsize companies at 3M+ revenue paying 5k-25k/mo that close in 1-2 weeks, since four of them reach roughly 100k profit, and land 50-100M+ firms paying 25K-200K/mo through a roughly 4-week compliance cycle into 22-24 month partnerships [SygbkYDB3iM @ 00:01:20]. On first deals, Ruben Frederic's outbound play strips every barrier: Fiverr data into GHL, a text blast pitching $249-300 appointments, no ad spend, no retainer, no setup fee and the first appointment free, which closed 8 clients in 3 days [B6M6968u10c @ 00:04:19]. Michael Gardiner answers the guarantee question from the other side with a flat no. Proof of work, meaning a script, a list or 1,000 free touches, or a short trial for engaged prospects replaces any promise, first month free is out, and a $500-1,000 retainer plus a performance fee is offered as the hybrid structure [JJHRXPMJfuo @ 00:16:24].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Lead quality disputes are the first margin killer, and the math is brutal on both leads and appointments. Cameron England prices leads at $20-75 with a 2-3x markup, then shows that a $20 CPL sold at $40 with 50% disputed only breaks even, while the agency is the one fronting the ad spend [yRgel0EBz2s @ 00:04:01]. Nick Wood lived it. Clients stopped at a comfort ceiling of 10-40 leads, then at 30 leads times $100 for 3 grand they red-penciled anything with no answer, no estimate or a location 20 minutes outside town, and every month became an argument over who eats it, even after paying a developer about $10,000 for tracking software with QuickBooks auto-invoicing [3YFMp1RGYG0 @ 00:03:30]. Ruben Frederic sees the same leak on appointments: one credited appointment wipes the margin, so credits have to stay at or below 10% [B6M6968u10c @ 00:15:31]. Michael Gardiner, despite making hundreds of thousands in profit on pay per call, ranks it last because no-shows are hard to track, every lead gets disputed, cash flow swings, and leads slip directly to the client, forcing the agency to run appointment setting itself [JJHRXPMJfuo @ 00:08:24].

Cash flow and churn are the second trap. Cameron England lists no upfront cash as a core flaw: CAC runs about $1,000 on a 5K spend, a churned client leaves the agency roughly $1.5K down, and around 50% churn in the first month, and the fix offered is 7K upfront for 90 days [yRgel0EBz2s @ 00:07:50]. Billy Robinson traces churn back to what happens after the lead lands. A mortgage broker buying 25 leads at about 100 each reaches roughly 40%, converts about one, and never reorders, a speed-to-lead problem paid for through churn [-_uHG2CwBjA @ 00:01:30]. Ruben Frederic's lead book shows what that treadmill looks like at scale: about 25 clients acquired and about 25 lost each month, an agency stuck at $100K since January, and 18 people delivering roughly 170 leads a day [B6M6968u10c @ 00:07:44]. Pavlo puts a clock on it, with SMMA clients lasting three or four months and the same lead delivery losing clients inside 2-4 months, because owners call once during work hours, get no answer and never call again, while clients with real sales teams stayed 4-6+ months [OiTif_DfgUE @ 00:00:50].

Rising media costs and premature builds are the third trap. Billy Robinson's December 2023 finance campaigns saw CPL climb from $35 to $55, so 170,000 in sales returned only about 31,000 profit, a 20.5% margin against a roughly 35% norm, leaving about 15K on the table, with event matching, the Conversions API and bid caps as the fixes [NStq42nosUk @ 00:05:15]. Ruben Frederic reports the same squeeze across three years: cost per appointment rose from $15 to $60, and CPMs now run $70-100, up from about $30 [B6M6968u10c @ 00:25:33]. Jakub Jablonsky's expensive lesson was building a custom lead exchange for small buyers, roughly 110k and six months of work that does not get a recommendation now, with the added warning that 80% of marketers' leads are low quality [SygbkYDB3iM @ 00:01:04]. My First Million's hosts ran the price check before building: a trucking lead site would pay $100 per verified CDL driver and trucking companies $150, yet ads to a capture page produced leads at $250, a gap they still judged could be made profitable, and their blueprint warning is that lead gen under $100 per lead tends to struggle [Poc5H6aVTlw @ 00:01:15].
