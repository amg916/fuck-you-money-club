# Chapter 11: FDCPA and Regulation F Compliance Risk

*Who counts as a debt collector (Barbato v. Crown Asset), Sections 1692e and 1692f, Regulation F rules, consumer rights, anatomy of an FDCPA lawsuit, eviction-related liability, credit-dispute case studies, deposing a collection attorney, and proposed legislation affecting collection litigation*

## Executive Summary

Consumer attorneys and collection-side insiders agree the FDCPA hits third-party collectors and debt buyers on consumer debt, and that Regulation F turned contact limits into countable violations: seven call attempts per seven days per account, no calls outside 8:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m., opt-outs on every text, and a validation notice within five days. Fee-shifting is the engine. Statutory damages run up to $1,000, and Aaron Swift's bronze file turned two technical violations into a roughly $4,000 debt wipe, a deleted tradeline, and about $4,500 in fees. Street mechanics center on Reg F restriction letters, certified mail, itemization audits, and contract-fee checks. Practitioners split on whether seven-in-seven is a hard cap or a presumption, whether payment restarts the limitations clock in almost every state or only some, and how much validation letters actually demand.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner draws the same perimeter first: the FDCPA bites third-party collectors chasing consumer debt, not original creditors and not commercial paper. Simple Explain frames it as third-party collectors only [njrU1ZwxRCY @ 00:00:57], and LawShelf limits coverage to personal, family, or household debts such as cars, credit cards, and some student loans, with commercial debt excluded and attorneys who regularly collect pulled inside [i6PAssklgJg @ 00:01:24]. Jeff Loman of Guardian Litigation Group sharpens the edge for street use: banks, payday lenders, rent-a-centers, and tolls sit outside, and business debt never qualifies [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:04:20]. The perimeter is wider than debt buyers want it to be. John Watts walks through Barbato v. Crown Asset, where the buyer argued it merely purchased charged-off receivables and farmed collection out; the court held that an entity meeting the principal-purpose definition cannot dodge the Act by hiring a third party to do its collecting [fPw38bPWFtw @ 00:02:40]. Watts frames the economics that make buyers fight so hard: Crown buys a thousand-dollar debt for fifty bucks and the consumer may settle for $700. For an operator, the first screening question is therefore who is actually collecting, what kind of debt it is, and whose business model depends on the payment.

The contact rules form the second layer of agreement. Attorney Kaiser Jansen, on SeniorLiving.Org, states the Reg F line bluntly: collectors can only attempt to call seven times in seven days, and after a connection only one contact per seven-day period, counted per account, so five accounts can mean up to 35 calls [9RtU4rpS3pw @ 00:05:51]. Jansen also warns that saying 'okay' to a callback counts as consent. WealthCurve adds the clock window shared by nearly every speaker: no calls before 8:00 a.m. or after 9:00 p.m., plus bans on threats, impersonation, and unauthorized fees [cKYmFnf_o2Q @ 00:03:43]. Workplace contact runs on notice. Loman says calls at work are allowed until the consumer tells the collector personal calls cannot be received there, and any call after that is a violation [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:20:44]. Digital channels carry their own trap for collectors. Night Ferry notes texts and emails are not counted toward the call cap but cannot harass, and each must offer a simple opt-out [7HVra7o-cZg @ 00:04:04]; Jansen adds that contact continuing after a 'stop' reply is actionable. The operating consensus is simple: every channel restriction becomes enforceable the moment the consumer puts it on the record, and every post-notice contact becomes a countable, documentable violation.

The third consensus pillar is the paper trail and the money behind it. Jansen describes the 5-in-5 rule: a validation notice within five days, with a tear-off dispute sheet, the original creditor, account number, and an itemization, illustrated with a balance that climbed from $200 to 1,396 [9RtU4rpS3pw @ 00:10:42]. WealthCurve says a written dispute sent inside the 30-day window forces the collector to stop all collection activity, and warns against mailing it on day 29 [cKYmFnf_o2Q @ 00:03:01]. On remedies, LawShelf lists actual damages, additional damages up to $1,000, the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth in a class action, plus costs and fees, and states that in many cases the potential for attorneys fees drives FDCPA litigation [i6PAssklgJg @ 00:05:23]. Aaron Swift turns that into a business thesis: two technical violations erased roughly $4,000 of debt and still produced $1,000 statutory damages, and his lesson is that no case is too small [owpVxkqEbEk @ 00:06:30]. Jansen notes FDCPA firms typically take a third on contingency. Loman puts it plainly: under fee shifting, the attorney is paid by the other side. Across the board, the speakers treat fee-shifting as the engine that makes even small, technical violations worth filing.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Aaron Swift's bronze-tier case is the cleanest street workflow on record, the 'blocking and tackling' files that keep the lights on. Intake filters each consumer into litigation, disputes, or a credit-repair affiliate partner, then the firm sends third-party collectors a letter by fax or email [owpVxkqEbEk @ 00:03:10]. Swift stresses it is not a validation letter, which only matters inside the first 30 days; it is built on Reg F, restricts contact channels and times such as 5:00 to 8:00 p.m., and disputes the debt. The trap then springs on its own. The collector kept texting, and the credit reports never marked the account disputed. Loman confirms the second hook: when a collector tradeline such as LVNV or Portfolio Recovery is disputed, it must be marked disputed [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:14:16]. Swift's payout on those two technical violations: about $4,000 of debt forgiven, the tradeline deleted, $1,000 in statutory damages, roughly $4,500 in attorney fees, and about $450 to the affiliate as contract paralegal [owpVxkqEbEk @ 00:05:22]. Swift calls the result a debt almost completely erased like it never existed. The unit economics are the point. A low-dollar file with a fax letter and two missed obligations funds the attorney, the affiliate, and a near-total debt wipe for the client.

Once a file goes to suit, John Watts lays out the procedural rhythm. He files in state court 99.9 percent of the time because of Spokeo standing arguments, and defendants, who get 30 days to answer or remove, remove 99.9 percent of the time [Ce0D7z4zJFo @ 00:00:30]. Motions to dismiss are often baseless because 'it makes the lawyers money,' so he usually amends the complaint as of right instead. Discovery then runs through interrogatories, document requests, requests for admission, and depositions, followed by summary judgment with damages usually left to the jury [Ce0D7z4zJFo @ 00:03:32]. At trial he calls the collector's own witnesses, and the collector's motion for judgment as a matter of law has always been denied in his trials. Swift tempers the trial math: the vast majority, 99 percent or more, never reach trial [owpVxkqEbEk @ 00:01:14]. Brian Parker shows what discovery can surface on the other side. Deposing collection attorney Bradley Johnson, he extracted that the firm files about 50 suits a week, roughly 10 a day, off a form complaint with an auto-generated summons, scrubbing through LexisNexis for deceased, new addresses, bankruptcy, and active military [RwXTJhX-rxo @ 00:21:52]. Volume filing on templates is exactly what deposition questioning is built to expose.

The balance itself is the next attack surface. Loman's hospital-debt workflow starts with a HIPAA request for medical records including billing, because the collector is not required to itemize under 'G' of the FDCPA, then pulls an insurance accounting of what was paid; he routinely finds double billing and code changes [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:41:14]. Host Hannah Locklear was charged $16 for one ibuprofen pill and got the debt lowered after demanding an itemized statement, and Loman has a client billed $6,000 for a $300 IV. Brian Parker hunts the same inflation through §1692f: every charge must sit inside the four corners of the contract, and an unauthorized 25% collection fee is a violation [YYdsx06msa0 @ 00:03:38]. He also stacks claims, since false credit reporting under §1692e8 supports an FCRA claim too, so 'you can hit them twice' [YYdsx06msa0 @ 00:01:13]. Parker frames these as strict-liability violations. None of this works without proof of delivery. Jermaine, drawing on his time working in debt collections, insists on copying every letter and sending it certified, not regular mail or email, for a 16-digit tracking number, because otherwise it is your word against the collector's [XSfDS_s1URg @ 00:06:09]. Many collectors, he warns, never fix errors after being called out, so the documented file is the weapon.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split on call frequency is whether seven attempts in seven days is a wall or a tripwire. Kaiser Jansen, appearing on SeniorLiving.Org, frames it as absolute: "now debt collectors can only attempt to call you seven times in seven days that's it," and adds that the count runs per account, so a consumer carrying five accounts can absorb up to 35 calls in a week [9RtU4rpS3pw @ 00:05:51]. Night Ferry reads the same rule as a presumption of violation rather than a hard cap, and notes that texts and emails are not counted toward it, though each must still offer a simple opt-out [7HVra7o-cZg @ 00:03:24]. Jeff Loman of Guardian Litigation Group pushes the frequency fight further, citing case law where 18 calls in a single day were not treated as harassing unless the collector actually spoke with the consumer [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:08:09]. Michael Stillman, past president of the NCBA, attacks the rule from the collection-bar side: after a right-party contact, no further contact is allowed within seven days without consent, which collides head-on with judges who order a hallway call, prompting the attorney to ask the bench, "are you also going to indemnify me" [9VNthZZ6JNk @ 00:08:37].

Practitioners also split on how dangerous a single payment is on old paper. Jansen tells SeniorLiving.Org viewers that a payment resets the statute of limitations in "almost every state," naming Texas as the exception, and pairs that with the ban on threatening suit over time-barred debt [9RtU4rpS3pw @ 00:17:58]. WealthCurve opens with a narrower warning that paying can restart the clock only "in some states," puts typical limitation periods at three to six years, and points to a 2023 CFPB advisory barring suit threats on time-barred debt [cKYmFnf_o2Q @ 00:04:42]. Loman widens the trigger list beyond money: a payment, a payment plan, or even an admission can reset the clock, and the statute only limits lawsuits, so collection can continue "forever" while credit reporting runs seven years [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:29:22]. Brian Parker of KillDebt takes the most aggressive posture on threatened suits under section 1692e5, telling consumers to "wait it out" when a collector threatens litigation, and stressing that nobody can be garnished without a judgment [YYdsx06msa0 @ 00:07:04]. The playbook choice is stark: Jansen and Loman treat any engagement on stale debt as a reset risk, while Parker treats an empty threat of suit as a strict-liability violation in its own right.

The paperwork camp splits hardest on how much to demand from a collector. Meet Jermaine, drawing on time spent working in debt collections, hands out a free four-letter bundle covering pay to delete, goodwill, cease and desist, and debt validation [XSfDS_s1URg @ 00:00:19]. Loman flatly rejects the 28-item validation demands circulating online: "That is absolutely not the law," explaining that only three items are required, and warning that a written pay-for-delete can get an attorney classed as a credit repair organization, so the safer route is getting deletion by email or informally [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:34:56]. Attorney Aaron Swift runs a third playbook, sending third-party collectors a letter by fax or email that is explicitly not a validation letter, because validation only matters inside the first 30 days; it is built on Reg F, restricts contact channels and times such as 5:00 to 8:00 p.m., and disputes the debt [owpVxkqEbEk @ 00:03:29]. Jermaine insists every letter go by certified mail for a 16-digit tracking number, because with regular mail it is "your word against" the collector's, while Swift reports that his letter, followed by two technical violations, wiped roughly $4,000 of debt for a bronze-tier client.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The cheapest losses are self-inflicted on the phone and at the mailbox. Jansen warns that simply saying "okay" when a collector asks to call back counts as consent, which quietly unlocks contact the seven-in-seven rule would otherwise block [9RtU4rpS3pw @ 00:06:29]. WealthCurve flags two timing traps: the dispute must be in writing and should not be mailed on day 29 of the 30-day window, and the one-year limit to sue runs from the violation, not from when the consumer discovers it [cKYmFnf_o2Q @ 00:07:18]. Night Ferry puts the scale of the biggest trap on the table, citing Pew 2020 data that more than 70% of collection suits end in default judgments, and noting garnishment is capped at 25% with weekly income of $217.50 or less exempt [7HVra7o-cZg @ 00:06:18]. Loman is blunt about ignoring a lawsuit: never do it, because bank accounts can be levied and frozen years later, defenses are gone once judgment is entered, and there is no statute of limitations on a stipulated judgment; vacating one is a state-court fight that would need a $5,000 to $10,000 retainer [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:32:24]. Loman also reports that Arizona judgments last ten years, are renewable, and can reach four times the original amount.

Consumers who do sue hit their own walls. Meet Jermaine walks through a Southern District of Ohio case against Portfolio Recovery Associates where the plaintiff alleged inaccurate disputed-status reporting and collection without verification, and the court granted summary judgment for PRA because it followed reasonable procedures and the errors were unintentional [XSfDS_s1URg @ 00:02:59]. LawShelf identifies that same bona fide error defense, along with good-faith reliance on an FTC advisory opinion, as the main escape hatches for collectors [i6PAssklgJg @ 00:06:40]. Watts adds a structural trap that catches debtors who think a violation erases the balance: a section 1692g violation is not a defense against the debt, only a claim against the collector [qTSer3pu6io @ 01:01:14]. Loman tempers damages expectations, putting statutory damages at $100 to $1,000 and reporting that some clients have received $1, while warning that non-lump-sum settlements get filed with the court, so a single missed payment produces an immediate judgment, and roughly 30% of litigated files fail to update to Metro 2 code 64 with a zero balance afterward [n3SZjPQDSHc @ 00:25:48]. The lesson from Jermaine is operational: copy every letter and keep documentation, because many collectors do not fix errors even after being called out.

On the other side of the table, the economics run on volume, and volume breaks. Brian Parker's deposition of collection attorney Bradley Johnson extracted the admission "we do about 50 a week so 10 a day," with the complaint built from a form, the summons auto-generated, and address scrubbing outsourced to LexisNexis with no state-ID search [RwXTJhX-rxo @ 00:21:52]. The same deposition exposed a December 2022 affidavit that called Security Credit Services "plaintiff" before any suit existed, never attached its Exhibit A, and carried no penalty-of-perjury clause, prompting Parker's line that "an affidavit is not an affidavit unless it's signed under penalty of perjury"; Colin Ward still paid $2,500 in settlement [RwXTJhX-rxo @ 01:53:36]. Swift's silver case shows stubbornness turning into losses: Discover lost one suit, then sued again seven years later through a new collector using the same lawyers, and the consumer counterclaimed for over $50,000 [owpVxkqEbEk @ 00:08:31]. Watts lays out why buyers keep pushing: Crown "buys a thousand dollar debt for fifty bucks" and a consumer may settle for $700, yet the court held that hiring a third party to collect does not let a principal-purpose buyer escape the FDCPA [fPw38bPWFtw @ 00:03:48].
