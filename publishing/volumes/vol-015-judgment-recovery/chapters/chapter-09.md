# Chapter 09: Bankruptcy, Creditor Claims, and Claims Trading

*Bankruptcy basics and Chapter 11 restructuring, the claims process and bar dates, optimizing creditor recoveries, debtor mistakes, judgments under New York bankruptcy, buying distressed assets, and bankruptcy claims trading economics and risks*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Claims-trading practitioners agree the game is played on general unsecured, vendor and customer claims, bought from sellers who need cash now rather than a slow, uncertain distribution later. Buyers range from $4B funds to high-net-worth individuals and crypto traders, chasing either a distribution above the price paid or control of votes and plans. Street numbers anchor the trade: Joe Sarachek's firm bought FTX claims at 9 cents in a case that traded at 10 cents and now sits at 150%; a classic trade claim is bought at 50 hoping for 70; a seller weighs $5K today against a $20K claim paid over seven years. The bar date (about 70 days, 180 for government) kills late claims outright. Panelists split on who holds the information edge, whether 99% of sellers keep claim risk, and whether infirmities travel with the claim or are severed by a sale.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The claims-trading panels converge on one picture of the market. David Karp of Schulte Roth & Zabel puts the action in general unsecured, vendor and customer claims, and says the buyer pool now runs from $4B funds down to high-net-worth individuals and crypto traders, a market that grew out of Lehman, Madoff and MF Global [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:09:02]. Matt Christensen lists three reasons buyers show up: return, voting leverage and competitor information [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:06:50]. The panel adds that once a case draws attention, as FTX did, the trading population grows and further interest follows. The National Law Review risk segment compresses buyer intent into two verticals: buy for a distribution above the price paid, or buy for control of plans, class votes or a credit bid in a loan-to-own deal [aQwpKSW3PEA @ 00:00:00]. On the advanced panel, Joe Sarachek frames the same split as active versus passive, while the panel contrasts economic buyers, who pay 60% where a 50% recovery is expected, with strategic buyers [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:02:58]. On the sell side, the motive is consistent: cash flow, such as taking $50K now on a $100K claim. That trade only works because the distribution is slow and uncertain.

Every speaker who walks the waterfall lays it out in the same order, and that order is what a claim buyer is pricing. Capital Decode's restructuring breakdown runs super-priority and secured debt first, then administrative claims, priority unsecured, general unsecured and finally equity, with the fulcrum security being the class where value runs out and converts into the new equity [yhlRryGjPSU @ 00:06:54]. Capital Decode also notes only impaired classes vote, and impaired simply means not getting 100% of the money back. Thompson Coburn's Bankruptcy 101 presenters give the identical stack: secured, administrative, priority claims such as taxes, wages and support, then general unsecured, then equity [LBVpN-q6M-A @ 00:39:20]. The Five Minute Legal Master adds the contest layer: assets get converted to cash and paid through a statutory waterfall, and anyone can challenge who shares and in what amount [gLlOpG3V6GY @ 00:00:15]. Khan Academy's restructuring walkthrough shows why that matters for unsecured paper: in its worked plan, $4M of junior debt receives $3M of new stock, unsecured holders are impaired, and old equity goes to zero [mxVWyzzMOXM @ 00:09:12]. The consensus takeaway is blunt: a general unsecured claim sits near the bottom, survives only if it is allowed, and gets paid only after every senior and administrative layer is satisfied.

The third point of agreement is procedural and unforgiving: no timely claim, no money. David at Fairmax Law defines the bar date as the deadline to file a proof of claim, gives non-government creditors roughly 70 days and government entities up to 180, and says late claims get rejected even when the debt is valid [2eXm6HnwE1g @ 00:00:49]. FinanceKid's corporate bankruptcy breakdown states the same rule from the Chapter 11 side: missing the bar date forfeits the claim [9CYRQ14FrFw @ 00:12:05]. Chuck Lanquist and Dipy Patel at G2 Risk Solutions put a dollar figure on the cost of ignoring it. Unfiled claims get redistributed to the creditors who did file, and one creditor filed only 3% of its eligible claims, leaving more than $2M on the table [1PpzRRYapuA @ 00:14:58]. Lanquist and Patel peg the proof-of-claim deadline at 70 days, matching the Fairmax figure. Filing is only half of it. The Five Minute Legal Master warns that the trustee checks every claim for timeliness, documentation and fit, can file an objection, and that a claimant who ignores the objection loses by default [gLlOpG3V6GY @ 00:02:00]. For a buyer, that means a purchased claim is worth nothing until it is filed on time, documented and defended.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Sarachek's entry workflow starts with monitoring the services that track 3001E transfer notices, but he warns that in a case like Saks, with more than 2,500 vendors, the unsecured claims were worthless [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:36:12]. When the paper is live, the numbers move fast and the field gets crowded. His firm bought FTX claims at 9 cents on the dollar at the outset, in a case that traded around 10 cents and now sits at 150%, and Karp reports the FTX auction drew 40 interested parties at roughly 15 cents [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:11:14]. Separately, Karp counts 45 funds inside the FTX data room, while Rutenberg says some buyers embargo the Rule 3001(e) filing and simply accept the counterparty risk that comes with it [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:26:53]. The anonymity kit also includes participation agreements, SPVs and a law-firm dropbox that receives the checks, and Sarachek's bidding tactic is to copy Silver Point and beat them by a point [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:14:34]. The advanced panel also notes the deal documents themselves are now far more standard and shorter than ever before. The operating pattern across both panels is consistent: watch the transfer notices, size the case before anything else, and know exactly who else is bidding before naming a price.

Once a price is set, the contract carries the risk. Karp lists the standard kit in a claims transfer agreement: representations, indemnities, put rights priced at the purchase rate plus interest, and holdbacks, and the panel says to expect mutual big-boy waivers in just about every claims transfer agreement [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:27:51]. The National Law Review risk segment spells out what those tools are guarding against. A claim can be designated and disallowed for voting, equitably subordinated to the bottom of the waterfall for the holder's conduct, or blocked entirely by an unpaid preference, since preferences are pre-petition transfers a debtor can claw back under section 547 and an unpaid one bars the claim from sharing in recoveries [aQwpKSW3PEA @ 00:02:11]. The panel frames every purchase as carrying two risks, the claim itself and the case, using a $50K suitcase example and noting that Lehman allowed $800K on a $1M claim [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:29:07]. The panel also tags that risk as comparable to the risk people take buying the bonds. Rutenberg's diligence warning is concrete: a client bought the wrong claim, a guarantee claim versus one with no guarantee [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:40:05]. The buyer needs a contractual remedy for each of these, because indemnities only shift the problem into counterparty risk on the seller.

The unit economics start with the cost of filing itself. Lanquist and Patel put the cost of a proof of claim in the hundreds of dollars against a recovery of about 25%, so a $1,000 claim yields $250 spread over five years, and 50% of Chapter 13 cases dismiss [1PpzRRYapuA @ 00:14:17]. They lay out three filing models: in-house keeps 100% of the recovery at the highest upfront cost, a contingent vendor takes a cut, and a claim sale hands it to buyers who may return dismissed accounts, with some checks as small as 25 cents [1PpzRRYapuA @ 00:11:18]. G2's counterpoint is automation: auto-generating forms saves about 80% of the time across roughly 40 fields per form. Christensen gives the seller's side of the same calculation: $5K now versus a $20K claim paid over seven years [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:44:36]. At the far end of the payoff curve, Plain Finance dramatizes a $2,400 purchase of a $2.8M allowed claim showing zero recovery; a hidden $7.6M capacity-reservation fee drove a $5M settlement, and after $1.4M in costs the remaining $3.6M paid 50 cents on the dollar, delivering $1.4M to the claim and $1,379,200 net of the buyer's own costs [i54UmAtM9Y0 @ 00:15:03]. The plain-vanilla baseline remains the trade-claim spread: buy at 50, hope for 70.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The claims-trading bench splits first on who actually holds the information edge. Stephen Rutenberg of Polsinelli says sellers often know more than the buyer, pointing to the Delta claim pool, and answers the buyer-advantage framing with a flat “My general experience is the opposite,” while David Karp argues buyers hold the structural strength, with a PG&E tree vendor as the example [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:13:15]. The same two split on who eats the downside after closing. Rutenberg puts it at “99%” of sellers keeping the risk, with only an SPV seller walking away clean; Karp says the real number depends on the document and the negotiation [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:35:26]. The infirmity fight runs deeper. Under the KB Toys view, a defect attaches to the paper, so “whoever holds the claim is subject to that infirmity”; under the Enron view, a sale severs it but an assignment does not, which The National Law Review's risk speaker calls a “very fine, nuanced distinction” [aQwpKSW3PEA @ 00:03:10]. Matt Christensen of Johnson May refuses to play the label game at all. In Christensen's reading, the sale-versus-assignment caption on the agreement does not matter, because “the claim risk resides with the claim” no matter which form of transfer document the parties choose to sign [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:38:06].

Buyer playbooks diverge just as sharply. On the Advanced Bankruptcy Transactions panel, buyers split into economic players, who will pay 60% where they expect a 50% recovery, and strategic players chasing leverage, a divide Joe Sarachek reframes as active versus passive money [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:02:58]. Sarachek's own entry tactic is blunt: stay anonymous behind participation agreements, SPVs and a law-firm “dropbox” for checks, then copy Silver Point and “beat them by a point” [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:14:34]. Rutenberg describes a different posture, where some buyers embargo the Rule 3001(e) transfer filing and knowingly carry counterparty risk instead. On the asset side, the Purchasing Assets in Bankruptcy panel cannot agree on what a stalking horse should get paid. Richard Corby puts the breakup fee at 3 to 5%, “generally 3%,” plus expense reimbursement, and views the protection as a good thing because “your bid can't be shopped behind your back” [eA9c0DMpp60 @ 00:36:26]. James Sullivan quotes a lower 1 to 4% band, paired with a minimum overbid and 10 to 20% deposits, and the panel agrees a 10% fee likely won't be approved [eA9c0DMpp60 @ 00:46:53]. The same panel flags the counter-risk: a stalking horse may just be doing competitors' homework, and there is no diligence out once signed.

The restructuring camp splits over what old equity is worth once a company files. Khan Academy's playbook is to buy the bonds of a reorganizing company, because “the debt holders become the new shareholders of the company” and the business is “usually” undervalued; in the upside case, a company later worth $10M carries $2M of debt and $8M of equity, and the new holders “make out like bandits” [mxVWyzzMOXM @ 00:10:09]. The same lesson then runs an equity counter-plan at an $8M valuation that hands old shareholders $2M of new stock. FinanceKid takes the harder line that equity “lose[s] the interest” immediately upon filing, and points to Icahn buying bonds at 5 to 10 cents and ending up with about 30% of the equity [9CYRQ14FrFw @ 00:09:08]. The venue question splits too. Capital Decode's Novatech case lays out the trade: out-of-court is faster, cheaper and quieter, while court delivers cramdown, lease rejection and emergency financing [yhlRryGjPSU @ 00:03:29]. FinanceKid calls prepacks “much better than workouts,” citing research that firms restructuring outside Chapter 11 “were less able to reduce their leverage,” and adds the tax sting: debt forgiveness is taxable income in a workout but not in bankruptcy, and NOLs are lost if original equity drops below 50% [9CYRQ14FrFw @ 00:29:19].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The losses on the claims desk are concrete. Sarachek's panel names Sears administrative claims bought at 90 cents as a loser, and the post-mortem is one line: “Sears was administratively insolvent,” so even administrative-priority paper landed on the losers list [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:43:42]. Toys 'R' Us sits on the same list. In Saks, with more than 2,500 vendors, Sarachek says the unsecured claims were worthless, and widens the warning: “in most cases today, the claims the unsecured claims are worthless” [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:36:57]. Diligence is the second leak. Rutenberg recounts a client who bought the wrong claim entirely, paper without a guarantee instead of the claim backed by one, which is why buyer diligence sits at the center of the trade [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:40:05]. Judge Carey's Woodbridge ruling shows the underlying documents can kill the position outright: an anti-assignment clause can void the claim the buyer thought they owned [dGbPfDmUGhk @ 00:33:07]. Litigation is the last trap. Oaktree versus BJ's turned on a $29M claim bought at $20M, roughly 70 cents on the dollar, and Enron/Bartman, a 2025 BAP ruling, plus Bear Stearns/Hitachi round out the docket of claim trades that ended up fought in court rather than collected.

Several defects that sink a claim trace to the holder's own conduct. The National Law Review's risk speaker lists them: vote designation, which means “you could have your claim disallowed for voting purposes”; equitable subordination, where “the penalty for that bad conduct is the claim drops to the bottom of the chain” [aQwpKSW3PEA @ 00:01:33]; and preferences, pre-petition transfers the debtor can claw back under section 547, where an unpaid preference bars the claim from sharing in recoveries, so the buyer needs a contractual remedy [aQwpKSW3PEA @ 00:02:11]. Indemnities meant to cover those gaps simply create counterparty risk. Rutenberg separates claim risk from case risk, illustrating it with a $50K suitcase example, and notes that Lehman allowed $800K on a $1M claim, a 20% gap between filed and allowed [lSw0SJbVp-A @ 00:29:07]. Plain Finance's $2,400 case study shows the timing trap: the holder owned “a claim, not the contract,” refused a $160K offer worth more than 66 times the money, sank $20,800 into costs including a $1,600 lost assignment, and faced a scenario where a $900K gross offer would have left nothing after costs. The closing verdict is that refusing $160K “wasn't smart just because it worked out” [i54UmAtM9Y0 @ 00:15:22].

Creditors filing their own claims bleed value at the procedural level. Fairmax Law's David puts the bar date at roughly 70 days for non-government creditors and up to 180 days for government entities, and warns that late claims are rejected even when the debt is valid; an unsecured creditor without a timely claim simply is not paid [2eXm6HnwE1g @ 00:00:49]. G2 Risk Solutions puts numbers on the leak: one creditor filed only 3% of eligible claims and missed more than $2M, unfiled claims get redistributed to the creditors who did file, each claim costs “hundreds of dollars,” recovery runs about 25% so $1,000 yields $250 over five years, and half of Chapter 13 cases dismiss [1PpzRRYapuA @ 00:14:58]. Their rule: don't file below expected recovery. The Five Minute Legal Master Series adds the default trap: when the trustee objects, the court sets a response time and hearing, and ignoring it loses the claim, though far-away or small claims may not be worth defending depending on the distribution percentage [gLlOpG3V6GY @ 00:03:04]. Thompson Coburn's Brian and Cheryl close the loop for collectors: once notice of a filing lands, “flip the switch off” on collection, because stay violations are void and willful ones draw punitive damages [LBVpN-q6M-A @ 00:26:28].
