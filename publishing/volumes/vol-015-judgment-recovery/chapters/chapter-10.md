## Executive Summary

Commercial recovery operators converge on one ladder: soft collection, a letter before action, a court claim, then enforcement agents, with mediation or litigation as the fork. Shergroup runs no-collect-no-fee collection at 15% plus VAT, The Business Disputes Register puts US agency fees at 20-25% with success often above 50 percent, and Mike Martinez says a $1 million file yields $170,000 to $350,000 in the first few months. Speed dominates: debtors stall with resend requests and partner excuses, 90% simply refuse, and Martinez wants accounts placed after six months. A judgment collects nothing alone; The Tribe stresses the sheriff will not hunt assets, so operators trace tuition payments, depose debtors, and run skip-trace data waterfalls retested quarterly. Disagreement is sharp: Zindo calls agencies harassment that damages a brand, while Taurus favours mediation over the irreversible lawsuit.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who walked through a commercial file describes the same staircase, and nobody skips the first step. Thomas Higgins Limited opens with a letter before action giving the debtor 7 days to pay, carrying the name, address and amount owed, and recommends a solicitor send it; the firm flags that this letter is not a court order, so its force comes entirely from what it threatens next [MxMjZqkhltA @ 00:01:02]. Shergroup TV runs 30 days of emails, letters and phone calls under its collection tier before issuing a free letter before action, then hands the survivors to its SRA-regulated legal arm and finally its enforcement arm [aPUKKsyL_NM @ 00:02:33]. Taurus Collections frames the threat of litigation as the lever that drags evasive debtors to the mediation table, while calling the lawsuit itself the nuclear option [PQz21Mf4OZc @ 00:02:09]. The Business Disputes Register lays out the US version of the same ladder as four plays: mediation for quality disputes, a contingency agency, small claims, and going public with a $20 dispute filing that stays up until the invoice settles [bgQht-y6jWY @ 00:01:53]. The consensus is sequencing: cheap pressure first, court second, enforcement last, with each rung making the next threat credible to a debtor who has already ignored the polite reminders.

Practitioners agree the late-paying business debtor is rarely confused; it is stalling, and time works against the creditor. Fair Capital's skit runs the standard script: resend the invoice that already went out three times, at 60 days 'speak with my partner,' at 90 days 'we'll see,' and a business owner admits having to spend another year chasing those balances alone [svZlO-c19tA @ 00:01:33]. Mike Martinez, who built a 160-person agency, puts numbers on it: roughly 90% of the people on the files are refusing to pay and not cooperating, and only about 10% did not know they owed anything [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:09:25]. Martinez tells businesses to assign delinquent receivables to an agency after six months of delinquency rather than letting them rot in-house [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:10:27]. Retrievables Collection Attorneys pushes the front end earlier still, with explicit contracts carrying payment deadlines and late-payment consequences, follow-ups as the due date nears, and collections treated as customer service that balances firmness with empathy [YT8Fu3qHWM0 @ 00:00:17]. The shared baseline is written terms before the sale, a collection clock that starts on the first day of lateness, and zero patience for repeated excuses that only buy the debtor more time to move money elsewhere.

The hardest consensus point is that winning a judgment is the middle of the job, not the end of it. The Tribe's secured-transactions lecture is blunt that a judgment is not a lien, and lays out the unsecured creditor's five steps: judgment, discover assets, paperwork, levy, sheriff sale [r1sk4alg3RM @ 00:28:03]. The same lecture warns that the sheriff will not search for assets on the creditor's behalf, which is exactly why debtor depositions built around a dozen pointed questions matter [r1sk4alg3RM @ 00:30:03]. Richard Evans of Evanns Collection Law Firm, citing '20 30 $40 million' in judgment debt collected or satisfied over 10-plus years, treats every file as a mystery: the firm knows which private school a debtor's kids attend and their names, and traces how the tuition is paid to find collectible money, including against debtors hiding behind attorneys, corporations and LLCs [XOwPGudMeaY @ 00:01:32]. Shergroup TV closes the loop with physical leverage, describing enforcement agents removing goods and hauling a debtor's car away on a low loader [aPUKKsyL_NM @ 00:08:54]. Across UK and US speakers the rule holds: paper wins nothing until someone locates an asset and a sheriff, bailiff or enforcement officer actually seizes it.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The pricing grid is where the operators get specific. Shergroup TV prices its collection tier as no collect, no fee at 15% plus VAT with no take-on fee, and tells clients to rewrite their terms of business so those collection costs become recoverable from the customer instead of eaten by the creditor [aPUKKsyL_NM @ 00:01:38]. On enforcement, Shergroup routes judgments over £600 to the High Court with a fixed fee that was £161 at recording and is mostly recovered from the debtor, while sub-£600 work stays no collect, no fee [aPUKKsyL_NM @ 00:07:37]. In the US, The Business Disputes Register quotes agencies at 20-25% of the amount collected for debts over $2,000, with no enforcement power but success often above 50 percent; small claims limits run $2,000 to $15,000 depending on the state, and larger claims mean an attorney costing thousands of dollars plus separate enforcement costs on top [bgQht-y6jWY @ 00:00:38]. Taurus Collections prices the fork directly: mediation fees split 50/50 at $1,500 to $5,000 per day for a total in the low thousands, against litigation retainers of $10k, $20k or $50k and a discovery phase billed at $400 to $600 an hour that runs into tens of thousands before expert witnesses are even hired [PQz21Mf4OZc @ 00:03:10].

The UK court track is mapped step by step, and each step carries its own hard deadline. Thomas Higgins Limited files a county court claim form once the letter before action expires, giving the debtor 14 days to respond, and decides whether to accept an offered payment plan depending on the relationship with that customer [MxMjZqkhltA @ 00:02:02]. Ignore the claim and a county court judgment lands on the public register of judgments, orders and fines for 6 years, removable only if paid within one month, which hands the creditor a sharp reputational deadline to wave at the debtor [MxMjZqkhltA @ 00:02:19]. Enforcement splits by size: Thomas Higgins instructs the county court bailiff for debts under £600 and the High Court enforcement officer for anything larger, holding a winding-up order as the last resort against company debtors [MxMjZqkhltA @ 00:02:58]. Shergroup TV names the instrument as a writ of control, the document known as fieri facias until 2004, and runs it through a dedicated enforcement tier sitting behind its collection and legal tiers [aPUKKsyL_NM @ 00:06:24]. The operator takeaway is a calendar rather than a feeling: 7 days on the letter, 14 days on the claim, one month to clear the judgment, then bailiffs or enforcement officers at the door.

Inside the agency, the mechanics are people, scripts and data. Mike Martinez hires off a scripted Indeed ad through a three-stage interview, starts classes of at least five with a one-hour orientation, trains on the CRM, the script, rebuttals and call recordings, and has new hires on the phones by the end of day one with managers coaching; anyone who cannot figure it out in the first few days is not right for the role [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:19:02]. Martinez treats speed as king, arguing that the operation that moves faster simply makes more money [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:16:59]. Will Turner of TEC Services builds the skip-trace side as a data waterfall: exclusionary scrubs for deceased, bankruptcy and litigious debtors first, then recovery scores, then skip tracing, drawing on about 120 data sources connected to the platform [1M9DIxuxJGA @ 00:06:21]. Turner insists on keeping part of the inventory permanently in A/B testing and retesting no less than quarterly, because five vendors run in a different order produce very different outcomes and contact data decays fast enough that no strategy stays optimal for long [1M9DIxuxJGA @ 00:19:59]. The street version: hire fast, put collectors on live calls immediately, and never trust last quarter's data order.

1. Lock the Terms Before the Sale
   - Write explicit payment deadlines, late-payment consequences, and recoverable collection costs into customer contracts.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over whether a third-party agency earns its cut. The Business Disputes Register puts agency fees at 20 to 25 percent of the amount collected on debts over $2,000, concedes agencies hold no enforcement power, and still reports success "often above 50 percent" [bgQht-y6jWY @ 00:00:38]. Zindo takes the opposite line: what agencies "can do is harass," they "don't work," and they ruin the creditor's name, so it pitches its own model of getting paid in 30 days with no upfront cost [Xpf-6IU1qtQ @ 00:00:35]. Mike Martinez, whose agency is licensed in every state, sets his own benchmark: assign him a million-dollar file and he expects to recover $170,000 to $350,000 in the first few months, and he tells businesses to place delinquent receivables with an agency after six months of delinquency [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:10:08]. Shergroup competes on price instead, running a no collect, no fee tier at 15 percent plus VAT with no take-on fee, working the account through 30 days of emails, letters and calls before a free letter before action [aPUKKsyL_NM @ 00:01:38]. The real decision is when to hand off and at what percentage, weighed against how many months an operator is willing to keep chasing the same invoice alone.

On escalation, the mediation camp and the court camp read the same debtor differently. Taurus Collections argues the threat of litigation is what draws evasive debtors to the table, prices mediation at $1,500 to $5,000 per day with the fee split 50/50, and sets that against litigation retainers of $10k, $20k or $50k, 12 to 18 months to judgment and roughly another year if there is an appeal [PQz21Mf4OZc @ 00:02:54]. Its core claim is that it sees "a much higher rate of people voluntarily complying with a mediated settlement than with a court order that was forced on them" [PQz21Mf4OZc @ 00:07:16]. The Business Disputes Register reserves mediation for quality disputes, notes some courts offer it free while otherwise it runs "well over a thousand dollars," and points creditors toward small claims, where limits run $2,000 to $15,000 by state and the creditor self-represents [bgQht-y6jWY @ 00:00:20]. Thomas Higgins Limited lays out a straight UK court ladder instead: a seven-day letter before action, a county court claim with 14 days to respond, a CCJ, then a bailiff's warrant of control, with a winding-up order kept as the last resort [MxMjZqkhltA @ 00:02:58]. Mediation buys confidentiality and payment plans; court buys a public, binary result.

Practitioners split hard on what a UCC filing is for. The Tribe treats collateral as the line between two collection worlds: an unsecured creditor grinds through judgment, asset discovery, paperwork, levy and sheriff sale, while a secured party can use self-help repossession and sale under 9-609 and 9-610, and exemptions do not defeat the security interest [r1sk4alg3RM @ 00:42:03]. Credit Commerce & Currency frames the UCC-1 from the same lender side: filed with the Secretary of State in the borrower's state, public record, valid five years though that varies by state, continuable, and able to choke new funding because lenders are reluctant to take second position [TEEr5IdEtHY @ 00:01:18]. R.E.A.D.Y. Nation runs a different playbook, calling the UCC-1 "not about a loan," splitting the all-caps debtor "strawman" from the living secured party and trustee, and building filings around "two planes of law" [CPZ9XWAJAIY @ 00:05:17]. Even so, R.E.A.D.Y. Nation keeps the core mechanics conventional: the security agreement creates the interest, the UCC-1 perfects it, both are needed, and "first in time, first in right" sets priority [CPZ9XWAJAIY @ 00:07:07]. For a commercial creditor trying to move collateral, The Tribe and Credit Commerce treat the filing strictly as lender security for an obligation, not as a filing against one's own name.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start in the operator's own chair. Mike Martinez admits he began as "a pushover," taking offers like $50 a month [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:05:07]. Later, earning $350k to $500k a year, he coined "baller syndrome" for taking the foot off the gas once the money arrives. He also hit a wall in 2019, when a $1M Navy Federal lawsuit landed while he had $40k in the bank and payroll for 25 employees; he dates his absentee ownership to around 2015 or 2016 and now says "I always need to be the CEO" [3AJVt6F_pYQ @ 00:12:12]. On the creditor side, Fair Capital's skit maps the stall sequence self-collectors hear: "send it again" after three sends, "speak with my partner" at 60 days past due, "we'll see" at 90, and a business owner who says "I had to spend another year chasing those" [svZlO-c19tA @ 00:01:33]. Zindo lists the fixes that fail: untrained AR hires, agencies it says only harass, and attorneys it calls slow and costly, while some industries write off close to 30 percent, and it tells owners to "ask your CFO" what was written off [Xpf-6IU1qtQ @ 00:00:19]. Every month spent soft-pedaling or self-chasing an account compounds the loss.

Court wins carry their own traps. Taurus Collections calls the discovery phase "the real cost driver," billed at $400 to $600 an hour and climbing into the tens of thousands before expert witnesses are added, and warns that suing first is irreversible [PQz21Mf4OZc @ 00:08:12]. The Business Disputes Register adds that claims above small-claims limits need an attorney at thousands of dollars, and that enforcement costs extra on top of the win [bgQht-y6jWY @ 00:01:15]. The Tribe hammers the gap between judgment and cash: a judgment is not a lien, the sheriff will not search for assets, and an unsecured creditor who simply takes the debtor's property commits both the tort and the crime of conversion [r1sk4alg3RM @ 00:09:38]. Collection then depends on the creditor's own asset discovery, including 12 deposition questions down to "how much money do you have on you today," and priority can turn on being first to levy or on relation-back to writ delivery, depending on the state. The Tribe's sheriff-sale example has the creditor credit bidding a $1,000 judgment against a $2,000 TV. In bankruptcy the automatic stay freezes collection, and a creditor is secured only up to collateral value, so a claim splits into $30k secured and $20k general [r1sk4alg3RM @ 00:51:14].

Back-office data rots on a clock. Will Turner of TEC Services puts consumer data at 60 to 70 percent incorrect after two years, and names three repeat mistakes: overconfidence with a static mindset, single-threading on one provider, and trusting creditor-provided data, as in agencies that say "we don't skip trace" [1M9DIxuxJGA @ 00:23:31]. Automation breaks too: Martin Kravchenko's Octoparse build cannot take dynamic text input like today's date, throws an error if the search is entered directly unless the page is scrolled to the bottom after load, and gets harder against CAPTCHAs [aB0whCtGI5k @ 00:12:10]. Saul Ewing's Ryan Gallagher reports an Equifax coding glitch that miscalculated about 12 percent of scores between March and April 2022, some by 20 to 130 points, now heading to a $100M class settlement, and warns that "accuracy failures at this type of scale now carry nine figure exposure" [MM_dCPRf9LE @ 00:20:55]. Peter Nanov's cases punish slow handling and loose fees: in Sixty v. IQ Data a single unwanted letter, sent after a two-week delay processing a representation letter, was enough for standing, and in Glover pay-to-pay fees of $7.50 to $12 were barred unless the contract or state law permits them; his lesson is "when you receive a representation letter, process it" [MM_dCPRf9LE @ 00:32:32].
