*PI investigation strategies, locating bank accounts and offshore assets (Joe Dickerson, OffshoreAlert), skip tracing at scale and with data, free skip tracing, process-server asset secrets, and searching PACER, CourtListener, Bloomberg, and federal and bankruptcy dockets*

## Executive Summary

Judgment-recovery operators converge on one rule: build the asset map from records before the debtor knows you are coming. Joe Dickerson pegs uncollected civil judgments at 80 percent and attacks them with 24-to-36-month bank subpoenas, UCC lender trails, mortgage-escrow insurance schedules and coordinated multi-county subpoena days. Tim Santoni layers trash pulls and 4-7 days of surveillance on top of baseline property and lien searches. Data operators at Arbeit U and Receivables Info warn that skip tracing is an agency's second-largest expense, so vendors must be waterfall-ranked, re-skipped and cut on portfolio-level KPIs. On the court-record side, PACER runs roughly 10 cents a page with fees waived under $30 a quarter, while CourtListener's RECAP archive is free. Disagreements center on PACER's free threshold, database-only skipping versus field work, and free one-at-a-time lookups versus paid bulk data.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every serious recovery operator starts from the paper trail, not the debtor's mouth. Tim Santoni's first move is a basic asset and background investigation: find every county where the debtor owns property and record the abstract of judgment there, then check employment, competing creditors, bankruptcies, judgment liens and default notices, which he calls the basis and foundation of the whole enforcement file [rDAzEvzlcbo @ 00:00:20]. Joe Dickerson, with more than five decades in the field, turns that sequencing into a hard rule. He argues that firing off early depositions, interrogatories and subpoenas duces tecum simply tips the debtor off, and he labels that habit something that should be malpractice; his method is to answer roughly 80 percent of the questions from records first, then ask only the remaining 20 percent under oath so any false answer becomes perjury on the record [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:11:03]. In his OffshoreAlert session he frames the same workflow as two passes: preliminary research on the probability that assets exist, followed by documenting those assets through public records, with corporations, LPs, LLCs and trusts treated as the four parking places where money gets stashed [2ApchMyxmWg @ 00:02:34]. The consensus is blunt: surprise is the asset, and records protect it.

The second point of agreement is that money cannot move without leaving tracks. Dickerson puts it in one line, money always leaves a trail and real estate leaves a trail, and he pairs it with a warning that any transfers after his subpoenas land are fraudulent transfers that can be unwound [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:17:18]. His operating rule is never to garnish an account without first subpoenaing at least 24 to 36 months of credits and debits, including wire transfers, because the intelligence buried in those statements, pointing to brokers, real estate and royalty payors, is worth more than whatever balance happens to sit in the account on levy day [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:13:28]. Anthony DeMario of Skip Tracers lands in the same place from the field side: while some people go to great lengths to hide wealth, there is always a trail, and the assets are often hiding in plain sight across offshore accounts, brokerage accounts, current employment and unreported income [UqntxJnXdto @ 00:06:28]. He also attacks the lazy version of the job, noting that many skip tracing shops rely solely on online databases even though databases only tell part of the story, which is why his team layers in social media, employment records, utility bills and surveillance [UqntxJnXdto @ 00:04:46].

Court records are the third pillar, and the federal side is where operators keep finding things others miss. County Portal opens with the baseline fact that bankruptcy is a federal matter, so the filings live in federal court rather than the county courthouse, and the docket sheet carries the petition, creditor list, motions and discharge [IDWN8CuNtfA @ 00:00:18]. Larry K. of ShadowAnyone calls PACER OSINT gold because federal criminal, civil and bankruptcy records exist nowhere else, and he uses them as a pivot: an out-of-area federal charge points straight to local jail, arrest and county court records where the target actually lives [esyMX8o3OR0 @ 00:01:23]. Wilhelmina Randtke adds the discipline layer, telling researchers that before doing anything in a case they should always check the docket to make sure nothing strange happened since the last look, because order dates start appeal clocks and new filings open 15- or 21-day response windows [nxcVuXjUaaA @ 00:05:14]. On the skip-data side, Joey Wilkins of the Receivables Info panel rejects the one-and-done skip entirely, continually re-skipping pre-legal and pre-served inventory, and both he and Stephanie Clark put TransUnion at the top of their vendor lists [PSWN-POX4Uc @ 00:06:30].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Dickerson's lender play is the clearest unit-economics lesson anyone teaches. Pull UCC filings to identify the secured lender, then do the math everybody else skips: equipment bought for $150,000 with $50,000 down and $25,000 already paid leaves $75,000 owed against a current value of $125,000, which nets $50,000 in equity that, in his words, everybody else overlooked [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:02:46]. The lender is also a map to the money, because subpoenaing it for the debtor's payment checks reveals which bank those payments clear from [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:04:36]. His mortgage-escrow chain runs the same logic further: escrowed insurance payments identify the lender, the lender file yields the insurer name and policy number, and the policy schedules list jewelry, art, furs, antiques and rugs, untitled property that appears in no public record [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:12:36]. He reports that more than a dozen cases over two years settled at 100 percent without ever executing the writ once those schedules surfaced [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:15:28]. Elsewhere he pushes operators to take the asset itself, oil and gas royalty holdings or rental property via turnover order, rather than chasing the monthly checks those assets throw off, and to pull loan applications carrying tax returns and financial statements.

Timing and wire mechanics separate pros from amateurs. Dickerson garnishes around the 17th to 18th or the 3rd to 5th of the month rather than on random dates [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:25:24]. When repeated garnishments came back at zero, a treasury-management subpoena exposed a nightly sweep to an offshore account, and the fix was garnishing the holding company's any-and-all-banks rather than the operating account alone [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:26:19]. For international money, he says about 95 percent of professionals miss intermediary bank discovery: roughly five or six New York City banks clear all wires, so subpoena every name variant, expect false positives, and trace the hits to the foreign banks where international freezing orders can be sought [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:29:01]. Federal courts have held those records belong to the intermediary, and he calls the process costly but chilling. Execution is staged like a military operation. His D-Day serves 20 subpoenas through 20 process servers across 15 counties in 5 states within one to two days, with any asset moves after that treated as fraudulent transfers that can be unwound [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:16:57]. Raids are pre-planned with the sheriff or US Marshal at a set rendezvous, plus a photographer, inventory sheet, locksmith, moving van and approved storage crew.

High-volume skip tracing runs on vendor discipline. Chris Ball on Arbeit U warns that vendors match on SSN, first and last name and address combinations and often run matching loose so they can sell a hit, so operators must measure match quality and keep tweaking it rather than setting it and forgetting it [d5DXPK2ggSk @ 00:03:12]. Because attribution is hard, he measures at the portfolio level, comparing collection and contact rates on skip-hit accounts against the rest of the book, layering in time-value measurement, and cutting vendors that underperform [d5DXPK2ggSk @ 00:07:22]. Wilkins builds the same idea into a waterfall, ranking vendors by actual service results and prioritizing returned addresses by length of residence, so a vendor that keeps producing servable addresses earns first call on every new file [PSWN-POX4Uc @ 00:30:31]. For court records, Tommy from DocketDrafter steers users to CourtListener's free RECAP archive instead of paying PACER, noting that anything purchased through the RECAP browser extension becomes free for everyone afterward, and that attorney names inside docket entries are useful leads [ARBpLFKK8iA @ 00:04:26]. The working stack is cheap federal docket intelligence feeding a ranked, measured data pipeline that gets pruned every cycle.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Practitioners do not agree on what federal docket access actually costs, and that split decides how aggressively an operator pulls documents. Tommy from DocketDrafter prices PACER at about 10 cents a page and says, as his own belief, that users get $15 of free downloads per quarter [ARBpLFKK8iA @ 00:03:59]. ShadowAnyone's Larry K. quotes 10 to 20 cents a page and says the free account is billed quarterly only if it reaches $30, after which the balance resets [esyMX8o3OR0 @ 00:02:53]. County Portal states the same $30-per-quarter waiver for bankruptcy pulls [IDWN8CuNtfA @ 00:00:51], while Badwolf Unincorporated quotes "10 cents or 3 cents" a page, roughly $3 per 30 pages. The playbooks also split on workarounds. Tommy pushes the CourtListener RECAP archive because any document bought through the RECAP extension becomes free for everyone, so the cheapest pull is the one somebody else already paid for [ARBpLFKK8iA @ 00:04:26]. County Portal goes the other way on middlemen: third-party record sites simply run PACER and charge more, so the move is to register direct at pacer.uscourts.gov. The working rule both camps share is to exhaust the free layer first, then pay per page only for the filings that actually move the asset hunt forward.

The bigger split is whether data vendors or field work find the debtor. Chris Ball on Arbeit U treats skip tracing as a vendor-management problem: buying "public data" alone means you are only buying on price, so the edge comes from better matching or unique sources, such as one vendor that ran Walmart employment verifications [d5DXPK2ggSk @ 00:02:26]. Joey Wilkins on the Receivables Info panel says data that once sat across tax assessor sites and manual skip services is now consolidated into one or two sources, and his shop puts TransUnion at the top of its vendor waterfall [PSWN-POX4Uc @ 00:38:40]. Anthony DeMario of Skip Tracers pushes back hard: a lot of skip tracing services rely solely on online databases, but databases only tell part of the story, so his team layers social media, employment records, utility bills, and surveillance on top [UqntxJnXdto @ 00:04:46]. Tim Santoni goes furthest from the screen, pulling publicly available residential trash for mailed financial statements and running four to seven days of lifestyle surveillance to document movements, contacts, and bank visits before a debtor exam or bank levy [rDAzEvzlcbo @ 00:00:58]. Jacob Blank sits at the budget end, swapping a 10-cent paid skip for a free reverse address lookup on cyberbackgroundchecks.com.

Joe Dickerson, interviewed by Hanna Hasl-Kelchner, attacks the standard litigation reflex of opening with depositions, interrogatories, and subpoenas duces tecum. He says early discovery tips off the debtor and "should be malpractice," so operators should answer about 80 percent of their questions from records first and use the remaining 20 percent to put perjury on the record [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:11:03]. His alternative is a "D-Day": 20 subpoenas served by 20 process servers across 15 counties in 5 states inside one to two days, after which any asset move becomes a fraudulent transfer that can be unwound [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:16:57]. Tim Santoni instead carries surveillance evidence into the judgment debtor exam itself as leverage [rDAzEvzlcbo @ 00:01:10]. The trust question splits professionals too. On OffshoreAlert, Dickerson describes an attorney who wrongly told a client that a revocable trust blocked collection; his experience is that revocable trusts are an alter ego of the settlor with no asset protection, while irrevocable trusts generally carry 100 percent protection unless fraudulent conveyance can be proven and the transaction unwound [2ApchMyxmWg @ 00:03:58]. Dickerson also rejects chasing monthly checks alone, pushing turnover orders on the oil and gas royalty or rental property itself.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest bank-side failure Dickerson reports on OffshoreAlert is garnishing into an empty account. One debtor kept showing zero balances because a nightly treasury-management sweep moved the money offshore; the fix was a subpoena that exposed the sweep and a garnishment against the holding company's "any and all banks" [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:26:19]. He times garnishments around the 17th and 18th or the 3rd through 5th of the month to hit when money lands [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:25:24], and his rule with Hanna Hasl-Kelchner is to never garnish an account without first subpoenaing 24 to 36 months of credits and debits, including wire transfers [ha0J206Xt6k @ 00:13:28]. The host's own loss shows the timing trap from the other side: the sheriff had 30 days to execute, and by day 30 the business was padlocked and empty. Dickerson's counter is a pre-planned raid with the sheriff or US Marshal, a rendezvous point, a photographer, an inventory sheet, a locksmith, a moving van, and an approved storage crew. The hard tripwire is pretexting banks: he warns the GLBA penalty reaches up to $10K or 10 years and hits the investigator, the vendor, the client, and the attorney alike [j34w1ZyVNSc @ 00:23:29]. His sanctioned route is subpoenas plus an account-ping service that confirms activity without returning balances.

On the agency side, the failure is spend without follow-through. Chris Ball on Arbeit U calls skip tracing the second-largest agency expense after personnel and warns that data dumped into the system simply goes unused [d5DXPK2ggSk @ 00:04:30]. He describes an agency that bought credit triggers, signals that a debtor just applied for a car loan or mortgage and is acting right now, but had no follow-up process, so only niche managers ever worked them [d5DXPK2ggSk @ 00:05:17]. Vendors also match "loose" on SSN, name, and address combinations to sell a hit, so match quality has to be measured and tweaked constantly rather than set and forgotten. Joey Wilkins on the Receivables Info panel names wrong-party contacts as the biggest risk and refuses to pad fees with unjustified re-attempts at the same address [PSWN-POX4Uc @ 00:40:16]. Stephanie Clark of Verifax caps the bleed by setting labor and data-cost stopping points, tracking verification and response rates, and telling clients exactly what more spend would buy [PSWN-POX4Uc @ 00:27:08]. Ball's measurement fix is portfolio-level: compare collection and contact rates on skip-hit accounts against the rest of the book, add time-value measurement, and cut underperforming vendors. Jacob Blank hits the opposite bottleneck: free reverse lookups run one at a time, which stalls on bulk lists.

Court-record research carries its own dead ends. County Portal warns that bankruptcy cases filed before the late 1990s are not digitized, forcing a trip to the clerk and then NARA, which is slower [IDWN8CuNtfA @ 00:01:42]. David Holt finds state court coverage on Bloomberg inconsistent and says to contact the clerk, and Wilhelmina Randtke shows Texas county sites ranging from Medina's detailed docket to Guadalupe's nothing-online setup where the only option is calling the clerk [nxcVuXjUaaA @ 00:02:49]. Randtke's standing rule is to check the docket before doing anything in a case, because something strange may have happened since the last look. Page fees also compound: Brandon Joe Williams notes the Snipes docket runs 602 filings and downloading the whole case would cost "hundreds" [LmiTRGMtKr0 @ 00:15:55]. His own Amex case shows the attrition trap: 28 filings deep with nothing signed by the judge, a defense citing courts that "thoroughly rejected the vapor money theory," and his own admission, "I don't claim to be a pro." Tommy from DocketDrafter found that automation needs checking too; his Claude plugin attributed entry 52 to Microsoft when it was OpenMarkets' motion, and strict API rate limits forced him to save PDFs locally [ARBpLFKK8iA @ 00:09:08].
