# Chapter 03: Post-Judgment Enforcement Fundamentals

*How a judgment is entered and e-filed, judgment duration and renewal, the creditor's collection toolkit (Texas, California, England and Wales), execution-case asset tracing, self-help center guidance, and the civil-procedure and case-law research foundations behind enforcement*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the judgment is only the halfway mark: the court goes hands-off, debtors abscond and dissipate assets, and creditors race each other to collect first. The working stack is consistent across Georgia, Texas, California and Mississippi: record a lien, run post-judgment discovery under oath, then garnish wages and bank accounts or send the sheriff to levy. Street numbers include a 3-day bank freeze in Georgia, a $30 California wage-levy deposit with employers remitting 25% of net wages, a $300-per-8-hours keeper, and the federal cap at the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or earnings above 30 times minimum wage. Disagreement centers on cost and delegation: receivers take 25%, collection agencies can take a third permanently, and post-judgment fees can exceed what the win itself cost.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who addressed the question frames the judgment as a starting line, not a finish. Calhoun School of Law puts winning at 'about halfway' and notes the court goes 'hands-off' once the ruling issues [ObrPaCTQ6ZQ @ 00:00:00]. Eric Nano of the Sacramento County Public Law Library tells creditors that getting the judgment was 'possibly the easy part,' using the Better Off Dead paperboy who is owed $2 and never collects as the warning [bX8Hhszjuxs @ 00:00:45]. The consensus fix is to think about collection before filing. Timothy Anenberger on The National Law Review panel says to ask whether the defendant is judgment-proof, map assets, and use provisional remedies such as attachment up front, because debtors abscond, transfer, and dissipate assets 'all the time' [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:02:25]. Attorney Michael Ehline is blunter: do not sue without insurance or a known asset, and remember a settlement is not a judgment [jPO9_I_LBSw @ 00:00:00]. Jeffrey Leon closes the same loop from the panel, pulling Dun & Bradstreet reports so the client avoids suing a 'dry hole.' The operator baseline is therefore an asset thesis before a complaint, with insurance, bank relationships, and real property identified before the debtor has a chance to move them.

The core toolkit repeats almost word for word across jurisdictions. Will of Downs Law lists three primary Georgia methods: record a fifa with the superior court as a lien on real or personal property, garnish wages or bank funds, and run post-judgment discovery for documents and a sworn asset statement [K1KakWduA04 @ 00:00:23]. Calhoun School of Law names the same three instruments, liens, writs of execution and writs of garnishment, and adds that writs must be localized where the property sits, Chicago versus Dallas [ObrPaCTQ6ZQ @ 00:02:02]. Hendershot Cowart puts post-judgment discovery first in Texas to locate non-exempt assets, then layers writs of execution, garnishment, turnover orders and, last, a receiver [-kyDpaMUqFY @ 00:00:03]. The Sterling Firm opens with a written demand, then picks among a debtor appearance order, a writ of execution, and an abstract of judgment that creates a lien [UV-WyQ1-NC4 @ 00:01:01]. Across all four, the lien is the passive anchor and garnishment is the active cash tool. Will is explicit that a lien 'doesn't necessarily get you paid right then,' but forces payoff when the debtor sells or refinances, which is why operators record it immediately and run garnishment and discovery in parallel rather than waiting on a sale.

Speed is the third point of agreement, bounded by the appeal clock. The Private Investigator Videos operator says to wait out the 30 to 60 day appeal window before seizing anything, but to start the asset search immediately [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:00:56]. Calhoun School of Law gives the same sequence: let the appellate timeframe run out, then start the next day [ObrPaCTQ6ZQ @ 00:01:16]. Scott Bernstein explains why the next day matters: debtors stretch payables across two or three vendors suing at once, and 'it's a race once you have the judgment to see who can collect first,' so nobody should sit on the paper [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:07:07]. Timothy Anenberger adds the bankruptcy penalty for slowness: an unrecorded judgment leaves the creditor unsecured when the debtor files [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:45:03]. The window is long but not unlimited. The Sterling Firm treats a judgment as valid for 10 years and renewable, Calhoun says at least 10 years extendable by 10 more with interest accruing, the investigator cites limitation periods of 5, 10 or 20 years, and SQE1window gives 6 years. The shared rule is to record fast, search during the appeal window, and calendar renewal before the clock expires.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Enforcement starts with finding the debtor and then the money. Eric Nano works outward from what the case already produced: where the debtor lived during the suit, the service address on a default, and contact details from trial or settlement. Under the FDCPA, the creditor can ask friends and family only for location, such as a forwarding address, and must leave them alone once the debtor is found; mutual friends who no longer like the debtor are flagged as prime sources, alongside Google, White Pages and public social media, with professionals holding subscription databases too expensive for individuals [bX8Hhszjuxs @ 00:04:02]. The Private Investigator Videos operator then locks the debtor into a financial disclosure or examination under penalty of perjury and runs an independent asset search to compare the two [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:02:10]. Steven Rinold hands operators a ready-made question list: the federal bankruptcy schedules of assets and liabilities, used as a post-judgment discovery checklist [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:11:32]. Will of Downs Law specifies the document haul: tax returns, bank account information and other assets [K1KakWduA04 @ 00:01:34]. Any gap between the sworn statement and the search becomes the leverage point, since no-shows and liars can be ordered to pay fees and produce tax returns.

The cash tools carry hard numbers. The Sterling Firm prices a California wage levy at a $30 deposit, with the employer remitting 25% of net wages starting 10 days after service for up to 10 years; a bank levy is another $30 deposit with the bank remitting within 10 days, and collected funds are held 21 days [UV-WyQ1-NC4 @ 00:04:15]. Hendershot Cowart separates the Texas writ of garnishment, which targets a known bank and exempts only current wages the debtor can prove the balance equals, from the broader turnover order, which can be served on any bank that then searches for the debtor's accounts itself [-kyDpaMUqFY @ 00:01:03]. Timothy Anenberger's Mississippi practice treats a bank garnishment as a lien from service plus 30 days and serves banks even when nobody is sure an account exists [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:14:54]. LawShelf supplies the federal ceiling: CCPA Title III caps wage garnishment at the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or earnings above 30 times the federal minimum wage, with support orders, federal tax debt and Department of Education loans potentially outside the cap [fQZc8moX-c4 @ 00:01:13]. Business targets cost more under the Sterling schedule: an $85 till tap, a $300 keeper per 8 hours, and roughly $1,000 for a real estate levy.

When the obvious accounts run dry, practitioners turn to pressure. Jeffrey Leon's playbook is to become a nuisance: depositions, subpoenas, account freezes that leave only a living-expense allowance, and credit-report hits, noting that recorded liens can trip mortgage defaults [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:19:30]. Scott Bernstein lays out the escalation ladder against a stonewalling debtor: an order to compel, then contempt with daily fines, then a civil arrest warrant [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:34:17]. The Private Investigator Videos operator calls wages slow and goes after leverage instead, seizing business websites, email and phone numbers, and points to storage units, ATVs and boats as overlooked targets [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:07:25]. On the panel, Anenberger skips defunct LLCs entirely and pursues personal guarantors, using a judgment debtor exam to bring them to the table, while charging orders reach a debtor's financial interest in an LLC or LP but not management rights. Hendershot Cowart keeps the receiver as the last resort because receivers typically take 25% of whatever is collected [-kyDpaMUqFY @ 00:01:30]. The stated end state, from Bernstein, is a forbearance agreement with a payment schedule, reached through persistence rather than a single knockout writ.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The lien camp splits on whether a recorded judgment is a weapon or a waiting room. Will at Downs Law treats the Georgia fifa recorded with the superior court as one of three core methods, but concedes up front that it 'doesn't necessarily get you paid right then'. The payoff only lands when the debtor tries to sell or refinance the house, lot, or investment property [K1KakWduA04 @ 00:00:43]. LawShelf pushes that warning further: a judgment lien blocks a sale yet cannot force a foreclosure, it may sit for 20 years without producing a dollar, it has to be renewed, and interest keeps accruing the whole time [fQZc8moX-c4 @ 00:03:01]. The National Law Review panel runs the lien as an active pressure lever instead of a parking spot. Jeff Leon stacks liens with depositions, subpoenas, freezes, and credit-report hits to become a nuisance, noting that a lien can trip a default on the debtor's mortgage [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:19:30]. Tim Anenberger adds a priority angle the passive camp never touches: when property is held as tenancy by the entirety, record in chancery court immediately, because it is 'first in time, first in right' [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:16:35]. The operator read: record fast for priority, but never count the lien as cash.

Wage garnishment draws the sharpest split between the first-strike camp and the skeptics. Tim Anenberger calls it the first tool in Mississippi. It creates a lien on the income stream, and when the debtor changes jobs you simply re-issue it, though North Carolina bars it outright except for child support and taxes [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:12:48]. The Sterling Firm lays out its version as a low-cost grind: a $30 deposit, after which the employer remits 25% of net wages starting 10 days after service, for up to 10 years [UV-WyQ1-NC4 @ 00:04:15]. LawShelf undercuts the strategy for a big slice of debtors. The CCPA Title III cap limits the take to the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or earnings above 30 times the federal minimum wage, and garnishment simply fails on low income or unemployment [fQZc8moX-c4 @ 00:02:41]. Private Investigator Videos goes further and calls wages slow, pushing operators toward leverage plays instead: seize the debtor's business websites, email, and phone numbers so the business itself feels the squeeze [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:07:25]. The playbook choice comes down to the debtor profile. Steady salaried earners get the wage order. Self-employed operators with digital storefronts get hit where the revenue actually flows.

The practitioners also disagree on who should do the collecting and how hard to chase. Hendershot Cowart P.C. ranks a Texas receiver as the 'last resort' because receivers 'typically take 25% of whatever is collected,' and the firm pitches its own work as running without that cut [-kyDpaMUqFY @ 00:01:44]. Attorney Michael Ehline lists collection agencies as a normal California step alongside wage garnishment, liens, and renewing the judgment, treating the agency as just another tool in the kit [jPO9_I_LBSw @ 00:00:28]. Private Investigator Videos rejects that framing hard. Agencies are contingency phone-callers, giving away 'a third' may be permanent, and passive 'collection' is a different business from active 'recovery' [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:11:17]. On how far to push, Jeff Leon argues for compromise priced on the time value of money and risk, warning that clients who chase a judgment 'to the end of the earth' end up running 'two litigations in one' [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:23:38]. The split is about the cost of handing off the file. Outsource and surrender a quarter or a third of every recovered dollar, or keep the work in-house, apply relentless pressure, and settle the moment the math turns against you.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure starts before the complaint is filed. Attorney Michael Ehline's opening rule is blunt: don't sue without insurance or a known asset, and remember that a settlement is not a judgment [jPO9_I_LBSw @ 00:00:00]. Eric Nano of the Sacramento County Public Law Library warns that debtors living off the grid are hard to find, and the ones who truly live that way likely have nothing worth collecting anyway [bX8Hhszjuxs @ 00:06:17]. Jeff Leon frames the cost side just as bluntly: weigh the cost of collection, especially on a contingency file, because post-judgment fees can exceed what was spent winning the judgment in the first place [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:08:27]. The panel boils the whole calculation down to a single line: 'you don't spend a million dollars to collect half a million dollars' [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:22:53]. That means every enforcement step needs a projected return attached before it is filed, not after the invoices land. The economics check is simple and unforgiving. Before spending a dollar on enforcement, know exactly what asset you are chasing, what it will cost to reach it, how long it will take to convert into cash, and at what point the file stops being worth another hour of billable work.

The second trap is assuming the debtor will sit still. Tim Anenberger says debtors abscond, transfer, and dissipate assets 'all the time,' which is why execution planning, asset mapping, and provisional remedies like attachment belong at the start of the case, before the complaint is even filed, not after the verdict [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:02:25]. Once the judgment lands, Tim reports debtors stonewalling and dodging the sheriff for months, and warns that if the debtor files bankruptcy while your judgment sits unrecorded, you are just another unsecured creditor [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:45:03]. Private Investigator Videos flags the classic dump: a $100K car sold for $5K is a below-value transfer that counts as fraudulent conveyance, and storage units, ATVs, and boats are the targets worth hunting [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:04:52]. Attorney Michael Ehline points to debtors who 'escape' to Florida, citing the OJ and Goldman wrongful death case [jPO9_I_LBSw @ 00:00:44]. Each of these moves forces the creditor to spend more just to get back to where they started. The operator lesson is speed and paper. Record immediately, freeze what you can, and document every suspicious transfer before the trail goes cold.

The third failure mode is the creditor becoming the defendant. Eric Nano warns that under the FDCPA you cannot tell friends or family the debtor is a deadbeat. You may only ask about location, such as whether a forwarding address was left, and once the debtor is found you leave everyone else alone [bX8Hhszjuxs @ 00:04:02]. Steven Rinold adds that wrongly liening someone's property can be 'slander of title,' turning an aggressive lien into a liability [R3nWxu374QE @ 00:55:04]. The Sterling Firm flags the back-end trap that catches creditors who finally get paid: file the satisfaction of judgment within 15 days or face liability yourself [UV-WyQ1-NC4 @ 00:01:24]. On the flip side, Private Investigator Videos tells creditors to watch the docket for unauthorized satisfaction filings against their own judgment [q-khGCaOPck @ 00:10:34]. Aggression only pays when the paperwork is clean. Every levy, lien, garnishment, and third-party contact needs to survive a challenge from the debtor's side, because one sloppy step flips the leverage and turns the collection file into a defense file. Track deadlines on both ends of the case: the clock that starts when you collect, and the filings someone else might slip in while you are not looking.
