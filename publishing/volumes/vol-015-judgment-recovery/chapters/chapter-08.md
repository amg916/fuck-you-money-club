# Chapter 08: Domesticating and Enforcing Foreign and Out-of-State Judgments

*Registering sister-state judgments, domestication in Florida, statute-of-limitations challenges, the Uniform Foreign Money Judgments Recognition Act, UK, Ontario, Vietnam, and India (DRT) enforcement regimes, serving overseas defendants, asset-protection limits abroad, and jurisdiction, transfer, and forum non conveniens*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners across the US, Canada, England, Vietnam, and India agree that a judgment is only a right to collect, not cash: it must be recognized or domesticated where the debtor's assets sit, then executed with local tools. For sister-state money judgments, full faith and credit and the Uniform Enforcement of Foreign Judgments Act make domestication a paperwork drill: certified copy, filing, service, and a 30-day response window, with defenses confined to jurisdiction and service defects. Foreign-country judgments face real screens: due process, notice, fraud, public policy, and a limitation capped at the earlier of the foreign validity period or 15 years. Street mechanics include Florida 'machine gun' garnishment writs at $50 to $100 each across about 10 banks and New York clearing-bank freezes. Disagreement centers on Florida limitation math and on whether foreign collection abroad is even economic.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who works the collection side opens with the same premise: a judgment is paper until someone forces it into money. LTS Law Firm frames it bluntly, 'judgments don't enforce themselves,' and routes every Vietnamese recovery through THADS, the Ministry of Justice enforcement agency with provincial and district offices, which moves only on a formal request plus a complete dossier [cv-rg7aHsRE @ 00:00:06]. BlackBeltBarrister gives the same order of operations for England and Wales: recognize first, then enforce, because the enforcement tools stay locked until the foreign judgment is recognized locally [EKjVl_JQ5QE @ 00:00:56]. The Proper Person warns that US states do not automatically recognize each other's judgments, so the creditor must register where the debtor or the assets actually sit [djAI4TQ1S00 @ 00:00:00]. John at OffshoreAlert hammers the same geography rule from the international side: judgments do not travel automatically between states, so you file where the assets are [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:04:12]. The baseline prerequisite is therefore location intelligence, not legal brilliance: knowing which courthouse sits nearest the bank accounts, real estate, receivables, or business premises before a single filing fee is spent. Everything downstream, from the choice of regime to the speed of recovery, depends on that first asset map.

On sister-state money judgments, the consensus is that domestication is a paperwork exercise, not a rematch. Snellings Law defines 'foreign' as any other state or country and explains that full faith and credit applies: the creditor domesticates by filing in the home state and then serves the debtor [W1XbSRwt1D8 @ 00:00:30]. James Vann of Vann Attorneys calls it 'not really a very difficult process' and lists the only openings the debtor gets: improper personal jurisdiction, bad service, or improper subject-matter jurisdiction. In Vann's words, 'we're not retrying the whole terms again' [YhtCR_GKvQ4 @ 00:01:44]. The Proper Person agrees that objections are limited to technicalities, with no retrial of the underlying dispute [djAI4TQ1S00 @ 00:01:05]. Attorney Thoughts counts the reach of the Uniform Enforcement of Foreign Judgments Act of 1986 at 47 states plus the District of Columbia and the Virgin Islands, with registration done by filing a copy of the original judgment with the clerk alongside an affidavit containing essential information [QgFvjr6fOpg @ 00:02:05]. The operator takeaway is consistent across all four speakers: if the original judgment was entered with proper jurisdiction and proper service, the debtor's attack surface inside the new state is narrow, and the creditor's real work shifts to finding and hitting assets rather than relitigating the debt.

Foreign-country judgments are where the agreement turns into a warning. Snellings Law states the US is not obligated to recognize them at all; some states do so by statute, through an action filed in the home state [W1XbSRwt1D8 @ 00:01:46]. Hesham Elrafei of Lex Animata lists the denial criteria courts run through: jurisdiction, finality, a fair trial with notice and appeal, and the public policy of the enforcing country [YB6KKY7Q7ts @ 00:01:46]. John at OffshoreAlert gives the US version of the same checklist: due process, personal jurisdiction, notice, fraud, public policy, and a seriously inconvenient forum, with defamation, IP, and First Amendment conflicts sitting inside the public-policy bucket [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:07:36]. Rob Lambert of AssetProtectionTraining.com, speaking from the debtor side, pushes the point to its extreme: no country automatically enforces US judgments, and there is always 'at least a mini trial' abroad, while arbitration awards travel more easily, which is exactly why Lambert tells debtors to avoid arbitration clauses [HqKU9rVRjNI @ 00:00:34]. Whether the speaker is collecting or defending, the shared reality is that cross-border recognition is a contested proceeding with defined escape hatches. The creditor has to build the file around jurisdiction, notice, and due process from day one, because those are the doors the debtor will try first.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The step-by-step domestication drill varies by state but follows a recognizable pattern. James Vann's North Carolina workflow starts with a certified copy of the judgment from the state of original filing, then a new lawsuit filed in North Carolina, served on the defendant, with the debtor getting 30 days to respond before the court issues a North Carolina judgment and the asset search begins [YhtCR_GKvQ4 @ 00:01:08]. Haber Law describes Florida, which the firm calls 'the most debtor friendly state,' as a record-and-notice system: the creditor records the judgment and mails notice, the debtor may contest by filing a complaint, and an initial hearing follows. Florida usually recognizes default judgments, while New York does not, and the Florida limitations period usually runs 20 years [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:06:30]. The Proper Person adds the Nevada detail that registration happens at the courthouse near the assets [djAI4TQ1S00 @ 00:00:49]. When the defendant sits overseas, The Global Process Server says the Hague Service Convention is mandatory for member countries under Volkswagen Werk, and three checks come first: membership, objections and declarations, and language and format. Those three answers decide timing, translation, cost, and whether alternative service methods are available [SUlnLzzcvIk @ 00:00:47]. Skip the treaty, the speaker warns, and service gets quashed, defaults get vacated, and enforcement gets refused.

Once the judgment is local, the speakers pivot hard to collection hardware. Haber Law's signature move is 'machine gun' writs of garnishment, roughly $50 to $100 apiece to serve, fired across about 10 banks at once, backed by a fact information sheet, subpoenas duces tecum, sheriff break orders, and proceedings supplementary to implead transferees [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:19:17]. Florida's homestead exemption shields the residence, but investment property is reachable, and head-of-household limits cap what can be taken from income. Nizam Hashmi lays out the Ontario toolkit: garnishment of wages and bank funds, a writ of seizure and sale over personal property or land, and an examination in aid of execution where the debtor answers under oath about employment, income, property, accounts, debts, and expenses [AT4U_1yLtaE @ 00:00:43]. Hashmi stresses that 'enforcement also requires economic judgment,' weighing likely recovery, reachable assets, cost, and time while post-judgment interest keeps accruing [AT4U_1yLtaE @ 00:01:04]. BlackBeltBarrister's English menu runs parallel: third-party debt orders, writs and warrants of control, charging orders, and attachment of earnings [EKjVl_JQ5QE @ 00:05:45]. Across all three jurisdictions the sequence is the same: force the debtor to disclose assets under oath or on a sworn form, then hit every reachable account and property interest before money moves.

For foreign-country judgments landing in the US, John at OffshoreAlert supplies the hardest numbers. The Uniform Act is state law, adopted in 1962 and updated in 2005, and the recognition window runs to the earlier of the foreign judgment's validity period or 15 years [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:01:20]. New York is the preferred landing zone because of its clearing banks, a summary procedure, in rem jurisdiction, and the ability to file in anticipation of assets that have not yet arrived [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:05:12]. The payoff of that venue choice is the ability to freeze dollar flows as they pass through New York clearing banks, catching money in transit [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 01:02:16]. LTS Law Firm runs the Vietnamese equivalent as a five-part dossier: a judgment copy; a request naming the debtor, the amount, and the scope; proof of standing and power of attorney; asset information covering bank accounts, real estate, business locations, and receivables; and fees and costs [cv-rg7aHsRE @ 00:02:01]. The firm reports that delays are usually 'dossier problems,' not legal defeats, and that speed depends on whether assets exist and whether the creditor hands over actionable asset information. Its recommended homework is asset mapping and corporate registry checks before the request is lodged, and its closing line sums up the whole chapter: 'winning is not collecting.'

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how long a domesticated judgment stays alive. Arguing for Lisa Worthington before the Florida appellate bench, counsel treated a registration under Florida's foreign-judgment statute as a Florida judgment carrying 20 years measured from original entry, with a separate action on the judgment available within 5 years to mint a fresh 20-year judgment; under that reading, New Mexico's 14-year limit leaves only “six more years” in Florida [9x7_I2ygY9E @ 00:10:31]. The bench resisted, worried the rule hands out more credit than the originating state would, read section 55.504(4) as saying nothing in it should “extend the limitation period,” and shut down forum-shopping the filing across states. Robert Donald, for the appellee, went further: registration never creates a Florida judgment, and the borrowing statute, 95.10, imports the shorter foreign clock [9x7_I2ygY9E @ 00:34:10]. Haber Law works from a usual 20-year Florida horizon and leans on the rule that the diligent creditor is always rewarded [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:11:39], while OffshoreAlert's John reads the Uniform Act for foreign-country money judgments as running only until the earlier of the foreign validity period or 15 years [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:01:20]. Same judgment, three different counts of remaining life, so the originating state's own expiry and renewal status gets pinned before any filing fee is spent.

Practitioners also split on how much of a fight domestication invites. James Vann of Vann Attorneys calls the sister-state route “quite easy”: a certified copy from the original state, a new lawsuit in North Carolina, service, a 30-day response window, then a North Carolina judgment, with defenses confined to improper personal jurisdiction, bad service or improper subject-matter jurisdiction and no retrial of the merits [YhtCR_GKvQ4 @ 00:01:44]. Haber Law paints Florida as “the most debtor friendly state,” where the judgment is recorded, notice is mailed, the debtor may contest by complaint and an initial hearing follows; Florida usually recognizes default judgments, while New York does not [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:07:10]. Cross a national border and the gap widens. Rob Lambert of AssetProtectionTraining.com, speaking from the debtor's chair, says no country automatically enforces US judgments, there is always “at least a mini trial,” and arbitration awards travel more easily, which is why that camp tells debtors to avoid arbitration clauses [HqKU9rVRjNI @ 00:00:34]. Hesham Elrafei frames the root of the divide: a liberal approach prizing certainty and an end to repeat litigation, against a conservative approach guarding reciprocity and sovereignty out of fear of corrupt proceedings or public-policy violations [YB6KKY7Q7ts @ 00:00:47]. Creditors chasing assets abroad budget for the conservative camp.

Even the filing vehicle differs by speaker and jurisdiction. Attorney Thoughts describes the 1986 Uniform Act model followed by 47 states, the District of Columbia and the Virgin Islands as a registration play: a copy of the original judgment lodged with the clerk alongside an affidavit containing essential information [QgFvjr6fOpg @ 00:02:05], while Vann's North Carolina workflow runs as a fresh lawsuit. OffshoreAlert's John treats forum choice as strategy, not paperwork: file where the assets are, favor New York for its clearing banks, summary procedure, in rem jurisdiction and the ability to file in anticipation of assets, and expect state court to often move faster than federal [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:05:12]. BlackBeltBarrister maps four English regimes and puts post-2020 US judgments under common law, which demands a definite sum and fresh proceedings, though summary judgment can shortcut them [EKjVl_JQ5QE @ 00:02:32]. Haber Law offers the bypass: when both the debtor and the assets already sit in Florida and the original judgment carries a service defect, skip domestication and sue there directly rather than invite a “do-over” [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:10:13]. The real choice is between speed of registration, leverage of a creditor-friendly banking forum, and the durability of a fresh local judgment.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures are clock failures. Dr. V.K. Kohli of Court Kutchehry walks through a ₹50 lakh mortgage loan from 1985 that defaulted in 1987: “The suit is decreed only in 1995, after 8 years of hearings,” and execution ran another 5 years while the security lost value and crores of rupees of public money sat stuck, which is the problem the DRT's 180-day target was built to kill [E-XLFQLiAEE @ 00:03:22]. The Florida appellate fight shows the same trap in US form: a 2003 Arizona judgment registered in Florida in 2006, Arizona's 5-year limit lapsing in 2008 without renewal, and execution not sought until 2012, leaving the creditor to litigate whether anything survived [9x7_I2ygY9E @ 00:25:40]. LTS Law Firm warns that statutory time limits apply once a Vietnamese judgment is legally effective, and that arbitral awards need recognition before enforcement even starts, so delay is the enemy [cv-rg7aHsRE @ 00:01:11]. JD Simplified's federal-procedure rule points the same direction: once the statute of limitations has run, transfer beats dismissal because “Transfer keeps the case alive,” and the standing warning is to watch for limitation facts before choosing a move [FUPJlxOU2nI @ 00:08:09].

The second cluster is procedural self-sabotage. The Global Process Server says the Hague Service Convention, with 80-plus members and a central authority in each, is mandatory when service goes to a member country; skip it and service is quashed, defaults are vacated and enforcement is refused: “Months of work gone” [SUlnLzzcvIk @ 00:01:06]. The three checks come first: membership, objections and declarations, language and format. Haber Law flags the domestic version: a defect such as lack of service in the original case hands the debtor a “do-over,” and Haber separately expects English defamation judgments to go unenforced in Florida [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:10:13]. LTS Law Firm finds most Vietnamese enforcement delays are “dossier problems,” not legal defeats, traced to missing pieces of the five-part file: judgment copy, a request naming debtor, amount and scope, standing and power of attorney, asset information, and fees [cv-rg7aHsRE @ 00:02:36]. OffshoreAlert's John frames the defense choice as contesting jurisdiction only, never the merits, calling it “a really important decision that has to be made at the very beginning of a case”; creditors face non-recognition fights on due process, notice, fraud and public policy [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:21:47].

The final check is pure economics. Haber Law says $15,000 to $20,000 judgments may not be worth pursuing, even though “machine gun” writs of garnishment cost only about $50 to $100 apiece across roughly 10 banks, and warns that covert bank-account searches by investigators are “probably a violation of federal law” [ZdVZvPMh4qM @ 00:20:36]. On the agency side, Rob Fisher at OffshoreAlert cites Caledonian, a $25M judgment with nothing left to collect, and a RICO courier paying $1,000 a month toward a $25M joint forfeiture [ruQAKGpCYE8 @ 00:41:59]. Rob Lambert's debtor-side math is harsher abroad: foreign lawyers will not take contingency cases, a Swiss lawyer can want “a quarter million dollars,” bonds are often demanded in cash, and funds move in “5 10 15 minutes” while the creditor litigates for six months, spending “$10 to collect 10 cents” [HqKU9rVRjNI @ 00:04:54]. Nizam Hashmi's Ontario filter is the counterweight: weigh likely recovery, reachable assets, cost and time before choosing garnishment, a writ of seizure and sale, or an examination in aid of execution, remembering that post-judgment interest may keep accruing [AT4U_1yLtaE @ 00:01:04]. Leverage still pays when the numbers line up: Haber took a $350,000 judgment on contingency and settled near 100 cents on the dollar.
