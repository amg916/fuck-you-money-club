## 1. Define the fee-bearing unit before calculating a management spread  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with the signed service agreement, not the solicitation's estimated ceiling. The canonical revenue basis is recognized service fees, excluding client contract value, subcontractor pass-through principal, and unexercised options. The supplied mapped play, government_rfp_subcontractor_brokerage, uses SAM.gov, state procurement portals, and municipal bid boards to locate low-bid-density service opportunities and coordinate qualified fulfillment. Its advertised 15–30% management spread is an underwriting range, not an automatic accounting treatment. On a $100,000 client service package, $80,000 of supplier delivery and $20,000 of separately earned desk fees produce a 20% fee share of package value and a 25% markup on supplier cost. Those percentages answer different questions. If the desk instead contracts as principal and controls the promised service, gross presentation may be required; a $20,000 margin cannot simply be relabeled $20,000 of revenue. Keep that principal model outside this fee-based scorecard until the accounting policy and scenario are rebuilt. Maintain a deal record linking the customer, solicitation identifier, fee payer, delivery obligations, acceptance evidence, supplier invoices, and recognized fee. A purchase order ceiling is pipeline capacity; only satisfied contractual performance supports the operating revenue ledger.

For this chapter, seven mapped plays mean seven operating variants of that single supplied catalog identifier, not seven invented catalog entries. Federal sourcing and municipal sourcing monetize bid-desk deliverables; janitorial, grounds, courier, translation, and IT staffing coordination monetize distinct management work. Price sourcing through fixed retainers or completed milestones where appropriate, rather than assuming an award percentage is permissible. FAR Subpart 3.4 and FAR 52.203-5 restrict contingent-fee arrangements within their applicable scope; the bona fide employee or agency exception requires factual qualification, not a label in an invoice. Likewise, 41 U.S.C. 2102 protects specified procurement information, and 41 U.S.C. 8702 prohibits kickbacks connected with covered prime contracts and subcontracts. No pricing model should require buying source-selection information or paying an undisclosed reward for favorable treatment. A percentage can illustrate coordination economics without authorizing a commission for obtaining an award. State and municipal requirements must be checked separately. Record the actual service, fee trigger, customer benefit, and applicable restrictions before a salesperson promises a spread. The matrix shows illustrative standalone units; its rows are alternative delivery motions, not additive components of the canonical annual revenue totals.

Screen structure before calculating contribution. For covered small-business service contracts, 13 CFR 125.6 generally limits payments to subcontractors that are not similarly situated to 50% of the amount paid by the government for contract performance, subject to the rule's definitions and exclusions. A similarly situated entity must meet the relevant program status and size requirements; location alone proves nothing. Further subcontracting can affect the limitation calculation, and ostensible-subcontractor concerns remain a separate affiliation issue. A broker retaining 20% while sending 80% to an ineligible fulfillment company can therefore have attractive arithmetic and an unusable structure. Check FAR 52.215-23 when applicable: a pass-through charge needs substantive added value, not merely invoice routing. The fallback is an unrestricted opportunity whose actual terms permit the structure, including suitable commercial or municipal work, or a properly qualified similarly situated team. Municipal status does not erase local subcontracting restrictions. Document scheduling, quality inspection, customer reporting, replacement coverage, and dispute resolution as management deliverables. If those services cannot justify the fee, sell a narrower fixed-price bid-desk engagement instead. Reject a tempting award when eligibility, delivery control, or fee legality cannot support the proposed economics.

## 2. Reconcile annual cohorts from gross fee billings to owner profit  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Use an annual cohort of completed fee-bearing engagements to reconcile the three canonical scenarios. The illustrative customer counts are six Conservative, fourteen Base, and twenty Scaled, producing average recognized annual fees of $20,000, $40,000, and $50,000 per customer. Gross earned fee billings before scope credits are $126,000, $580,000, and $1,040,000. Expected contractual credits and performance reversals of $6,000, $20,000, and $40,000 reduce those amounts to exactly $120,000, $560,000, and $1,000,000 of recognized service revenue. This is a steady-state annual cohort illustration: no opening deferred balances or unfinished performance are embedded in the bridge. In a live ledger, reconcile invoicing, earned amounts, contract liabilities, and unbilled receivables separately. The credit assumptions equal 4.76%, 3.45%, and 3.85% of gross fee billings, respectively. They represent fee reductions for narrowed scope, rejected deliverables, or documented service failures; they are not interchangeable with losses on valid receivables. Exclude unawarded pipeline entirely. A bid lost before contracted work is earned creates acquisition expense or idle capacity, not a reversal of imaginary award revenue. Each cohort must reconcile to signed fee schedules and actual performance before its margin is used for hiring.

Delivery labor is $54,000, $252,000, and $420,000 across the cases. An explicit staffing assumption makes the low case intelligible: its two full-time people are a working owner taking residual profit and one employee budgeted at $36,000 fully loaded, plus three contractors sharing $18,000 of delivery invoices. That employee budget requires location-specific wage and benefit validation and may be inadequate. The Base case assumes $72,000 for the paid employee and $180,000 across three contractors; the Scaled case assumes two paid employees at $90,000 each and $240,000 across four contractors, alongside the working owner. Contractor counts describe a delivery bench, not full-time-equivalent salaries. Acquisition spending of $12,000, $56,000, and $80,000 yields customer acquisition costs of $2,000, $4,000, and $4,000 using the cohort counts above. Include unsuccessful proposal work in that spending and do not also charge those hours to delivery. After labor and acquisition, contribution is $54,000, $252,000, and $500,000. No additional market-rate owner salary is deducted in this illustration; residual profit compensates ownership and the owner's full-time labor before personal taxes. The Conservative case is consequently a fragile owner-operated business, not evidence that five people receive comfortable full-time compensation. Split the combined financing line internally: Conservative budgets $1,000 each for financing, collection losses, and payment administration; Base budgets $8,000, $4,000, and $2,000; Scaled budgets $10,000, $6,000, and $4,000. Collection losses remain an expense against otherwise valid receivables, while contractual fee credits stay above recognized revenue. Reserve releases must follow resolved evidence rather than a target margin. Reconcile gross profit, contribution, and owner profit as distinct subtotals so a strong supplier spread cannot conceal an expensive bid desk.

## 3. Fund acceptance holds and invoice DSO without borrowing against fiction  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Measure the cash clock from the first delivery outlay, not from the award notice. Separate mobilization before performance, acceptance or milestone-release holds before invoicing, invoice-to-cash days sales outstanding, and supplier payment dates. The illustration assumes immediate operating outlays, no supplier credit, and a fifteen-day pre-invoice acceptance hold in every case. Invoice DSO is thirty, sixty, and seventy-five days, producing cash gaps of forty-five, seventy-five, and ninety days. These are planning assumptions, not statutory payment promises. If the desk invoices a prime rather than an agency, the relevant collectible obligation is the prime's signed agreement with the desk. Federal prompt-payment provisions do not automatically turn every subcontract invoice into a direct government receivable. Check 31 U.S.C. 3903, FAR Subpart 32.9, and applicable payment clauses for the actual transaction. FAR 52.232-40 addresses accelerated payments to small-business subcontractors when its conditions apply, but it does not supply an unconditional accelerated cash date. For municipal work, obtain the jurisdiction's payment provisions and the customer's acceptance workflow. A missing purchase-order reference, incorrect remittance record, or disputed timesheet can extend collection even when the buyer has adequate appropriations.

Use total annual expense divided by 365 as a deliberately conservative daily funding proxy: approximately $279.45 Conservative, $1,189.04 Base, and $1,917.81 Scaled. This includes collection-loss allowances and other budgeted charges that may not be immediate cash payments, so it is a screening calculation rather than a substitute for a dated cash forecast. Multiplying by the assumed cash gaps gives approximately $12,575, $89,178, and $172,603 of operating float. Add thirty days of expense buffer, approximately $8,384, $35,671, and $57,534, producing rounded funding targets of $20,959, $124,849, and $230,137. Calculate totals before rounding the displayed components. The Scaled target exceeds the stated $20,000–$90,000 startup capital range; expansion therefore needs accumulated cash, contracted advances, suitable credit, or slower starts. The stated $5,000–$25,000 monthly operating range also cannot describe all-in spending at scale: canonical annual expenses imply approximately $8,500, $36,167, and $58,333 monthly. Keep the monthly range as an early-stage planning reference rather than silently forcing mature delivery expenses underneath it. Neither an unsigned credit proposal nor an anticipated award closes a funding gap.

Build a thirteen-week cash schedule by customer and weekly payment date. Enter earned invoices, expected acceptance dates, disputed amounts, supplier payroll deadlines, taxes, insurance renewals, and available credit. A fifteen-day hold belongs before invoice DSO here; if an actual arrangement instead withholds cash after collection, model that separate restriction without counting the same delay twice. The Base case's $124,849 target falls to $104,849 if two customers each provide an available $10,000 advance, but the advances remain contract liabilities until earned and must be restored if refund obligations arise. Confirm that their terms permit operational use. Supplier credit can reduce float only where enforceable terms support it; a contractor's informal willingness to wait is not financing. A thirty-day DSO extension adds approximately $8,384, $35,671, or $57,534 to required funding before incremental borrowing charges. Reserve a committed facility for that timing shock rather than using it to justify structurally negative contracts. Validate assignment restrictions, lender collateral terms, and any applicable federal assignment requirements before pledging receivables. Review actual bank availability weekly, because borrowing-base exclusions can remove disputed, aged, or concentrated invoices precisely when the desk needs liquidity most.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Map the contractual cash clock
   - Record first outlay, acceptance conditions, invoice eligibility, customer payment terms, and supplier due dates.
   - Assign an accountable person to every acceptance document and distinguish agency payment from prime-to-desk payment.
2. Reconcile the weekly collection population
   - Match earned fees, issued invoices, contractual credits, receivable losses, and bank receipts by engagement identifier.
   - Investigate invoice rejection reasons and unbilled completed milestones before changing the forecast collection date.
3. Stress the available liquidity
   - Apply thirty extra collection days and remove disputed invoices from assumed credit availability.
   - Compare the lowest weekly cash balance with committed funds after supplier obligations and refund exposure.
4. Authorize only funded delivery starts
   - Schedule new engagements only when available cash and executable financing cover the stressed weekly deficit.
   - If funding fails, reduce concurrent starts, obtain permitted advances, or renegotiate milestone and supplier payment intervals.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Shorten the administrative cash gap before buying more leads**
> 
> Experienced bid desks collect evidence continuously and distinguish customer acceptance friction from actual funding scarcity.
> - Send a sample invoice and supporting-document checklist to the prime's accounts-payable team before the first delivery milestone.
> - Track acceptance aging separately from invoice aging so an apparently healthy DSO metric cannot hide unbilled completed work.
> - Negotiate smaller accepted milestones and supplier billing intervals rather than relying on an unpriced promise to wait for payment.
> - Keep customer advances, credit-loss allowances, contractual fee credits, and unrestricted operating cash in separate ledger accounts and forecast lines.

## 4. Stress revenue, replacement labor, and acquisition before expanding capacity  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Run sensitivities against recognized fees, not the government contract's headline value. A 10% reduction in recognized revenue with annual costs held fixed reduces owner profit to $6,000 Conservative, $70,000 Base, and $200,000 Scaled. The resulting margins are approximately 5.56%, 13.89%, and 22.22%. Separately, a 10% increase in delivery labor reduces profit to $12,600, $100,800, and $258,000. Combining both shocks leaves only $600, $44,800, and $158,000 before any additional financing expense. These are deliberately blunt fixed-cost tests, appropriate when employees, subscriptions, and subcontractor minimums cannot be removed immediately. Do not claim that all costs automatically decline with lost awards. An increase in contractual fee credits equal to two percentage points of gross billings costs another $2,520, $11,600, or $20,800, respectively; use the gross-billings denominator rather than recognized revenue for that calculation. Keep each stress separate unless explicitly constructing a combined scenario. A credit-loss shock belongs below recognized revenue, while a scope credit changes the transaction price above it. This separation preserves a useful diagnosis: weak scope control, weak collections, and weak labor pricing require different corrective actions.

Customer acquisition deserves its own loss funnel. With a hypothetical 25% conversion from fully qualified proposals to paying customers, six, fourteen, and twenty customers require twenty-four, fifty-six, and eighty proposals. Acquisition budgets therefore support average proposal costs of $500, $1,000, and $1,000. Those costs include screening, estimating, document preparation, and unsuccessful submissions; they exclude delivery already charged to contracted customers. If conversion falls to 20% while proposal counts and spend remain fixed, expected customer counts fall to 4.8, 11.2, and sixteen, and expected acquisition cost per customer rises to $2,500, $5,000, and $5,000. Fractional customers describe an expected-value planning model, not actual engagements. Protect the funnel by checking amendment history, incumbent advantages, mandatory site visits, wage determinations, and realistic subcontractor availability before paying for a proposal. SAM.gov alerts and bid calendars reduce monitoring effort but do not establish eligibility or commercial viability. For covered federal service work, the Service Contract Labor Standards statute and applicable wage determination can invalidate a cheap labor quote. Rebid with verified rates or decline; automation cannot repair a spread built on unpaid benefits or unavailable personnel.

Translate these tests into operating limits rather than decorative charts. Give each engagement a maximum funded exposure, a minimum post-delivery contribution, and a documented replacement-provider budget. Under the Base waterfall, $560,000 of fees less $252,000 delivery labor leaves $308,000 before acquisition; that pool must still cover $182,000 of acquisition and other overhead to produce $126,000 owner profit. A single $15,000 replacement expense therefore consumes approximately 11.9% of annual Base owner profit even though it is only 2.7% of revenue. Secure backup janitorial crews, alternate courier capacity, or replacement linguists before the service failure, with documented prices and applicable qualifications. Review cohort credits monthly against actual root causes and revise future quotes without retroactively disguising failed jobs. For an early-stage desk, the stated six-to-eighteen-month time to first deal also requires a separate pre-revenue runway; mature annual margins do not fund months before the first collection. Expand only when the existing book demonstrates repeatable acceptance, collectible invoices, and funded replacement capacity. When those conditions fail, narrow the scope to paid bid-desk deliverables, reduce concurrent launches, or pursue permitted unrestricted work instead of borrowing to preserve an unsupported headline spread.
