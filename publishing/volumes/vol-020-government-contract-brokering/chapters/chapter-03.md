# Chapter 03: Micro-Purchases, Simplified Acquisitions and Commercial Items

*Micro-purchase thresholds and GPC card buys, finding sub-$10,000 opportunities on SAM.gov, Simplified Acquisition Procedures as low-hanging fruit, and FAR Part 12 and DFARS 212 rules for selling commercial products and services*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree that micro-purchases under $10,000 and simplified acquisitions under $250,000 are the fastest door into federal work for small firms. SamSearch reports that 65% of simplified acquisition contracts went to small businesses, versus 26% of traditional contract spending. The consensus play is relationship-driven. Micro-purchases are rarely advertised, so operators call buyers, accept purchase cards, and stack small jobs into past performance. The street mechanics include Humam's SAM.gov and FPDS filter chains, Eric Coffie's Sources Sought CSV drill, Sheenapreneur's intelligence on card limits, and Kizzy Parks' $1,300-a-month Coast Guard profit. The disagreements run deep. Speakers quote micro ceilings from $2,500 to $10,000 and split on whether SAM registration is required. They contradict each other on lowest-price versus best-value awards and on whether two or three quotes are needed. Commercial-item buys add a separate FAR Part 12 layer.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Operators anchor this lane in FAR Part 13 and two dollar lines: micro-purchases under $10,000 and the simplified acquisition threshold under $250,000. SamSearch frames Part 13 around three goals: lower administrative cost, easier small-business wins, and a streamlined process. It says Part 13 covers products, services, and construction. It also flags an $800,000 emergency threshold that beginners can skip [e7wypJIJZpw @ 00:02:00]. Humam draws the same $10K and $250K lines and tells newcomers to ignore the exceptions that stretch to 'seven digits' [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:02:17]. Eric Coffie dates the current numbers to February 2018. That is when the micro line rose from $3,000 to $10,000 and the SAT climbed from $150K to $250K. He insists that all SAP work goes to small businesses that fit the NAICS size standard for the buy [JVBkE6J1jPc @ 00:00:37]. The payoff is speed and odds. SamSearch puts simplified awards at weeks or months, against years for standard contracts. It cites $30B of SAP spend in FY2022. It reports that 65% of simplified acquisition contracts went to small businesses, compared with 26% of traditional contract spending [e7wypJIJZpw @ 00:05:43]. Other operators put annual spend nearer $20B, but every one of them pitches this tier as the place where a firm with no track record can realistically land its first federal dollars.

The strongest consensus is that micro-purchases are a relationship game, not a search game. Kizzy Parks says flatly that no website lists them. Her own wins came from existing clients. In one, a training job, the agency asked 'can you take a credit card.' In another, a GSA-schedule training under $4,000 was paid on the card [Ac9lYLwR6QQ @ 00:01:12]. Government Contract Blueprint says awards under $10K are 'rarely advertised.' They move through relationships, vendor networks, and internal processes, and a brief conversation at an event can turn into years of business [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:01:18]. In his long SAM.gov session, Eric Coffie says micro-purchases are 'not typically solicited through Sam' and that 'there is no database' of them. Historical data has to come from the people who actually did the work [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:38:02]. Fed Biz Access compresses the whole play into one line: a market of more than $20B a year that you win by introducing yourself to buyers [mAsjZ4QW_48 @ 00:00:37]. The shared logic is simple. The contracting officer can award without soliciting competitive quotations, so the vendor the buyer already knows gets the order. A newcomer's job is to become that known vendor before the need surfaces, not after a notice posts.

Every beginner-facing operator pushes the same sequence: start tiny, collect past performance, then climb. The second SamSearch tutorial warns that when new contractors chase big contracts, the win 'usually never happens,' because they lack reputation, experience, and patience. It pitches small awards for their shorter turnaround, lighter paperwork, and agency relationships [xeEO2U-Vy38 @ 00:00:30]. Humam names the same barriers (no past performance, no reputation, no connections) and prescribes 'micro steps' [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:01:37]. Sheenapreneur argues that even a $500 job counts as past performance. She tells vendors to accept credit cards, because purchase-card holders are the buyers in this tier [B6uLsI5zZTQ @ 00:08:43]. Government Contract Blueprint lays out the ladder explicitly: progress from micro work to $20K, then $50K, then $100K-plus awards as the record builds [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:06:40]. The card is the common rail. SamSearch lists the government purchase card as one of four SAP methods. The others are purchase orders paid within 30 days, blanket purchase agreements for recurring needs such as office supplies or landscaping, and RFQs, which it calls the most common route. Operators treat card acceptance as table stakes rather than a perk, because a buyer who cannot swipe simply moves on to a vendor who can.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Humam runs the cleanest free workflow. On SAM.gov he clicks Contracting, then Advanced Search, and keys in 'simplified acquisition' for 1,154 results. He then narrows by notice type (presolicitation, solicitation, and combined synopsis/solicitation), a response date in the coming month, and a NAICS code. That cuts the list to 262 [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:07:44]. To find buyer contacts, he resets the search, selects award notices plus inactive, and accepts that the list comes back 'very small.' His bigger lever is FPDS. An advanced search with an action obligation such as $20,000 returns 86 million records. Setting solicitation procedures to 'simplified' with a date signed from March 1 trims that to about 30,000. A description keyword like 'cleaning' narrows it to 535, which he sorts by date signed [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:11:13]. The action obligation figure doubles as a pricing benchmark. Pasting the solicitation ID back into SAM.gov with inactive notices selected surfaces the point of contact's name, phone, and email [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:13:19]. SamSearch warns that SAM.gov has no SAP filter, so every notice has to be read by hand. Its own product layers a 'simplified acquisition' filter that AI-scans attachments, followed by 'active only' and an RFX type of RFQ [e7wypJIJZpw @ 00:08:39].

Eric Coffie's daily drill starts in SAM.gov. He logs in, searches Contract Opportunities, and sets the notice type to Sources Sought. Filtering to responses due in the next three days yields roughly 300 results. He then uses Actions to download a CSV and imports it into a free Google Sheet for triage [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:15:07]. He skips any notice that demands government past performance, calling that requirement 'not standard.' He also points out that a set-aside decision can hinge on Sources Sought responses, and a strong answer can sometimes lead to a direct award [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:40:18]. On margin, he says commercial off-the-shelf items carry thin markup, while specialized items run 30 to 40%. He has seen 5 to 10% under heavy competition, and pricing double when only one player serves the market [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:30:07]. Government Contract Blueprint adds a demand-mapping routine. Target five to ten agencies such as DoD, VA, and GSA, find contracting officer contacts, and send an intro email. Then study past awards to spot repeat buys, such as training purchased again and again at $500 to $3,000 [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:04:36]. Its suggested categories are software training, equipment repair, admin support, short studies, and office equipment, which are all jobs a small shop can deliver under the micro line.

The purchase-card play has its own mechanics. Sheenapreneur notes that cards are issued per department rather than per agency, that holders must be trained, and that limits vary by month or quarter. Her example: 'Bob only has 3 500... Jeff has 4 500.' Unlike contracting officers on a bid, cardholders may simply tell you their budget [B6uLsI5zZTQ @ 00:07:25]. TendersPage tells vendors to build a website optimized for search with the SmartPay logo and to visit P-card holders in person. It warns that routing card payments through PayPal gets treated as 'high-risk,' so take Visa or Mastercard directly [xobcboYBB6w @ 00:03:10]. Kizzy Parks prescribes spending 10% of your time on 'Dialing for Dollars.' Her example is a student who is a service-disabled veteran-owned business owner and walked into his local VA. She also reports a Coast Guard award sourced on Unison worth $14,000 a month, split across 10 vendors, that netted $1,300 a month in profit. She compares that against a rental unit earning $250 a month and restaurant margins of 3 to 5% [Ac9lYLwR6QQ @ 00:11:25]. Government Contract Blueprint flags the cash-flow side. You might invoice the agency and wait 30, 60, or even 90 days to get paid, so line up cash before stacking several jobs at once [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:05:50].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Four channels teach four different micro-purchase ceilings. Eric Coffie dates the jump to February 2018, when the micro-purchase line moved from $3,000 to $10,000 and the simplified acquisition threshold moved from $150,000 to $250,000, with every SAP award going to small businesses that meet the NAICS size standard [JVBkE6J1jPc @ 00:00:37]. Sheenapreneur lands on the same $10,000 and $250,000 figures as of June 2023, framing the SAT band as $10,001 to $250,000, but tells a different origin story: micro rose from $3,500 'within the last year' and the SAT climbed from $150,000 'since covid' [B6uLsI5zZTQ @ 00:01:19]. TendersPage Corp. still teaches a $3,500 maximum, raised from $3,000 on October 1, 2015, against $15 billion to $20 billion in yearly spend [xobcboYBB6w @ 00:01:26]. The U.S. Department of Commerce training does not use one number at all: services cap at $2,500, professional services at $10,000, and construction at $2,000, with anything above the construction figure referred to the procurement office [Z3BTLzceXHU @ 00:02:31]. The operator read: the ceiling depends on the category of work and the rulebook the office trains to, so ask the card holder or contracting officer which cap applies to your line item before quoting against the full $10,000.

Registration splits the room. SamSearch tells new contractors that micro-purchases need no sam.gov registration and no competition [xeEO2U-Vy38 @ 00:01:59], and GovCon with Humam repeats that micro-purchases need neither competition nor SAM registration and are reserved for small businesses [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:03:40]. Eric Coffie reads the FAR the other way: SAM is the primary vendor source, so registration is required [JVBkE6J1jPc @ 00:03:13]. Government Contract Blueprint puts registration first in its workflow, telling viewers to register on sam.gov and get a free nine-digit DUNS number [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:01:35]. The second split is where to hunt. SamSearch and Humam both run sam.gov keyword searches for 'simplified acquisition' layered with notice types and NAICS codes, with Humam's search returning 1,154 results before narrowing to 262 [xeEO2U-Vy38 @ 00:06:10] [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:07:44]. Coffie counters that 5.202 imposes no public advertising, which is why only about 40 percent of these contracts showed up on FedBizOpps [JVBkE6J1jPc @ 00:03:46], and Blueprint says micro-purchases are rarely advertised and flow through relationships, vendor networks, and internal processes [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:01:18]. The workable read is to register anyway, run the portal searches for solicited SAT work, and treat under-$10,000 card buys as a phone-and-email game with contracting officers.

The award logic conflicts too. SamSearch's first tutorial calls the RFQ the most common method and says it usually goes to the lowest-cost capable quote [e7wypJIJZpw @ 00:07:18]. The second SamSearch tutorial stresses that contracting officers cannot select on personal preference or restrict to well-known brands, and that the standard is best value, not just the cheapest [xeEO2U-Vy38 @ 00:04:58]. Quote counts drift as well. The U.S. Department of Commerce training tells buyers to get at least two prices from two vendors and never split requirements [Z3BTLzceXHU @ 00:00:20], while ASI Education says the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul now requires soliciting at least three sources, stricter than the old Part 13 [_0QnHiuRnhc @ 00:02:24]. ASI also reports the structural shift under the whole chapter: simplified procedures for commercial buys under $9 million now sit in Part 12, Part 13 becomes non-commercial, standing price quotations are allowed, and Part 8 priority sources such as schedules, GWACs, and BIC vehicles come first [_0QnHiuRnhc @ 00:00:16]. The playbook consequence: a bare low price may carry an RFQ under the lowest-cost read, but under best value and the three-source rule your quote has to stand next to at least two competitors, and the buyer looks at Part 8 priority vehicles before your quote.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is aiming too high. SamSearch warns that new contractors chase big contracts and the win usually never happens because they lack reputation, experience, and patience [xeEO2U-Vy38 @ 00:00:00]. GovCon with Humam names the same barriers, no past performance, no reputation, no connections, and prescribes micro steps instead [IEV3HAgB8jA @ 00:00:41]. Sheenapreneur adds that a $500 job still counts as past performance, and that card holders may tell you their budget outright, something contracting officers on bids will not do [B6uLsI5zZTQ @ 00:08:43]. The second trap is cash. Government Contract Blueprint warns that after invoicing, payment can take 30, 60, or even 90 days, so cash flow has to be secured first, and the channel lists obligations that ride along even on small jobs: workers' compensation, labor standards, and records [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:05:50] [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:05:21]. Blueprint's growth path is deliberately staged, moving from micro awards to $20,000, then $50,000, then $100,000-plus [ZL3JyvzdhyE @ 00:06:40]. The economics check is plain: size each job to the receivables you can carry for up to 90 days, and let the stack of small completed jobs build the past-performance file before you chase bigger awards.

Search and payment mechanics hide their own losses. SamSearch warns that sam.gov has no simplified-acquisition filter, so every notice has to be opened and read [e7wypJIJZpw @ 00:07:51]. Eric Coffie's live sam.gov session shows the noise: a bulk fertilizer listing turned out to be a misfiled solicitation [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:03:50], and Coffie tells viewers to skip notices demanding government past performance because that requirement is not standard [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:20:07]. Coffie's margin math is the economics check: commercial off-the-shelf items carry low margin, specialized items run 30 to 40 percent, heavy competition has pushed markups to 5 to 10 percent, and a single-player market can double the price [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:30:07]. Coffie adds there is no database of micro-purchases, so past pricing must come from the people who did the work [AOa0-iEhmks @ 00:38:02]. TendersPage Corp. warns that routing card payments through PayPal is treated as high-risk, so accept Visa or Mastercard [xobcboYBB6w @ 00:03:10]. The U.S. Department of Commerce training lists the buyer-side limits a vendor runs into: no splitting requirements, an IT checklist on purchases over $3,500, and convenience checks capped at half the micro-purchase threshold with none over $5,000 [Z3BTLzceXHU @ 00:00:20] [Z3BTLzceXHU @ 00:04:20].

Commercial-item work carries heavier downside. The government contracts panelist on the How To Sell To & Win Federal Government Contracts channel lists the exposure: excess reprocurement costs, past-performance hits that stay live about three years, False Claims Act treble damages, and suspension or debarment [CbeDp1nctog @ 00:12:33]. Richard Rector calls fixed-price commercial work higher margin but a double-edged sword, and warns that contracting officers typically don't want to negotiate tailorable clauses even when you show they clash with customary commercial practice [qWeiLfjB7k0 @ 00:05:17] [qWeiLfjB7k0 @ 00:15:57]. Win Government Contracts with GovSuccess warns that a quote alone may not create a contract and that old Part 13 checklists should not be reused [IiWbsx5Iudg @ 00:01:58]. ASI Education adds that under 12.301(b) a brief explanation replaces the debriefing, and losing vendors should not stay silent [_0QnHiuRnhc @ 00:05:46].
