# Chapter 09: Limitations on Subcontracting, Affiliation and Enforcement

*13 CFR 125.6 and the 50% rule, SBA's updated rules, similarly situated entities, ostensible contractor and affiliation risk, FAR Part 44 and flow-down compliance for subs, and DOJ fraud prosecutions such as Operation Ghostbusters*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Federal set-aside primes agree on one hard rule: the small business that wins the award must keep a fixed share of the dollars, measured as amount paid to non-similarly-situated subs. Services and supplies cap subcontracted pay at 50%, general construction at 85%, and specialty trades at 75%, with materials excluded. Similarly situated entities, first-tier subs holding the prime's status and small under the subcontract NAICS, count as the prime's own work, and PilieroMazza says a WOSB can push 100% to a WOSB SSE. Compliance is measured per base or option period or task order, tracked monthly, and enforced through negative CPARS, debarment and rising DOJ probes. Practitioners split on whether construction self-performance is 15% or 25%, which NAICS governs SSE size, and whether leasing a sub's employees is a legitimate workaround.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same premise: the limitation on subcontracting is a performance rule that binds the small business that won a set-aside, not the subs and not full-and-open winners. GovPrep tells primes to pull up SF 1449 and read Block 10 before assuming the clause is live, walking through a 2013 set-aside under NAICS 311422 as the example [bg9NY9VUfsE @ 00:00:41]. PilieroMazza widens the net: the limit attaches to all set-asides, and the only carve-out is a small business set-aside below the simplified acquisition threshold [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:10:51]. The measuring stick has also changed. PilieroMazza traces the 2016 overhaul that moved the calculation from personnel cost to amount paid [qxE-37z0qfw @ 00:06:28], and the SDVOSB prime behind Vet GovCon Blueprint compresses the point into two words, dollars paid, rejecting hours or headcount as the yardstick [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:01:34]. Under that standard, the number that matters is what gets paid to each non-similarly-situated sub against the total paid on the contract. The baseline sequence the speakers share is to confirm set-aside status in the award document, identify the governing clause, which GovPrep pins to 52.219-14, and build the subcontract budget around dollars before any teaming agreement gets signed.

The percentage ladder is where the speakers line up most tightly. Mark Ryan lays it out cleanly: services at 50%, construction at 85%, specialty trades such as plumbing and electrical at 75%, and manufacturers at 50%, with resellers required to buy from a small domestic manufacturer [ne7XpEiLNKE @ 00:01:06]. PilieroMazza gives the identical ladder for services, manufacturing, general construction and specialty trade, adding that materials come out of the math where applicable [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:14:21]. The Vet GovCon Blueprint operator frames the same numbers as caps on pay to subs that are not similarly situated, excluding materials on supplies, general construction and special trades, and measures mixed contracts portion by portion [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:01:59]. Theodore Watson adds the warning that not every NAICS runs on a 51/49 split, flagging the 236 and 237 construction series at 15% prime and 85% subs, a figure he prefaces with 'I believe' [28K_7987r8w @ 00:01:19]. Watson also confirms that under a 51/49 split the prime's 51% can be a blend of its own work and similarly situated sub work. For an operator, the takeaway is simple: identify the contract type first, because the allowable subcontract share swings from half the dollars to 85% depending on whether the work is services, supply, general construction or a specialty trade.

The similarly situated entity is the consensus pressure valve. PilieroMazza defines it in three parts: a first-tier sub, holding the same status that qualified the prime, and small under the NAICS code assigned to the subcontract [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:07:08]. Theodore Watson hammers the last prong, saying most people miss that the size test runs on the subcontract NAICS the prime assigns, not the prime's own code [28K_7987r8w @ 00:04:26]. Status must match pairwise, as Watson lists it: small business to small business, 8(a) to 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB and WOSB. When it does, the SSE's dollars count as the prime's own performance, which Watson says actually helps the prime. The consensus limit on that valve is that it does not survive a second hop. The Vet GovCon Blueprint operator warns that work an SSE passes downstream counts against the prime [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:03:54], echoing Watson's rule that anyone other than prime employees or first-tier SSE employees counts against the prime. Enforcement has teeth on the agency side as well. Mark Ryan reports that contracting officers are now required to issue a negative CPARS when a prime misses the requirement [ne7XpEiLNKE @ 00:04:54]. PilieroMazza stacks suspension, debarment and fines on top, and flags DOJ investigations as a rising trend.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The arithmetic is where operators either stay clean or quietly drift out of compliance. PilieroMazza's worked example runs a $1M-per-month services contract carrying $100K in materials: strip the materials, take half of the remaining $900K, and the prime owes $450K of self-performance every month [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:32:49]. The same firm's earlier session clarifies what can be stripped on services work: G&A and overhead stay in the base, while materials that are separately line-itemed can be deducted, illustrated with a janitorial contract [qxE-37z0qfw @ 00:54:18]. Mark Ryan shows the SSE lever in dollars: on a $100K 8(a) IT contract, $20K subbed to another 8(a) still counts toward the prime's $50K [ne7XpEiLNKE @ 00:02:25]. The Vet GovCon Blueprint operator runs the failure case on the same $100K services contract, where at most $50K can flow to subs that are not similarly situated, so a $70K check to one such sub breaks the rule outright [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:02:38]. GovPrep adds that the services figure is calculated fully burdened with fee. The operating move is a monthly tracking sheet that subtracts eligible materials first, applies the cap to what remains, and then credits only first-tier SSE dollars back to the prime's side of the ledger.

Timing is the second mechanic, and the speakers agree the old back-loaded self-performance play is dead. Mark Ryan says he has watched firms sub 70% in year one, 40% in year two and 10% in year three, then self-perform in year four and get away with it; the updated rule measures compliance per contract year, and on IDIQs per task order, specifically to make audits easier [ne7XpEiLNKE @ 00:03:26]. PilieroMazza calls the belief that a firm can fall short early and make it up over the remaining option years the most common misconception about the rule, because compliance is measured per base or option period [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:38:18]. That pushes tracking cadence to the front of the playbook. PilieroMazza wants the numbers pulled monthly and no less than quarterly [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:43:56], and the Vet GovCon Blueprint operator names silent drift over time as one of three traps, prescribing the same fix of tracking dollars to each sub every month [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:04:20]. PilieroMazza's earlier session adds that on full-and-open vehicles, the measurement runs per order rather than over the whole vehicle. For a broker structuring a multi-year deal, the build is a running monthly ledger per period and per order, so the prime sees a breach coming before the period closes rather than after.

Paper is the third mechanic. PilieroMazza says a prime may rely on a sub's SAM representations or written representations as of the date of the subcontract offer, with the caveats that HUBZone status must be confirmed against SBA certification and WOSB remains self-certified for subcontracts [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:22:14]. The same firm wants a clause forcing prompt notice of any status change plus indemnification, and its earlier session lists the teaming agreement terms to lock down: NAICS assignment for the sub, size and status representations, and a limitations on subcontracting awareness clause [qxE-37z0qfw @ 00:30:19]. Theodore Watson goes further on the back end, suggesting primes make subs certify compliance with each invoice; he concedes no case law backs it, but says it may help against False Claims and pass-through scrutiny [28K_7987r8w @ 00:08:07]. On the front end, Watson's affiliation session advises spelling out in the proposal itself what percentage of the statement of work the prime will perform versus the sub or teaming partner [k1ZkyITmwWM @ 00:12:00]. PilieroMazza also points to other direct cost exclusions recognized through VA, GSA and DOE class deviations, including airline travel, environmental remediation, cloud computing and mass media, though the Vet GovCon Blueprint operator stresses such exclusions only apply as the solicitation allows.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over which NAICS code decides whether a sub counts as similarly situated. Natalie Rosario of GovCon Coach tells primes to prove the sub's matching status in SAM and says that registration sits 'under the NAICS code of the contract that was awarded' [G44VRBscagY @ 00:01:00]. Theodore Watson contradicts that directly: the sub must be small under the NAICS the prime assigns to the subcontract, not the prime's own code, and Watson warns that 'most people miss this' [28K_7987r8w @ 00:04:26]. PilieroMazza sides with Watson and adds that the subcontract NAICS may carry a higher size standard than the prime's [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:08:35]. The construction numbers fracture the same way. Watson puts general construction at 15% prime and 85% subs, hedged with 'I believe,' and PilieroMazza states 85% for general construction and 75% for specialty trades. Eric Coffie instead describes a '25% requirement' on construction, so that 75% can be subbed [8-4Lml2bZj4 @ 00:03:58], while GovPrep separately states specialty trade as '25.' An operator pricing a construction bid off the wrong speaker builds the self-performance budget on a misstatement, and GovPrep's own answer is to pull the clause, 52.219-14, and check Block 10 of the SF 1449 to confirm the set-aside before committing a number.

How a prime manufactures its self-performance share is the second fault line. Rosario's street play is to select the sub, then ask to 'lease one to two of your employees for the duration'; Rosario reports that hungry subs usually agree and gives those leased workers only what the contract requires, with no handbooks or perks [G44VRBscagY @ 00:02:31]. The counter-pressure comes from how other speakers define the prime's own labor. GovPrep insists the work be done with 'your w-2 employees,' not 1099s and not subcontractors, and PilieroMazza likewise treats independent contractors as subcontractors rather than employees [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:11:35]. Watson attacks the adjacent move of absorbing someone else's people: on affiliation, Watson tells primes to avoid hiring an incumbent's workforce en masse, especially its managers, and not to rely on a large sub to manage the contract [k1ZkyITmwWM @ 00:10:42]. Rosario and Watson do converge on management. Rosario says management and admin do not count toward the prime's 51% of labor, and Watson warns that mere supervision may itself violate the ostensible subcontractor rule because the prime must perform the primary and vital parts [28K_7987r8w @ 00:09:51]. Rosario pairs the leasing play with a hard warning: primes submit yearly prime-versus-sub labor hours and must keep them accurate.

The third split is whether the similarly situated structure is a loophole to exploit or a trap. PilieroMazza walks through a WOSB prime subbing 100% to a WOSB similarly situated entity and concedes 'it's not in violation of the statute or the regulation so sba recognizes the loophole and we'll see' [qxE-37z0qfw @ 00:15:05]; the firm adds that only the CO, the prime or SBA can protest a sub's size. Eric Coffie frames the same rule as help for small firms, describing December 2018 class deviation '0003' letting firms pool similarly situated entities across matching statuses such as HUBZone with HUBZone [8-4Lml2bZj4 @ 00:02:32]. Vet GovCon Blueprint, speaking as an SDVOSB prime, takes the opposite view of pass-through arrangements: 'That's not a loophole — that's the exact scheme this rule was written to stop' [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:04:42]. Watson lands on the enforcement side, warning that pass-throughs can bring criminal violations [28K_7987r8w @ 00:10:32]. The timing playbook has also flipped. Mark Ryan says firms used to sub 70%, 40% and 10% across years and then self-perform in year four 'and get away with that,' which Ryan says no longer works because compliance now runs per contract year and, on IDIQs, per task order. Where FAR and SBA texts conflict, NSBGCA's DCAA speaker Amanda Reed answers simply: follow the contract.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common failure is leakage below the first tier. PilieroMazza's worked example has a prime performing 40% and a similarly situated sub performing 20%, but that sub farms out 15%, leaving the prime credited with only 45% and non-compliant [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:19:17]. Mark Ryan describes the same mechanic from the other direction: on a $100K 8(a) IT contract, $20K subbed to another 8(a) still counts toward the $50K, but any further subbing is clawed back [ne7XpEiLNKE @ 00:03:07]. Watson's rule of thumb is blunt: work by anyone other than the prime's employees or first-tier similarly situated employees counts against the prime [28K_7987r8w @ 00:09:10]. Vet GovCon Blueprint names three traps from running an SDVOSB prime: pass-through arrangements, mixed status where the sub's status does not match the set-aside, and silent drift over time, summed up as 'Certifications lapse' [go1eoNNgFRM @ 00:04:20]. Their fix is mechanical, tracking dollars to each sub monthly, and PilieroMazza sets the floor at monthly tracking and no less than quarterly. Back-loading is dead as a recovery plan: PilieroMazza calls 'we'll make it up' over the remaining years the most common misconception, because compliance is measured per base or option period and shortfalls cannot be recovered later.

The economics check starts with the math, and the math is easy to get wrong. PilieroMazza's worked example of $1M per month with $100K in materials produces a $450K self-performance requirement [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:32:49], and Vet GovCon Blueprint shows that on a $100K services contract, paying $70K to one non-similarly-situated sub breaks the rule. GovPrep's own example illustrates the danger of trusting a speaker's arithmetic: the GovPrep speaker says a '$100,000 contract' requires '$500,000,' which does not reconcile with the stated 50% services rule [bg9NY9VUfsE @ 00:04:23]. Getting it wrong is expensive. Mark Ryan reports that COs are now required to give a negative CPARS for missing the requirement [ne7XpEiLNKE @ 00:05:33], PilieroMazza lists suspension, debarment and fines and reports DOJ investigations rising, and Amanda Reed of DCAA, speaking at NSBGCA, adds withheld payments, treble damages under the False Claims Act, termination for default, and suspension or debarment [ka1Q_JhjM8w @ 00:19:40]. Watson's hedge against False Claims and pass-through scrutiny is to have subs certify compliance with each invoice, though Watson concedes no case law supports it. Federal-funds enforcement reach shows in Court Files' report of 47 ghost-student cases across 27 of the FBI's 55 field offices, though Scamverge stresses these are charges, not convictions.

Status and paperwork traps sink primes who trust the wrong document. PilieroMazza says primes may rely on a sub's SAM or written representations as of the subcontract offer date, but HUBZone status requires confirming SBA certification and SDVOSB self-certification ended in January 2023 [i9prjadeAyM @ 00:24:39]; the firm's defensive clause requires prompt notice of status changes plus indemnification. On affiliation, Watson flags 70% or more of revenue from one company in the prior year as economic dependence and treats an incumbent sub as a red flag under the ostensible subcontractor rule, while stressing 'there is no bright line rule every case is fact specific' and that 'affiliation is a rebuttable presumption' [k1ZkyITmwWM @ 00:04:50]; Watson's firm won Argus & Black after OHA held that mechanically applying economic dependence to a startup was wrong. Flow-downs are the third trap. Devine Hewitt warns 'Most primes flow everything down,' including clauses that do not apply to the sub, and tells subs to reject blanket comply-with-everything language [yp5LO51vPi8 @ 00:18:25]. Chase K. of Capital Edge adds that naming a sub during negotiations does not satisfy 52.244-2, and that consent must be written consent from the CO because verbal consent does not count [36eRQkU2X_I @ 00:13:16].
