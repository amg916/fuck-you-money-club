## Executive Summary

Practitioners from boosters to former contracting officers agree on one floor: whoever wins is the prime, carries full performance liability, and must add real management value; a 100% pass-through on set-asides is the line that draws jail, fines, and restitution. Kizzy Parks teaches the street workflow: filter SAM.gov for small business set-asides, pull three vendor quotes before writing, add a 20% to 100% markup, and price a bid Parks rates at 50-55% win odds for a couple hours of monthly oversight. The fight is over viability: Derrick Green “Theo” calls paper primes fraud, GovClose says middlemen write 20 to 50 proposals per win, and GovCon TechLab won four contracts but floated $30,000 when an agency paid months late.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: The Prime Owns Everything

Across camps that otherwise fight, the floor is identical: whoever wins the award is the prime, and the prime eats every failure. Squared Compass states it flatly: “The Government holds the prime fully responsible for performance, even if subcontractors do most of the work” [IXmVYXIEfdQ @ 00:01:04]. GovClose strips the label off entirely, arguing “there's no such thing as a middleman you just put in a proposal you win it you're a prime contractor” [GSss1UvHnQg @ 00:01:46]. Derrick Green “Theo,” a former federal contracting officer at DHS, DoD, and VA, goes further: “the federal government never holds the subcontractor responsible” [h72zKiJRBe4 @ 00:14:07]. Even Kizzy Parks, the loudest advocate for the model, opens a VA dumpster-removal walkthrough by killing the find-and-flip fantasy: “that's not how it works you still have to provide added value” [kejmg4Gzuuc @ 00:00:53]. The working consequence is that the operator is not a broker collecting a finder's fee. The operator is a manager carrying contractual liability for the sub's no-shows, missed deadlines, sloppy quality, and damaged property, and the agency will come to the prime, not the sub, when something breaks. Every legitimate playbook starts from that premise, and every fraud warning starts from operators who pretended otherwise and tried to hide behind the subcontractor.

The second point of agreement is where the legal line sits: a 100% pass-through is the thing that gets operators hurt. GovCon with Humam calls full pass-through flatly illegal and lists jail, fines, restitution, and returning contract funds as the exposure, then lays out minimum self-performance on set-asides: 50% of personnel costs on services, 50% of manufacturing costs on supply, 15% on general construction, and 25% on specialty construction [NRMYxAAOPYo @ 00:01:41]. Squared Compass sorts the field into four models (Integrator Prime, VAR/Distributor, Pass-through Prime, and Set-aside Front) and flags the last two as the danger zones, because “the Government does not like toll booths,” then offers a three-question gut check: what value do we add, where is profit applied, and are we doing the primary and vital work [IXmVYXIEfdQ @ 00:08:42]. Dr. Wes Fisher lands in the same place from the clause side, pointing to 52.215-23, which allows pass-through charges when the prime adds value by managing sources, coordinating deliveries, and running quality assurance [7NhHddZW0kE @ 00:05:27]. Even Kizzy Parks concedes “there is not a 50% rule for every Federal contract” and that “it all depends” [386MNSeLv9E @ 00:42:08], which means reading every clause before pricing a single line item.

The baseline kit is equally consistent. Kizzy Parks lists a CAGE code, a physical address, and an EIN as table stakes [2GThNGqMOJQ @ 00:23:36], and in the Fort Devens catering cut names vendor, capital, and time as the three real prerequisites [9DngtysNXpA @ 00:06:35]. Capital is the one beginners underestimate. Stephanie of GovCon TechLab, who holds DBE, MBE, and WOSB certifications and has written more than 100 proposals, won four contracts with the model and still floated $30,000 out of pocket when a net-30 agency paid months late; the pre-bid test that came out of it is whether the operator can float sub payroll for 60 to 90 days on a line of credit or cash reserve, summed up as “Revenue is not cash flow” [uYFWvLCSDgA @ 00:07:27]. GovClose pushes the same warning harder: a new firm will “probably” win nothing for six to 18 months, and “it could be a year before you start getting paid” once an award lands, while the prime still owes the sub [GSss1UvHnQg @ 00:02:11]. The consensus operator therefore walks in with registration done, at least one vetted vendor, enough cash or credit to carry payroll through a slow first invoice cycle, and the time to manage the job personally rather than handing it off.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Kizzy Parks runs the hunt inside SAM.gov Contract Opportunities, using Advanced Search filtered to “small business total and partial” set-asides [kejmg4Gzuuc @ 00:00:17]. The demo target is a VA dumpster-removal set-aside with more than ten days to respond, a lane where Waste Management is locked out because it is not small. The first read is the subcontracting language: wording such as “own operate and or lease facilities” signals the work can be subbed. Before spending effort, Parks pulls the incumbent's contract amount and number through the POC, USA Spending, FPDS.gov, ChatGPT, and SAM.gov, because the classic loser bids $120,000 on a requirement that paid $40,000 the year before [2GThNGqMOJQ @ 00:02:28]. Timing matters too: Parks says agencies buy about 30% of their budget in fiscal Q4, July through September. The clause check comes next. Steve, a former federal contracting attorney appearing on Parks's channel, says small business set-asides under the simplified acquisition threshold, now $350,000 for most acquisitions, are exempt from limitations on subcontracting, while 8(a), HUBZone, and SDVOSB set-asides are not [R-2hdJLvKUM @ 00:03:22]. Dr. Wes Fisher's shortcut is to look at whether the 52.219-14 box is actually checked; if it is not, the limit does not apply [7NhHddZW0kE @ 00:02:18].

Pricing starts with vendors, not with the proposal. “before even opening a Word document you are going to need three price quotes,” Parks instructs, and those quotes should come from large firms such as WM and Republic rather than small competitors who would simply bid the job themselves; the request to each vendor covers a technical description, warranty terms, federal discounts, and three references with name, phone or email, task, and dates [kejmg4Gzuuc @ 00:12:46]. Markup is layered on top, and Parks says it “could be 20% could be 50% you could add 100%,” while warning that landing 50% above competitors is hard to justify and recommending three pricing options in the submission. The $0-$100k guide pushes the quote count to as many as ten, splits the difference, and has the operator ask each sub what profit it already baked in, because a $5,000 quote carrying a $2,000 markup loses [2GThNGqMOJQ @ 00:09:25]. On the VA dumpster bid, Parks pegs win odds at 50 to 55% and sizes the economics at $500 a month in the worst case, up to a $2,000-a-month example, for what “might only be a couple hours a month” of oversight [iOVkNIsRYV4 @ 00:06:23]. Squared Compass frames where that margin should live: “Healthy primes earn profit on prime work” [IXmVYXIEfdQ @ 00:08:57].

Assembly is clerical but unforgiving. Parks fills SF 1449 block 17A with CAGE/UEI, name, address, and phone plus blocks 30A, 30B, and 30C; if the form arrives already digitally signed, the move is to screenshot it, export to PDF, and edit in Adobe Pro rather than handwrite anything [kejmg4Gzuuc @ 00:02:33]. Reps and certs come straight from the SAM workspace, the narrative runs in Times New Roman 12-point, the offer is stated good for 90 days, and the package says “we accept and will adhere.” The money side is where the attorney input matters most. Steve tells primes to write their own subcontract terms because large subs push one-sided paper, to use pay-when-paid language, to watch for subs demanding 75% of the work against a 50% limit, to line up backup subs, and to include both termination for default and termination for convenience [R-2hdJLvKUM @ 00:10:37]. Cure notices, which give at least ten days to fix a problem, can be flowed down and show the government the prime is managing. J. White goes further on the service agreement: “That's not optional,” with a clause stopping sub work if the prime is not paid [LWdgQS6TyHg @ 00:05:27]. Parks closes the loop by confirming the payment route, such as the PIEE system, before kickoff [2GThNGqMOJQ @ 00:26:19].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether the model is a business or a crime. Derrick Green (Theo), a former contracting officer at DHS, DoD, and VA, calls the paper prime procurement fraud: find a contract, hire a subcontractor, do no real management, add a fee, then lie about it. The line is blunt: lying about who did the work is not marketing [h72zKiJRBe4 @ 00:08:39]. Kizzy Parks answers from the other corner, telling viewers that middleman is alive and middleman is legal, and that the illegal label is what people say to keep you out of it [9DngtysNXpA @ 00:05:57]. Squared Compass refuses to treat middleman as one thing, splitting it into Integrator Prime, VAR/Distributor, Pass-through Prime, and Set-aside Front, then tagging the pass-through prime as paying for air and the front with SBA affiliation, False Claims Act, and DOJ exposure [IXmVYXIEfdQ @ 00:01:24]. That channel insists middleman is not a dirty word, provided the prime can say what value it adds, where profit is applied, and whether it performs the primary and vital work. Even Parks, the loudest defender, opens the dumpster-removal walkthrough by insisting you still have to provide added value [kejmg4Gzuuc @ 00:00:53]. The camps fight over labels; they converge on management.

The numbers themselves are contested. GovCon with Humam lays out limitations on subcontracting for set-asides over $150,000: services primes perform 50% of personnel costs, supply primes 50% of manufacturing costs, general construction 15%, specialty construction 25%, and the simplified acquisition threshold sits around $250,000 [NRMYxAAOPYo @ 00:04:41]. Steve, the former federal contracting attorney on Kizzy Parks' channel, puts the threshold at $350,000 for most acquisitions and says plain small business set-asides under it, not 8(a), HUBZone, or SDVOSB, escape the limitations entirely [R-2hdJLvKUM @ 00:20:50]. Dr. Wes Fisher, reviewing a student who cited 50/75/85 limits after roughly 10 losing bids, tells operators to stop guessing and look at the clause checkbox: if 52.219-14 is not checked, the limit does not apply. Fisher also argues administrative work can count toward the 50%, while admitting a contracting officer may disagree [7NhHddZW0kE @ 00:02:18]. Parks lands in the same place from the field, saying there is not a 50% rule for every federal or state contract and that it all depends, though some opportunities do restrict subbing [386MNSeLv9E @ 00:42:08]. The playbook difference is whether you price off a remembered rule or off the clauses in the actual solicitation. Steve's top advice backs that up: look up every clause incorporated by reference, because these little clauses can come back and bite you.

Beyond the legality fight, practitioners pitch rival ways to make money off the same contracts. Michelle Brown at Fed Biz Exchange rejects the consultant-connector setup where a small janitorial firm becomes prime, because the consultant has no control over the money and may never get paid; the recommended move is to be the prime, manage the contracting officers, subs, and paperwork, and learn to price floors and rooms [kgPkC78OKzs @ 00:07:16]. Brown's third lane, selling products through DLA and DIBBS, is pitched to people keeping a day job. GovClose goes further and calls the whole strategy a waste: a consulting and advisory business gets paid faster, a sale can close in a week, and the preferred government path is SDVOSB certification plus construction subcontracting aimed at the VA, which that channel says places more contracts with SDVOSBs than any other agency, over 90% in some codes [GSss1UvHnQg @ 00:02:58]. Theo's favorite legal structure is the joint venture, alongside disclosed teaming and a prime integrator who manages subs, controls the schedule, runs compliance audits, and carries risk [h72zKiJRBe4 @ 00:25:38]. J. White teaches getting the contract and subbing it out but never called it middleman and never teaches spending money up front [LWdgQS6TyHg @ 00:02:40]. White adds that saying no first, when you cannot deliver at a high level, is business 101.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Cash, not compliance, is what actually burned the practitioners who tried it. Stephanie of GovCon TechLab won four contracts with the strategy and still floated $30,000 out of pocket. The contract said net-30, but office turnover moved invoicing away from the WAT portal, points of contact stopped replying, and payment came months late while the sub kept expecting net-30, until work was finally stopped [uYFWvLCSDgA @ 00:04:52]. The pre-bid test that came out of it: can you float sub payroll for 60 to 90 days, and do you hold a line of credit or cash reserve, because revenue is not cash flow [uYFWvLCSDgA @ 00:07:27]. GovClose puts the timeline even worse for newcomers, with award taking about six months and the first payment possibly arriving a year out, while the winner still owes subs before the government pays [GSss1UvHnQg @ 00:02:11]. Kizzy Parks' fix is negotiated up front: have a conversation with the vendor before you even submit to confirm it is okay to pay them after you have been paid, which is where sales and negotiation skills pay off [386MNSeLv9E @ 00:34:38]. J. White, reacting to Stephanie's loss, calls a service agreement with a stop-work clause mandatory, not optional.

The second trap is assuming the sub carries the risk. Theo states it flatly: the federal government never holds the subcontractor responsible [h72zKiJRBe4 @ 00:14:07]. In the scenario Theo walks through, the government lets a 15-man construction crew work for 90 days, catches the pass-through around day 70 to 80, and pays nothing; every invoice is a legal statement and site visitors may be federal officials building a case [h72zKiJRBe4 @ 00:09:46]. Steve, the former contracting attorney, frames the defense: write your own subcontract instead of signing a large sub's one-sided terms, use pay-when-paid language, watch for subs demanding 75% against a 50% limit, line up backup subs, include both termination for default and termination for convenience, and flow down cure notices that give at least 10 days to fix problems [R-2hdJLvKUM @ 00:12:08]. Stephanie learned the enforcement side the hard way: if a sub does not show up for day one, the prime is responsible, and suing a small sub is not going to be worth it [uYFWvLCSDgA @ 00:10:11]. That is why the backup sub has to be lined up at the same price you bid, because that is what the government was priced for. In this business, Stephanie says, you are not really selling labor; you are managing risk.

The third failure is bidding economics that never close, even when the paperwork is clean. Kizzy Parks' warning example is a bidder who submitted $120,000 without knowing the agency paid only $40,000 the year before, which is why the incumbent's amount gets pulled from the point of contact, USA Spending, or FPDS before pricing [2GThNGqMOJQ @ 00:02:28]. Parks adds that a $5,000 sub quote plus a $2,000 markup loses, and that while markups of 20, 50, or 100% are on the table, landing 50% above competitors is hard to justify. Performance math bites too: on the VA dumpster bid, the third missed pickup triggers a 2% deduction [kejmg4Gzuuc @ 00:11:31]. GovClose reports that middlemen privately admit writing 20 to 50 proposals to win one award, and that new firms likely win nothing for 6 to 18 months, if they win anything at all [GSss1UvHnQg @ 00:00:13]. Stephanie names the slow bleed underneath: scattered past performance reads as having no specialty, a sandwich-making bid meant starting from scratch, and every contract won outside your specialty means starting over from zero [uYFWvLCSDgA @ 00:18:19]. The counterexample came from focus, when a six-month DOT contract grew past two years. The pace is unsustainable, Stephanie concludes, because it takes many bids for each win.
