# Chapter 08: Teaming, Subcontracting and Prime–Sub Relationships

*Prime vs. sub paths for beginners, finding primes (SAIC) and handling ghosting subs, teaming agreements vs. subcontracts vs. JVs, negotiating from the sub's side, the financial trade-offs between prime and sub, and VA OSDBU and PCI teaming guidance*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Sixteen GovCon practitioners converge on one rule: the prime is whoever the contracting officer signs with, it carries full liability, and subs only get paid what is locked in writing. Operators report teaming lifts win probability when it fills a named gap, while handshake deals end in ghosting, six-month removals and a $20,000 loss. Street mechanics include pulling price history from FPDS, mining SAM.gov contract awards (2,000 help-desk hits filtered to 87 woman-owned targets), setting 51-52% prime workshare, enforcing the 50% services limitation on subcontracting, and funding roughly 14% of contract value upfront as prime versus 20% as sub. Practitioners split on whether rushing to team helps or damages small firms, how much exclusivity matters, and whether written teaming agreements are actually enforceable in court.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same hard line: the prime is the company the contracting officer signed with, and that is where all liability sits. ASI Education explains that the prime holds privity of contract, the government has no privity with subs, and FAR Part 44 bars the agency from dictating which subs a prime uses; even a multi-company team still has one accountable 'belly button' [jYYGaPkanFU @ 00:01:41]. Shane, the legal voice on the VA OSDBU panel, puts it bluntly: the prime is 100% responsible to the government, including when a sub goes bankrupt or trips Service Contract Act problems [iLuva0z2cMA @ 00:57:00]. Federal Contracts Lab frames prime and sub as responsibility levels rather than status levels. The prime signs, manages, invoices and eats the performance risk, while subs plug in as HVAC, janitorial, pest control, help desk or cyber specialists [3eoJWm0WffU @ 00:03:19]. Neil McDonnell attacks the assumption that 'prime' means 'large': a small service-disabled veteran-owned business won a $175M prime with Booz Allen and Boeing working underneath it, and an aircraft carrier runs on roughly a thousand subs [zqg5q_pZvdc @ 00:07:38]. The operating takeaway is simple: whoever signs owns the risk, and everyone else at the table is negotiating for a protected slice.

The second point of near-total agreement: if it is not written, it does not exist. Stephanie of GovCon TechLab opens with the classic burn, a handshake teaming deal where the sub does 80% of the work for 20% of the revenue or gets ghosted after the partner wins, and dismisses a one-page letter of intent as a 'hope document,' because after award the prime holds all the leverage [JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:04:26]. Dr. Mel lists exactly what happens without a real teaming agreement: lost control of IP, exclusion from the final deal, ownership disputes and proposal hours that never pay back, then tells operators to stop running on 'handshakes and hope' [HKtAZzSL_GQ @ 00:01:23]. The VA OSDBU panel warns that any prime offering 49% of a project but refusing to put it in writing deserves extreme caution [iLuva0z2cMA @ 00:26:33]. Centurion Research Solutions sharpens the drafting standard: the work percentage should name a specific percentage or number of staff, because agreements that promise 'best efforts' or 'goals' rarely pan out [L-BpgTTiESM @ 00:10:18]. Across all four practitioners, the rule is identical: lock precise numbers on paper before the proposal ships, never vague percentages, verbal assurances or friendly intentions.

Practitioners also agree that subcontracting is a legitimate entry and growth lane, not a consolation prize. Humam Finding Sam points out that the FAR mandates primes subcontract set percentages to small businesses, so prime compliance goals create built-in demand, and a firm with zero government past performance can get its foot in the door this way, adding that primes need subs as much as subs need primes [T_HZxPdh7B8 @ 00:00:25]. Federal Contracts Lab states flatly that subcontracting is not a downgrade, pitching a hybrid of subcontracting plus selective small primes in the same service and buyer type, and telling subs to document every engagement for references [3eoJWm0WffU @ 00:08:57]. Akiesha Foster, who won as key personnel in a tier-two sub slot, says teammates are typically considered six to twelve months before the request for proposal is released, so operators should stop waiting until the last minute and start building relationships now [qnkHiXcQCHM @ 00:05:18]. ELINT Capital's panel adds the money logic: subcontracting beats getting nothing, but the single most important move is picking the prime carefully and keeping a good relationship with it [EpUc9OVjb4g @ 00:18:07]. The shared sequence across all four is sub first, collect documented proof, then prime selectively when ready.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The prospecting mechanics are concrete. Neil McDonnell's SAM.gov workflow: log in, go to Search, then Contracting, then Contract Awards, and search the exact phrase 'help desk' for about 2,000 results; filter to woman-owned small businesses, certified or not, to get 149; download and strip duplicates by awardee UEI, which removed 62 and left 87 companies to approach [zqg5q_pZvdc @ 00:20:35]. He favors firms with $20 million and above in awards and says the point is future relationships, not asking for a cut of existing contracts. Akiesha Foster Googles the top 100 federal contractors, builds a spreadsheet, uses usaspending.gov to pick a target like SAIC, then maps its small business program, specialist contact, supplier portal and teaming process [qnkHiXcQCHM @ 00:00:45]. Humam Finding Sam runs the channel's own SAM Search tool with subcontracting-source filters on names like Lockheed Martin or Deloitte, using a 'high' confidence filter to surface FAR-required subcontracts, plus plain-English queries and weekly saved-search alerts [T_HZxPdh7B8 @ 00:03:53]. Kizzy Parks works the other direction as prime: call vendors the moment an opportunity posts, before they are exhausted by the 107th caller, and find who won similar work in the same location, then ask who they subbed to, such as a gravel supplier [F6Hwh89ornQ @ 00:08:58].

Workshare math is where deals live or die. On the VA OSDBU panel, Shane lays out the limitation on subcontracting: services 50%, general construction 15% with 85% to subs, specialty trade 25% with 75% to subs, measured separately for each base and option period with no averaging; on a $1M woman-owned set-aside with $100k of airfare excluded, the prime must perform $450k of the remaining $900k, and a similarly situated sub's $200k stacks with the prime's $300k to reach $500k [iLuva0z2cMA @ 01:10:26]. Jackie Lopez on the same panel sets a 51-52% floor whenever socioeconomics are involved. Theodore Watson says the prime generally performs at least 51% of contract cost, less depending on the industry code, and that 31% of companies using teaming agreements or JVs are not familiar with the landmines competitors use in bid protests [NP_UB6qiZhw @ 00:00:31]. Stephanie at GovCon TechLab turns this into a filter: a prime promising 60% of the work on a services set-aside is offering a deal that is illegal on its face [JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:12:50]. The Centurion Research Solutions session confirms small-business primes usually retain 51% of revenue [L-BpgTTiESM @ 00:04:04]. The operator rule: run the dollar math per period before signing anything, because the limitation can cost the award itself.

The money mechanics are just as specific. ELINT Capital's worked example: a $2M-per-year services task order on a set-aside, with the prime doing 55%, burns about $80k a month and waits 70-80 days for payment; with a roughly $175k payroll reserve, total upfront hits $275-300k, about 14% of contract value, while a sub holding a $400k slice needs about $80k upfront, about 20% [EpUc9OVjb4g @ 00:13:32]. The same panel says subs now push pay-when-paid clauses capped at about 60 days, because an improper invoice restarts the 30-day clock, and should fund 2-2.5 months of payroll. On pricing, Kizzy Parks tells primes to ask the agency what it paid before and to check fpds.gov, warning that taking a sub quote and simply tacking a profit percentage on top is not going to happen [F6Hwh89ornQ @ 00:04:37]. Jackie Lopez builds price to win from FPDS or a Googled contract number, adjusted for changes, noting most bidders come in 5-10% under, and insists price to win and price to execute must be one and the same [iLuva0z2cMA @ 01:26:27]. ASI Education describes the squeeze in sole-source negotiations: either the prime tells the contracting officer the sub cannot go any cheaper, or everyone cuts about 10% [jYYGaPkanFU @ 00:07:17].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the target list
   - Mine SAM.gov Contract Awards for your exact service phrase, filter by socioeconomic status, and deduplicate by UEI.
   - Search the exact phrase, filter to the set-aside category, download, remove duplicates by awardee UEI, and prioritize firms with $20 million and above in awards [Neil McDonnell — zqg5q_pZvdc @ 00:24:05].
2. Name the gap and assess the partner
   - Answer the four Assess questions before any outreach or signature.
   - Confirm what gap teaming fixes, reject duplicate capabilities like two WOSB cyber firms, check the workshare math, and verify the partner pays through references, factoring companies and protests [GovCon TechLab — JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:11:24].
3. Paper the deal before submission
   - Sign the NDA first, then the teaming agreement before the proposal goes in, using attorney-drafted templates.
   - Sign an NDA before disclosing relationships and sign the teaming agreement before submission, with a 51-52% prime floor when socioeconomics are involved [VA OSDBU (On Demand) — iLuva0z2cMA @ 00:43:38].
4. Track the relationship post-award
   - Run a fixed cadence of check-ins and invoice reviews and put every rate change in writing.
   - Hold weekly 15-minute check-ins, monthly invoice reviews and quarterly relationship reviews; on a FEMA option period, reduced rates were passed through to the sub in writing [GovCon TechLab — JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:19:53].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Kizzy Parks breaks hardest from the teaming chorus. She says SBA, SCORE and similar outfits push the line that “government loves teams,” and she calls rushing to team “one of the most damaging” practices; her rule is to define the goal first, whether that is higher win odds, fatter margins, or a new client type, and then ask why a stronger party needs you at all [ol1KkwjDTqg @ 00:00:45]. Jackie Lopez reads the same market the opposite way, crediting teaming principles with having “significantly increased our Revenue” and pinning 50–60% of her growth on a reputation as a good teaming partner [iLuva0z2cMA @ 00:14:52]. Stephanie of GovCon TechLab lands between them with a one-line filter: “If you can't name the specific gap that you're trying to fill, then you don't really need to team,” and two WOSB cyber firms pairing up is a “hard pass” [JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:12:00]. Mike Lore adds the relationship trap, warning operators to “avoid teaming just because it's somebody you already know” and to “team to win,” setting selection criteria before picking anyone [L-BpgTTiESM @ 00:08:57]. The practical split: Lopez teams early and often to lift P-win, while Parks teams only when her chips are on the table.

The sequencing fight is just as sharp. Kizzy Parks teaches middleman priming from day one: you win as prime, others deliver the service or product, and she closes by saying you need no past performance and no product to play [F6Hwh89ornQ @ 00:11:55]. Akiesha Foster runs the opposite clock, holding Level 3 certification and 12+ years of experience yet planning about 10 years as a tier-two sub before priming, because “start off as a subcontractor and then that will position you better so you can become a prime” [qnkHiXcQCHM @ 00:07:08]. Neil McDonnell rejects the ladder framing altogether, arguing subcontracting is not just for small or new firms and pushing three revenue streams in parallel: subbing under a small, priming, and subbing under a large, with all three in play once traction passes $3M [zqg5q_pZvdc @ 00:11:06]. Federal Contracts Lab treats prime and sub as responsibility levels rather than status, insists “Subcontracting is not a downgrade,” and prescribes a hybrid of subcontracting plus selective small primes in the same service and buyer type, summed up as “It's about sequence” [3eoJWm0WffU @ 00:14:25]. Foster's timing warning compounds the choice: teammates are typically picked six to 12 months before the RFP, so “don't wait to the last minute.”

Paperwork depth splits the field. Parks tells middleman primes no exclusive agreement or NDA is needed with vendors, only “some type of written paper trail of pricing,” even by text or WhatsApp [F6Hwh89ornQ @ 00:07:00]. Stephanie demands the full seven-clause stack, including a mutual NDA, proposal-cost terms, termination if not awarded, a workshare floor where “no less than 30%” beats “approximately,” and mutual exclusivity per opportunity only, never blanket [JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:14:55]. David Gallagher of Kutak Rock tempers both camps on enforceability: “The agreement to agree interpretation of a teaming agreement is by far the most common,” the enforceable EG&G v. Cube outcome “is rare,” and “Exclusivity is not critical, but it helps” [NB57wtT4xfs @ 00:48:53]. Karen Long, negotiating from the sub's seat, wants exclusivity at the task-order level rather than the IDIQ level and no unilateral right for the prime to drop you, warning that “if you're going to be exclusive you should understand what you are giving up” [W-f5dCQEEkE @ 00:09:32]. Gallagher's own baseline is blunt: “Never sign just a standard teaming agreement,” because no standard federal or DOD form exists, so you start from a template and tailor it.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest losses came from deals that lived on trust. Jackie Lopez was pushed off a five-year contract within six months by a greed-driven prime, and her lawsuit recovered only part of the first year's profit; and her warning is blunt: “if someone tells you that they're going to give you 49 of a project but they're not willing to put it in writing I would be very careful about that” [iLuva0z2cMA @ 00:25:31]. Kizzy Parks took a man's promise of a monthly “bonus” for access to her existing client, collected 1 of 12 payments, had no signed agreement, and ate roughly $20,000 [ol1KkwjDTqg @ 00:06:22]. Mike Lore watched his team get “awarded eight of these 11 but my company only received actual work on two,” and he warns: “avoid agreements that promise best efforts or goals these rarely pan out,” calling a poor teaming agreement “a shotgun wedding” [L-BpgTTiESM @ 00:12:41]. Stephanie's warning covers the same ground: a one-page letter of intent is a “hope document,” after award the prime holds all the leverage, and a handshake deal is how you end up “doing 80% of the work and getting only 20% of the revenue” [JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:03:34].

ELINT Capital says prime-or-sub is often dictated by capital, credit line and cash position. Its worked example on a $2M-per-year set-aside services task order has the prime doing 55% at roughly $80k a month, waiting 70–80 days for payment, holding a payroll reserve near $175k, and fronting $275–300k, about 14% of contract value, while a sub with a $400k slice needs about $80k up front, about 20% [EpUc9OVjb4g @ 00:13:32]. The same panel flags subs pushing pay-when-paid clauses capped near 60 days, improper invoices restarting the 30-day clock, DOGE freezes holding payments 3–4 months, and near-annual shutdowns; its advice is to fund 2–2.5 months of payroll, and “it's better to subcontract than to get nothing” [EpUc9OVjb4g @ 00:18:29]. Jackie Lopez prices the bid side of the burn: a 30k proposal cost across 10 proposals is 300k spent with nothing won [iLuva0z2cMA @ 00:14:31]. ASI Education lived the LPTA version: as a sub it refused to drop lower and lost, then watched the winner post low DC salaries on LinkedIn; it calls LPTA “a race to the bottom” where lowballers try to get “made well” through modifications [jYYGaPkanFU @ 00:23:25]. Kizzy Parks adds the margin trap: taking a vendor quote and simply bolting on a profit percentage, “it's not going to happen” [F6Hwh89ornQ @ 00:04:57].

Compliance landmines turn wins into forfeits. Theodore Watson says 31% of firms using teaming agreements or JVs are “not quite familiar” with the landmines competitors use to get an award revoked in a bid protest, starting with limitations on subcontracting, where the prime generally performs at least 51% of contract cost, and affiliation when a partner “exercises too much control over the contract” [NP_UB6qiZhw @ 00:00:14]. Stephanie makes it concrete: “if a prime is promising you 60% of the work on a service contract set-aside, that deal is illegal on its face” [JYbjE63rG-Y @ 00:12:50]. VA OSDBU's Shane adds that limitation-on-subcontracting compliance is measured per base and option period with no averaging, and the prime stays 100% responsible to the government, including for sub bankruptcy and Service Contract Act issues [iLuva0z2cMA @ 01:03:40]. On partner vetting, Mike Lore pulls a D&B report on small primes, cites one prime that was declared financially unacceptable, vets the prime's proposal capability, asks the client's view of partners, verifies citations, and makes contingent hires [L-BpgTTiESM @ 00:11:13]. His exit rule for stalling partners: “in the case of reluctant suitors the sooner you walk away the better.”
