# Chapter 12: Funding the Contract and Getting Paid

*Credit cards, invoice factoring and PO financing, FAR 32 payment triggers, WAWF/PIEE invoices, combo and receiving reports, resolving DLA payment issues, the Prompt Payment Act, and SBA/EIDL loan risks, collections, franchise directory checks and loan scams*

## Executive Summary

The practitioners agree that winning an award does not put cash in the bank. A contractor typically works about 30 days, invoices, and then waits roughly another 30, so someone has to cover that gap before the first dollar arrives. They teach four ways to fund it. Invoice factoring costs 3% to 5%, and one named factor charges 2.5%. Purchase-order terms and PO financing are the second. Credit cards cover small product buys. Success-fee mobilization lenders fund against a signed contract or Notice to Proceed. Getting paid on DoD and DLA work runs through WAWF inside PIEE. The wrong document type or a mismatched DoDAAC stalls payment and forfeits prompt-payment interest. A missing final-shipment Z draws DLA corrective action. The operators differ on which tool fits which job. Debra Smith matches factoring to services and credit cards to products. Business Cash Now warns that low-bid margins of 5% to 7% leave nothing to pay for any financing.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

An award looks like money, but it is not money yet. GovSuccess opens with the warning that a strong backlog can sit beside a weak bank balance. It treats a purchase-order total, a funded amount, a financing authorization, and cash collected as four different facts. None of them is payroll money until the clauses and payment instructions say so [jTTWqhTfPMM @ 00:00:36]. Mobilization Capital describes the same squeeze from the field. After leaving the military, the founder talked with working contractors and found their recurring problem was cash flow. A contractor works about 30 days before earning the right to invoice, then waits another 30 days to get paid [oOQSsJO94kM @ 00:00:19]. Debra Smith calls winning only half the battle, because you still have to deliver and prove you can pull it off [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:00:30]. She puts the typical government wait at 30 days, sometimes 60 or 90, and says most small shops at this stage do not have the capital to carry it [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:02:56]. The shared baseline is to model the gap between award, performance, a proper invoice, and collected cash before committing labor or suppliers. Pick the funding tool before the purchase order arrives, not after.

Three speakers point to the same set of tools: outside money that relies on the government's credit rather than yours. Debra Smith names factoring, credit cards, and purchase-order terms as the three easiest options. A factor buys your invoice, pays you up front, and keeps a cut, so you do not have to wait on the agency [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:02:12]. She adds that the factor checks the agency's credit, not yours, and the government has no credit issues, whether the agency is the National Park Service or DoD [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:04:57]. Chris Curtin of Business Cash Now says his firm has done PO financing and receivables funding on government work for 20 years. He claims 99% of U.S. government entities are creditworthy and that every contract has a section allowing it to be assigned for financing purposes [4V1zq5q3VuQ @ 00:00:08] [4V1zq5q3VuQ @ 00:01:50]. Mobilization Capital advances startup capital against a signed contract or Notice to Proceed. It is repaid when the government pays the contractor and charges a success fee based on contract or invoice value [oOQSsJO94kM @ 00:00:49]. One Mobilization client said they visited six banks, one bank forgot they were there, and they cancelled their remaining bank appointments after one meeting [oOQSsJO94kM @ 00:01:06]. In every case, the agency's contract, not the founder's own balance sheet, is the collateral that unlocks working capital.

On collections, the speakers agree on one point: the payment clock starts with a proper invoice, not with your email. GovSuccess notes that FAR 32.904 generally ties the due date to a proper invoice plus acceptance. It warns against assuming payment a fixed number of days after you hit send [jTTWqhTfPMM @ 00:01:13]. Josh, a warranted contracting officer who runs Acquisition Pro Tips, says the prompt payment clause requires payment within 30 days of a proper invoice. If the government is late, DFAS adds interest automatically at a Treasury rate he quoted at 4.6%. If the invoice has missing or wrong details, no interest is owed [WXCymE9NjBg @ 00:07:17]. Elda of Don't Know Much About Construction puts federal construction payment at roughly 14 to 30 days after a proper invoice. She describes interest penalties for late payment, monthly progress payments based on percent complete, and a duty for the prime to pay subs promptly once the prime is paid [uUf-xLjfPf8 @ 00:01:11]. Tristan Thomas of MeND Sourcing adds the mindset: no one cares about your business as much as you do. Never assume the government will pay; check every invoice yourself [yXqp5I7wQK0 @ 00:01:01]. The shared requirements are an accurate invoice the first time and constant follow-up.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The fee then sits inside your profit margin, and it is as if it never existed. **SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the payment-trigger worksheet
   - Map every expected receipt to its clause, CLIN, trigger, and evidence before spending.
   - For each receipt, record the clause and line item, the trigger, the supporting evidence, the submission route, the acceptance owner, the forecast date, and the actual receipt date. Put the matching supplier and payroll payments next to each one [Win Government Contracts with GovSuccess — jTTWqhTfPMM @ 00:03:06]
2. Lock in funding before the PO arrives
   - Pick a factor, a PO financer, or a mobilization lender, and price its fee into the bid.
   - Add the 3% to 5% factoring fee to the contract price so it comes out of the bid rather than your profit [Debra Smith — NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:04:37]. Line up a mobilization lender that funds against a signed contract or Notice to Proceed [Mobilization Capital — oOQSsJO94kM @ 00:00:49]
3. Prep the vendor quick kit and submit in WAWF
   - Gather the contract, modifications, codes, quantities, and PDFs, then build and preview the document.
   - Have the contract and modifications, CAGE code, contract and order numbers, your internal invoice number, quantities, units, prices, and supporting PDFs ready before you log in. Having these ready heads off 90% of the headaches [annica at Bill Bright — WDekT_xRXC0 @ 00:01:18]. Preview the commercial invoice and receiving report and check them line by line against the contract before you submit [Acquisition Pro Tips — cdachjXPX3k @ 00:10:35]
4. Run a Friday My Invoice check and escalate
   - Check every open invoice weekly and act immediately on any see reason and remark codes status.
   - Every Friday, check whether each invoice is being researched, scheduled, or paid. For any hold, have shipped quantities, tracking numbers, proofs of delivery, and photos ready, then call the DLA service desk for quantity issues and DFAS for payment issues [Tris10 @ MeND — yXqp5I7wQK0 @ 00:03:10]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Debra Smith runs a three-tool playbook and ranks factoring first: a factor buys the invoice, fronts the cash, and should charge 3% to 5%, with anything over 5% flagged as a bad factor and RTS Financial quoted at 2.5% [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:02:36]. Smith's pricing move is to load that 3% to 5% straight into the bid so the fee rides inside the profit margin, and Smith pegs factoring for contracts in the 500k to 5 million range. Chris Curtin of Business Cash Now, twenty years into purchase order and receivables funding for government work, attacks the thin-bid habit from the other side: bidders who come in at 5% to 7% gross margin to get a foot in the door lose the next round when they try to climb to 20%, 30% or 40%, because there is only one thing worse than no sales and that is selling at a loss [4V1zq5q3VuQ @ 00:00:53]. Mobilization Capital skips invoice purchase entirely, funding startup capital the moment a signed contract or Notice to Proceed exists and charging a success fee based on contract or invoice value, recovered when the government pays [oOQSsJO94kM @ 00:00:49]. One client visited six banks, got forgotten at one of them, and cancelled every remaining bank appointment after meeting Samir [oOQSsJO94kM @ 00:01:20].

How fast the money lands depends on who is talking. Debra Smith says it is typically 30 days but plans for 30, 60 or 90, and during their own logistics run a 90-day customer forced them to factor loads so carriers got paid right away [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:23:03]. Josh at Acquisition Pro Tips puts the prompt payment clock at 30 days from a proper invoice, with DFAS adding interest automatically at a Treasury rate quoted at 4.6%, but only once the invoice is proper [WXCymE9NjBg @ 00:07:17]. Elda at Don't Know Much About Construction quotes 14 to 30 days on federal construction, progress payments monthly on percent complete, and argues the Anti-Deficiency Act means funding is allocated before award so nobody argues about money [uUf-xLjfPf8 @ 00:01:11]. Elda adds that once the prime gets paid, subcontractors and suppliers must be paid promptly too. GovSuccess pushes back on any fixed-days promise: do not assume every invoice pays a set number of days after you email it; identify who receives it, what makes it proper, and what evidence proves acceptance [jTTWqhTfPMM @ 00:01:32]. An invoice is a request, not proof that acceptance occurred.

On legacy SBA debt the two lawyers split on the line that matters most. Keck Legal puts the personal-guarantee trigger on COVID EIDL loans at amounts above $200,000 [x1-UtiYGpVc @ 00:03:41], while Joshua Sternberg puts it at $150,000 and says a borrower with no guarantee who closes the business does not have to worry, unless the money was misused [IMjYXOH8UoU @ 00:01:43]. Both say read the paperwork rather than guess. Keck's ladder runs hardship accommodation and deferral first, then an offer in compromise lump sum, then a Chapter 11 plan that a federal judge approves so the SBA only has to not object, with the rest of the debt wiped out. Sternberg's red flag is spending: a quarter million personally guaranteed and dumped into Robinhood for Bitcoin is a problem, while payroll, advertising, inventory and R&D are clean. On paid help the split is sharper. CO- panelists say the borrower cannot be charged a packaging fee, the bank pays any packager, and the SBDC helps at no cost [C6VmvdA7JT4 @ 00:00:54], while Ryder at Skip sells VIP and platinum funding coaching and pushes a Bluevine line of credit up to 250k at rates as low as 4.8% [FZ6VO4mNzF4 @ 00:10:12].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Debra Smith's sharpest war story is a supplier that advertised purchase-order terms, then refused to quote unless it got the agency name and point of contact. Smith read it as a setup: hand over those two facts and the sub finds the contract, bids direct and pushes the broker out, so the answer was that the information is confidential under company terms and conditions until the purchase order issues, by which point it is too late for anyone to bid [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:12:42]. The rule Smith teaches is blunt: if a sub asks for agency and contact, run for the hills, and never claim to work for the county or agency [NusotbSsCa4 @ 00:15:24]. GovSuccess walks through a test-unit supplier whose forecast booked a payment at its own design review while the contract's financing event required a different documented accomplishment; the fix was rebuilding the forecast around the real trigger, modeling the cash gap and a second scenario with administrative delay [jTTWqhTfPMM @ 00:02:07]. It also warns against counting financing twice as revenue or cash, tracking what was submitted, approved, withheld, liquidated and collected. Business Cash Now adds that buyers may demand proof of financial capability, answered with a lender letter showing a half-million or million-dollar facility [4V1zq5q3VuQ @ 00:01:12].

Most lost weeks trace to keystrokes. Josh's top-ten rejection list runs contract data mismatch, wrong DoDAAC, missing attachments, wrong invoice type, quantity and price errors, slow acceptance, wrong pay office, one-digit typos, missing final-invoice marks and bad timing, boiled down to measure twice, invoice once [WXCymE9NjBg @ 00:08:47]. The costliest error in the combo tutorial is typing the admin DoDAAC into ship-to: the invoice lands in the resource advisor's inbox instead of the acceptor's, nobody knows it exists, and because the vendor caused it, no interest accrues [cdachjXPX3k @ 00:15:08]. The Proposal Pro Coach admits a rushed read would have grabbed combo on a services contract because the clause headline said invoice and receiving report, until the full line showed services take an Invoice 2-in-1; that coach was also told that marking final invoice yes by mistake takes about a month to get the contract re-opened [EesWMxgpXqk @ 00:12:10]. Tristan Thomas of MeND Sourcing has seen companies draw DLA corrective action reports for leaving the Z off a final shipment number, and writes the exact piece count plus tracking number into Mark For and Comments so the receiving clerk does not short-count 3,414 pieces as 3,400 [weLOISkZc5k @ 00:11:42].

When money stalls, MeND Sourcing treats any 'See reason and remark code' status in My Invoice as a problem it owns: one hold was $1,899, another was large enough that the due date would pass unpaid, and on a CLIN of 3,409 pieces the company was paid for only 1,409 [yXqp5I7wQK0 @ 00:12:18]. The fix was a prepared file of quantity per exterior container, FedEx tracking numbers, proof-of-delivery PDFs and photos, then a call to the DLA service desk at 877-352-2255 for quantity, which opened a case and found two web SDRs from a labeling issue, then DFAS at 800-756-4571 for the accounting side. On the SBA side, The Money Rules runs the franchise math: on a $400,000 project, 10% down leaves a $360,000 loan at a 10% cap, about $4,757 a month, while a $350,000 loan can price to 11.5%, costing $163 more monthly and about $29,600 more interest over ten years [rlj1s_mZ7uo @ 00:05:29]. Skip's Ryder flags SBA's EIDL data migration, where the agency leaned on contractor row counts to reconcile nearly $400 billion in loans, while the inspector general has already found $84.3 billion of potentially fraudulent COVID EIDL loans [FZ6VO4mNzF4 @ 00:08:08].
