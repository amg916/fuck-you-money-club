## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The hardest consensus in this chapter is about who can spend the government's money. GovSuccess opens FAR Part 1 with a customer asking for extra work and says the first question is authority, not enthusiasm: contracting officers bind the government only within delegated authority, while a COR works under a written designation and cannot change price, quantity, delivery or terms [x7xmTiXnwQ4 @ 00:00:17]. Tim Sullivan of PCI, who logged two years as a contracting officer and decades as a contractor lawyer, hammers the same line in plain terms: CORs have no contracting authority, so loop every scope question back to the CO, in writing [Gisvrg813kY @ 01:12:28]. Christopher Williams, a KO for 17 years, puts teeth on it from the construction side: a COR carries no warrant and cannot alter the scope of work [pktnZU4AWIE @ 00:04:31]. Sky and Michelle, former Air Force procurement litigators, add why this bites so often: constructive changes frequently happen orally in meetings, and the changes clause expects the contractor to notify the CO, typically within 30 days, though they tell operators to read the actual clause [h1yszOmFw3w @ 00:14:08]. GovSuccess adds that job titles, relationships and urgency never create authority, and that ratification is a conditional process, not a dependable business plan.

The second point of agreement: the clauses inside your own contract are what you actually live under, and they carry real legal weight. Sullivan reports that courts hold the FAR has the force and effect of law, and that a FAR violation that hurt a protester means the solicitation is probably going down [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:13:10]. David Gallagher of Kutak Rock, with 20-plus years in the field, maps the stack: the FAR sits in 48 CFR Chapter 1, and agency supplements such as DFARS and HSAR usually add to it and occasionally displace it, with data rights and country of origin as the examples [UV6zJLRHfHw @ 00:09:38]. FedAccess frames the operator version bluntly: the FAR runs about 2,000 pages, so don't read it, read your clauses, because invoicing, progress payments and fast payment are set by your contract rather than by generic FAR searching [T_cRpZF2IUo @ 00:00:00]. ASI Education lands in the same place from a study angle, noting Part 52 is the only part addressed to the contractor and the longest, and recommending one clause a day [MTy_zJd0RsY @ 00:02:41]. Gallagher counts more than 600 clauses and splits them into instructional, certification, prohibition and requirement buckets, with requirement-clause noncompliance meaning breach or default.

The third consensus covers the FAR overhaul: buying is getting faster, but the text you read online may not be the text your agency implemented. Sullivan traces the April executive order, Restoring Common Sense to Federal Procurement, which kept only content required by law or essential to sound procurement, and reports about 25% of the FAR moved to companion guides that cannot support protests [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:18:42]. Derrick Green, an ex-CO at DoD, DHS, FEMA, VA and Houston, tells vendors to have pricing, staffing, insurance, references and an operational story ready because awards may now arrive in days, not months [k1EsNHPfqXY @ 00:09:13]. GovSuccess runs the counterweight across multiple episodes: an overhaul model is not automatically the text your agency implemented [z7tDxORX_pQ @ 00:01:42], and codified and overhaul section numbers differ, so the operator has to verify which version actually applies [LgX6N2c9-S4 @ 00:00:33]. Fed Gov Today's guest Matthew Rose of Snowflake describes the culture shift behind it: more commercial technology buying, Part 10 market research, and agencies paying for outcomes rather than level of effort. Green's line on the tempo is that faster does not mean reckless, it just means smarter.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The clause-reading workflow starts with a keyword hunt. Gallagher's tactic is to Ctrl-F the solicitation for '52.2' to surface every FAR clause, then repeat for the supplement prefixes: DFARS clauses start with 252, GSA with 552 and VA with 852 [UV6zJLRHfHw @ 00:29:28]. Clauses show up in full text or by reference in Sections H, I and K, and the date matters: 52.243-1 dated April 1984 versus August 1987 is unchanged after 37 years, but Buy American versions from February 2009 and October 2022 differ materially. An undated clause most likely means the version in effect at signing, and Gallagher calls the idea of a self-refreshing clause wrong, just as there is no such thing as a self-deleting flow-down [UV6zJLRHfHw @ 00:38:30]. FedAccess's shortcut for the unfamiliar ones is to Google the clause together with its date [T_cRpZF2IUo @ 00:03:04]. Sullivan adds a tracing step: every clause says 'as prescribed by' a section, so trace each clause back to its prescription, and use the 52.3 matrix, which codes clauses R, A or O [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:57:25]. On fill-ins, Gallagher's rule is short: blank means blank, and the government cannot fill in a 52.244-2 blank six months later.

Post-award money runs on hard clocks. Sky and Michelle's numbers: notify the CO of a change typically within 30 days, recover the cost of the change plus reasonable profit, and remember commercial contracts allow no constructive change [h1yszOmFw3w @ 00:14:08]. Williams ties the same 30-day notice to the changes clause and warns that late claims are scraping the pan, because costs still have to be allowable, allocable and reasonable [pktnZU4AWIE @ 00:05:12]. Sullivan's rule while a dispute runs is to keep performing as directed and file a claim in parallel; the example is quoting $2M while the agency offers $1M [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:39:41]. The KW partners then stack the claim math: claims over $100,000 must be certified and start the disputes process, the limit is six years, CDA interest runs on claims, and REA preparation costs are recoverable but REAs earn no CDA interest [h1yszOmFw3w @ 01:01:54]. Their other clocks include a limitation-of-cost notice within 60 days of reaching 75%, a termination-for-convenience settlement proposal within one year with an inventory schedule inside 120 days, a 10-day cure notice for some default grounds, and 90 days to the board or a year to the Court of Federal Claims to appeal a default.

On the front end, Neybor Consulting lays out the Part 12 economics: more than 60% of federal buys use commercial authority, FAR 2.0 raises the threshold to $7.5M or $15M in emergencies, and a Part 12 award takes about 45 days versus 5-plus months under Part 15 [TFl20T0yyig @ 00:02:10]. The timeline Neybor teaches is a synopsis of about 15 days, evaluation in 1 to 4 weeks and award in 30 to 60 days, with the contractor signing block 30 and the CO signing block 31; firm-fixed-price is the default, so price accordingly, and cost-type is prohibited [TFl20T0yyig @ 00:15:50]. FedAccess flags that SCA and Davis-Bacon wage determinations drive pricing and that 52.219-14 limitations on subcontracting matters for middleman strategies. GovSuccess pushes delivery math: compare FOB origin and destination quotes on a total-delivered-cost basis covering freight, packaging, appointments and unloading, because the lowest unit price may not produce the lowest supported delivery cost [QvsXldnQyAg @ 00:02:31]. Registration hygiene closes the loop. Federal Processing Registry says to update a CAGE record within 30 days of an address, ownership or structure change; DLA review takes 3 to 7 business days, and past 10 days you call the DLA Customer Interaction Center [ifMU9O8Ydmg @ 00:01:23].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the authority sheet
   - List the contracting officer, the designated technical representative, your authorized company signer and your internal escalation owner, and keep the award, modifications and delegations accessible.
   - Drill staff on where to route scope requests so the team responds promptly without making commitments based on assumptions [Win Government Contracts with GovSuccess — x7xmTiXnwQ4 @ 00:01:24].
2. Map every clause and its date
   - Ctrl-F '52.2' and the supplement prefixes, note full-text versus by-reference clauses in Sections H, I and K, and record each clause date.
   - Treat an undated clause as the version at signing, never assume self-refresh or self-deleting, and remember blank means blank [Public Contracting Institute (PCI) — UV6zJLRHfHw @ 00:29:28].
3. Route and document every change
   - When a COR or field rep directs work outside scope, stop and get the KO's confirmation before performing.
   - Send the 30-day changes-clause notice and keep costs allowable, allocable and reasonable, because late claims are scraping the pan [Contract Warrior — pktnZU4AWIE @ 00:06:55].
4. Split protest files from claim files
   - Open a protest file (action, interest, forum, filing rules) separate from a claim file (contractual basis, relief, certification).
   - Never import protest deadlines into a claim or confuse the submission period with the appeal period, and keep evidence of delivery and receipt [Win Government Contracts with GovSuccess — -smvR-8_v1M @ 00:00:55].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is how deep to go into the regulation itself. FedAccess says the FAR runs 2,000 pages and flatly tells operators not to read it. Instead, read the clauses in your own solicitation, then Google each clause with its date [T_cRpZF2IUo @ 00:00:00]. ASI Education runs the opposite drill: memorize all 53 parts with the "Colt 45" mnemonic, read Part 52 one clause a day, and master Parts 15 and 13 [MTy_zJd0RsY @ 00:01:47]. ASI also anchors its read on 1.102(d): "if it's not prohibited it's okay." Even the clause numbering gets read two ways. FedAccess parses 52.219-14 as subpart 2, section 219, and flags it as the limitations-on-subcontracting clause that matters for middleman strategies [T_cRpZF2IUo @ 00:02:44]. David Gallagher of Kutak Rock reads the digits after 52.2 as the FAR part that prescribes the clause, and says the "numerology nomenclature" alone tells you where a clause lives and why [UV6zJLRHfHw @ 00:30:26]. Gallagher adds that supplements carry their own prefixes: DFARS 252, GSA 552 and VA 852. The page count splits too, with ASI Education saying 1,000 plus pages and FedAccess saying 2,000. Pick one reading system, but trace every clause back to its prescription and date before you price.

The FAR overhaul splits the room between operators sprinting for speed and operators slowing down to verify the text. Derrick Green "Theo," an ex-CO at DoD, DHS, FEMA, VA and Houston, says to stop wasting six months pretending 200 vendors exist. Have pricing, staffing, insurance, references and an operational story ready, because you may get "days, not months" [k1EsNHPfqXY @ 00:09:13]. Neybor Consulting pushes the same tempo through Part 12. More than 60 percent of federal buys use commercial authority, FAR 2.0 raises the threshold to $7.5M or $15M in emergencies, and a Part 12 award lands in about 45 days versus 5+ months under Part 15 [TFl20T0yyig @ 00:02:10]. GovSuccess runs the counter-playbook. Its Part 34 breakdown warns that an overhaul model is not automatically the text your agency implemented. Its Part 47 breakdown adds that FAC 2026-01, effective March 13, 2026, still has to be checked against deviations [z7tDxORX_pQ @ 00:01:42]. Fed Gov Today guest Matthew Rose of Snowflake reports a pricing split as well: buyers are moving from paying for level of effort to paying for outcomes, yet utility-based pricing is still "not the norm" [MM4DJrI2Od0 @ 00:02:16]. Neybor's hedge is blunt: expect FFP and price accordingly.

When a fight starts, practitioners split on which lever to pull. Tim Sullivan says a FAR violation that impacted the protester means the solicitation is "probably going down." Yet in one sole-source dispute, Sullivan skipped the protest and wrote to the competition advocate instead [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:23:38]. Sullivan also warns that the overhaul moved about 25 percent of the FAR into companion guides, and that material cannot support protests. On performance disputes, Sullivan's playbook is to keep performing as directed and file a claim in parallel, using the case where you quote $2M and the agency offers $1M [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:39:41]. Sky and Michelle, former Air Force procurement litigators, agree that the disputes clause demands continued performance. They add that a breach exists only for a cardinal change, so get counsel before stopping, and that commercial contracts allow no constructive change at all [h1yszOmFw3w @ 00:12:48]. GovSuccess refuses to blend the two paths. A protest file tracks action, interest, forum and filing rules. A claim file tracks contractual basis, relief and certification. A clarification request, debriefing, REA and claim are not interchangeable [-smvR-8_v1M @ 00:00:55]. Gallagher adds a subcontract wrinkle: the majority view keeps the Christian doctrine off subcontracts, and Chevron changes likely limit it further.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap reported firsthand is working off a COR's say-so. Christopher Williams of Contract Warrior, a KO for 17 years, walks through a construction job where the COR ordered ductwork offset 6 feet north. Three weeks later, the contractor filed an REA of about $38k [pktnZU4AWIE @ 00:04:13]. The COR had no warrant and could not alter the scope of work. The 30-day notice under the changes clause was blown, and a late claim at that point is "scraping the pan." Williams adds that costs still have to be allowable, allocable and reasonable, and that the COR could end up paying [pktnZU4AWIE @ 00:06:37]. Tim Sullivan hits the same wall from the lawyer side: a COR has no contracting authority, so loop scope questions back to the CO, in writing [Gisvrg813kY @ 01:12:28]. GovSuccess closes the escape hatch contractors bank on. Ratification is a separate process with conditions, and it is not a dependable business plan for work started without proper authorization. Job titles, relationships and urgency do not establish authority [x7xmTiXnwQ4 @ 00:00:50]. Sky and Michelle note that constructive changes often happen orally in meetings, which is exactly where unwritten direction gets lost.

The money leaks come from pricing assumptions, not just authority. FedAccess reports a firm that lost money on Service Contract Act paid vacation. The channel also warns that the government can terminate for convenience and has done so shortly after award [T_cRpZF2IUo @ 00:05:54]. Tim Sullivan, who says there is no three-offeror rule, describes a client rated in the '50s that spent "150 grand" chasing an award; the case settled. Agencies can also award on initial proposals, so holding back for best-and-finals is a gamble [Gisvrg813kY @ 00:30:24]. Sullivan's other losses are procedural. A bid that took exception to the default clause was rejected as nonresponsive. Late is late, unless an electronic submission sent before 5:00 the prior day was delayed by the government's fault. GovSuccess flags a hidden cost in schedules: a sensor program had a June test slot with no priced retest, and money spent is not the same thing as completed technical work [z7tDxORX_pQ @ 00:02:18]. On supply deals, the same channel warns that the lowest unit price may not produce the lowest supported delivery cost. Compare FOB origin and destination quotes on freight, packaging, appointments and unloading [QvsXldnQyAg @ 00:02:49].

The quietest failures are missed clocks and dirty records. Sky and Michelle lay out the deadlines that sink contractors: settlement proposals within 1 year of a termination for convenience, and inventory schedules within 120 days. A default can be appealed within 90 days to the board or within a year to the Court of Federal Claims, and default records damage you in SAM [h1yszOmFw3w @ 00:27:49]. Claims over $100,000 must be certified, and the claim window runs 6 years. REA preparation costs are recoverable, but REAs earn no CDA interest [h1yszOmFw3w @ 01:03:30]. GovSuccess warns operators not to import protest deadlines into a claim, and not to confuse the submission period with the appeal period. On registration, Federal Processing Registry says to update your CAGE code within 30 days of an address, ownership or structure change. DLA review takes 3 to 7 business days, and past 10 days you call the DLA Customer Interaction Center. "Entity not found" means you need to update IRS data [ifMU9O8Ydmg @ 00:01:23]. David Gallagher adds a drafting trap: if the 52.244-2 blank is left empty, the government cannot fill it in 6 months later, because "blank means blank" [UV6zJLRHfHw @ 00:44:39]. Unmarked data submissions, per Sky and Michelle, put your rights at risk.
