## Executive Summary

Ricky Howard's client won a nine-figure contract as a subcontractor. James Walk won $42K of VA cooling-tower parts. David Packouz bid just under $300M on munitions. The practitioners behind these wins describe the same engine: find a live solicitation, phone subs for quotes, and price in the middle. A contracting officer told Kizzy Parks that the winning band is 7-25%. Walk won at 15% without being the lowest bidder. Packouz ran a 10-20% margin and came in $52M under the next bidder. Dr. Wes Fisher compresses execution into five days: list 15-20 opportunities, get a quote within 48 hours, then submit price, past performance and technical approach. Derek James says bids 10-20 are 'in the money.' The practitioners split on SAM versus USASpending, on whether certifications matter, on prime versus sub cash flow, and on how hard to mark up.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Ricky Howard opens with the headline proof: a GovClose client won a nine-figure contract as a subcontractor, not a prime [z6ZNrmprd-o @ 00:00:00]. Howard frames the trade-off bluntly. The prime keeps control but owns the proposal, the accounting and the job of getting paid. The sub sheds that load and still builds past performance. Big firms switch between both roles, and if you lack a vehicle like GSA or 'NASA soup,' you partner with the holder as prime [z6ZNrmprd-o @ 00:00:37]. Kizzy Parks calls the same play the middleman model. In a Denver snow-removal example, the work gets subcontracted out, but the prime stays on the hook: 'You are responsible for that work' [qVThWm83wus @ 00:01:22]. Dr. Wes Fisher builds the middleman directly into the submission. When the government asks for past performance, it can be yours or your sub's [xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:03:57]. Sheenapreneur started as a prime on SDVOSB and WOSB status to get money 'straight from the agency.' Sheenapreneur now holds several primes and a couple of subs, 'as long as the money makes sense' [MB8IFp5mwWc @ 00:02:18]. None of them argues for prime or sub as a matter of principle. The shared rule is to take whichever seat gets you the award and the cash, then switch seats when the numbers change.

The setup checklist is nearly identical across speakers. Kizzy Parks lists the minimum: 'You need a physical address, a tax ID, a cage code.' No PO box, virtual address or registered agent will do, and no prior experience is required, so keep your job if you want [qVThWm83wus @ 00:03:47]. In the 2024 beginner build-out, Parks adds several steps. Form a legal entity, and file a DBA when the name is too niche ('Brenda's braiding' becomes BB Consulting). Open a business bank account with ACH. Accept cards through QuickBooks or a merchant account; smaller banks are better, and card runs ranged from about $3,000 to $30,000 [WR8VA1o3VMU @ 00:05:02]. CAGE registration takes one to two hours of data entry: address, tax ID, email (Gmail works), phone and banking. The UEI comes first, and the CAGE code arrives in days to weeks [WR8VA1o3VMU @ 00:06:59]. Federal Contracts Lab pushes the card point harder for micro purchases, which are often under $15,000. The purchase card is the preferred payment method under FAR 13.201, so the goal is to be easy to buy from: accept cards, respond fast and keep the purchase simple [NUKVGhkCHbg @ 00:02:02]. Robert Harvey III shows this working. The Colorado Air Force Base deal has a $7.5M ceiling across nine departments and pays by credit card through a bank merchant account [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:13:08].

The winners describe a numbers game played against surprisingly thin fields. Derek James lays out the learning curve. Bids one through five will be bad, six through ten improve, and ten through twenty are 'in the money.' Over a year the target is fifteen to twenty service bids, and two or three a week is 'insane' [OKLXh8NcJxQ @ 00:18:46]. James also reports an $8M award that drew only two bidders [OKLXh8NcJxQ @ 00:31:22]. Robert Harvey III's first contract was fire-retardant coveralls and lab coats for a California Space Force base, worth about $850 a month, and Harvey was the sole bidder [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:10:23]. Harvey has since put in roughly 90 bids since January, about a third of them presolicitations [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:24:10]. James Walk got a layoff notice on January 29 and started the company on February 7. Walk works SAM every evening around a corporate day job and has bid 30 to 40 times [AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:09:36]. Kizzy Parks sets the minimum at five responses a month and calls anything less 'business faking' [ITZqqfI1Eig @ 00:12:55]. Parks also notes the SBA's average of over a year to a first win [ITZqqfI1Eig @ 00:11:11]. Volume is the shared cost of learning, and every one of these operators treats an evening-and-weekend schedule as normal rather than a handicap.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Every workflow starts in SAM with hard filters. Kizzy Parks's 2026 search goes to Search, then Contracting, and selects Solicitation plus Combined Solicitation, due next month, with a total small business set-aside. That search returned 1,683 live results, and Parks steers beginners toward non-controversial 'entrepreneurial' work [qVThWm83wus @ 00:03:09]. James Walk scans every solicitation opened in the last 24 hours, about 1,000 a day this quarter, and filters for VA and fitness. Walk keeps 12 federal tabs, 5 Texas tabs and 1 MAS tab open at once [AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:02:30]. Dr. Wes Fisher's five-day plan spends day one searching Contract Opportunities with both active and inactive boxes checked, which shows 5.1M results. From there the operator lists 15 to 20 opportunities and works only one to three of them [xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:02:17]. Ricky Howard targets earlier signals: a sources sought, RFI or acquisition forecast that matches 50 to 80 percent of what you do. Howard then sends the office clarifying questions, so you are already in communication with the buyer before you call any teaming partner [z6ZNrmprd-o @ 00:03:02]. The four methods differ in detail, but each runs the same daily discipline. Scan wide, filter narrow, and commit to only a handful of targets you can quote quickly.

Once a target is picked, the work happens on the phone. Fisher's script asks subs exactly two things: 'can you do the job how much you're going to cost that's it.' Fisher holds that 'there's no reason why you shouldn't be able to get a quote in 48 hours.' Days two and three are for calls, and day four is for watching the inbox [xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:02:55]. Parks wants three to five quotes, preferably local, but warns against assuming geography, since a New Mexico firm may serve nationally [kpISY6VCkNY @ 00:01:39]. James Walk's VA Dallas cooling-tower win shows what that digging buys. Only one Texas distributor carried the parts, and the distributor said Walk was the fifth caller. Walk proposed two to three weeks against a 100-day requirement and attached the distributor's 'authorized customer' letter. Walk also caught part-number errors (O versus 0) and delivered in under ten days [AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:04:48]. For teaming, Howard contacts about five firms with past performance at the target agency. The pitch is that you are already talking with the office and can do part of the work while they do the rest. Howard expects three of five to say yes, finds that calling beats email, and never sends the link or exact opportunity, only generalities [z6ZNrmprd-o @ 00:04:59].

The pricing numbers cluster tightly. A contracting officer told Parks that newcomers bid too low or too high. The middle is 7 to 25 percent, and on gravel the sweet spot was 10 to 13 percent [etvo2gL7_Cs @ 00:02:23]. Walk won the cooling-tower job at a 15 percent markup on about $42K of parts, for 'three phone calls and 10 emails,' and was not the lowest bidder [AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:08:02]. Robert Harvey III's incumbent rule: if the incumbent charged $2, bid around $2.25, not $7 or $8 [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:08:20]. A contracting officer also told Harvey to add 5 to 10 percent per year for inflation on five-year contracts [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:07:27]. Parks's Tammy's Toilets math shows how margin stacks down the chain. The prime is paid $100, pays Tammy $50 and keeps 50 percent. Tammy spends $5 on equipment and $10 on labor and keeps $35, and the cleaners keep $5 of their $10 [kpISY6VCkNY @ 00:04:14]. Invoice monthly and expect payment in 15 to 30 days typically, or 30 to 60 on average. Speed it up by emailing your POC with the invoice attached [kpISY6VCkNY @ 00:03:16]. Walk matched terms on both ends, with the distributor on net 30 and the government on net 30, and only bid what Walk could cover [AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:11:31].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the Target List
   - Spend day one listing 15-20 SAM opportunities, then narrow to the 1-3 you will actually work.
   - Fisher's day-one rule is 'shoot for like 15 or 20 on your list' and then work one to three of them [Dr. Wes Fisher — xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:02:17]. Walk feeds the same list by scanning every solicitation opened in the last 24 hours [Kizzy Parks — AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:02:30].
2. Phone for Quotes
   - Call subs on days two and three with the two-question script and collect three to five quotes.
   - Ask only whether the sub can do the job and what it will cost, and expect a quote within 48 hours [Dr. Wes Fisher — xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:02:43]. Collect three to five quotes, preferably local, without assuming geography [Kizzy Parks — kpISY6VCkNY @ 00:01:39].
3. Price in the Middle Band
   - Mark up 7-25% over the sub quote and check the result against the incumbent's price.
   - A contracting officer put the winning middle at 7-25%, with 10-13% on gravel [Kizzy Parks — etvo2gL7_Cs @ 00:02:23]. If the incumbent charged $2, bid around $2.25 rather than $7-8 [Kizzy Parks — YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:08:20].
4. Submit and Debrief
   - On day five, submit price, past performance and technical approach, then request a debrief.
   - 'Nine times out of 10' the government wants price, past performance (yours or your sub's) and a technical approach [Dr. Wes Fisher — xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:04:12]. Request a debrief within 3 days of notice; the usual finding is that the price was too high [Kizzy Parks — etvo2gL7_Cs @ 00:04:42].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is where to sit in the money chain. Sheenapreneur started as a prime on SDVOSB and WOSB status to get paid “straight from the agency,” learned that primes carry payroll before the government pays, and now runs several primes and a couple of subs “as long as the money makes sense”; under $10K, primes usually pay subs upfront while the prime itself waits 30 days [MB8IFp5mwWc @ 00:03:48]. Ricky Howard of GovClose frames the sub seat as relief: the prime owns the proposal, the accounting and getting paid, while the sub builds past performance, and a GovClose client won a nine-figure contract as a subcontractor [z6ZNrmprd-o @ 00:00:37]. Kizzy Parks runs the margin math from the prime seat instead: a prime paid $100 hands Tammy's Toilets $50 and keeps 50%, Tammy spends $5 on equipment and $10 on labor and keeps $35, and the cleaners keep $5 of their $10 and sub out the rest [kpISY6VCkNY @ 00:04:14]. Derek James draws the hard boundary on that stack: limitations on subcontracting apply, and an “illegal pass-through scheme” is fraud [OKLXh8NcJxQ @ 00:03:35]. The operator read: prime when margin and float allow, sub when the vehicle, cash or past performance is missing.

Certifications split the room. Diamond Hunter Enterprises credits the 8(a) and WOSB certification received on January 12, 2017 with the first multimillion-dollar contract, and names 8(a), WOSB, SDVOSB and HUBZone as the doors to set-asides, mentorship and joint ventures [hdZFRp2VG8E @ 00:00:41]. Kizzy Parks says flatly that certifications are not required and builds winners on volume and search discipline instead [ITZqqfI1Eig @ 00:03:38]. The same tension runs through targeting data. Federal Contracts Lab mines USASpending for FY24–26 awards at exactly $14,999 under NAICS 3341, surfacing DoD and Army buyers, but warns that past data is a pattern, not current demand [NUKVGhkCHbg @ 00:11:19]. Parks goes further and rejects USASpending as a predictor, pointing to the infrastructure bill and FAA air-traffic staffing as proof that past purchases do not forecast future ones, and works SAM advanced search on sources sought plus pre-solicitations, roughly 24,000 to 26,000 items, filtered by agency or location down to a Hawaii table rental [ITZqqfI1Eig @ 00:04:11]. Parks also tells operators to respond even after a sources-sought deadline, ask about procurement strategy, and request a capability brief within a couple of weeks. The operator choice: certify when the set-aside pool fits your lane, but source live demand from SAM before trusting historical spend.

Bid tempo and outreach style are the third fault line. Derek James sets the curve at 15 to 20 service bids over a year, calls two or three a week “insane,” and expects bids one through five to be bad, six through ten to improve, and ten through twenty to land “in the money” [OKLXh8NcJxQ @ 00:18:46]. Robert Harvey III ran the opposite tempo: after stalling at one bid a week against a five-a-day goal, Harvey logged about 90 bids since January, roughly a third of them presolicitations, and now pushes two to three a week while Kizzy Parks presses for 12 to 24 contracts [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:24:10]. Dr. Wes Fisher compresses a single cycle into five days: list 15 to 20 opportunities on day one, work one to three, get a sub quote inside 48 hours, and submit price, past performance and technical by day five [xH6KWT7x8Ac @ 00:02:17]. On contact, Harvey called one contracting officer 17 times despite Fisher's advice to email, then later kept a buyer warm by emailing a capability statement every two weeks. Pricing bands diverge as well: Parks relays a contracting officer's 7–25% middle with a 10–13% gravel sweet spot, yet allows doubling on upfront-cash, weird or overseas bids [etvo2gL7_Cs @ 00:02:23].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Mispricing is the most expensive firsthand failure. Robert Harvey III took a $15K plumbing quote for a Tennessee prison, stacked $20K on top, and watched the contracting officer reject “$335,000 for a two-day job”; Harvey quit for six months, and on a later uniform bid priced shirts at $5 and pants at $7 against rentals costing about 98 cents, landing at $2.2M while Dr. Karen bid $250K over five years [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:05:16]. James Walk, 30 to 40 bids in, admits the old reflex was to add $50K and “hit home runs”; the cooling-tower win instead came at a 15% markup on about $42K of parts, under an LPTA award where Walk was not even the lowest bidder [AHQVro9N9HM @ 00:09:21]. Kizzy Parks warns that a 4X tech markup will not be bought, that housekeeping and lawn vendors already bake in 10–30% so adding another 20–50% overbids the job, and that debriefs usually come back with price too high [etvo2gL7_Cs @ 00:04:42]. The trap cuts both ways: Harvey's Colorado contracting officer asked for $1 more per item because the prior vendor priced too low and ghosted [YHptbPFEaPQ @ 00:16:37]. Harvey's own closing warning: don't underbid yourself just to get an award, because it will cost you more than anything.

Winning is not the bottleneck; floating the contract is. Kizzy Parks says winning is easy and the hard part is funding and execution, citing a $155,000 outlay to clear $10,000 in profit [WR8VA1o3VMU @ 00:12:00]. David Packouz hit the same wall at scale: suppliers want payment up front, the government pays “30 days at best” after delivery, and the operator has to float the supplies, the transportation and everything else tied to the contract, which at peak meant three to four aircraft loads of about 45 tons each per week; Packouz's planned fix is an investor bidding system that funds students' won contracts [RrDUfb8vNms @ 00:47:03]. On the livestream, Parks lists the financing walls: business credit carries high interest, you cannot fund against the contract itself, and vendor credit requires references [BZyPDVXuMMk @ 00:42:06]. Derek James sequences the money question: first check whether funding is needed at all, since subs often accept net 30 or paid-when-paid, then budget for a first invoice that may take 60 days, and only then reach for SBA loans or factoring with the cost of money priced into the bid [OKLXh8NcJxQ @ 00:33:18]. Every operator in this chain prices the float before celebrating the margin.

The broker's other exposure is getting cut out or blowing up. Kizzy Parks warns that a prime who fails to project-manage the sub will watch DHS go direct to Tammy's Toilets [kpISY6VCkNY @ 00:07:12], and Derek James lists the government shifting work direct to the sub, FAA staffing cut from 30–50 FTEs to 15–20, and profit concentrated in one CLIN as live pricing risks [OKLXh8NcJxQ @ 00:35:17]. Ricky Howard's defense is to never send a prospective partner the link or exact opportunity, only generalities [z6ZNrmprd-o @ 00:05:28]. The extreme case is David Packouz: a just-under-$300M munitions award, then Chinese-origin ammunition the contract barred directly or indirectly, a choice to repackage rather than request a waiver, 71 aircraft loads producing 71 certificates of conformance and a 355-year exposure, seven months of house arrest for Packouz, four years for the partner, about $500K in fines and a ban of roughly 15 years [RrDUfb8vNms @ 00:27:32]. Packouz adds that the Army knew the origin and kept taking delivery for six more months, about $30M worth. Packouz's rules from the wreckage: put partners on legally binding agreements, after being offered a $100K salary plus 1% in place of a 25% profit share, and never write an email you would not want read out in court or published in the newspaper.
