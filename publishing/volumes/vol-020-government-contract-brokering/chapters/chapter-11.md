## 1. Assign seven operator plays to five accountable seats  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Define the business as a procurement-services desk, not an award-resale machine. For this chapter, map its seven operator plays as P1 solicitation intelligence, P2 opportunity qualification, P3 bid-calendar control, P4 proposal assembly, P5 teaming diligence, P6 submission quality assurance, and P7 post-award coordination. These are operating workstreams, not seven additional revenue assumptions. In the five-person pod, FT1 is the principal and account lead, accountable for P2, P5, client scope, pricing, and cash collection. FT2 is the delivery manager, accountable for P3, P6, workload sequencing, and acceptance evidence. Contractor C1 performs P1 research and qualification preparation; C2 produces P4 response packages; C3 supplies specialist reviews and P7 administrative support. Each play has one accountable employee even when several people contribute. The client retains responsibility for its representations, pricing decisions, and authorized submission signatures. Use SAM.gov saved searches, agency acquisition forecasts, and the actual municipal purchasing portal as source systems. Record the solicitation identifier, amendment number, source URL, due time, and time zone before work enters the queue. A notification email is merely a lead; the controlling solicitation and amendments define the deliverable. Assign a backup employee to every deadline so principal absence does not suspend service.

Purchase contractor outputs and reserved capacity rather than pretending three contractors equal three full-time employees. An illustrative Base budget buys 720 annual research hours at $45, 720 proposal hours at $65, and 320 specialist hours at $90: $32,400 plus $46,800 plus $28,800 equals $108,000. That is 1,760 purchased hours across three people, with surge work requiring an explicit scope adjustment. These rates are planning inputs, not market quotations. Contracts specify deliverable format, source traceability, revision boundaries, confidentiality, conflict disclosure, and a signed assignment of copyright under 17 U.S.C. section 204(a). Do not rely solely on work-made-for-hire wording for commissioned materials. Require research delivery within two business days of assignment, proposal drafts three business days before the internal cutoff, and specialist review within one business day after a complete package arrives. Reserve review capacity when accepting the assignment; an SLA without capacity is fiction. Pay accepted hourly work or defined milestones, never a routine percentage of federal awards. Apply FAR Subpart 3.4 to contingent-fee arrangements, including the facts supporting any applicable exception. Independence must also be genuine: output specifications do not cure worker misclassification when actual control and economic dependence point toward employment.

Add the seventh-person configuration only when measured workload supports it. FT3 becomes the account-operations lead, taking P7 and recurring client reporting from FT1; C4 provides independent proposal review and amendment coverage under FT2. The resulting three full-time seats and four contractors remain seven people, not seven unrestricted production equivalents. Keep authors and final reviewers separate on every material submission, including municipal responses with different procurement codes. Use SharePoint version history for controlled documents and an Asana board for owners, dependencies, and internal cutoffs; neither replaces the government portal receipt. Track client permission for credentials and signatures, and avoid shared personal accounts. Before proposing prime/sub arrangements, check the actual solicitation, applicable FAR 52.219-14 clause, and 13 CFR 125.6 limitations on subcontracting. Review ostensible-subcontractor exposure under 13 CFR 121.103 where relevant. A bid desk cannot manufacture eligibility by placing a nominal qualifying firm above the real performer. Treat supplier capability as evidence-based: staff availability, relevant past performance, required licenses, and signed commitments. The pod sells coordination and execution discipline; it does not sell access to officials. Procurement-integrity and anti-kickback requirements apply to conduct regardless of whether the business calls itself a broker, consultant, or teaming adviser.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Protect scarce reviewer hours before chasing more solicitations**
> 
> The practical constraint is usually reliable review capacity near clustered deadlines, not the number of searchable opportunities.
> - Reserve a named reviewer when opening each pursuit; never discover review demand on submission morning.
> - Save the solicitation and every amendment with retrieval timestamps; email alerts can arrive after material changes.
> - Price municipal portal registration and upload rehearsal as explicit scope when the client lacks working credentials.
> - Give contractor invoices the same client, pursuit, and play codes used in the margin ledger so overruns remain traceable.

## 2. Run a weekly scorecard that rewards accepted work and collected fees  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Run a forty-five-minute Monday operating review from a frozen Friday dataset. FT2 publishes the queue; FT1 resolves scope and collection decisions. Score P1 on qualified opportunities accepted by FT1, not scraped notice volume. Score P2 on documented bid/no-bid decisions before production begins. Score P3 on amendments incorporated before internal cutoffs, and P4 on first-pass acceptance of complete response packages. Score P5 on verified supplier commitments and unresolved eligibility issues, P6 on accepted submissions with portal receipts, and P7 on timely client reports and resolved invoice exceptions. Define first-pass acceptance as packages accepted without material correction divided by packages reviewed that week. A material correction changes eligibility, pricing, required evidence, or responsiveness; a stylistic edit does not. Start with a 95% first-pass target and zero missed external deadlines, then inspect the underlying cases instead of rewarding denominator manipulation. Record sample sizes beside every percentage. One accepted package out of one is not evidence of stable quality. Track award outcomes separately by solicitation cohort, because agencies control evaluation schedules and award decisions. Never dock a contractor simply because a fully responsive client lost on price. Use debrief findings to improve qualification rather than rewriting the historical submission score.

Convert the weekly board into capacity decisions. For each seat, divide committed production and review hours over the next fourteen days by available contracted hours over that same window. Treat utilization above 85% as a planning warning, not a universal productivity benchmark; the remaining capacity absorbs amendments, client delays, and portal failures. A yellow condition prompts scope resequencing, while a predicted missed external deadline requires immediate principal intervention. Contractor QA uses a written defect taxonomy: critical defects include wrong offeror identity, unsupported certification, omitted mandatory attachment, or unauthorized disclosure; major defects include broken cross-references or inconsistent pricing schedules. Critical defects trigger package isolation and independent reinspection before submission. Require defect acknowledgment within four business hours during agreed coverage, with a correction plan by the next business day. Those are response commitments, not permission to exceed a government deadline. Allocate rework charges according to the contract: contractor error, client scope change, and newly issued amendment are different causes. Maintain a backup reviewer with equivalent access permissions and conflict screening. Do not improve apparent throughput by sending protected procurement material into unapproved software. Tool convenience never substitutes for the client's information-handling requirements or the contract's specific security obligations.

Apply a 25% ceiling to both customer revenue concentration and critical delivery-counterparty dependence. Measure each customer and related customer group against trailing-twelve-month recognized service fees; repeat the calculation on signed forward service backlog to expose pending concentration. Separately measure each critical subcontractor or supplier against delivery spend, and identify indispensable single-source dependencies even when their spend share is small. At the Base case, 25% of $560,000 is $140,000; at Scaled, 25% of $1,000,000 is $250,000. These are customer-fee limits, not caps on the customer's government award value. A young desk may exceed the ceiling before its fourth meaningful account; report that breach plainly, stop expanding the dominant account's exposure, and prioritize diversification rather than disguising related entities as separate customers. Also report exposure by agency, municipal jurisdiction, and prime contractor, since apparently diverse clients can depend on one funding source. Track receivables aging weekly and suspend new discretionary work when contractual payment triggers are breached. Predefine escalation and continuity duties so a collection dispute does not cause an avoidable live-submission failure. Weekly decisions must identify an owner, due date, and measurable closure condition.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze Friday evidence
   - Export the pursuit register, capacity ledger, QA log, and receivables aging.
   - FT2 timestamps the snapshot and reconciles every active pursuit to its current solicitation amendment.
2. Calculate denominators before interpreting ratios
   - Publish accepted packages, reviewed packages, committed hours, available hours, and related-account revenue.
   - Show small samples and concentration breaches directly; do not remove failed work from the denominator.
3. Resolve exceptions in Monday review
   - Assign one employee owner to every deadline risk, critical defect, collection issue, and concentration breach.
   - Each decision records the correction, purchased capacity if needed, due date, and evidence required for closure.
4. Verify closure before the next cutoff
   - Inspect corrected packages, updated commitments, and payment evidence against the prior snapshot.
   - Carry unresolved items forward visibly and escalate anything that threatens an external submission deadline.

## 3. Reconcile service-fee cohorts to the canonical owner-profit cases  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Keep the canonical cases intact: Conservative recognizes $120,000 of service fees, incurs $102,000 of total costs, and leaves $18,000 at 15%; Base recognizes $560,000, incurs $434,000, and leaves $126,000 at 22.5%; Scaled recognizes $1,000,000, incurs $700,000, and leaves $300,000 at 30%. A client's $8 million award contributes zero to desk revenue unless the desk separately earns contracted service fees. Never book that award as brokerage turnover or use it to inflate acquisition metrics. An illustrative Base cost envelope allocates $180,000 to the two employee seats, $108,000 to the three contractor engagements, and $146,000 to remaining delivery and operating costs. A Scaled envelope allocates $300,000 to three employees, $160,000 to four contractors, and $240,000 to remaining costs. These envelopes reconcile arithmetic; they do not establish prevailing compensation. The Conservative $102,000 ceiling is especially tight for two full-time seats plus purchased contractor work. Document actual compensation, owner labor, and deferred economic requirements rather than claiming it funds market-rate replacement staffing. The stated $5,000–$25,000 monthly operating range cannot represent all-in Scaled costs: $700,000 divided by twelve is approximately $58,333 monthly. Separate overhead from total delivery costs when using that planning range.

Close books on accrual principles with client, engagement, play, and origination-cohort codes. Recognize a monthly stand-ready retainer over the service period when that reflects the performance obligation; recognize a discrete proposal milestone when the applicable recognition criteria are satisfied. A deposit received before performance remains a contract liability, not earned revenue. Where ASC 606 applies, document the performance obligations and recognition method with the accountant. Accrue accepted contractor work in the same reporting period as delivery, even if the invoice arrives later. Reconcile QuickBooks Online or Xero project detail to signed scopes, acceptance records, receivables, bank deposits, and contractor commitments. Treat reimbursed costs according to the actual principal-versus-agent analysis, not whichever presentation produces larger revenue. Keep unbilled earned fees separate from conditional opportunities and unperformed backlog. Track bad-debt estimates and credit notes by originating cohort so weak clients do not disappear inside current-month totals. Each month, bridge opening receivables plus invoices minus collections and credits to closing receivables. Then bridge recognized revenue to billings through contract assets and liabilities. An attractive profit statement without those bridges can conceal delayed billing, disputed acceptance, or cash taken for work still owed.

Build cohort reporting around the month the service relationship began, not the government's eventual award month. For each cohort, report opening recurring fees, expansion, contraction, churn, recognized project fees, direct contractor costs, allocated employee delivery time, and cash collected. Keep recurring retainers separate from one-off proposal projects; a client purchasing two bids does not automatically create annual recurring revenue. Measure gross retention only on an identified recurring-fee population, and publish the formula alongside the result. Track proposal margins by complexity band so a simple municipal quote does not subsidize an unpriced federal technical volume indefinitely. Use time records to test the allocation method, including founder hours that are absent from payroll. Model a thirteen-week cash forecast using contractual payment dates and observed collection behavior. A client's government payment expectations do not automatically determine when the desk gets paid. The stated 3–12 months to first deal makes the $20,000–$90,000 startup range a runway constraint, not a hiring instruction. At $25,000 monthly cash burn, $150,000 funds six months before collections or additional capital. Rephase hiring and discretionary spending when signed fees cannot support the projected cash trough.

## 4. Price transferable earnings rather than founder-dependent award stories  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Prepare a buyer-ready record before requesting a multiple. Assemble signed client scopes, renewal terms, termination rights, assignment provisions, contractor agreements, intellectual-property assignments, monthly accrual statements, cohort bridges, and receipt-backed delivery logs. Separate reusable desk templates from client-owned proposal content and government information subject to handling restrictions. Section 204(a) requires a signed writing for a copyright ownership transfer; an invoice alone does not establish ownership. Inventory any third-party licenses and identify which can transfer. A buyer also needs evidence that account relationships survive the founder: record nonfounder client contacts, renewal ownership, documented workflows, and successful delivery during planned founder absences. Distinguish assignment of ordinary advisory contracts from transfer of an actual federal contract. If the business holds federal contracts, an asset transaction may require novation analysis under FAR 42.1204 and the transfer restrictions in 41 U.S.C. section 6305. Do not promise that a buyer inherits awards, set-aside eligibility, or agency relationships automatically. Where services create organizational conflicts of interest, preserve the FAR Subpart 9.5 analysis and client restrictions. Redact and stage diligence access rather than exposing protected proposal material to every prospective acquirer.

Use valuation ranges as negotiation sensitivities, not reported transaction comparables. For a small owner-operated desk, test roughly 2–3 times properly normalized seller discretionary earnings; for a demonstrably manager-run book, test roughly 3–4 times normalized EBITDA. Those are different denominators and must never be mixed. The canonical owner-profit figures are neither automatically EBITDA nor automatically seller discretionary earnings. Bridge them through actual owner compensation, interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, and supportable adjustments before selecting a valuation basis. For a simplified replacement-adjusted sensitivity, assume the canonical profit includes no compensation for the owner's delivery role, all other normalization adjustments are zero, and that role requires additional fully loaded replacement cost. Base profit of $126,000 less $90,000 then leaves $36,000; a 2.5–3.5 times sensitivity implies $90,000–$126,000 of enterprise value. Scaled profit of $300,000 less $120,000 leaves $180,000; 3–4 times implies $540,000–$720,000. Conservative profit of $18,000 less $90,000 is negative $72,000, so this method supports no positive earnings-based valuation. Do not subtract replacement cost twice when equivalent management compensation is already included in the expense base.

A premium within any negotiated range must come from reduced transfer risk: diversified fees, repeatable retention, current IP assignments, low defect rates, reliable collections, and delivery that does not require founder heroics. Award win rate alone cannot establish durability because the mix of solicitations, client capabilities, and agency budgets changes. Compare offers on cash at close, seller financing, earnout definitions, working-capital adjustments, escrow, and retained liabilities. Enterprise value becomes equity proceeds only after the transaction's cash, debt, and other agreed adjustments; headline price is not distributable cash. For an earnout, define recognized service fees, eligible customers, attribution of renewals, collection conditions, and the buyer's ability to change staffing or pricing. Exclude client award principal from every revenue definition. If offers undervalue dependable distributions, retain the business as a managed cash cow and test quarterly whether the principal can step away without degraded delivery or collections. Keep the same concentration ceilings, replacement-cost analysis, and cash reserves after deciding not to sell. Transferability is useful even without a transaction: it protects continuity during illness, contractor departure, supplier failure, and uneven government procurement cycles.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Verify four conditions before presenting a transferable book**
> 
> A sale process should expose documented operating strength, not depend on explanations that appear only after diligence begins.
> - Verify signed contractor IP assignments, client ownership boundaries, and transferable software rights before placing materials in the diligence room.
> - Verify each related customer group and critical delivery counterparty remains at or below the 25% ceiling, with breaches disclosed and remediation documented.
> - Verify twelve monthly accrual closes reconcile fees, contractor costs, contract liabilities, receivables, and cash without including client award principal.
> - Verify founder replacement, client assignment terms, any federal-contract novation requirements, and the exact earnings denominator used in every valuation sensitivity.
