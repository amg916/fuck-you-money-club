## Executive Summary

DLA brokers on DIBBS agree the game is volume inside a narrow lane: register on SAM and DIBBS, pick three to five FSCs, screen each RFQ for approved-source restrictions, and quote every day, because roughly 99% of awards go to the cheapest bid and bid count tracks award count. Street mechanics are precise: read solicitations only to page 10, anchor price to procurement history, target 10 to 15% margin, take Net 30 since the government pays in 23 to 25 days, and call suppliers instead of emailing. Tooling splits the field, from FedOpp's browser-built batch files to DVN's DLA BUS catalog uploads and Gov Scraper at $100 a month. Practitioners clash over chasing RFQs older than 60 days, whether batch bidding needs thousands of NSNs, and how fast a bidding catalog can realistically be built.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner opens at the same front door: register on SAM to pull a UEI and CAGE code, then complete a separate DIBBS registration before a single quote goes in [wxZNfwOuung @ 00:02:38]. The prize behind that paperwork is enormous and tilted toward small shops. Ki Hendris of National Material Supply puts last year's DLA spend at $51.4 billion, with about 80% of awards going to small businesses [jjX9klCZFKI @ 00:10:46]. Tristan Thomas of Mend Sourcing and Gov Scraper counts $52.6 billion in 2024 obligations and $47 billion in revenue, with over 40% flowing to small business against a 5% SDVOSB goal [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:12:06]. DVN frames the daily firehose: about 10,000 awards a day, more than 100,000 open solicitations, 5 to 8 million NSNs, and roughly $50 billion a year [MyfmHjDF9HI @ 00:12:48]. The percentages differ by speaker, but the direction is identical: this is a small-business market moving thousands of buys every day, and the operators who win are the ones who treat it as a daily volume game rather than a lottery ticket. None of these operators sells it as fast money; the work is registration, a narrow lane, and relentless quoting.

The second agreement is the lane, and the lane is the Federal Supply Class. Thomas says about 95% of searches run by the four-digit FSC, and filtering 5306, 5305 and 5310 to small business and SDVOSB set-asides returned 876 RFQs in a single pull [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:18:41]. myContractScout tells newcomers to start with three to five FSCs, aim for a first $100,000, and stay disciplined about scope: don't go try to sell tires on day one [SeWe_WAhyVg @ 00:08:07]. The third agreement is that approved-source restrictions decide whether a broker can even play the buy. Tris10 tells beginners to look for items whose PID is blank with no approved sources, which any supplier can fill, and to pair those with local manufacturers [JE_OOJ3NAVk @ 00:09:49]. FedOpp warns flatly that the codes limiting a buy to approved sources can knock you out, and puts an AMSC filter directly on its bid-board search for exactly that reason [bTlxn6BVHJ0 @ 00:02:02]. The operator logic stacks cleanly: keep a short FSC list, screen every RFQ for who is allowed to supply the part, and spend sourcing hours only where the door is open or where a distributor can reach the named manufacturer.

The final consensus is that awards follow volume, price, and voice. Tris10 states there is a direct correlation between the amount of bids you put into the system and the amount of awards you win, and pushes operators to keep quoting even when they suspect they are priced out [v4ulUMKE5xA @ 00:15:55]. Humam Finding Sam estimates about 99% of awards go to the cheapest bid, which makes the cheapest landed cost the entire contest [wxZNfwOuung @ 00:06:24]. Thomas ranks DLA's priorities in order: price first, then delivery time, then credibility through the SPRS score [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:30:17]. On the supplier side the agreement is just as firm. Tris10 says phone beats email and in person beats phone, because it is much harder for someone to say no to you over the phone than it is via email [JE_OOJ3NAVk @ 00:11:07]. That makes the daily rhythm obvious for a broker: a short FSC list, aggressive price-first quoting every day, delivery treated as a secondary lever, and suppliers worked by voice instead of by inbox. Price is the contest, and volume is how an operator gets enough at-bats to win it.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Execution starts with the pull. On raw DIBBS, myContractScout runs FSC 6510, gets 452 records, and copies the whole list into Google Sheets to work it outside the portal [SeWe_WAhyVg @ 00:06:21]. FedOpp's morning routine is tighter: filter Parts to Quote down to open RFQs only, then sort by last award unit price before building any quote [bTs2wy13K1c @ 00:03:21]. Reading the solicitation is where most hours disappear, and Thomas cuts it hard. They read only through page 10 of a 24-page RFQ, up to the asterisks, and extract the solicitation number, PR number, procurement history, NSN, CMMC level, approved sources, CLIN quantity, delivery days, FOB terms, and ASTM packaging; their worked example carried 831 units at 108 delivery days with FOB origin, meaning the government pays shipping [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:23:27]. Tris10 treats procurement history as the price compass. One shear bolt history showed 1,966 pieces at $556 in 2022 and 46 pieces at $49.90 in 2021, which gives a broker the price band before calling a single supplier [Z9MLGFqFXtM @ 00:02:17]. The extraction sheet becomes the template for every vendor request, so the same dozen fields get captured the same way on every RFQ.

Pricing is built backward from the last award, not forward from a supplier list. Humam Finding Sam pulls past awards of $33 on February 25, 2026, $35 on January 28, and $27 on August 4, 2025, so they enter the vendor request already knowing what the item should cost [wxZNfwOuung @ 00:06:38]. DVN's live wiper-blade bid shows the markup math in plain dollars: a 10% markup came to about $2 per unit and $642 of total profit, with ASTM D3951 packaging and MIL-STD-129 labels priced in [MyfmHjDF9HI @ 00:31:22]. Thomas puts the sweet spot at 10 to 15% margin, warns that asking 20 to 30% gets you underbid, and walks a worked build: 250 units at $185 for $46,250, plus a $350 packaging minimum, plus a 250 DCMA inspection line, plus $7,035 of margin, all divided by 250 for the unit bid [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:38:03]. Terms move cash too. Tris10 picks Net 30 over a 10-day term because the shorter term is a 2% haircut that turns $100,000 into $98,000, and the government pays within 23 to 25 days on average anyway [v4ulUMKE5xA @ 00:05:57]. The pattern across all four is identical: anchor on history, stack every landed cost, add a thin margin, and protect it through payment terms.

The bid form is where volume is won or lost. Tris10's rule on the DIBBS web quote is to keep filling until no section is still yellow [v4ulUMKE5xA @ 00:02:32]. Hendris lays out the three quoting methods: the web form at about two pages and time-consuming, EDI through a value-added network that is antiquated, expensive, and not recommended for small businesses, and batch quoting as a one-click upload locked to active vendors [jjX9klCZFKI @ 00:11:21]. Batch is where the serious distributors live, and the tooling filters dead lines before they waste an upload. DVN's DLA BUS demo pushed 20 solicitations through and returned 11 green passes and 9 red failures that were already awarded, cancelled, or closed [YlwuuaCoHzQ @ 00:12:25]. FedOpp's batch builder runs a Check Line pass that flags every error in red with a running count of items left to fix, covering item description code, quality, trade and Buy American answers, packaging, and manufacturer or dealer fields [iTxJkLPYuqc @ 00:02:56]. The street logic is simple: a web form handles a few quotes a day, but an operator chasing real volume moves the prep into spreadsheets or a catalog, validates every line against DLA's format, and uploads the whole stack at once instead of retyping CAGE codes and prices one solicitation at a time.

   - Filter open DLA RFQs to your handful of FSCs, then layer the set-aside filter.

2. Extract the approved manufacturer and part number
   - Pull the named approved manufacturer and its part number from the RFQ before sourcing.

   - Email the supplier the NSN, part number, drawings and delivery date, then follow up by phone.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over how old an RFQ can be before it becomes dead money. Tris10 @ MeND treats the return date loosely, using a no-bid as a placeholder and often bidding after expiration because DIBBS is more lenient than SAM, yet draws a hard line against touching anything older than 60 days and works the newest RFQs because a contracting officer just put them into the system [v4ulUMKE5xA @ 00:05:07]. Tristan Thomas of Mend Sourcing and Gov Scraper, speaking on Secureframe, lands in the same camp and calls the popular go-back-60-to-90-days advice false, arguing that stale RFQs are leftovers from DLA staff reassignment rather than proof that nobody bid, and sorts every search by newest issued [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:20:44]. The DLA Vendor Network runs a looser play in its live walkthrough: a wiper-blade solicitation whose September 28 deadline had passed was still open on October 4, and they quoted it anyway [MyfmHjDF9HI @ 00:25:44]. Their logic is bid and bid often, because a submitted bid can always be revised later [MyfmHjDF9HI @ 00:35:56]. The usable synthesis is that a passed return date alone does not kill a buy, but the age of the RFQ separates live demand from abandoned records.

Delivery promises and margin targets split the camp just as hard. On the wiper blades, The DLA Vendor Network faced a 284-day required delivery and bid 90 days, landing at $23.31 per unit after a 10% markup worth about $2 per unit and $642 in total profit [MyfmHjDF9HI @ 00:35:09]. Tristan Thomas argues the reverse on delivery: quote your real lead time, because they won with 300 days against 25 requested, and DLA ranks price first, then delivery, then credibility through the SPRS score [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:30:17]. Tris10 @ MeND backs the price-first ranking from a different angle, pricing 103 pieces at 120 delivery days and stating that the government does not have delivery days as the number one priority [v4ulUMKE5xA @ 00:09:28]. On margin, Tristan Thomas puts the sweet spot at 10 to 15%, warns that asking 20 to 30% gets you underbid, and reports that judging the profit dollar amount instead of the percentage brought more awards [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:38:03]. The DVN markup sits at the bottom of that band, so the live argument is whether to anchor on percentage or on dollars per award, and whether a short delivery promise is worth the risk of missing it.

Tooling and catalog strategy form the third fault line. Ki Hendris of National Material Supply, presenting for The DLA Vendor Network, flatly rejects the idea that you need thousands of NSNs to batch bid, because every NSN you bid, won or lost, joins your catalog [jjX9klCZFKI @ 00:20:13]. FedOpp takes the opposite design stance from the DVN catalog generator: the batch TXT is built line by line in the browser, checked against DLA's format rules, and uploaded by the user, and FedOpp never submits bids [iTxJkLPYuqc @ 00:00:04]. Even what to read before quoting is contested. Tris10 @ MeND tells bidders that flow-downs can be ignored until an award lands, arguing you do not need all the information at the beginning just to submit a bid [Z9MLGFqFXtM @ 00:05:42]. Tristan Thomas lists missing flowdowns among the five mistakes that sink vendors, next to not reading to the asterisks, trusting one quote, promising unachievable delivery and ignoring SPRS [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:45:05]. Both still read the front of the RFQ hard: Tris10 pulls solicitation and PR numbers, procurement history, NSN, approved manufacturer and CLIN quantities, and Tristan stops at page 10 of 24, so the real dispute is only whether flowdown review happens before the bid or after the award.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common bleed happens before a bid is ever typed. Tris10 @ MeND opens on suppliers who no-bid, never respond, or quote far above the eventual award, then admits they came to find out suppliers had no idea what an NSN number is, which is why each NSN now gets mapped to the supplier part number through the nsn-now public search [JE_OOJ3NAVk @ 00:03:52]. When a supplier still declines, they phone to ask why it was a no-bid instead of moving on blind [JE_OOJ3NAVk @ 00:15:51]. Humam Finding Sam flags the vendor-side margin killer: surcharges left out of a quote are either going to eat your profit margin or end the bid with a loss, so the request has to carry NSN, part number, drawings and delivery date and demand all-inclusive pricing [wxZNfwOuung @ 00:08:30]. Tristan Thomas closes the same loop from the other side: always follow an email with a call, including after a no-bid, because it is harder to say no to a person when you hear their voice [v7nJCLRc0uo @ 00:54:07]. The economic check is simple: no line goes into DIBBS on a single unverified number while lead time, certs, country of origin, cert fees and traceability are still unanswered.

DIBBS itself punishes sloppy operators. myContractScout warns that you cannot hit the Enter button to run a search, that a soldier-hand error means refresh and redo, and that the browser back button times the session out [SeWe_WAhyVg @ 00:05:23]. The DLA Vendor Network's demo upload of 20 solicitations passed only 11, with the other nine flagged red as already awarded, cancelled or closed, and the same session warns that DLA will tell you when you did not win but not when a buy was cancelled [YlwuuaCoHzQ @ 00:12:25]. Ki Hendris adds that DLA posts 2,000 to 3,000 solicitations a day and often cancels and reissues them, so a dead solicitation number is routine rather than a glitch [jjX9klCZFKI @ 00:32:59]. Manual entry, per the same DVN camp, compounds the damage, burning three to five minutes per bid with errors such as the wrong CAGE code or the wrong price. FedOpp's answer is a check-line pass that flags errors in red with a running count of items left to fix, and an exporter that only exports files that pass DLA's format rules [iTxJkLPYuqc @ 00:04:03]. The operator lesson: expect part of every batch to bounce, and recheck status before blaming your price.

Eligibility traps cost the most because they surface after the sourcing work is done. Tris10 @ MeND warns that parts from a non-qualifying country such as China or Afghanistan disqualify the quote, and states flatly, 'in our case, we only buy American' [v4ulUMKE5xA @ 00:12:48]. They also caution that a procurement-history line only shows that someone won the award; a cancellation or protest may have followed, so the past price is a guide rather than a lock [Z9MLGFqFXtM @ 00:02:03]. Ki Hendris answers a question about passing JCP tech data to an uncertified shop with 'No, it is not legal,' unless the manufacturer is also certified, a hard stop for brokers who want to hand C-folder drawings to an outside manufacturer [jjX9klCZFKI @ 00:34:01]. The DLA Vendor Network frames the volume side just as soberly: GovCon is not a sprint, no company has a 100% hit ratio, and the target of ten bids a day comes with traceability requests you should expect [MyfmHjDF9HI @ 00:40:33]. Run every quote past those three checks, origin, tech-data rights and documentation, before the price ever gets entered.
