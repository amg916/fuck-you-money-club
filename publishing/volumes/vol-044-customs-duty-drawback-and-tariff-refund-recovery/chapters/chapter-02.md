## Executive Summary

Brokers, attorneys and software teams agree on the core math. Drawback returns 99% of eligible duties, taxes and fees, including Section 301 and 201. Section 232, AD/CVD and over-quota duties are not recoverable. Substitution now depends on an 8-digit HTS match. 'Other' lines get pushed to 10 digits, 'other other' lines are barred, and the lesser-of rule caps every substituted claim. Execution runs through the 7553 notice, the 7551 claim, exact ACE data matching, and a privilege application filed on a chosen day to lock the 5-year look-back. Flexport reports optimal matching beating greedy logic 70 to 11 and averaging roughly 35% more on substitution. Practitioners split on notice periods, record retention, accelerated payment speed, liquidation delays and the minimum claim worth filing, which ranges from a $10-20k lookback to CEDA's $250K floor.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner opens with the same number. Drawback refunds 99% of eligible duties, taxes and fees, and in Monica Pascarella's words it is only 99% because 1% is kept in customs for administrative fees [1_L7fnmAAU0 @ 00:02:37]. The eligible bucket is consistent across brokers and counsel. Joe Harper of Star USA lists ordinary duty, Section 301, HMF, MPF, marking duties, IRS taxes and 201 duties. Harper also flags that MPF minimums and maximums require special math [NK99CBNOeJA @ 00:06:34]. The Scarbrough webinar team adds voluntary tender and prior disclosure duties to the list, and describes Section 301 recovery as getting 'that 25 percent back' [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:19:43]. The exclusion list is just as uniform: AD/CVD, Section 232 and over-quota agricultural duties do not come back. Aaron Mandelbaum of Thompson Hine extends the line into the newest tariffs. IEEPA reciprocal duties fall on the eligible side and IEEPA fentanyl tariffs on the ineligible side, while some 232 carve-outs are starting to emerge [WqGvMD-AOqg @ 00:11:23]. The operating consensus is blunt. Before modeling any refund, strip 232, AD/CVD and over-quota lines out of the duty base, because no claim structure recovers them. The 301, 201, MPF and HMF lines stay in the recoverable pool that the rest of the program is built around.

Substitution is where the consensus sharpens into hard classification rules. Scarbrough Global describes the old 'commercially interchangeable' test, which weighed four factors: government or industry standards, part numbers, tariff classifications and relative value. They call it unwieldy and subjective, while the TFTEA standard is a much more objective 8-digit HTS match [fI28fmPlbPA @ 00:01:39]. Their commingled-bin customer illustrates the upside. That customer sources the same item from four countries plus a domestic supplier and still substitutes, as long as it is not an other provision. The rule set repeats everywhere. If the 8-digit line is an 'other' provision, drop to the 10-digit code, and if the 10-digit code is also 'other', substitution is barred [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:14:47]. Alliance Duty Drawback Services confirms that part numbers are ignored under the HTS match. Substitution is barred for exports to Canada, Mexico, Chile and US territories. The lesser-of rule caps recovery, so a $100 import matched to a $50 export recovers half [mkogoOMopss @ 00:03:26]. Mandelbaum states the cap plainly: claimants are 'only getting refunded the smaller of the two duty amounts which prevents over refunds when substituted goods are used' [WqGvMD-AOqg @ 00:27:58]. Alliance's operator takeaway is to substitute exports of similar value so the cap does not eat the claim.

The third point of agreement is the clock and the privilege structure built around it. Matt Robison of Amber Road credits TFTEA 2015 with two changes. It brought 8-digit substitution, and it collapsed the old 3-years-to-export plus 3-years-to-file timeline into a single 5-year clock. Robison also credits ACE's fully electronic filing, live in 2019, with replacing mailed PDFs [BCWMDlUCSx4 @ 00:08:19]. Pat Caulfield of Scarbrough explains how to use the one-time retroactive waiver. Operators should 'pick a day' for the Notice of Intent first, or lose three to six months of claimable imports while paperwork drifts [wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:32:45]. The Scarbrough webinar calls the look-back 'your one-time get out of jail free card that allows you to look back five years', and sets the clock by the submission date [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:09:10]. Tisha Paige of CEDA operationalizes the same logic. CEDA files claims the same day the privilege application goes in, locking the 5-year window from each import date. It then waits 3 to 18 months for CBP approval and about 45 days per claim for payment [SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:33:40]. ITM and CEDA both call drawback a privilege, not a right. Every month the submission slips, the oldest entries age out of the five-year window.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The filing mechanics run on a fixed form sequence. The Scarbrough one-time workflow starts with a CBP 7553 carrying the import documents, a pro forma invoice, booking information and the port of export. Customs takes about a week to review and then waives or examines, so examination time has to be built into ship dates. The 7551 goes in after export with proof of export [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:02:41]. Joe Harper's electronic rules are exact: file in ACE using the 10-digit HTS and matching units of measure, and upload supporting documents within 24 hours [NK99CBNOeJA @ 00:30:49]. The Scarbrough team reports a claim rejected over a PSC nobody had seen. They insist that PSCs and protests be resolved, and the 21-month Recon cycle completed, before any drawback filing [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:22:48]. Tisha Paige lists the full packet for an unused-merchandise privilege application. The import side needs the commercial invoice, packing slip and 7501. The export side needs the invoice, packing slip and proof of export: a bill of lading, B3/CAD, pedimento, airway bill or seaway bill. Inventory receipt and withdrawal dates complete the packet, and a manufacturing application adds production in/out dates and the bill of materials [SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:29:46]. CEDA accepts paper records and has flown staff to clients to collect them.

Refund size is engineered through accounting method and matching logic, not just paperwork. Monica Pascarella walks through six accounting methods: FIFO, LIFO, low-to-high, low-to-high average inventory turnover, low-to-high blanket and average. Low-to-high blanket is the most common, but 'not always' the highest recovery [1_L7fnmAAU0 @ 00:11:41]. The Flexport team adds two levers. Grouping entry lines lowers per-unit duty, and importers who moved sourcing out of China should switch from low-to-high to FIFO [1_L7fnmAAU0 @ 00:32:02]. On substitution, J2 matching runs HTS-to-HTS off ACE data, claiming the highest duty first. Alex Neutloff shows why the matching engine matters. A greedy algorithm yields 11 where the optimal match yields 70. A 10-by-10 matching problem carries more possible combinations than there are sand grains on 170 billion Earths [A7RUPXhyx4M @ 00:12:52]. Flexport ran the same data through its software instead of legacy software in head-to-head comparisons. Apparel moved from $1.58M to $1.88M, and consumer goods from $3.9M to $5.7M. Automotive manufacturing rose from $2.6M to $3.6M while consuming $9.9M of imports instead of $18.8M, a drop of 47% [A7RUPXhyx4M @ 00:28:14]. Flexport also tells claimants that a higher duty rate does not always produce a higher refund, because the lesser-of rule bites.

The unit economics are simple, and the practitioners publish them. Flexport offers two sizing methods: duty spend multiplied by export share of sales, or export COGS multiplied by average duty rate [1_L7fnmAAU0 @ 00:26:28]. Robison's version is annual duty times export percentage, chasing a five-digit-plus ROI. Cash timing is the second variable. Accelerated payment lands in 4 to 6 weeks but requires a bond. Without it, refunds take 6 months to almost 3 years, because 'there are only like 25 drawback Specialists across the country and they are super backed up' [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:10:52]. A drawback bond is dollar-for-dollar, so a $300k claim needs a $300k bond. A continuous bond, by contrast, covers 10% of the amount. Flexport's instant drawback advances 70% in one day for a 2 to 10% advance fee. That fee depends on whether the claim has been approved for accelerated payment [1_L7fnmAAU0 @ 00:36:06]. Fees run on contingency, and CEDA's pitch is 'we don't get paid until you do'. CEDA sets a $250K recoverable-duty minimum and refers smaller files out [SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:28:03]. Caulfield calls a first lookback under $10-20k probably not worth it. The Scarbrough webinar adds that a $250 refund may not justify service fees and staff hours, while a $15k refund is worthwhile for many firms.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Size the prize
   - Estimate recoverable duty and confirm the program clears provider thresholds.
   - Multiply annual duty by export percentage and seek a five-digit-plus ROI. Confirm exporter-of-record and importer rights, and get officer and CFO sign-off [TalkingLogistics — BCWMDlUCSx4 @ 00:15:10]
2. Lock rights and pull the data
   - Collect the power of attorney, ACE O3 report and AES report, plus endorsements or waivers.
   - An IOR-only claimant needs the exporter's letter of endorsement, and an exporter-only claimant needs the importer's waivers. CEDA pulls documents through client portal access [World Trade Center Denver — SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:25:22]
3. Pick the submission day
   - File the privilege application and the first claims together, on a fixed date.
   - Pick a day for the Notice of Intent first, or lose 3 to 6 months of claimable imports under the one-time retroactive waiver [Scarbrough Global — wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:32:45]
4. Scrub against ACE and file
   - Match claim data to ACE exactly, clear open PSCs and protests, then transmit.
   - Claim data must match ACE down to unit of measure. One claim was rejected over an unseen PSC, so resolve PSCs, protests and Recon before filing [Scarbrough Global — 6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:21:06]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Advance notice to CBP is where the playbooks split hardest. Pat Caulfield and Adam at Scarbrough Global teach that an export notice gives CBP 72 hours, and that destruction is usually supervised, with bottles broken and textiles cut [wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:37:24]. Aaron Mandelbaum at Thompson Hine LLP breaks the Form 7553 clock into pieces. CBP decides within 2 working days and examines within 5. Manufacturing exports need no notice, rejected-merchandise exports need 5 days, and destruction needs 7 days [WqGvMD-AOqg @ 00:36:54]. Jill La Madeleine at International Tariff Management runs destruction as a live event with 5 days' notice to CBP, carried out by a third party with affidavits [gf35xQR8Lz0 @ 00:06:15]. Allison Shower at Scarbrough Global budgets about one week for Customs to review a one-time 7553 before it waives or examines the goods, and tells operators to build examination time into shipping deadlines [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:03:01]. Shower adds that Customs wants to witness destruction '98 of the time,' so destruction slots are scheduled events rather than ad hoc disposals. The common thread across all four is that notice periods differ by drawback type and by who is talking. Set 7553 timing per claim type, with examination time built into the ship date.

Refund speed is the second big split, and it drives cash-flow planning. Joe Harper at Star USA, Inc. quotes about 3 weeks with accelerated payment. Without it, the money waits for liquidation, which can take 'up to four or five years' [NK99CBNOeJA @ 00:34:14]. Allison Shower at Scarbrough Global puts accelerated payment at 4 to 6 weeks and notes it requires a bond. Without it, refunds run 6 months to almost 3 years because, in Shower's words, there are 'only like 25 drawback specialists across the country and they are super backed up' [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:10:52]. Aaron Mandelbaum at Thompson Hine LLP quotes 45 to 60 days and calls 45 days a floor that CBP itself describes as ambitious [WqGvMD-AOqg @ 00:39:45]. Flexport sidesteps the wait entirely. Monica Pascarella describes an instant drawback product that advances 70% of the claim in 1 day for a 2 to 10% advance fee. The fee level depends on whether the claim is already approved for accelerated payment [1_L7fnmAAU0 @ 00:35:25]. The alternative playbooks, then, are to trade a slice of the refund for speed, or to post a dollar-for-dollar drawback bond and wait weeks instead of years.

Section 232 eligibility and the FTZ-versus-drawback choice produce the sharpest strategic splits. Allison Shower at Scarbrough Global states flatly that 'section 232 we can't claim' [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:20:06]. Aaron Mandelbaum at Thompson Hine LLP counters with a live exception: Section 232 auto parts now qualify for manufacturing drawback through the medium- and heavy-duty vehicle action [WqGvMD-AOqg @ 00:52:18]. The CEDA team, presenting for World Trade Center Denver, goes further. Their carve-out list covers lumber, some automotive, copper and aluminum in some manufacturing instances, and some pharmaceuticals, though they concede 'a majority of the 232s are not eligible' [SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:44:09]. On zones, Scarbrough Global frames the choice as a cost contest: a 'twenty percent recovery' contingency fee against the cost of standing up a zone, decided case by case [m4ePKta6BXo @ 00:02:58]. CEDA rejects the either-or framing. An FTZ is forward-only and suits duties drawback cannot touch, a drawback privilege approval is permanent, and the retroactive 5-year claim can run while the zone is still being built. Don adds that eligibility tends to expand rather than shrink, so operators who wrote off 232 categories should re-screen them against the current carve-out list.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures are clock failures. Jill La Madeleine at International Tariff Management describes a wine company that held an approved privilege, went beyond the five years, and 'lost $1,000,000' in recovery [gf35xQR8Lz0 @ 00:08:37]. Scarbrough Global's Pat Caulfield and Adam name the single biggest pitfall: missing CBP information requests inside the 5-year window, which sinks claims that were otherwise filed on time [wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:30:38]. The same Scarbrough team warns that the one-time retroactive waiver forces a decision. You have to 'pick a day' for the Notice of Intent before anything else, or bleed 3 to 6 months of eligible imports off the back end of the look-back [wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:32:45]. CEDA's countermeasure, presented at World Trade Center Denver, is to file claims the same day the privilege application is submitted. That locks the 5-year window counted from each import date. CEDA targets 90 days from contract signing to first claim, then 3 to 18 months for CBP approval and about 45 days per claim to payment afterward [SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:33:40]. CEDA also reports that CBP kept working during the government shutdown but Treasury refund payments paused, so a perfectly timed claim can still sit unpaid.

Data mismatches are the second failure cluster, and they surface at filing rather than at audit. Allison Shower at Scarbrough Global reports that claim data must match ACE exactly, down to unit of measure. Shower recounts a claim rejected over a post-summary correction nobody on the drawback side had seen [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:21:06]. Shower's rule is to resolve PSCs and protests, and let Reconciliation run its 21 months, before filing. Shower also warns that 'almost every time you file your first claim you are going to get hit with a full desk review.' Joe Harper at Star USA, Inc. adds the mechanical checks: use the 10-digit HTS with matching units of measure, and upload supporting documents within 24 hours [NK99CBNOeJA @ 00:30:49]. Classification is the deeper trap. Scarbrough Global warns that 'other/other/other' lines are a 'big swath of lots of potential issues' and exactly where importers 'get in trouble,' even when the duty-free result 'looks great' [fI28fmPlbPA @ 00:04:40]. Scarbrough's Caulfield and Adam tie audit risk to the same root: filing drawback does not itself raise scrutiny, but misclassification does [wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:57:30]. A drawback program inherits every unresolved entry error sitting in the import history.

Economics checks cut both ways: some claims are too small to pay for themselves, and penalties can be large enough to erase a program. Allison Shower at Scarbrough Global warns that a $250 refund may not justify service fees and staff hours, while a $15,000 refund is worthwhile for many firms [6Ogi9iKVSTE @ 00:06:23]. Scarbrough Global's Caulfield and Adam set the first look-back bar higher, calling anything under $10,000 to $20,000 'probably not worth it' [wEYqdvTPaqM @ 00:56:21]. CEDA, presenting at World Trade Center Denver, will not take a client below $250,000 in recoverable duties and refers smaller cases to other providers [SO9sA-x5K0k @ 00:28:03]. On the downside, Aaron Mandelbaum at Thompson Hine LLP lays out §1593a negligence penalties of 20%, 50%, then 100% of the loss of revenue for first, second, and subsequent violations, with fraud at 3 times. §1592 can apply concurrently, and prior disclosure cuts negligence to interest if the overpayment is tendered within 30 days [WqGvMD-AOqg @ 00:42:34]. Joe Harper at Star USA, Inc. adds $10,000 per recordkeeping violation, plus criminal exposure under 18 USC 550 and 1001. Flexport's drawback team adds that courier proof-of-delivery records expire, so they must be archived before CBP asks for them.
