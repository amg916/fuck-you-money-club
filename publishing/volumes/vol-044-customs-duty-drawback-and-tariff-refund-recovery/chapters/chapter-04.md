# Chapter 04: Section 301, AD/CVD and Bond Exposure: Refund Paths and Liability Traps

*Section 301 refunds via protest or post summary correction, refund litigation and new 301 actions, forced-labor tariffs, antidumping and countervailing duty basics and best practices, EAPA evasion allegations, customs bonds and open bond liability, and the liquidated versus unliquidated refund trap*

## Executive Summary

Customs refund operators agree that liquidation status decides everything. Unliquidated entries move through PSC or CAPE, and liquidated entries get a protest inside 180 days. Older liquidated IEEPA entries need entry-specific court orders, while CBP processes $80B on the unliquidated side. Section 301 drawback recovers up to 99% through ACE-only filing, but AD/CVD goods sit outside drawback entirely. AD/CVD scope language also overrides the HTS code, which exposes importers to retroactive 250% bills after clean 0% clearances. Bond specialists treat the bond as a revenue guarantee, not insurance. They size it at 10% of trailing annual duties, taxes and fees, with a $50K floor, and warn that one-size bumps fail nine times out of 10. Practitioners split on refund odds: Great Lakes Customs Law reports very successful protests, while CSIS panelists say small importers lose and DOJ is banking on fatigue.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every refund operator in this lineup keys the play to one variable: liquidation status. Jason Wapanik of Great Lakes Customs Law lays out the two classic Section 301 exclusion routes. The first is a post summary correction, filed through the licensed broker (usually the original filer) inside roughly 300 days of entry and before liquidation. The second is a protest within 180 days after liquidation, which lands about 310 days after import [kBvWPMXLLgs @ 00:01:41]. Ask The Customs Manager runs the same status triage for IEEPA money. Unliquidated entries wait for CAPE because PSCs are now "completely forbidden." Liquidated entries inside 180 days get a protest on a hard deadline. Anything older generally needs legal action because CBP cannot refund it [xq8bHfr9ke8 @ 00:05:19]. Freightos describes liquidation as CBP's audit of duties paid. It reports CBP processing $80B for unliquidated entries while demanding an entry-specific court order for liquidated ones, which may push importers into trade court [cIMBwZ9J60I @ 00:00:58]. CSIS pegs liquidation at 312 days and notes about $20B paid against an estimated $166B, with electronic funds transfer enrollment a prerequisite for getting paid at all [PJpF0M_rjYs @ 00:23:04]. The shared lesson: map every entry to its liquidation date before choosing a weapon.

The second consensus point is that AD/CVD exposure hides outside the tariff schedule and outside the refund machinery. Ask The Customs Manager warns that orders follow a written scope description, not the HS code. A broker sees a 0% rate and clears goods that later draw a retroactive 250% bill, plus interest and false-declaration fines, in a post-clearance audit about a year out [Ze1pOvT4KsI @ 00:01:41]. Lisa Gelino and Alexander Amdur at the NCBFAA Educational Institute make the same point: HTS numbers are guidance only, and the written scope is dispositive. AD origin can also differ from CBP origin, so Chinese cells inside Mexican panels stay subject. Because there is no drawback on AD/CVD, goods an importer refuses to pay on should never be entered for consumption [CGsvr1SDN8s @ 00:29:04]. USA Customs Clearance independently flags that goods subject to AD/CVD may be ineligible for Section 301 drawback, which kills the up-to-99% recovery play on those lines [khfmXSwD3DE @ 00:01:59]. Tyler Zaichkin of Trade Risk Guaranty closes the loop: posted rates of 30%, 50% or 100% are deposits, not final duties. If nobody requests an administrative review in the anniversary month, the deposit rate becomes final [38zRYvMd5Ps @ 00:18:04]. The rate on the entry summary is a placeholder.

Bond specialists agree the customs bond is a revenue guarantee, not a shield. Trade Risk Guaranty describes the C1, or Activity Code One, bond as a mandatory three-party contract with a Treasury-listed surety. Goods do not clear without it, the surety pays up to the bond amount, and the importer stays liable to the surety for every dollar paid out [P-dHB1oQLCI @ 00:01:55]. Freight & Trade makes the same point: a bond is not payment of duties, it protects the government's revenue stream, and "it is not insurance that eliminates the importer's responsibility." The channel lists five rookie errors, including assuming the broker absorbs responsibility and confusing bond amount with premium [ez065LeX6vE @ 00:04:37]. Yuri Starikov frames the CBP-importer-surety triangle as protection of revenue and compliance rather than punishment. The bond is posted on CBP Form 301 across consumption, AD/CVD, IT, I&E and drawback entry types, and poor customs credit can trigger a cash deposit requirement [d6omeUFeZfA @ 00:04:54]. Travis Smith of Trade Risk Guaranty ties this to the refund era. The surety is exposed for the bond amount plus the unliquidated tail, so a steady bond stacks to twice its face. AD/CVD liquidation stretching 2 to 8 years then drives full collateral demands [M68ZVWdgp9I @ 00:21:40].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

USA Customs Clearance runs Section 301 drawback as a four-move workflow. It covers China-origin goods that were exported, destroyed under customs supervision, or used as components in exports, with recovery up to 99% once each import is linked to its export in the records. The sequence is: pull CBP Form 7501 entries, match imports to exports, build the claim including the Chapter 99 code, and file through ACE, because paper claims are not accepted [khfmXSwD3DE @ 00:03:46]. Substitution drawback requires commercially interchangeable goods under the same HTS and is called especially powerful for mixed inventory. First-time filers wait 6 to 12-plus months, while accelerated payment lands in about 4 weeks. The named killers are the five-year filing deadline, HTS mismatch and weak documentation, and the parting order is not to write off 301 costs [khfmXSwD3DE @ 00:04:20]. For exclusion refunds, Wapanik stresses that protests are entry-by-entry: one protest can cover multiple entries, but there is no blanket claim. The filer must also prove the exclusion applies, with the entry paperwork in hand [kBvWPMXLLgs @ 00:03:29]. Ask The Customs Manager adds the record-pull tactic: screen entry summaries for Chapter 99 headings, and keep one ethical broker who hands over entry copies so the audit trail stays complete [xq8bHfr9ke8 @ 00:02:32].

Travis Smith gives the sizing formula Trade Risk Guaranty underwrites against. Annual duties, taxes and fees times 10% equals the minimum bond. The floor is $50K, sizes rise in $10K steps to $100K, and then in $100K steps: "there's no $25,000 import bond" [M68ZVWdgp9I @ 00:02:15]. The DTF window is a rolling 365 days, and liquidation status is ignored. A $50K bond covers $500K of DTF, and a new bond does not reset sufficiency. Going up only one size fails within 12 months "nine times out of 10." The forecast should therefore run at the IEEPA-level rate of about 25% plus standard duties, with a 5% buffer on top [M68ZVWdgp9I @ 00:15:06]. Insufficiency letters state only the minimum increase and give 30 days to act. The old bond must be terminated at least 15 days before that mark. Reason one, DTF exceeded, drives 98% of letters. Mid-term jumps from $5M to $7M to $10M stack $22M of exposure plus wasted premium, so the best move is to start at $10M and the second best is to skip $7M. Freight & Trade sets single-entry bonds at entered value plus duties, taxes and fees, separate from premium [ez065LeX6vE @ 00:03:07]. Starikov pegs other-agency bonds at 3x total entered value, excluding duties, taxes and fees [d6omeUFeZfA @ 00:09:41].

On AD/CVD, the NCBFAA panel treats ACE as the system of record and the ES-15 Active Case report as the bulk download of public case data. That data spans more than 325 orders and 140-plus commodities, which produced $1.2B in cash deposits, double the prior year [CGsvr1SDN8s @ 00:12:54]. The panel's operating rules: classify by scope, check for both AD and CVD orders on the same merchandise, track rates by exporter/producer case number, and correct errors through PSC or prior disclosure. ABI queries at 10 digits miss 8-digit flags. Ask The Customs Manager tells importers to distrust the supplier and the forwarder's software. They should manually check the AD registers for metal, wood, paper, plastics and chemicals, and get a binding scope ruling before shipping anything ambiguous [Ze1pOvT4KsI @ 00:03:04]. The panel notes that only Commerce issues those rulings. On offense, CBP's own walkthrough shows competitors filing EAPA evasion allegations through the TVR tool. The filer enters the AD/CVD number, describes the merchandise and scheme, and names the violating importer. They then upload trade data, market research, first-hand accounts and emails, and supply a public version of any confidential document [CXFkPqsyXnI @ 00:04:07]. Informants who provide original information can claim moiety compensation if a net amount is recovered [CXFkPqsyXnI @ 00:06:02].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Three refund lanes compete for the same Section 301 money, and practitioners route it differently. Attorney Jason Wapanik of Great Lakes Customs Law runs exclusion refunds two ways: a post summary correction filed through the licensed broker, likely the original filer, within about 300 days of entry and before liquidation, or a protest due within 180 days of liquidation, built entry by entry with proof the exclusion applies [kBvWPMXLLgs @ 00:01:41]. USA Customs Clearance works a different clock and pushes drawback instead: export, destroy under customs supervision, or use the goods as components, match 7501 entries to exports, attach the Chapter 99 code, file in ACE, and recover up to 99% inside a five-year window [khfmXSwD3DE @ 00:03:46]. Ask The Customs Manager draws a hard line on the IEEPA side: Section 301 lines are not eligible for the IEEPA refund program at all, and for unliquidated IEEPA entries the PSC route is now 'completely forbidden,' so operators wait for CAPE [xq8bHfr9ke8 @ 00:05:19]. The practical split: exclusion-driven 301 money moves through PSC or protest, export-driven 301 money moves through drawback, and anyone trying to fold 301 lines into an IEEPA refund claim is filing in the wrong lane [xq8bHfr9ke8 @ 00:03:20].

The sharpest split is over money already liquidated. Brightup, briefing the International Housewares Association, reports Judge Eaton at the CIT ordering refunds for all tariffs despite the 180-day protest statute, and expects no clawback on phase one, where CBP accepted $90B of $127B in claims and completed $23B [RaySaQSBqfE @ 00:02:16]. Freightos reads the same moment as a trap: CBP is processing $80B for unliquidated entries, but for liquidated entries it says it needs an entry-specific court order, which means importers may need to sue in trade court [cIMBwZ9J60I @ 00:00:58]. Ask The Customs Manager draws the line at 180 days after liquidation: inside it, file a protest as a hard deadline; beyond it, CBP generally cannot refund and legal action is the only path [xq8bHfr9ke8 @ 00:06:00]. On CSIS, the panel flags the Justice Department arguing that Trump v. CASA bars universal injunctions, leaving class actions as the fallback, and warns that small importers lose because, in one panelist's words, the department is banking on the thought that a lot of people won't bother [PJpF0M_rjYs @ 00:26:15]. Operators reading Brightup sit tight on phase one; operators reading Freightos and CSIS file protests inside 180 days and prepare entry-specific trade court suits for anything already liquidated.

Practitioners also split on how durable the new Section 301 layer is and who pays what rate. On CSIS, Alan Wolff's constitutional argument is set against Bill Reinsch, who expects narrower, fact-specific challenges to the 'unreasonable' finding, with Scott Miller siding with Reinsch [PJpF0M_rjYs @ 00:18:02]. Trade Winds Report backs the durability case: Sections 232 and 301 require an investigation record, so they are slower to impose but harder to overturn in court [xJrKO4dLXJ4 @ 00:04:47]. Cindy Allen of Simply Trade predicts 301 will fare better in court than IEEPA but still expects challenges [GSPaDDm0OVQ @ 00:06:30]. The rate map is where readings collide. CSIS puts the forced-labor 301 at 12.5% for 54 economies and 10% for the EU; Brightup calls the notice confusing and reads 16 economies, including Canada and the EU, at 10% with the other 44 at 12.5% [RaySaQSBqfE @ 00:22:08]. Allen counts more than 50 countries at 12.5%, dumped into buckets, against an 'all 60' headline. Stacking is contested too: CSIS describes the forced-labor 301 stacking on 232, turning 25% into 35% or 37.5%, while Brightup reads IEEPA and 122 exemptions carrying over with no stacking on 232. Brightup's bottom line: cumulative 301 layers stack like a cake and likely land between 30% and 40%.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The AD/CVD failures carry the biggest dollar losses, and they start with trusting the HTS number. Ask The Customs Manager walks through a $50K aluminum container drawing a $150K bill, because orders follow a written scope rather than the HS code, a broker sees 0% and clears, and a post-clearance audit about a year later brings a retroactive 250% duty plus interest plus false-declaration fines [Ze1pOvT4KsI @ 00:02:43]. Lisa Gelino and Alexander Amdur at the NCBFAA Educational Institute put hard numbers on the downside: a garlic importer hit at 367% owed $3.24M against a $50K bond, producing a $2.8M loss, and a solar importer saw a 13.8% rate deactivated to 238%, roughly $2.2M of exposure, a $300K sufficiency demand, and suspected Korea transshipment [CGsvr1SDN8s @ 01:02:44]. The same session warns that ABI queries at 10 digits miss 8-digit flags, and that AD country of origin can differ from CBP origin, so Chinese cells in Mexican panels stay subject [CGsvr1SDN8s @ 01:19:50]. Drawback does not rescue these entries either: NCBFAA says there is no drawback on AD/CVD, and USA Customs Clearance warns AD/CVD goods may be ineligible for 301 drawback [khfmXSwD3DE @ 00:01:59]. Ask The Customs Manager's fix is manual: check the AD registers for metal, wood, paper, plastics and chemicals, and get a binding scope ruling before shipping.

Bond mistakes compound quietly until a letter or a claim lands. Travis Smith of Trade Risk Guaranty warns that going up only one bond size fails within 12 months nine times out of 10, and that a new bond does not reset sufficiency because the past year's duties, taxes and fees still count [M68ZVWdgp9I @ 00:07:11]. Mid-term moves are worse: stepping a bond from $5M to $7M to $10M produces $22M of surety exposure plus wasted premium and collateral risk, where starting at $10M was the best play and skipping $7M the second best, and Smith's rule is not to change size mid-term unless Customs requires it [M68ZVWdgp9I @ 00:24:31]. AD/CVD multiplies the drag, since liquidation runs 2-8 years, deposit rates can move from 5% to 50%, and collateral only comes back after every entry liquidates plus 120 days. Freight & Trade flags the mindset errors behind these losses: treating the bond as duty payment, waiting until arrival, assuming the broker takes on responsibility, and confusing bond amount with premium, stressing the bond is not insurance that eliminates the importer's responsibility [ez065LeX6vE @ 00:04:37]. Trade Risk Guaranty's explainer closes the loop: the surety pays up to the bond amount, and the importer remains liable to the surety [P-dHB1oQLCI @ 00:02:12].

Refund cash is slower and narrower than the headline numbers suggest, and every practitioner tracking it reports a different completion figure. Brightup reports phase one at $90B accepted of $127B claimed with only $23B, about 20%, completed, and phase two is exactly what the administration is challenging [RaySaQSBqfE @ 00:45:52]. On CSIS, about $20B had been paid against $166B estimated, importers must enroll in CBP electronic funds transfer to get paid, and liquidation itself takes 312 days [PJpF0M_rjYs @ 00:23:04]. Travis Smith saw the CAPE relief arrive differently than TRG expected: instead of adjustments at liquidation, accepted filers had all ACE entries reassessed between May 5 and May 7 to standard duties plus 122, and Smith expects that relief to last only a month or two before 301 arrives [M68ZVWdgp9I @ 00:18:57]. Cindy Allen adds the operational drag: IEEPA stacking errors complicated refunds, the new 301 took effect at midnight after an end-of-day notice that left programmers under 12 hours, and importers are now using refunds to pay the new tariffs while class actions proceed [GSPaDDm0OVQ @ 00:10:33]. The economics check is simple: a refund that arrives just as a new 301 layer bills the same entries is cash recycled, not cash recovered.
