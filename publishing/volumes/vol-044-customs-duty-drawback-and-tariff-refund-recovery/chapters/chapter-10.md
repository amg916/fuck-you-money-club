## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The loudest point of agreement is that liability sits with the importer of record and never transfers to the broker. Shapiro's Director of Compliance puts it bluntly: “you can Outsource the entry responsibility but not the liability,” and flags over-reliance on brokers as the core weakness, prescribing an SOP kept on file with the broker [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:04:11]. How Stuff Moves lays out the same structure: the IOR answers for classification, value, origin, duties and fees, must hold records for five years, and hiring a broker “does not move the liability” [GGGUB13U6vk @ 00:01:00]. GHY International's Corey extends that ownership to the identity itself, listing legal name, EIN or importer number, physical and mailing addresses, phone, email and an authorized signer, and stressing that the identity “belongs to you, not the broker” [pyMQL8u25fU @ 00:09:21]. Trade Counsel supplies the contract angle: under FOB the buyer is the IOR, under DDP the seller usually is, and whoever holds the role is liable for duty and accuracy [-WXoF_u08g0 @ 00:00:35]. The operator reading is simple: the FOB-or-DDP choice decides who carries the exposure, the broker is a vendor to be supervised rather than a shield, and the IOR profile in ACE is an asset the importer has to own and police.

Every compliance voice here pushes proactive self-auditing over waiting for CBP to arrive. Shapiro's guidance is to “be proactive and audit your entries so that little problems don't turn into big problems” [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:02:16]. Jen Diaz of Diaz Trade Law calls the “hide and hope Customs doesn't notice me” posture “not a strategy” and “a huge liability,” and defines pre-compliance as reviewing classification, valuation, origin, FTA and special-program usage, AD/CVD exposure, forced-labor risk and partner-agency rules such as FDA and consumer product safety before a seizure, penalty or audit lands [j_DJeCw3cCw @ 00:00:58]. The manual is the shared backbone, but only if it runs. JSJ International's Hame Salazar frames a written compliance manual as proof of reasonable care and lists the failure pattern as generic templates, no compliance officer, no updates, no training and no broker oversight [n4YfLCDwg1M @ 00:04:25]. Former CBP auditor Kathy August reports finding identical internet-bought manuals at different companies and insists on “executable documents” such as CF-28 logs, closing with “It's not enough to have a manual, you have to execute” [Q8n9OOAYZ8s @ 00:27:18]. The consensus deliverable is a tailored, living manual tied to an actual review cadence and a named owner, not a template binder that sits untouched on a shelf until CBP calls.

On tariff refunds, the practitioners agree the money is real but the claim itself is a spotlight. IEEPA Tariff Watch stresses that refunds are not automatic, “You have to step up and actively file a formal claim to get your money,” and warns that a refund claim can trigger an audit, citing a claim that 85% of entries are misclassified [V9_1adwF1c8 @ 00:02:55]. Grassi's Michael Violano and CargoTrans' Nunzio Di Filippo size the pool at about $170B but note that the CAPE checkbox attests to classification, origin and value, turning every refund filing into a representation about the underlying entries [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:14:02]. Their closing instruction is to move with urgency while making sure “every step can stand up to an audit” [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:28:02]. Former CBP auditor Kathy August supplies the enforcement backdrop: roughly 450,000 IORs, active CBP targeting, and CF-28 requests that are rising and now cover whole entries [Q8n9OOAYZ8s @ 00:03:48]. August also notes that every company has internal controls, but they are often undocumented. The shared conclusion is to scrub entries before filing, because a refund request invites scrutiny of everything behind it, and an importer who cannot defend classification, origin and value on those entries is effectively volunteering for a review.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Shapiro's entry-audit program is the most granular build on offer. Cadence: “monthly is ideal but quarterly is fine” [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:27:37]. Audit data goes into a spreadsheet or database rather than a hard-copy sheet in the file, so reports and statistics can be extracted, and the target is at least a 95% pass rate; routing reviews through a law firm carries privilege. To benchmark against CBP, Shapiro explains focused-assessment sampling: 10% of entries if under 250, otherwise 25 to 50, pulled by line item [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:30:16]. Data is cheap: ACE reports are free, and an ITRA costs $250 to $300 and reveals unauthorized filers. The line-level checks are concrete: reconcile payables against the 7501, never take origin from the ISF sheet, and report even non-revenue errors through a quarterly PE or PSC, escalating systemic errors to an attorney for prior disclosure. Diaz Trade Law sequences the same engine as an ACE data risk assessment, entry review with an “ACE report card,” a tailored manual, employee training, periodic internal ACE audits and an error plan pairing prior disclosure with an internal escalation chain [j_DJeCw3cCw @ 00:01:36]. Jen Diaz is categorical on access: “There is zero zero reason any company should not have access to their ACE data” [j_DJeCw3cCw @ 00:01:53].

The second mechanic is defending the IOR record itself, because a voided or stale record blocks ACE access and the refund workflow that runs through it. Trade Risk Guaranty's Colette Lane lays out step one: ask the broker or bond provider for a bond query showing IOR, physical and mailing address, bond size, bond number and active or sufficient status; TRG runs it free for non-clients, usually within a day, through a QR code or its Eagle Eye ACE dashboard [PymimTwXXVM @ 00:04:25]. The fix package is a paper 5106, a POA executed by an officer such as the CEO, COO, treasurer, secretary, managing member or owner, and an IRS 147C letter preferred over a 1040, because “CBP won't have any questions if you're using a 147C” [PymimTwXXVM @ 00:06:30]. The street trick for getting the 147C is calling 1-800-829-0115, pressing 3, around 6:30 Central. GHY's Corey routes the self-check through ACE: Accounts tab, Importer view, then review records and change history [pyMQL8u25fU @ 00:17:21], grades the result green for matching, yellow for stale or red for unsubstantiated, and tells importers to log in monthly. Grassi adds the refund link: stale ACE emails get fixed with a 5106, and refund bank details sit in a separate ACE module [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:16:58].

Refund sequencing is where Grassi's team gets tactical. A CAPE CSV upload pushes entries into liquidation, which bars further PSCs, so corrections must happen before the upload, not after [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:13:07]. Phase 1 excludes warehouse, recon and AD entries; the reported numbers were a 70% rise in ACE logins, 75,000 declarations filed with 47,000 passing, 11.2M entries accepted, 2.1M rejected and 1.74M liquidated for refund [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:18:21]. The play: put the 6% interest toward an audit, file the cleanest entries first, sequence the rest, and expect money 60 to 90 days after reliquidation; the panel even suggests working ACE after hours. The downside math frames every decision. Shapiro lists negligence at 2x lost duties or 20% of dutiable value if duty-free, gross negligence at 4x or 40%, and fraud at 8x or 80% [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:06:43]. Jennifer Diaz, interviewed on Meaningful Mediation, prices prior disclosure at duties plus interest against penalty exposure listed as 2x, 4x and 5x for fraud, valid only before CBP opens an investigation, under a 5-year statute of limitations, and warns that an unpayable disclosure becomes “exhibit A” in a criminal case [OFX0_mQm-Qo @ 00:18:36].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is who files the refund claim. IEEPA Tariff Watch pushes the counsel route hard: a broker filing gives you no privilege, cannot reopen liquidated entries, and leaves you waiting one to three years, while counsel can arrange a cash advance in "a couple of weeks" [V9_1adwF1c8 @ 00:03:27]. The channel's framing is blunt: "A broker can file paperwork. An attorney can protect your rights" [V9_1adwF1c8 @ 00:04:52]. It quotes Court of International Trade suits as recovering 30-40% of refunds [V9_1adwF1c8 @ 00:04:36], and its description links out to attorneys, so the pitch carries a referral motive. Grassi and CargoTrans run the opposite playbook. They file through CAPE in ACE, put the 6% interest toward an audit, file the cleanest entries first and sequence the rest [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:23:40], all under a "hurry up and slow down" mindset [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:13:45]. Shapiro's Jane Tager sits between them on privilege. Law firm reviews carry privilege [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:29:28], but the core rule is that you can outsource the entry responsibility but not the liability [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:04:11]. Operator read: whichever channel files, the importer of record owns every attestation, so privilege matters most when your entry data is dirty.

Practitioners do not agree on the fraud ceiling, and the spread matters when you price a disclosure. Shapiro's Tager teaches a multiplier ladder. Negligence runs 2x lost duties, or 20% of dutiable value if duty-free [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:06:43]. Gross negligence runs 4x or 40%, and fraud runs 8x or 80% [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:07:20]. VoraPrep matches 2x and 4x but says fraud "can be up to the entire domestic value of the merchandise itself, not the lost duties, the value of the goods" [xq7kXAXH3GQ @ 00:03:47]. Jennifer Diaz, on Meaningful Mediation, puts fraud exposure at 5x when comparing a prior disclosure against a penalty case [OFX0_mQm-Qo @ 00:18:36]. The cost of disclosing also differs by culpability. VoraPrep says negligence or gross negligence disclosures pay duties plus interest, while fraud disclosures pay 1x lost duties [xq7kXAXH3GQ @ 00:04:54]. On the back end, Diaz quotes mitigation guidelines of 10-30% and expects stricter guidelines for repeat offenders. Diaz also describes three bites: petition, supplemental petition, court [OFX0_mQm-Qo @ 00:43:14]. Diaz reports a $4M penalty reduced to $4,000 using inability to pay, first offense, and CBP's own errors [OFX0_mQm-Qo @ 00:15:49]. Operator read: model your worst case on the harshest number any of these practitioners quotes, then decide whether disclosure is cheaper.

Practitioners also split on what a compliance manual is for. JSJ International's Hame Salazar treats a written manual as proof of reasonable care under 19 USC 1484 [n4YfLCDwg1M @ 00:01:14]. Salazar lists the failure set as generic templates, no compliance officer, no updates, and no training or broker oversight [n4YfLCDwg1M @ 00:04:25]. Former CBP auditor Kathy August, on Simply Trade Podcast, goes further. Identical internet-bought manuals turn up at different companies [Q8n9OOAYZ8s @ 00:07:54], so August says to build executable documents such as CF-28 logs [Q8n9OOAYZ8s @ 00:08:10], because "It's not enough to have a manual, you have to execute" [Q8n9OOAYZ8s @ 00:27:18]. Diaz Trade Law puts the manual third in the workflow. First comes an ACE data risk assessment, then an entry review with an ACE report card, then the manual. Training, periodic internal ACE audits, and an error plan with a prior disclosure route follow [j_DJeCw3cCw @ 00:01:36]. Audit cadence also varies. Shapiro's Tager says monthly is ideal but quarterly is fine [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:27:37], and wants audit data recorded electronically so reports and statistics can be extracted [cjNgBgUI1As @ 00:28:07]. Operator read: Salazar's manual earns the reasonable-care defense on paper, while August's and Diaz's playbooks make that paper survive a CF-28 with live logs, ACE pulls, and an escalation chain.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The quietest money-killer is a voided or hijacked importer of record. Trade Risk Guaranty reports that brokers sometimes entered their own address or phone on the 5106, which was "never intended," and that CBP scheduled a 30-day scrub starting September 18 [PymimTwXXVM @ 00:02:23]. Fixes that normally took five business days now run 10-plus days because CBP is "at capacity" [PymimTwXXVM @ 00:03:19], and even correct data may be voided [PymimTwXXVM @ 00:07:27]. GHY International's Corey adds that CBP deactivated 5.3 million IOR numbers earlier in 2026 [pyMQL8u25fU @ 00:06:04]. Corey also says unknown filer codes are "symptoms of fraud" [pyMQL8u25fU @ 00:12:19], and that pre-2019 records without an email block ACE signup [pyMQL8u25fU @ 00:16:15]. This collides with refunds. Grassi's team tells claimants to fix stale ACE emails with a 5106 before filing, and notes that refund bank details live in a separate ACE module [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:16:58]. Economics check: a refund claim is only as good as the IOR record it lands on. Trade Risk Guaranty's fix kit is a paper 5106, a POA executed by an officer, and an IRS 147C letter, because "CBP won't have any questions if you're using a 147C" [PymimTwXXVM @ 00:06:30].

Refund filings carry their own reversal risk. Grassi and CargoTrans warn that a CAPE CSV upload pushes entries into liquidation, which bars further PSCs [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:13:07]. The upload checkbox also attests to classification, origin and value [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:14:02]. Phase 1 numbers show the friction: 75,000 declarations filed, 47,000 passed, 11.2M entries accepted and 2.1M rejected [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:18:21]. IEEPA Tariff Watch claims 85% of entries are misclassified, so the refund claim itself can trigger an audit [V9_1adwF1c8 @ 00:02:55]. August's warning is that "Customs is a a worthy chess player" [Q8n9OOAYZ8s @ 00:12:50]. Economics check: a refund attestation over dirty entries trades a known refund for unknown penalty exposure. Grassi's answer is to go after the low-hanging fruit first and spend the 6% interest on an audit [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:24:14]. The closing line: "Move with urgency, but you need to make sure that every step can stand up to an audit" [xA8LxqtatDo @ 00:28:02].

The foreign IOR rule makes DDP setups a failure mode on a fixed date.
