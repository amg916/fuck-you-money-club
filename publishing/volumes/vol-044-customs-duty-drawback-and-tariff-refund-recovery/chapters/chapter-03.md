# Chapter 03: IEEPA Tariff Refunds: Claiming the $166 Billion and Who Gets It

*The February 20, 2026 ruling, the CAPE refund process and interest, why filing now matters, IEEPA refunds versus drawback, refund buyout financing, and why refunds flow to importers of record like Target rather than shoppers*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties flows only to the importer of record, through CBP's CAPE module inside ACE, with refunds paid by ACH plus interest. Downstream buyers and shoppers hold no government claim. Their only roads back are carrier-collected duties and contract leverage over the importer. The street mechanics come down to data discipline: pull the ES003 report, map every entry's liquidation status against the 80-day cutoff, and file clean declarations. Only about 70% passed initial validation, and an accepted filing cannot be amended. As of October 2, about $126 billion was certified to Treasury. Practitioners disagree on three points. The first is speed, which ranges from one week to 90 days against a quoted 2 to 5 years. The second is whether to file protective Court of International Trade suits. The third is claim-buyout pricing, which moved from about 20 cents to 55-75 cents on the dollar.

## The Practitioner Consensus: The Name on the Entry Gets Paid

Every practitioner lands on the same hard rule: the refund follows the customs entry, not the wallet. CBP's own filings put the pot at roughly $166 billion collected from more than 330,000 importers across 53 million entries. Michael Watson of Tariffs Lawsuit frames it as money the business already paid out, not money it hopes to win [DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:01:16]. PolitiClash reads CBP's refund page bluntly: only the importer of record, or the broker it authorized, may file a declaration listing every entry claimed. No declaration means no refund [uhEb06Zt2cw @ 00:02:15]. Robert Shapiro of Thompson Coburn, a former customs broker, draws the line for anyone who bought on delivered-duty-paid terms. If the supplier paid the duty, the buyer holds no claim against the government, only a possible commercial claim against that supplier [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:06:17]. Ted Row of Descartes, speaking on the Leyton webinar, gives the same answer to downstream purchasers who absorbed tariff uplifts from a US seller. The quick and easy answer is no, because the refund is granted specifically to the company that legally handled the importation [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:06:46]. So the first diligence question in any engagement is not how much duty was paid. It is whose name sat on the entry when that duty cleared the border.

The second point of agreement is eligibility scope and plumbing. Dave Maguire of Maguire Sponsel, presenting with Lutz, limits the refund to IEEPA duties on imports between February 2025 and February 2026. He stresses that steel and aluminum tariffs, the first-term washing machine tariffs, and anything outside IEEPA do not qualify, even if the business is still paying them today [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:04:08]. Jade Christopher at Evana, a licensed broker with 15 years working exclusively in drawback, adds that IEEPA duties are not drawback-eligible and run only through the separate CAPE phases. Standard ad valorem duties, Section 301 duties, some Section 232 duties, and MPF and HMF stay inside drawback at up to 99% [YVqE1V68BkI @ 00:01:00]. Every operator then routes the claim through ACE. Row notes that before the refund push, only about 43 to 45% of US importers had an account on the free portal. Refunds now move only by ACH into a banking profile entered inside ACE [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:08:12]. Shapiro treats the ACE account as dual-purpose. It is the source of the import data that shapes the claim, and it is the only channel through which electronically issued refunds reach the importer's bank [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:13:23].

The third consensus is urgency. Maguire warns that the administration is appealing the universal refund order and could shut processing down while appeals run, so anyone sitting on money should move as fast as possible. He adds that the appeal targets whether non-suing importers must be paid, not whether refunds already received get clawed back [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:17:06]. PolitiClash lays out the litigation backdrop. The Court of International Trade ordered universal refunds on March 4, and the Justice Department appealed. In October the court certified the Freestyle World class, covering importers who paid between February 1, 2025 and February 19, 2026 without suing on their own [uhEb06Zt2cw @ 00:04:01]. Border & Bench cuts the class hype down. Certification is not a refund order: members are paid only if a class-wide order issues and Customs processes their entries. The order also does not say whether it pauses the two-year clock to sue [Zq36LwbWWJc @ 00:05:33]. Watts Law Firm, on the Tariffs Lawsuit channel, pushes clients to file their own Court of International Trade case. That way they sit directly in front of Judge Eaton if the government's standing argument wins [dWEik4YquEE @ 00:00:35]. The shared read is that delay burns money and options, whichever lane an importer chooses.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: ES003 to ACH Deposit

The mechanics are a data-matching exercise. Ted Row describes the workflow in three moves: pull the ES003 report from ACE, filter every entry tagged with IEEPA duty, and rebuild that data in CAPE format. The result is one consolidated mass adjustment instead of individual post-summary corrections or protests [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:08:57]. Freightos calls the upload close to a copy-paste of the protest flow: a spreadsheet of entry numbers and duty paid, plus supporting details. An automated pass runs first, then a manual CBP review that the firm expects to be strict. That manual stage is where Freightos says a customs broker earns its place, and it plans to file automatically for many of its own customers [bZWPrOasGFY @ 00:01:40]. Row adds that ACE reportedly uses AI validation to confirm the recreated submission matches the original entry data. Mismatched claims get rejected and must be resubmitted. Once a filing is clean, CBP works to a 45-day refund target [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:13:53]. Watson's numbers show why precision is everything. By CBP's own reporting, only about 70% of declarations passed initial validation. They failed on mismatched data, file formatting, missing banking setup, or entry numbers already used in a prior submission, and an accepted declaration cannot be amended [DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:01:53].

Speed claims vary, but practitioners report money moving fast once data is clean. Maguire notes that the government quotes 60 to 90 days from filing, yet his team is seeing refunds land in as little as a week or two [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:14:17]. PolitiClash gives the scoreboard from CBP's October 2 filing: about $136.6 billion accepted into the pipeline and about $126 billion, interest included, certified to Treasury. Roughly $29 billion of the pool had not even entered the pipeline yet [uhEb06Zt2cw @ 00:03:20]. Border & Bench flags the cheapest leak to plug. As of October 2, about $1.1 billion in refunds was held up purely because importers had not entered bank details for electronic payment [Zq36LwbWWJc @ 00:04:30]. Maguire's phase arithmetic drives triage. Phase one covered about 80% of tariffs paid and phase two roughly another 15%, so most dollars sit in the cheap lanes. In his hypothetical, $100,000 comes back in phase one and $15,000 more in phase two. A $5,000 phase-three residue that costs $4,000 to chase may not be worth pursuing, while $50,000 stuck in phase three probably is [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:25:09]. Recovery cost rises at each phase, so operators should size every bucket before committing spend to it.

Pricing for outside help clusters around contingency. JD Remete of Leyton says there is no upfront charge and no financial risk. The fee is a percentage of the refund only, nothing is owed if the client does not qualify, and ACE and CAPE setup are bundled into that percentage [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:34:07]. Opscale Exchange runs the same no-pay-until-Customs-pays model. Shapiro says he keeps small files affordable by spreading work across his larger clients with big recoveries. Opscale also offers products that put cash in hand within 5 to 10 days. Its free ES003 review found that a client who believed he had filed $2.1 million still had another $1 million unclaimed [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:16:56]. For importers who want cash now, Commercial Funding Marketplace describes selling the economic rights through a participation agreement, because the claim itself cannot legally be transferred. The importer stays the claimant, receives the refund, and forwards the buyer's share. Offers have moved from about 22 cents on the dollar to 55% to 75% [LaWXf8aLv1o @ 00:03:29]. Casually Capital cites Sidley's account of press reports showing claims trading around 20 cents before the ruling and as high as 60 afterward. Fortune reported importers selling for roughly a quarter of the tariffs they paid [yHFIXvO5siM @ 00:02:15].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Open ACE, load banking, grant proxy access
   - Stand up the free ACE account with US dollar ACH banking and give the filer proxy access.
   - The importer must open the account itself; providers can only walk it through setup. Proxy access lets a third party pull entries and reports without touching finance settings [Leyton USA — JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:12:50]
2. Pull ES003 and map liquidation status
   - Audit every entry, port and duty line from the tariff period and sort each by liquidation status.
   - Liquidation status decides which deadline and which path applies to each entry, so map all of them before filing anything [Tariffs Lawsuit — DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:04:03]
3. Build and validate the CAPE declaration
   - Rebuild the IEEPA-tagged entries in CAPE format and match the original entry data exactly.
   - Remove entry numbers used in prior submissions and fix formatting first, because an accepted CAPE declaration cannot be amended and bad data forces a new filing [Tariffs Lawsuit — DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:02:26]
4. Route out-of-window entries to court or protest
   - For entries beyond the 80-day post-liquidation window, file a Court of International Trade action or a protest.
   - Shapiro calls a Court of International Trade filing pretty much guaranteed to produce a refund, while protest timing is uncertain; a customs broker cannot file the court action [Opscale Exchange — t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:11:52]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to sit inside CAPE or force your way into court. Michael Watson's firm at Tariffs Lawsuit pushes every client to file a claim at the Court of International Trade now, because the government's appeal argues Judge Eaton can only order refunds for parties standing in front of that court [dWEik4YquEE @ 00:00:35]. Robert Shapiro at Thompson Coburn lands in a similar place for older paper: once an entry sits outside the 80-day post-liquidation window, Customs has no authority to act alone, a protest is uncertain and has no timeline, but a court action is pretty much guaranteed to produce a refund [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:11:52]. Dave Maguire of Maguire Sponsel, on the Lutz webinar, takes the opposite temperature: roughly 80% of tariffs sit in phase one and another 15% in phase two, so most importers are covered without litigation, and the appeal targets only whether non-suing importers must be paid, so money already received will not be clawed back [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:09:23]. Border & Bench splits the difference, noting the October 8 class certification is not a refund order and does not say whether it pauses the two-year filing clock, so a protective lawsuit may still be the safer route for large exposures [Zq36LwbWWJc @ 00:06:02].

The second fight is over who actually pushes the button. Freightos tells importers who already have ACE access that anyone can file their own refund, yet still recommends routing it through a broker, because CAPE's automated check feeds a manual CBP review where scrutiny gets heavy [bZWPrOasGFY @ 00:00:32]. Opscale Exchange and Thompson Coburn report the opposite risk: clients who leaned on their customs broker were still asking how the broker missed a million dollars, and Shapiro points out that a broker cannot file an action in court, which matters more every month as entries age out of the 80-day window [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:24:08]. Maguire describes brokerages that simply emailed clients the steps and said it is all on you [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:16:45]. Ted Row of Descartes, on the Leyton USA webinar, adds that big carrier-brokers like FedEx and UPS will not initiate anything without notice, because the refund stems from policy rather than a filing mistake and carries real cost to process, so the importer of record has to start it [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:33:26]. The practical split: self-file or broker-file for clean unliquidated entries, but bring in a specialist or trade counsel once entries need a protest, a court case, or an ES003 audit for what got skipped.

Timing estimates are all over the map, and that spread is what powers the claim-buyout trade. Commercial Funding Marketplace pitches a two-to-five-year wait as the normal path and offers the exit: sell the economic rights through a participation agreement, take cash in two to three weeks, and accept 55% to 75% of claim value, up from roughly 22 cents before the court rulings [LaWXf8aLv1o @ 00:03:29]. The buyer still demands audited financials and an ACE report matching government data exactly, because it stays partnered with you for years. Casually Capital tracked the same market from the outside, citing Sidley on claims trading near 20 cents before the ruling and up to 60 after, plus Fortune reports of importers selling for roughly a quarter of what they paid, then noting about $126 billion was certified in under eight months anyway [yHFIXvO5siM @ 00:02:15]. On the ground the clock looks far faster: Ted Row says CBP targets 45 days and expects close adherence when entries match [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:15:17], while Maguire reports refunds landing in as little as a week or two against the official 60-to-90-day guidance [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:14:17]. Casually Capital frames the discount as a price on timing and legal risk, set when nobody knew whether refunds would take months or years.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first wall is CAPE validation itself. Tariffs Lawsuit cites CBP's own reporting that only about 70% of declarations passed initial validation, so roughly one in three bounced on technicalities before anyone reviewed the merits: data that did not match CBP's records, file formatting errors, missing account or banking setup, and entry numbers already used in a prior submission [DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:02:08]. Worse, once a declaration is accepted it cannot be amended; bad data that slips through forces a brand-new filing and burns time you may not have [DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:02:26]. Watson's firm says the businesses getting paid cleanly came in with someone who understood the entry data and deadlines before filing. Ted Row explains the mechanism on the Leyton USA webinar: ACE reportedly uses an element of AI to check that the recreated CAPE entry matches the original entry data, and any mismatch gets the claim rejected for resubmission [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:14:33]. Freightos warns that after the automated pass, claims move to a manual CBP review where scrutiny tightens, and that is the stage where you want a broker in the loop [bZWPrOasGFY @ 00:01:55]. The common thread: build every claim from the ACE pull and run it clean the first time, because the system punishes rework far harder than it rewards speed.

The second trap is plumbing, not law. Finn's Fine Print reports that by early November Customs had approved about $128.4 billion with interest, yet about $737 million sat trapped because importers had never given Customs their bank details: the coat check only hands coats to people who show up [Pf2fDVQXfmk @ 00:03:45]. Border & Bench put the October 2 figure for the same failure at about $1.1 billion delayed [Zq36LwbWWJc @ 00:04:30]. Ted Row explains why: CBP now pays these refunds only by ACH, no paper checks, and a Canadian non-resident importer learned the account must be a US bank account denominated in USD [JL0JHTlUx4I @ 00:24:39]. Getting into ACE is its own bottleneck, since only 43 to 45% of importers used it before CAPE. Opscale Exchange flags a September 9 congressional letter after CBP failed to approve some importers' ACE accounts in time, and the banana-importer client on that call spent most of the setup effort reactivating access, sitting on hold, and coordinating a top-account token before landing a proxy account [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:15:28]. The Leyton panel adds that a new ACE application can be rejected if the 5106 contact data does not match CBP's records, and lockouts during peak volume took the ACE help desk about a week to reset.

Deadlines and after-tax math eat the rest. Shapiro calls out Customs playing dirty tricks: some entries were liquidated a month after import instead of near the usual 314 days, pushing them outside the CAPE window, and Mexico entries carried a 25% rate against 10 to 15% elsewhere, so early-2025 Mexico paper fell out of the window by May, leaving court as the only door [t98WTC5Cm9o @ 00:25:42]. Watson's firm warns protest rights generally expire 180 days after liquidation and that which deadline applies depends on each entry's status, something owners cannot see without pulling the data [DOHCXtY_zI0 @ 00:03:14]. Maguire runs the economics check most operators skip. A $300,000 refund on a $1.3 million machine depreciated at a 30% tax rate nets about $210,000 once the $90,000 basis swing is unwound, and it may force an amended return or accounting-method change [6Y30Cf9AeYE @ 00:23:03]. The same webinar sets a cost floor for phase three: $5,000 still outstanding that costs $4,000 to recover may not be worth pursuing, while $50,000 probably is. Plainly Public adds the taxpayer side of delay, citing a Cato estimate that interest piled up at about $700 million a month while refunds waited [h-PcJgVckuw @ 00:05:17].
