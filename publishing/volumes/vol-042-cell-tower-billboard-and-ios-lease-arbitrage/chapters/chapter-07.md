# Chapter 07: Starting and Building a Billboard Company

*Seven startup steps from ordinance research, land lease vs. easement vs. buying land, land lease contracts, building on your own property, Frank Rolfe's bootcamp, the $3M rented-billboard model, and operator interviews from the field*

## Executive Summary

Billboard operators agree that the permit, not the structure, is the asset. Highway Beautification Act grandfathering, county moratoriums and cap-and-replace laws freeze supply and give incumbents pricing power. Site filters cluster around 10,000 to 20,000 daily vehicles. Landowners take roughly 15 to 20 percent of gross, and leases run 20 years or longer, with rent starting only once the sign is permitted, built and powered. Build costs run from $5,000 wood structures to $100,000-plus digitals. One static two-face board nets about $68,000 a year, and a digital conversion turns two ad slots into sixteen. Operators split on four questions: owning land or leasing it, flat or percentage rent, converting to digital or keeping static faces, and developing new sites or slowly buying distressed boards and portfolios.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every serious operator here treats the permit, not the steel, as the thing being bought and sold. Mr. Money frames the whole business around one line: 'the permit is the asset' [t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:01:37]. Business Breakdown traces the moat to the 1965 Highway Beautification Act. The Act froze supply and left grandfathered 'non-conforming' signs that new entrants cannot replicate, which gives whoever holds them pricing power over advertisers [exXUEqaAB2k @ 00:10:03]. Ken McElroy sees the same squeeze in Nevada. Clark County has stopped approving new boards, and only a few brokers work the niche. McElroy's group buys fixed two-sided boards sitting on 25-to-50-year leases without ever owning the land [bteMgQqmSCk @ 00:05:40]. REtipster, reviewing Frank Rolfe's bootcamp, points to Michigan's cap-and-replace law: a new billboard goes up only if another one comes down somewhere else. The same review lists Vermont, Hawaii, Maine and Alaska as outright bans [Yo8dSroHdMk @ 00:06:45]. All four speakers draw the same conclusion. Regulation manufactures the scarcity, so a legally permitted face is a hard-to-copy position, and the edge goes to whoever locks up the site and the paperwork before the rules close the door.

On site quality, the practitioners apply one filter before anything else: eyeballs. Bama Billboards screens for daily traffic above 10,000 vehicles and marks stoplight locations as preferable when building a target list [rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:02:03]. Franchise Inspectors works from Frank Rolfe's playbook. It sets a floor of 5,000 vehicles a day for off-highway locations and 20,000 on a highway, then checks whether advertisers exist nearby, the rent spread, and legality, confirmed with the state first and City Hall second [mNNTe8Yz_PI @ 00:04:28]. Codie Sanchez comes from the digital-platform side and lands in the same band of 10,000 to 20,000 cars a day. Sanchez adds two qualifiers: pick growing areas such as Miami's Brickell, and make sure the passing crowd matches the demographic the advertiser wants [DXWpZSGVI4w @ 00:09:44]. Clear Multifamily gives real estate investors the simplest mental model: think of the billboard as a 'mini mall,' because the more eyeballs a location gets, the more it is worth [LMD9ecO4o1M @ 00:02:51]. The working floor is therefore roughly 10,000 daily vehicles for local boards and 20,000 for highway boards. Growth trajectory and audience fit break ties between otherwise similar parcels, and visibility from the travel lane is part of every check.

The third point of agreement is the ground deal: the landowner gets a minority cut and the operator gets a long runway. Mr. Money pegs the landowner's take at 15 to 20 percent of gross revenue [t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:02:23]. Bama Billboards opens with a low flat rate or an 18 to 20 percent revenue split, whichever is higher. Bama argues this protects the landowner from inflation and keeps them happy, and lowers the split on expensive builds [rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:02:54]. Shaun Academy's instructor, Sarah, sets the same ceiling: ground rent should not exceed about 20 percent of revenue. Sarah benchmarks expected advertising rates at $800 to $5,000 a month, or $200 to $300 in a small town [dzIF5i2KhaA @ 00:01:10]. On term, Robert Wallace accepts nothing shorter than 20 years. The alternative is a 10-year term with two or three automatic renewals, which stretches control to 40 to 60 years [0Lv5_kKNNWg @ 00:03:42]. The baseline, then, is a ground lease that keeps land cost near one-fifth of revenue and holds the site long enough to justify the build. Revenue-split language should scale down as structure cost rises, so the landowner stays whole without eating the margin on a monopole or digital.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Execution starts at the desk and finishes on the road. Bama Billboards begins by pulling 10 to 15 candidate parcels from the county GIS and recording the PPIN and owner details for each. Every parcel is then checked against spacing rules of 600 to 1,500 feet from other boards and 600 to 1,000 feet from commercial activity, and only then do landowner letters go out [rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:01:30]. Mr. Money prices the state permit at $500 to $2,000, with a 60-to-120-day wait between application and approval [t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:04:27]. Justin Booher of Vital Outdoor shows what reading the code line by line can produce. Booher's fifth site came from a rare CBD zoning class, the last one listed in the sign regulations, and the ODOT permit was secured first, before anything else moved [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 01:01:04]. Operators on The Bell Board Podcast push back on pure screen work. They tell new entrants to ride the roads and join the local chamber, because 'you can't just do it by Google Maps' [r6MxdTpj3sg @ 00:19:43]. The working sequence is a GIS pull, an ordinance and spacing check, a physical drive-by, landowner contact, and then the permit filing. Each step either kills the site cheaply or feeds the next one.

Operators use the lease to protect cash before the first ad runs. Robert Wallace's template runs 360 months and starts rent only after the permits are obtained, the structure is installed and ads are posted. Wallace reports this trigger has worked many times and warns against paying monthly land rent from the day the contract is signed [hxwD9JLVZs8 @ 00:05:26]. Justin Booher writes the same protection into Vital Outdoor leases: rental does not start until the sign is fully built and has power to it [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:27:37]. Mr. Money adds an exit: smart operators include a clause that lets them cancel the lease without penalty if the permits fail [t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:04:49]. Wallace calls assignability the most important sentence in the lease. It lets an operator with no capital sign leases and sell them, ideally without the landlord's written consent [hxwD9JLVZs8 @ 00:19:46]. The rest of the Wallace template builds on those terms. It gives the tenant sole permit rights and requires the landlord to clear obstructions within 24 hours. It bars competing off-premise signs on adjacent land the landlord controls. It lets the tenant terminate on 30 days' notice if visibility or traffic drops, and it sends condemnation compensation to the tenant.

Practitioners give hard numbers for both build costs and revenue. Bama Billboards prices structures on a ladder: wood at $5,000 to $10,000 and an I-frame at $15,000 to $20,000. A monopole runs $50,000 to $90,000 and has the best resale multiple. A digital board costs $100,000 to $200,000 and carries six to eight advertiser slots [rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:04:51]. Mr. Money's model starts with $12,000 in monthly gross, then subtracts 18 percent ($2,160) for the landowner and about $1,500 in costs. That leaves roughly $8,000 a month, or about $96,000 a year [t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:03:50]. Business Breakdown's Elena case is an $85,000 build on a $9,000-a-year ground lease. Two faces at about $3,500 a month bring in $84,000 a year, and roughly $16,000 in costs leaves about $68,000 net, a margin above 80 percent [exXUEqaAB2k @ 00:04:12]. Ken McElroy's Columbus, Ohio deal shows the larger-scale version: about $20,000 a month top line, roughly $250,000 gross a year, and $180,000 to $190,000 net on a $1 million cost [bteMgQqmSCk @ 00:01:50]. Each speaker separates out the same two variables: the landowner's share of gross and the choice of structure type. Those two numbers set both the upfront capital and the monthly spread, and they determine how fast a board pays back its build.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Screen the ordinance and jurisdiction
   - Find out whether the city or the county controls the site and record its spacing rules before choosing any parcel.
   - Check spacing of 600-1,500 ft to other boards and 600-1,000 ft to commercial activity, and confirm city versus county jurisdiction [Bama Billboards — rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:00:41]. In Booher's fifth site, reading the zoning list all the way to the last class turned up a site competitors had missed [The Bell Board Podcast — yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 01:01:04].
2. Build and qualify the target list
   - Pull 10-15 parcels from the county GIS, record the PPIN and owner, and filter by traffic count.
   - Keep parcels with more than 10,000 vehicles a day and prefer stoplight locations [Bama Billboards — rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:02:03]. Drive every site and join the chamber instead of relying on Google Maps [The Bell Board Podcast — r6MxdTpj3sg @ 00:19:43].
3. Sign a lease that protects cash and resale
   - Offer a flat rate or an 18-20% split, whichever is higher, with rent deferred until the board is live and full assignability.
   - Rent starts only after permits, installation and posted ads [Robert_Wallace — hxwD9JLVZs8 @ 00:05:26]. Include assignability so the lease can be sold without the landlord's consent [Robert_Wallace — hxwD9JLVZs8 @ 00:19:46], and add penalty-free cancellation if the permits fail [Mr. Money — t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:04:49].
4. Permit, build and sell the faces
   - Submit the permit package, choose a structure type, then fill the faces through signage, mail and move-over calls.
   - The permit package needs the lease, the deed, drawings and insurance or a bond [Bama Billboards — rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:03:47]. Once the board is up, call existing advertisers up and down the highway and undercut their current rate to move them onto your face [CREUniversity — QDvEU9XEEA4 @ 00:01:24].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split among operators is whether to rent the dirt or own it. Robert_Wallace stays on standard ground leases because the parcels in play sit on $2–3M land, and when a landowner asks for an easement instead, the expected price is a lump sum equal to 8 to 15 years of rent paid upfront [0Lv5_kKNNWg @ 00:03:20]. Dan Franklin, a third-generation operator, runs the opposite playbook: the company owns land or easements under roughly 30% of about 501 sites, and that slice produces about 35% of revenue [nCrchtKHbFw @ 00:03:29]. Host Neil Bell pushed back in the same episode, warning that easements cannot be depreciated and that roads can move; Franklin's counter is to check future road plans before paying and to note that a $10,000 easement carries far less exposure [nCrchtKHbFw @ 00:04:15]. Franchise Inspectors frames ownership in the bluntest terms: put the sign on land you already own and the ground-rent line vanishes, which they call 100 percent margin [mNNTe8Yz_PI @ 00:05:18]. Leasing preserves capital and lets a thin-wallet operator stack sites quickly; owning the dirt trades cash today for permanent rent elimination, and the operators who choose it are betting the road stays put and the permit stays live.

Practitioners also split on how the landowner gets paid. Bama Billboards opens with a letter offering a low flat rate or an 18–20% revenue split, whichever is higher, arguing the structure protects the landowner from inflation and keeps them happy, and offers a lower split on sites that are costly to build [rxbKA112Tdc @ 00:02:54]. Dan Franklin pushes the other way: the company's leases are mostly flat, because percentage deals breed disputes, down to an argument over a $17 trade for window blinds [nCrchtKHbFw @ 00:47:38]. The flat camp is buying peace; the split camp is buying landowner goodwill that rises alongside the board's revenue. Timing of the first rent check is contested as well. Justin Booher of Vital Outdoor uses a lease stating that rental does not start until the sign is fully built and has power to it, which means the landowner waits out every month of permitting and construction [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:27:37]. The host of the same episode runs a different trigger, with rent starting within 180 days of permits being issued [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:28:50]. Booher's clause shields the operator's cash through a slow build, while the host's clause hands the landowner a fixed outer date for the first payment, so an operator has to pick which side absorbs construction delays.

Digital is where the loudest disagreement lives. Ken McElroy pitches conversion as pure multiplication: spend $250–400K and a two-sided board goes from two ads to 16, eight per side [bteMgQqmSCk @ 00:00:58]. On paper that is the same structure, the same ground lease and the same permit carrying eight times the inventory. Business Breakdown backs the upside with a case where converting one face more than doubled its income [exXUEqaAB2k @ 00:14:04]. Justin Booher lived the downside. Converting a static board to digital cost the existing advertiser, and some buyers balked at paying the same price for a one-eighth share of the screen [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:12:38]. Shaun Academy flags the same friction from the advertiser side: some businesses are reluctant to share an electronic billboard that changes advertisements every 10 seconds [dzIF5i2KhaA @ 00:02:51]. The practical split is between operators who see digital as an occupancy multiplier and operators who know their anchor tenants bought exclusivity. An anchor paying for a static face is not automatically a buyer of one slot in a rotation, so the conversion math only works if the market has enough short-term and small-budget advertisers to refill the freed slots.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start on paper, not on the pole. On The Bell Board Podcast, an operator named Jonathan spent 13 months landing what was called the best lease yet: 50 years, fixed rate, $2,000 a year. The steel was delivered, then the state took the property, and because the lease had never been filed with the county as an addendum, the team expects no reimbursement while the steel sits on a farm [r6MxdTpj3sg @ 00:01:31]. Robert_Wallace's template closes that hole from the other side by routing condemnation compensation to the tenant, not the landlord [hxwD9JLVZs8 @ 00:15:14]. Mr. Money shows what that clause can be worth, pointing to St. Paul paying $4.3M for a single digital board taken by eminent domain [t4mEx2qxaZ8 @ 00:06:25]. Acquisitions carry their own paper traps. Dan Franklin deliberately buys messes, yet still lists surprise ownership claims, landlords who had gone unpaid for two years, and overpaying for land among the mistakes made along the way [nCrchtKHbFw @ 00:12:47]. The pattern across all four: the structure is only as safe as the recorded lease, the condemnation clause and the title work behind it, and a missing filing can zero out a site that cost more than a year to sign.

Weather and the non-conforming rulebook combine into the second trap. Business Breakdown walks through Elena's storm loss: grandfathered non-conforming signs lose their protection if reconstruction runs above roughly 50% of replacement value, so instead of rebuilding, the damage was repaired and every cost documented, because a fresh build would have voided the protection and, as the narrator put it, "She could not replace it" [exXUEqaAB2k @ 00:11:18]. Wind is the routine killer. A 50-foot backlit fuel-sign vinyl blew out twice, each time with a rented lift, until a breathable mesh replacement survived later storms [r6MxdTpj3sg @ 00:04:51]. Justin Booher reports wind frames blowing out vinyl even with a self-promo underneath, which is why Booher prefers metal faces, especially on remote signs [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:45:11]. Lightning is the digital-specific version: a one-year-old Booher digital was struck on Father's Day 2025, insurance with loss-of-income coverage paid to reface both sides, and Watchfire would only write a letter saying it could not cover latent damage [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:47:15]. The economics check is simple: a static face blown out twice costs two lift rentals and two prints, while an uninsured, unprotected hilltop digital can take a full face replacement in one strike.

Fights over permits and dirt eat the margin quietly. Justin Booher's ninth sign drew a lawsuit from a wealthy neighborhood against the city over the permit, triggering a stop-work order; an offer of free nonprofit ad space was refused, and although Booher won on the TxDOT 660-foot question and again on the district-court appeal, the fight ran 4 years and cost $40K, after which the street got an overlay banning new signs [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:30:18]. The same build produced a second hit: concrete filled an unmarked private sewer lateral, roughly 150–200 feet had to be dug up, and Booher brought in porta-potties for the affected businesses [yE0a5pCCcZ0 @ 00:35:33]. Dan Franklin drills foundations nine times in ten and has a well driller probe a 100–150-foot window first, so soil surprises show up before the crew mobilizes [nCrchtKHbFw @ 00:18:37]. On the revenue side, CREUniversity sets a hard clock: if the phone number on a rent-this-sign face draws no call within 30 days, it will not, so a dead board needs move-over calls, direct mail and exit-now lists instead of patience [QDvEU9XEEA4 @ 00:00:17]. Legal bills, utility damage and empty faces all hit the same early cash flow, so each one needs a budget line before the build starts.
