# Chapter 03: Negotiating the Cell Tower Lease

*Seven deadly sins and five costly blunders, the property owner's perspective, ten questions to ask before signing, escalators and rent bumps, AI-assisted negotiation, and why carriers are not your partners*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Tower Genius, Vertical Consultants, JP Tower Consulting, and Site Concepts agree the carrier side is an adversary, that cell sites are priced as utility infrastructure rather than real estate, and that market rent is a myth because every site carries its own value. Street mechanics center on refusing to name a number first, refusing term sheets, and treating time, space, and use as commodities: give 2,000 to 3,000 square feet instead of 10,000, demand 3% annual escalators or CPI, and structure revenue share as a percentage instead of a flat $200. Odum's Ohio client went from about $900 to over $6,000 a month over eight years through structure. The practitioners split on timing: JP Tower Consulting sees the strongest leverage within 2 years of expiration, while Tower Genius warns not to let leases fall below two years.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner here opens from the same premise: the person across the table is an adversary, not a partner. Hugh Odum of Vertical Consultants, who worked inside the largest wireless carrier for several years, calls the carriers and tower companies bullies that operate like an elephant, knocking down walls in court every day against property owners and municipalities, and warns that the smiles stop the moment the ink dries [oiDHDp0Uhxk @ 00:00:11]. Steve Gazella of Tower Genius frames lease talks the same way: you are not negotiating a contract, you are going to war against big telecom, whose attorneys have more money and have done this thousands of times more than you, and whose likable site acquisition reps are hired guns paid to land the lowest rent [Jpi639ecjmg @ 00:00:47]. Odum's counter is agility. The elephant is strong but slow, so the owner who knows the leverage points can exploit it. The first reframe is that this is not a real estate transaction but a utility transaction, because every carrier values each site on the production it generates for the network [oiDHDp0Uhxk @ 00:01:57]. Owners who walk in armed with real estate instincts, even seasoned self storage or commercial operators, are playing the wrong game from the first call.

The second point of consensus is that market rent is a myth. Odum tells owners that a tower company never compares sites in the same area when it values a location, so two towers sitting near each other can carry completely different values, and the owner should price them differently too [pwMG_wkkefQ @ 00:01:06]. Asked by a Mississippi owner how to find fair market rent, Odum told them to drop both words: there is no such thing as fair and no such thing as market, nearby owners have been severely underpaid for decades, and copying them just perpetuates bad deal after bad deal. Some of the best sites in the country are in Mississippi and other rural areas, and the rural-means-cheap line is a sales pitch the tower companies keep pushing [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:10:30]. Gazella lists the same trap as a deadly sin: leasing reps try to pigeonhole owners into a price per square foot, a count of rooftop antennas, or a regional lease range, when value is set site by site [Eo568DFPXFk @ 00:02:47]. Gazella also warns against owners who walk in blind quoting Joe or Bob at $6,000 a month without knowing whether that deal sits with a carrier or a tower developer, which is not an apples-to-apples comparison and usually ends in a crash and burn.

The third shared rule is procedural: never blink first, and never accept the other side's framing as fixed. Gazella ranks blurting out a dollar figure in the first conversation as the top deadly sin, because reps will hold you to that number, and ranks signing an offer letter, deal memo, or term sheet second, since the non-binding pitch is baloney and in some states it actually binds [Eo568DFPXFk @ 00:00:28]. When a rep mails a blank template with rent, term, and escalation left empty, Gazella calls it lazy site acquisition and tells owners to politely send it back demanding written figures rather than negotiate against themselves, a play common in rural areas where reps pit neighbors against each other [DJfkRMlj2fg @ 00:00:59]. The Vertical Consultants founder adds that everything is negotiable and there are no absolutes, because tower companies routinely drop terms they swear they always need, and owners should get expert assistance before signing an agreement that runs for decades [NqS16do0JG8 @ 00:00:18]. The push-versus-pull framing ties it together: most owners get pulled through the lease on the tower company's terms, and an owner pulled through once keeps getting pulled through renegotiations for the life of the deal, so push back from day one [WkS4XtLjQ5M @ 00:00:20].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Odum reduces the deal to three commodities the tower company is buying: time, space, and use [NS76BzQHI_o @ 00:00:15]. On space, tower companies ask for up to 10,000 square feet when a build needs closer to 2,000 or 3,000; the leftover 2,000 to 6,000 square feet gets nothing built on it and is instead leased to Verizon, AT&T, or T-Mobile without the owner seeing another penny. Retained space can be leased directly to an incoming carrier or leased to the tower company when it needs room [NS76BzQHI_o @ 00:01:00]. Odum puts the typical waste at about 7,000 unused square feet the day after a 10,000 square foot tower goes up, and notes every new subtenant needs more ground space, so controlling the footprint is how owners get paid for colocation [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:24:18]. Gazella runs the same math on rural ground: refuse a 500 foot guyed tower eating three or four acres, resist even a 100 by 100 parcel, and push for a 190 foot tower that needs no red light on a 50 by 50 or 70 by 70 lease area [JyYFN2j-Voo @ 00:01:41]. On use, Odum warns against unlimited rights inside the premises, because unrestricted upgrades mean more value extracted with no added rent.

Escalator math is where the hidden money sits. Odum's floor is a 3% escalator every single year, or a CPI adjustment that tracks inflation, which tower companies hate because they must keep track of it [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:04:23]. A pitch of 10% or 15% every five years is not 3% a year, because it does not compound annually; one owner offered $1,500 a month on a long term was headed to roughly $500 in effective value by the end, and a 90-year lease starting near $1,200 with a 1% kicker would be worth about $159 a month at expiration [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:27:23]. On revenue share, Odum flags the fallback offer of a flat $200 per subtenant: against a $2,000 sublease that is 10%, against a $4,000 sublease it falls to 5%, and it never inflates, so the share must be structured as a real percentage [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:16:40]. Bart Perir of Site Concepts adds the fine-print trap from lease consolidators: profit splits limited to newly leased ground space pay zero on existing tenants, whose rents can be raised indefinitely while shrinking equipment means they rarely need more ground, even though existing tenants are where owners can double or triple income [hr88g_fSUMM @ 00:03:15].

Before any number moves, Odum makes reps answer two questions: do you have a commitment from AT&T, Verizon, or T-Mobile, and if I sign today, when does construction start? Reps who cannot answer fast are speculators, and every restriction in the lease attaches the day you sign, not the day rent starts [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:02:35]. On an existing tower, JP Tower Consulting starts by identifying carriers from electric meters, compound logos, or a direct question, then pins down the remaining term, since under 10 years is significant leverage and within 2 years stronger still; in states where zoning takes 2 to 4 years, the relocation threat weakens, and talks run 3 to 12 months or longer [gy9A1QN_8Jk @ 00:00:42]. The same consultant benchmarks the Big Three at up to $10,000 a month and new carriers at $1,000 to $2,500 [gy9A1QN_8Jk @ 00:01:49]. For new rural builds, where reps throw out $200 to $700 offers, Gazella's fix is an initial five-year term with no more than four renewals, getting the owner back to the plate in about 10 years once the tower is up, while expecting about two dozen redlines on a typical lease and three dozen on the worst [xv8TdIdnmRQ @ 00:05:55].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the Approach
   - Ask the rep whether a carrier has committed to the tower and when construction starts if you sign today.
   - No tower gets built without a commitment from AT&T, Verizon, or T-Mobile; reps who cannot answer quickly are speculators, and lease restrictions attach on signing, not on first rent [Vertical Consultants — 7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:02:35].
2. Map Site Value and Detriment
   - Identify carriers, remaining term, local zoning timelines, and competing towers before talking rent.
   - Check meters and compound logos for carriers, then size what the tower company would lose by relocating or building down the road, both in build cost and network capacity [JP Tower Consulting — gy9A1QN_8Jk @ 00:00:42] [Vertical Consultants — F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:10:11].
3. Lock Down Time, Space, and Use
   - Shrink the lease area, restrict unlimited use, and cap the term to force an early renegotiation point.
   - Give roughly 2,000 to 3,000 square feet instead of 10,000, refuse unrestricted upgrade rights, and keep rural builds to an initial five-year term plus no more than four renewals [Vertical Consultants — NS76BzQHI_o @ 00:01:00] [Tower Genius — xv8TdIdnmRQ @ 00:05:55].
4. Build the Pull-Up Structure
   - Insert a 3% annual or CPI escalator, a percentage revenue share on all subtenants, and relocation rights with only temporary easements.
   - Reject flat $200 revenue shares and splits limited to new ground space, and refuse any perpetual easement that a consolidator could later charge you to remove [Vertical Consultants — F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:16:40] [LandSkills — hr88g_fSUMM @ 00:05:12].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over whether a rent number even exists before the site is studied. Steve Gazella of Tower Genius runs hard personal floors: no rural ground deal under a thousand dollars a month, and no rooftop under two grand, because crews come in and out of the building at all hours and anything less is not worth getting out of bed for [JyYFN2j-Voo @ 00:03:53]. JP Tower Consulting hands owners carrier bands instead, saying new carriers typically pay $1,000 to $2,500 per month while the Big Three pay up to $10,000, with higher rents where zoning is difficult or long-term tenants are present [gy9A1QN_8Jk @ 00:01:49]. Hugh Odum of Vertical Consultants takes the hard opposite line, telling owners to strike fair market rent from their vocabulary because neighbors have been severely underpaid for decades and copying them perpetuates bad deal after bad deal [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:10:47]. The Vertical alternative is comparable situations rather than comparable locations, matching a Crown Castle lease in Nevada against one in New Jersey when tenants, space and terms line up [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:08:08]. The practical operator move: treat a personal floor as a walk-away line, then price the actual site on its own network value.

Revenue share is where Tower Genius and Vertical Consultants openly split on tactics. Gazella calls it a deadly sin to tell the site acquisition rep up front that you want revenue share or co-location, because a lot of them will walk; the Gazella play is bass fishing, keeping those words zipped until the carrier or developer has spent money on your project and swallowed the hook [Eo568DFPXFk @ 00:01:40]. Odum takes the opposite posture, answering whether owners should push for a revenue share with yes, yes and yes, because rent, escalator and term alone let the tower company fix its cost while it gets pulled up by every new subtenant [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:14:41]. Gazella's fallback concedes more: on sites with competing parcels, owners may have to get on base with a single, sometimes accepting no revenue share for the first 20 years or a step-up structure, then using a short initial term to reach the plate in about ten years with leverage once the tower is built [xv8TdIdnmRQ @ 00:04:42]. Odum warns that a token concession is its own trap, citing a $200 monthly share that shrinks to 10 percent and then 5 percent of what new carriers pay [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:17:11].

Exit and timing playbooks diverge just as hard. Odum says that 90-plus percent of the time Vertical tells owners not to sell, allowing only three reasons: you need the money, you can reinvest it better, or you are hedging against the rent going away [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:26:20]. Bart of Site Concepts, on LandSkills, is more transactional, listing retirement, a daughter's wedding, medical bills, starting a business or selling the land as valid reasons, and laying out a hybrid where an owner maximizes monthly income for three years, then sells the ground lease for top cash when a child leaves for college [hr88g_fSUMM @ 00:07:27]. On extension timing, JP Tower Consulting argues the closer to expiration the stronger the owner's position, with significant leverage inside two years, because tower companies cannot relocate every site nearing final expiration [gy9A1QN_8Jk @ 00:03:14]. Gazella sees that last stretch differently: below two years, Verizon and AT&T start issuing new search rings for backup option agreements, so the Tower Genius target is a deal between ten and fifteen years out, with more leverage under ten on a multi-carrier site [xv8TdIdnmRQ @ 00:02:14]. Same deadline clock, opposite instincts on how long an owner should let it run.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Most losses start in the first conversation. Gazella ranks blurting out a dollar figure as the top deadly sin, because the site acquisition rep will hold you to that number, and calls the claim that an offer letter, term sheet or deal memorandum is non-binding pure baloney, since in some states it actually binds [Eo568DFPXFk @ 00:00:45]. The same trap arrives as a blank lease template with rent, term and escalations left empty, a lazy move designed to make the owner negotiate against themselves, common in rural areas where reps play neighbors off each other [DJfkRMlj2fg @ 00:00:42]. The Gazella response is to politely demand the rent, term and escalation in writing. Odum flags a slower-burning version: tower companies that cannot say who has committed to the tower or when construction starts are speculators, not builders, yet every obligation in the lease encumbers the property the day it is signed, not the day rent begins, which could be next week, ten years out or never [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:03:19]. Gazella also warns of bottom-feeder tower companies pushing a 10-year initial term with 17 five-year renewals, needing two to three dozen fixes, and built to poach carriers off existing SBA, American Tower or Crown Castle sites [DJfkRMlj2fg @ 00:02:10].

The economics checks are brutal once the math compounds. Odum walked through a lease offer paying $1,500 a month with a sub-3 percent escalator that would be worth about $500 a month effectively by the end of the term, the leaky bucket in action [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:05:27]. A 90-year proposal starting near $1,200 with a 1 percent escalator against roughly 3 percent inflation was effectively worth about $159 a month by the end, with no revenue share attached [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:27:40]. The other blind spot is the block escalator: 15 percent every five years is not 3 percent a year because it does not compound annually, and the gap between a 2 and 3 percent escalator over a 20 to 30-year term runs tens of thousands, if not hundreds of thousands, of dollars [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:22:14]. The yardstick behind the fight is that on average each additional $1 of rent equals about $1,000 of lease value over a roughly 30-year life, so a $100 concession is a $100,000 concession. The upside proof is an Ohio renegotiation that moved rent from about $900 to about $2,000 eight years ago; with structure, it now pays over $6,000 a month versus roughly $2,800 on a plain 3 percent escalator [F1m9euwLJAQ @ 00:25:18].

The deepest losses are control losses that never show up on the rent check. Odum says 98 percent or more of cell tower leases carry no relocation clause, so an owner offered ten times market rent on condition the utilities move 50 feet east cannot deliver, and any redevelopment, like converting a single-story storage facility to four stories, goes back to the tower company's sole discretion [oiDHDp0Uhxk @ 00:05:44]. Space is the second leak: tower companies ask for 10,000 square feet when they need closer to 2,000 or 3,000, then sublease the unused ground to Verizon, AT&T or T-Mobile without paying the owner a penny more [NS76BzQHI_o @ 00:01:00]. The LandSkills warning from Site Concepts covers two more: perpetual easements that survive the tower and let a consolidator charge the owner to remove them, effectively holding the land for ransom, and future-profit splits limited to newly leased ground space, which pay zero on existing tenants no matter how high their rents climb [hr88g_fSUMM @ 00:05:26]. Odum adds the rent-reduction letter: the end-is-near pitch to cut $1,500 to $1,000, where 99.5 percent of the time the tower is not moving and nothing stops the company from coming back to do it again [7qwoLJg7Ya8 @ 00:17:38].
