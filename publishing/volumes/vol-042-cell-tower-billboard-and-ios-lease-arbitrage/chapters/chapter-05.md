# Chapter 05: The Economics of Owning a Cell Tower

*Build costs vs. ground rent, co-location and carrier tenant concentration, Crown Castle-style tower company economics, 5G investing, rent rate forecasts for 2024–2025, and whether Starlink rooftop cell sites make towers obsolete*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree a cell tower is a real estate business selling space at height, and that the first tenant pays the bills while every tenant after that pays the owner. Builds run $150K to $500K, with a typical monopole near $275K; anchor rents sit around $1,500 to $2,500 a month. One tenant yields as little as 1% to 3% with paybacks of 34 to 75 years, a second converts about 90% to 97% to profit, and a third pushes returns past 24%. Leases carry 5 to 10 year initial terms, about 3% escalators, and 1% to 2% churn, with permits as the moat. The splits are on the landowner side: buyout multiples quoted at 9 to 12x, 15 to 20x, and 15 to 25x rent, and Vertical Consultants rejecting market rent entirely.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who runs the numbers describes the same machine: a tower is a real estate business selling space at height, not a telecom business. Mr. Money traces how carriers sold or leased their towers and rented the space back, turning fixed costs into monthly bills [jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:01:01]. Profit Chain splits the deal into two separate contracts, the landowner's ground lease and the tower company's carrier leases, and names colocation as the economic engine, with multiple carriers paying rent on one structure [t9bXdQI0jPM @ 00:01:53]. Mr. Finance Pro compresses it into one line, "The business isn't about building towers, it's about filling them," and describes the run-state as "just rent in, rent out, and a small crew keeping things standing" [cSRkZ3JGfBA @ 00:05:51]. Holly Lane Radio calls the business "a stack of rights" where tower, land, access, zoning, and leases can sit with different parties, and lists entry routes from build-to-suit with an anchor tenant to buying an existing tower, a portfolio, a carrier sale-leaseback, or only the ground interest [RRrHddpY1pU @ 00:02:18]. The operator takeaway is identical across all four practitioners: you are underwriting occupancy on a scarce, permitted vertical asset, and the steel itself is the least interesting part of the trade.

The single most repeated rule is the tenant ladder. Mr. Money states it flatly: "The first tenant pays the bills, and every tenant after that pays you," modeling one tenant at $2K a month for $24K a year, about $18K at a 75% margin, and a roughly 15-year payback, while two tenants push to about $36K a year and under 8 years [jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:08:14]. Economic Oddities runs the same ladder on a $300K build carrying $20K of fixed cost: one tenant earns $4K for a 1.3% yield and a 75-year payback, two earn $28K at 9.3%, three earn $52K at 17.3%, and four earn $76K at 25.3% with a 3.9-year payback [QdFbgvbJJ-M @ 00:01:42]. Mr. Finance gets there on a $275K monopole: one tenant yields about 3% and a 34-year payback, the second converts 97% to profit for about 12%, and the third clears 24% with about a 5.5-year payback [ku_-0C-ZnV4 @ 00:04:19]. Holly Lane Radio sums up the shared verdict: "The second and third leases create the economics" [RRrHddpY1pU @ 00:08:10]. Economic Oddities draws the hard line in four words: "Two tenants is the line." Every model agrees the one-tenant tower barely clears its costs; the business only exists once the second lease is signed.

Practitioners also agree the moat is regulatory, not physical. GTA Spark puts build cost at $150K to $300K and permits at six months to years, cites the WIA naming permitting the number-one obstacle, and calls the result "a government enforced scarcity advantage" [SnuS5ZZX2qA @ 00:05:13]. Mr. Finance Pro clocks approvals at 12 to 24 months under the Telecommunications Act of 1996 and later shot clocks, concluding "Every approved tower is a small monopoly" [cSRkZ3JGfBA @ 00:09:35]. Alaric Invests names permits as the scarce asset inside every deal and dismisses the hardware outright: "The steel is just the thing holding it up" [BrW8RLqtOjQ @ 00:04:20]. That scarcity is what makes the leases sticky once a carrier is on the steel. Finance US 1 lays out 5-year initial terms renewing to 20 to 30 years, 2% to 3% annual escalators, and high switching costs that keep carriers in place, all wrapped inside REIT structures [Fu71JjwHXWY @ 00:05:07]. Inside that frame, nobody treats the tower as a construction play; they treat it as a long-dated rent roll. The baseline prerequisites everyone shares: a permitted site, an anchor tenant, multi-year non-cancelable terms with a built-in escalator, and enough structural and ground capacity to add the second and third carriers that actually generate the return.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The build budget starts with a line-item stack. Mr. Finance breaks a new tower into land at $15K to $35K, engineering $20K to $40K, permits $15K to $50K, foundation $40K to $90K, steel $50K to $120K, power and fiber $25K to $70K, and construction $30K to $65K, for a total of $200K to $470K with a typical monopole near $275K [ku_-0C-ZnV4 @ 00:02:10]. Alaric Invests runs a wider $200K to $500K range and splits it as land 10% to 15%, civil 20% to 25%, steel 25% to 30%, electrical 15% to 20%, and permits 10% to 15% [BrW8RLqtOjQ @ 00:13:51]. Steel and civil work alone eat roughly half the budget on that split. Mr. Money pegs the FCC range at $150K to $300K, quotes American Tower's older rule of thumb topping out at $275K, and builds the base model on that $275K figure [jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:02:30]. Mr. Finance Pro goes higher still, at $250K to $500K covering steel, foundation, fencing, power, permits, and crews, and runs a worked example on a $400K build [cSRkZ3JGfBA @ 00:02:30]. Whatever the starting number, every model treats the build as a sunk cost that only the tenant count can recover, which is why the operators below obsess over lease structure before pouring concrete.

Rent per carrier is where the cash flow math lives. Finance US 1 quotes $1,500 to $2,500 a month for the first carrier, with two tenants bringing $36K to $48K a year and three bringing $55K to $70K [Fu71JjwHXWY @ 00:02:34]. Mr. Finance tiers rents by location: urban $2,500 to $6,000 a month, suburban $1,000 to $3,000, rural $500 to $1,800, warning that low-demand sites become "stranded assets" [ku_-0C-ZnV4 @ 00:11:18]. Selina Gonzales puts anchor rent in master deals at about $1,900 a month, citing the Verizon and American Tower deal covering about 11,500 sites, and says US towers average about two tenants [x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:02:25]. The second revenue lever is the lease paper itself, not the steel. Alaric Invests notes a 3% escalator yields about 34% more rent after 10 years, Cellnex uses CPI-linked escalators, terms run 10 years plus 5-year renewals, relocation costs a carrier $20K to $50K, and churn runs just 1% to 2% [BrW8RLqtOjQ @ 00:08:46]. For an operator, the read is simple: price the anchor against the location tier, lock the escalator in writing, and treat every additional carrier at those rents as near-pure margin layered onto a lease the incumbent carrier is already expensive to leave.

The landowner side runs on entirely different math. Mr. Money documents one owner approached 19 times in three years, with buyout offers of 9 to 12 times annual rent, turning $15K a year into $135K to $180K [jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:05:23]. Selina Gonzales reports buyout offers of $100K to $500K, typically 15 to 20 times rent with peaks near 21 times in 2024, and treats anything under 15 times as the cheap side of the trade [x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:07:17]. Vertical Consultants prices rent off two inputs only, the site's value to the tower company and its cost to relocate or build elsewhere, and then trades three commodities: give enough time but not too much so you keep renegotiation leverage, retain excess land when they ask for 10,000-plus square feet but need closer to 2,000 to 3,000, and refuse unlimited use that permits sub-leasing and repeated upgrades without added rent [l6LQZL7XYPk @ 00:01:38]. On Empowered Investor, guest Hugh Odom pushes a "utility agreement" structure so rent rises with upgrades like 4G to 5G, because carriers only want rent, an escalator, and a term [Ul4fTKkTHho @ 00:03:32]. The common thread: the landowner who negotiates term, space, and use line by line captures a share of upgrade revenue that a flat-rent signer never sees.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Sign the Anchor Before Steel
   - Lock a signed anchor tenant before committing capital to construction.
   - Building before a signed tenant is trap number one; with $20K of fixed cost, a zero-tenant tower bleeds money every year, and Finance US 1 flags building where no second tenant ever comes as the main risk [Economic Oddities — QdFbgvbJJ-M @ 00:09:49].
2. Site Inside the Search Ring and Design for Load
   - Place the tower inside the carrier's search ring and engineer for future tenants from day one.
   - Carriers define a search ring where moving half a mile or cutting 30 feet can defeat the purpose, and the build must be designed for future loading because the second and third leases create the economics [Holly Lane Radio — RRrHddpY1pU @ 00:08:10].
3. Run Zoning Against the Shot Clocks
   - File approvals and track every statutory deadline.
   - Shot clocks run 150 days for a new tower, 90 days for added equipment, and 60 days under Section 6409, where a missed deadline means deemed approved; a new macro tower still takes about two years end to end [Mr. Money — jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:04:35].
4. Market Proactively for Colocation
   - Pitch the site to every carrier instead of waiting for inbound requests.
   - Proactive marketing to carriers lifts sale multiples; of two 200-site operators, the proactive one sold at a premium while the reactive one sold "for a fraction" [GTA Spark — SnuS5ZZX2qA @ 00:13:54].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Moves the Operators Swear By**
> 
> Four field-tested moves from the people who price, build, and sell towers and the ground under them.
> - Ask "How many tenants?" before anything else; returns run about 6.5% with one tenant, about 13% with two, and approach 20% with three [Mr. Money — jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:07:36].
> - Write capacity limits and amendment fee schedules into every lease; 4G-to-5G amendment revenue is a major growth source and cheap capital lets the giants outbid everyone else [GTA Spark — SnuS5ZZX2qA @ 00:12:48].
> - Never sell a ground lease five years before renewal; the buyout approach is designed to land soft, and as the warning goes, "The letter is friendly" [Selina Gonzales — x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:09:24].
> - Eliminate "market rent" from your vocabulary and value the individual site, because tower companies cite past deals where the property owners were underpaid [Vertical Consultants — c553DbS2mGI @ 00:01:18].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Ask four operators what a ground-lease buyout is worth and you get four different multiples. Mr. Money tracked one landowner who was approached 19 times in three years, with offers clustering at just 9 to 12 times annual rent, so a $15K-per-year lease drew bids of $135K to $180K; the same breakdown brands consultants as "sellers of advice" [jQ79QHMZWQE @ 00:05:23]. Finance US 1 puts the going buyout at 15 to 20 times annual rent and pegs ground rent itself anywhere from a few hundred to several thousand dollars per month [Fu71JjwHXWY @ 00:07:24]. Selina Gonzales lands in that same 15 to 20 times band, logs offers between $100K and $500K with peaks near 21 times in 2024, and labels anything under 15 times the cheap side of the trade [x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:07:17]. Hugh Odom of Vertical Consultants, guesting on Empowered Investor, stretches the ceiling to 15 to 25 times annual rent, anchoring on a $2,500-per-month site that throws off $30K a year [Ul4fTKkTHho @ 00:07:27]. The spread between a 9x bid and a 25x bid on identical dirt is negotiating posture, not engineering. Weigh who is quoting: Mr. Money distrusts paid advisers, while Odom's firm sells site valuations to landowners, so every multiple arrives with an angle attached.

The second fault line is whether a "market rent" for a tower site exists at all. Vertical Consultants, fronted by former AT&T attorney Hugh Odom, says flatly that "there's no such thing as market rent," tells landowners to strike the phrase from their vocabulary, and insists every cell site has its own individual value [c553DbS2mGI @ 00:01:18]. Vertical Consultants' 2025 rate breakdown prices the lease on two inputs only: what the site is worth to the tower company and what it would cost that company to relocate or build elsewhere [l6LQZL7XYPk @ 00:00:21]. The same camp, on Empowered Investor, wants rent to ride upgrades, structuring the deal as a utility agreement so a 4G-to-5G swap reopens the money conversation, because carriers only want to lock rent, escalator, and term [Ul4fTKkTHho @ 00:03:32]. Selina Gonzales reports what landowners are actually being offered: about $500 a month typical, new offers rarely above $1,000, second-tier builders around $700 with five-year bumps, against $1,500 to $2,000 a decade ago [x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:04:08]. So one playbook treats every site as a bespoke negotiation over relocation cost and upgrade rights, while the observed tape shows typical ground rents compressing from four figures toward a few hundred dollars a month.

The third split is whether satellites and small cells will eventually kill the macro tower. Vertical Consultants calls the Starlink-makes-towers-obsolete story a buyer tactic used to talk landowners down and frames satellite as "a supplement, not a substitute" [BoWyq83WIVk @ 00:00:21]. Orbital Ledger lays out both sides of the spectrum fight: T-Mobile CTO John Saw argues Starlink holds about 80 MHz against T-Mobile's roughly 400 MHz, so the satellite pipe looks thin on paper [G3zZhjoRDUg @ 00:04:42]. Yet the same channel notes T-Mobile joined a venture with AT&T and Verizon and scrubbed the Starlink name from its web page, concluding that "companies don't do that for threats they truly believe are laughable" [G3zZhjoRDUg @ 00:14:41]. Holly Lane Radio sides with the incumbents and disputes that small cells or satellites can replace towers wholesale [RRrHddpY1pU @ 00:22:41]. That leaves operators with two readings of the same headlines. In the Vertical Consultants frame, any satellite talk inside a buyout pitch is a discount lever and should be treated as noise. In the Orbital Ledger frame, the carriers that anchor every tower rent roll are visibly hedging, and a spectrum-rich satellite operator stacking antennas on rooftops is a competitor for exactly the height that tower owners sell.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive mistake the practitioners model is pouring steel on hope. Economic Oddities' illustrative opener features Lee Thomas, who built a $300K tower, cleared just $4K in year one, while the landowner collected $12K off the same structure [QdFbgvbJJ-M @ 00:00:00]. Finance US 1 names the core risk plainly: building where no second tenant ever shows up [Fu71JjwHXWY @ 00:08:26]. Mr. Finance runs the single-tenant math on a typical $275K monopole: $20K of revenue against $12K of costs leaves $8K of profit, roughly a 3% return and a 34-year payback [ku_-0C-ZnV4 @ 00:04:19]. Holly Lane Radio turns the same lesson on buyers of existing towers, warning that "a purchase price based on three future tenants is speculation dressed as infrastructure" [RRrHddpY1pU @ 00:24:22]. Across all four, the pattern is the same: the anchor tenant covers ground rent, insurance, and upkeep, and almost nothing else. A tower without a signed second lease is a fixed-cost machine with a thin cushion, and an acquisition priced off tenants who have not signed yet is paying today for revenue that may never arrive. The operator check is blunt: underwrite on signed leases only, and treat every unsigned colocation as upside rather than as part of the price.

Concentration is the second killer. Profit Chain documents the live case: in January 2026 Crown Castle terminated its Dish agreements and is claiming more than $3.5B, Dish filed Chapter 11, an FCC-ordered trust was created, and recovery remains uncertain [t9bXdQI0jPM @ 00:08:38]. Mr. Finance quantifies a merger hit: about 35K of 110K sites overlapped, shaving roughly four points of organic growth, and if the anchor tenant walks, revenue drops 60% while profit falls about 78% [ku_-0C-ZnV4 @ 00:12:46]. Economic Oddities runs the sensitivity: losing one tenant cuts income 86% on a two-tenant tower, 46% on a three-tenant tower, and 32% at four; the same channel says "two tenants is the line" to beat a 7% index alternative [QdFbgvbJJ-M @ 00:08:28]. Selina Gonzales flags the worst-case portfolio: Array's 4,455 towers running 0.98 tenancy, where up to 1,600 could go empty under T-Mobile's 2,015-site minimum with a January 2028 deadline, and decommissioning them is not free [x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:09:38]. When a handful of carriers fill the rent roll, one bankruptcy or one merger cascades straight through the tower. The check before any purchase: list every tenant, model the anchor leaving, and confirm the asset still covers ground rent and its carrying costs with the weakest carrier gone.

The third trap sits under the steel and inside the balance sheet. Economic Oddities lists mismatched land-lease terms as one of five core traps for tower builders [QdFbgvbJJ-M @ 00:10:56]. Holly Lane Radio sketches the exposure: a ground lease with only 11 years left sitting under a $90K tower, where whoever controls the dirt holds the renewal leverage [RRrHddpY1pU @ 00:17:58]. Selina Gonzales flips the trap onto landowners: the buyout letter is "friendly," but selling five years before renewal surrenders that leverage right before it arrives [x2ZfqDSKoOI @ 00:09:09]. Mr. Finance Pro adds the capital-structure trap: Cellnex scaled on cheap debt, then rates rose through 2022 and 2023 [cSRkZ3JGfBA @ 00:22:15]. Stack those together and the economics check becomes a lease audit before it becomes a cash-flow model. Confirm the ground lease outlasts the payback period being underwritten, since a 15-year payback sitting on 11 years of land control is a stranded bet. On the landowner side, refuse to sell into a friendly letter when renewal is close, because renewal is where the owner's leverage peaks. On the operator side, stress the deal at higher rates before signing, because a model that only works on cheap money breaks the moment financing reprices.
