# Chapter 08: Billboard Unit Economics: The Full Accounting

*Structure costs vs. permit value, ground rent during vacancy, occupancy and rate math, Lamar's revenue concentration, and building a billboard empire*

## Executive Summary

Sixteen operators and analysts agree on one thesis: the permit is the asset, not the steel, and occupancy decides whether it pays. The 1965 Highway Beautification Act, local moratoriums, and four state bans freeze supply, so buying an existing permitted structure beats building a new one. Ground rent takes 15 to 25% of revenue. Static boards clear thin margins that leverage can erase. Operators underwrite at 85% occupancy rather than at rate card. Digital conversion turns a static face earning roughly $3,000 a month into $15,000 to $18,000 a month for about $200,000, and ledgerandland models net income nearly tripling. The practitioners split on entry cost, on exit multiples (3-5x versus 7-12x revenue), and on market size ($9.46B versus $57.8B). They also split on how much debt a board can carry before the mortgage crushes a money-making asset.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Across the board, the operators agree that the steel is the cheap part and the government permission is the asset. Felix Finance puts it in five words, "The permit is the product," and adds that you cannot build a new one in most protected corridors [Zfn3IARm5no @ 00:01:26]. The Finance Explainer frames the same idea as "You're not buying steel, you're buying scarcity," and notes that standalone permits can trade for over a million dollars [pHu5ZfxNtfg @ 00:06:17]. Walt Runs The Numbers is blunter. The government-issued outdoor advertising permit is the thing of value, a lost permit renders your steel pole worthless, and new permits take 12 to 24 months to secure [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:01:34]. Josh Talks Business compares a capped permit to a taxi medallion. Once a jurisdiction stops issuing them, the existing ones become tradeable monopolies, and zoning plus average daily traffic decide which ones are worth owning [qH0UhNp0Now @ 00:04:39]. The consensus playbook follows from that scarcity. The 1965 Highway Beautification Act, local moratoriums, spacing rules, and four outright state bans freeze supply. An operator who already holds a legal face therefore has a moat that competitors cannot replicate by spending more on construction. The fight is over who controls the legal right to stand a sign on a high-traffic road, not over who builds the best structure.

The second point of agreement is that occupancy, not rate card, decides whether a board makes money. Josh Talks Business calls occupancy "the real machine" and argues that attention scales in a way land never does [qH0UhNp0Now @ 00:10:05]. Felix Finance repeats it almost word for word, "Occupancy rate is everything in this business," and sets the target above 85% [Zfn3IARm5no @ 00:13:01]. Walt Runs The Numbers turns that into what he calls the 85% occupancy rule, roughly 44 sold weeks a year, and underwrites every acquisition against it rather than against a full calendar [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:06:58]. ledgerandland gives the warning version: "If you underwrite a purchase at 100% of rate card, you bought a fantasy." In that speaker's numbers, real occupancy runs 70 to 85% and opex eats 25 to 40% of gross [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:16:54]. Everyone obsesses over fill because the inventory perishes. A week that passes unsold is revenue that never comes back, while the ground rent, insurance, and permit fees keep billing. The group converges on a practical rule: model at the occupancy you can prove from the seller's books, and treat anything above it as upside rather than the base case. A board that looks like a cash machine at full fill can be a slow bleed at two-thirds.

On entry strategy, the speakers line up behind buying what already exists. Profit Mechanics lays out six moves: buy an existing permit, own the land, build static and pay cash, sell ad space directly, reinvest the cash flow, and hold for ten-plus years. Chris Brown followed that sequence from one $75,000 board to roughly 30 boards in Bentonville [chCNj5IEUZw @ 00:18:31]. His second build, about $200,000 on three acres carrying eight faces, earns more than $8,000 a month, which shows land ownership compounding. Money Mechanics walks through five models (rural static, digital, premium highway, owned land, and acquisition) and flags buying existing as the recommended route [PIQPl5zhm5s @ 00:03:35]. The landowner is the silent partner in every deal. Stick to Wealth pegs ground rent at 15 to 20% of revenue, the same band Profit Mechanics and ledgerandland quote [4WZnJVbtMlk @ 00:01:24]. Once a site is locked, the consensus upgrade is digital conversion at the strongest locations. Rio Explains relays Lamar CEO Sean Reilly's numbers: a static face earning about $3,000 a month converts for roughly $200,000 and then earns $15,000 to $18,000 a month [OYv2q23Z36g @ 00:03:05]. Static is the cash-paid foundation, and digital is where operators deploy serious capital once the traffic is proven.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start with the capital stack. Walt Runs The Numbers itemizes a new highway structure: $26,000 to $45,000 for the build, another $12,000 to $18,000 for a double face, $6,000 to $15,000 for permitting, and $3,000 to $6,000 for electrical. Acquiring an existing board runs $80,000 to $250,000, which puts total entry anywhere from $60,000 to $270,000 [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:02:08]. Ned Talks Business quotes static at $15,000 to $40,000 and digital at $150,000 to $300,000, stretching to $500,000 in major markets. Ground leases in that speaker's numbers run $300 to $2,500 a month and occasionally above $5,000 [inSGoP3HxYA @ 00:01:16]. On the digital side, Felix Finance prices the LED itself at $80,000 to $250,000, notes that a P6 pixel-pitch screen costs 40% more than a P10, and puts the full digital build at $150,000 to $500,000 [Zfn3IARm5no @ 00:04:04]. Traffic sets the price of the face. ledgerandland's rule of thumb is $1,000 to $2,000 a month for every 10,000 vehicles of daily traffic [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:20:23]. That fits Felix Finance's I-10 example of 62,000 cars a day billing $9,000 a month. Ned's rate ladder fills in the rest: $200 to $500 rural, $1,000 to $3,000 suburban, and $5,000 to $20,000 prime urban.

The static P&L is thin, and every practitioner who models one shows it. Money Mechanics takes $60,000 of revenue, subtracts a $12,000 lease and $13,000 of other costs to land at $35,000, then shows a three-month vacancy deleting $15,000 of that [PIQPl5zhm5s @ 00:08:31]. Vaultonomics runs a smaller board. $3,000 a month at 75% occupancy is $27,000 a year. Subtract a $6,000 lease, roughly $4,000 in 15% commissions, and $4,000 of operating costs, and about $13,000 remains, enough to pay back a $70,000 build in five to six years [clsOKO3QgiM @ 00:07:15]. Econometrics finds the floor with a $40,000 board sold at $2,000 per four-week block across 13 blocks. It breaks even near 38% fill and loses about $800 at 35%, while 65% fill clears roughly $6,200 for a 6.5-year payback [q9ejuh31AvI @ 00:03:20]. Leverage squeezes the margin further. Walt Runs The Numbers takes a $3,500-a-month board to $1,760 to $1,955 of monthly NOI. Borrowing $120,000 at 8.5% over ten years then eats about $1,048 a month, leaving only $275 to $470 [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:03:53]. That math explains the pay-cash rule in the six-move playbook.

Digital changes the inventory math because one structure is sold many times over. ledgerandland's worked example starts with a static board grossing $72,000 and netting about $41,000. After conversion, the same site grosses $230,400, carries roughly $110,000 of opex, and nets about $120,000, close to three times the static net [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:17:38]. Money Mechanics shows how slot fill swings the result. A $300,000 build with eight slots at $2,500 a month grosses $240,000 a year, but filling only six of eight drops that to $180,000 [PIQPl5zhm5s @ 00:09:34]. Rio Explains cites analyst estimates of roughly a 43% return on Lamar conversions after a 20% lease and a 7% commission, and says Lamar converts 300 to 500 faces a year [OYv2q23Z36g @ 00:03:57]. The exit math makes the slow burn worth it. Profit Mechanics says buyers pay seven to twelve times revenue, so a board generating $96,000 a year prices at roughly $670,000 to more than $1 million [chCNj5IEUZw @ 00:22:10]. On those numbers, operators who scale static faces, convert their best sites, and hold occupancy above 85% are building an asset priced on a multiple, not just collecting rent from a pole beside the highway.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Confirm the Permit Before the Steel
   - Verify a legal, transferable permit and a secured site before committing any construction or purchase capital.
   - Building before the permit is confirmed is one of the biggest beginner mistakes. If the location is not legal, you end up holding a very expensive pole. New permits take 12 to 24 months, so buying an existing permitted structure skips that wait [Global Finance Desk — AkX52_z30r4 @ 00:09:27].
2. Price the Face Off Traffic
   - Pull daily traffic counts and set the rate card using the per-10,000-vehicle rule.
   - Charge $1,000 to $2,000 a month for every 10,000 vehicles of daily traffic. Roads carrying 50,000 to 150,000 vehicles a day support bulletin pricing [ledgerandland — 6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:20:23].
3. Underwrite at 85%, Then Stress Below It
   - Model revenue at 85% occupancy (44 weeks), then rerun the model at the breakeven fill rate.
   - Use the 85% occupancy rule as the base case [Walt Runs The Numbers — 9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:06:58]. Then confirm where the board breaks even: about 38% fill on a $40,000 static build, with a loss of roughly $800 at 35% [Econometrics — q9ejuh31AvI @ 00:03:20].
4. Ride the Boards, Then Hire Reps
   - Sell the inventory personally until the portfolio reaches about 15-20 faces, then add commissioned reps.
   - The owner sells directly until roughly 15-20 faces, then hires reps at 10-20% commission. Target more than 85% occupancy, and backfill unsold time with programmatic at $8-15 CPM [Felix Finance — Zfn3IARm5no @ 00:12:42].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Margin Levers the Operators Actually Pull**
> 
> These four moves came up repeatedly as the difference between a board that pays and a board that bleeds slowly.
> - Watch the discount trap. A one-off $1,500 deal still contributes $1,350, but if discounting becomes the norm the board needs roughly 87% fill to hold its economics [Econometrics — q9ejuh31AvI @ 00:05:16].
> - Cap lease escalators at 2-3% when negotiating ground leases of 10 to 25 years, and stagger lease expirations across the portfolio so they do not all reset at once [Felix Finance — Zfn3IARm5no @ 00:05:04].
> - Day-part digital slots: coffee in the morning, restaurants in the evening. With 6-10 slots on an 8-second rotation, this drives a 4-10x gross multiplier [Hidden Economics — XlZ7pkhH2fM @ 00:02:17].
> - Sell direct, because direct sales carry the highest margin compared with agencies and programmatic. Keep the creative to 7-8 words maximum to suit dwell time and read distance [The Finance Explainer — pHu5ZfxNtfg @ 00:06:59].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The build-versus-buy split is the first fork, and the operators land on opposite sides of the cost sheet. Profit Mechanics lays out a six-move ladder that starts with buying an existing permit, owning the land, building static paid in cash, selling direct, reinvesting, and holding ten-plus years [chCNj5IEUZw @ 00:18:31]. Money Mechanics runs five models side by side and still recommends buying existing over rural static, digital, premium highway, or owned-land plays, pricing a fresh build at roughly $145K all-in [PIQPl5zhm5s @ 00:14:00]. Walt Runs The Numbers breaks a ground-up job into $26-45K of structure, $6-15K of permitting, and $3-6K of electrical, against $80-250K to acquire a standing board, for total entry anywhere from $60K to $270K [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:02:08]. Ledgerandland goes harder and files new-build fantasy as one of seven traps, right alongside lease expiry, eminent domain, and no-rebuild rules [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:21:21]. The pattern is consistent across the cost sheets: the operators who buy permits talk about cash flow from day one, and the operators who build talk about steel, permitting calendars, and electrical hookups long before the first advertiser signs a contract. That timing gap, more than the steel itself, is what separates the two camps on paper.

Digital conversion is where the payback math splits widest, and the spread is not a rounding error. Felix Finance claims converting an $8K-a-month face to $30K costs $200K and pays back in nine months [Zfn3IARm5no @ 00:10:57]. The Finance Explainer runs a far tamer case: a $200K digital build sold as eight slots at $600 each, or $4,800 a month, clearing $3-10K of monthly profit with a two-to-three-year payback [pHu5ZfxNtfg @ 00:14:10]. Profit Mechanics stretches the payback further to three to five years even while crediting digital with four to six times static revenue across six to eight rotating advertisers [chCNj5IEUZw @ 00:05:55]. Ledgerandland's worked example shows why the camps diverge: a $72K static gross producing about $41K of NOI becomes $230,400 gross after conversion, but roughly $110K of opex eats in and leaves about $120K NOI, which is 'three times net' rather than the headline gross multiple [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:17:38]. Across these operators, quoted payback runs from nine months to five years on similar six-figure checks. The disagreement is mostly about what gets counted. The optimists quote gross lift on a prime face; the conservative operators load power, slot vacancy, and screen aging into the opex line before they let themselves call the upgrade a win.

Cash versus leverage is the sharpest philosophical split. Profit Mechanics' Chris Brown paid $75,000 for structure plus land with no debt, two faces at $600 a month each, and the ladder he teaches insists on building static paid in cash [chCNj5IEUZw @ 00:01:41]. Josh Talks Business takes the opposite line: borrow against the land, the permit, and projected revenue, with the blunt caveat that 'the bank gets paid first' [qH0UhNp0Now @ 00:07:14]. Ned Talks Business shows what leverage looks like at scale: a $4.2M purchase throwing off $1.1M of revenue against roughly $730K of costs and $288K of debt service, netting about $109K yet projected to a 20-25% IRR [inSGoP3HxYA @ 00:15:50]. Walt Runs The Numbers runs the single-board version and the margin almost disappears: $3,500 a month, NOI of $1,760-1,955, then about $1,048 of debt service on $120K at 8.5% over ten years leaves only $275-470 a month [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:03:53]. Note Brown's own figures drift: $2,400 a month early, $2,800 in the recap, so even the cash camp's headline number needs a recount. The operator takeaway is that debt converts a comfortable board into a thin one, and the IRR story only works when the revenue line holds.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Vacancy is the failure mode every operator has paid for in real dollars. Walt Runs The Numbers walks through an Oklahoma case: a $185K purchase, a four-month vacancy that cost $16,200, an $8,400 storm repair, and $29K lost by month eighteen [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:08:41]. Money Mechanics prices the same pain on a smaller board, where a $60K revenue line minus a $12K lease and $13K of other costs leaves $35K, and a three-month vacancy wipes out $15K of it [PIQPl5zhm5s @ 00:08:31]. Econometrics shows how thin the floor is: a $40K build selling $2,000 four-week blocks breaks even near 38% fill, loses about $800 at 35%, and a $1,500 discount that becomes the norm pushes the required fill to roughly 87% [q9ejuh31AvI @ 00:03:20]. Ledgerandland puts the underwriting rule bluntly: 'If you underwrite a purchase at 100% of rate card, you bought a fantasy' [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:16:54]. Every unsold period is unrecoverable inventory, which is why discounting to fill a gap feels safe in the moment but quietly resets the whole rate card downward. The fix the operators converge on is to underwrite at realistic occupancy, model a multi-month dark stretch in year one, and refuse to let a one-off discount become the posted price.

Permit risk is the trap that turns steel into scrap. Global Finance Desk names building before the permit is confirmed, signing a weak lease, and choosing cheap land over traffic as the classic beginner mistakes, and sums up the result in one line: 'Now you have a very expensive pole' [AkX52_z30r4 @ 00:09:27]. Walt Runs The Numbers is just as flat about it: 'A lost permit renders your steel pole worthless' [9d5MJAQGDk8 @ 00:08:06]. Ledgerandland adds the slower-moving version of the same loss: signs destroyed past a damage threshold cannot be rebuilt, which means one heavy damage event can convert a grandfathered location into an empty lot [6oamhwKj2wU @ 00:06:55]. Follow the Fee explains why there is no workaround once supply is locked: moratoriums and spacing rules cap inventory, Oregon's cap-and-replace system demands three static boards removed for a single trivision unit, and 'You cannot build your way into a moratorium' [lQ0ni5KmJiE @ 00:09:09]. The practical consequence is that the permit, not the structure, is the asset being underwritten. Operators who treat paperwork as an afterthought end up holding hardware with no legal right to display anything, while operators who verify permit status, lease terms, and rebuild limits before closing keep the scarcity value intact.

Debt and ad cycles are the traps that hit the biggest names hardest. Profit Mechanics puts Clear Channel Outdoor at $5.6B of debt, roughly 12 times cash flow, with about $420M a year of interest, against the 2-4 times a healthy business carries [chCNj5IEUZw @ 00:15:29]. Follow the Fee traces how it got there: a roughly $27B leveraged buyout that pushed debt from $8B to more than $20B, iHeart's Chapter 11 in 2018, and European exits for $625M and about $135M [lQ0ni5KmJiE @ 00:17:58]. Stick to Wealth frames the lesson in one line: 'The house can be a money machine and the mortgage can still crush you' [4WZnJVbtMlk @ 00:05:00]. MoneyLogics shows how fast the revenue side can break: Clear Channel Outdoor Americas revenue fell 39% in Q2 2020, and 'The customers just vanished' [8Emqeya9KGo @ 00:01:39]. Put those together and the failure mode is not a bad billboard; it is a good billboard carrying a capital structure that cannot survive a single bad quarter. Scarce locations do not pay lenders when advertisers stop buying. Small operators should run their own debt service against a sudden revenue drop before signing a loan, and size the note so the boards still clear interest in a down year.
