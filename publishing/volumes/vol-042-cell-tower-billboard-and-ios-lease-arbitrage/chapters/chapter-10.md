## Executive Summary

IOS operators agree that the land, the zoning, and the tenants are the asset. Buildings cover under 10 to 25 percent of the site and barely need underwriting. Zoning is the dominant risk; Alterra's Leo Addimando ranks it number one through five, and grandfathered non-conforming uses are the classic trap. National IOS vacancy sits at 2.5 percent versus 6.7 percent for traditional industrial, and market rent runs 29.8 percent above in-place rent. On the street, users want 1 to 3 acres, site work runs $75,000 to $300,000 per acre, and a 2-acre site can shrink to 1.5 usable acres. Ned of 9606 Capital buys Atlanta at 8.5 percent against 7 percent stabilized trades, and lenders want a 10 percent debt yield and 1.25x DSCR. The split is demand depth: Industrial Advisors calls it overstated for 5 to 7 acre sites, while institutional players report leasing has never been busier.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator starts from the same premise: in industrial outdoor storage the dirt, not the building, is the asset. Ryan Solberg of MaxLife frames it bluntly, saying that with IOS “there is barely a building to underwrite” and that the value sits in the land, the zoning, and tenants who cannot park a truck anywhere else [oYtLEutv1NQ @ 00:00:40]. Chase MacLeod defines the product as an industrial property with substantially more outside yard than building, with coverage typically under 10 to 25 percent; on 3 acres, roughly 130,000 SF, a good building runs about 15,000 to 20,000 SF, and some yards have no building at all [m96MC6Qe9oM @ 00:00:15]. Edison Basquez of EdisonIndustrial calls it “a small building on a large piece of land” [-7jTPPttg2s @ 00:00:29], and the Dolphin speaker on CREDA Global's NAIOP segment says the value comes from the land rent, with lower coverage used to store equipment, vehicles, and building materials [cKtqug8KPPk @ 00:00:03]. The tenant roster is consistent across the field: logistics firms, 3PLs, construction firms, fleet managers, container users, trailer storage, equipment rental, and pallet operators. Solberg's warning is the takeaway: investors who carry a warehouse checklist to a truck yard miss the items that actually decide the deal.

The loudest point of agreement is zoning. Leo Addimando of Alterra, speaking on CBRE's program, ranks zoning as risk “number one… through five,” says commercial zoning does not work, and treats illegal truck parking on a site as a warning sign [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:17:20]. The Dolphin speaker on CREDA Global's NAIOP segment names the top three underwriting challenges as “zoning, zoning, and zoning” and hires local land-use counsel on every acquisition [cKtqug8KPPk @ 00:00:58]. Steve Azar of Ambient Capital Partners runs in-house land-use expertise that goes deeper than the zoning report, warning that a buyer must confirm the site is not “just a grandfathered legal non-conforming use that's going to go away when the tenant goes away,” and noting that Southern California towns are cracking down on non-conforming sites [znP8wX2_k2w @ 00:12:40]. Justin Horowitz of Cooper Horowitz adds the buyer-side wrinkle: a seller's municipal ties can hide zoning limits, so hire zoning counsel instead of leaning on the seller's comfort [yb3YiKWxoFg @ 00:15:16]. The upside of a clean entitlement is optionality. Addimando says over 95 percent of Alterra's sites could support vertical industrial, but not the inverse. None of these operators treats zoning as a post-closing question.

The demand-side numbers line up across national sources. Commercial Real Estate Now cites CBRE's Q4 2025 national IOS vacancy of 2.5 percent against 6.7 percent for traditional industrial, with market rent 29.8 percent above in-place rent in Q1 2026, the widest mark-to-market gap of any industrial segment [dKQj8cpiqeE @ 00:01:53]. Steve Azar puts quality IOS vacancy near 2 percent versus near-double-digit vacancy for industrial broadly, says IOS nationally runs half the warehouse vacancy with twice the rent growth, and reports leasing has never been busier on the back of AI buildout demand [znP8wX2_k2w @ 00:11:42]. Leo Addimando sizes the universe at $200 billion to $500 billion with under 10 percent institutionally owned, while CBRE's Will Pike says every major industrial allocator now has an IOS strategy and the sector was recently added to NCREIF [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:15:15]. Jonathan Gonzalez of Capright lists what institutionalization brings: greater scale, more transactions and larger portfolios, more operating data, standardized leases, timely reporting, and pricing transparency that helps the asset class mature [p8t7CLKouHw @ 00:00:20]. The shared baseline is by-right zoning, a functional yard, and location. Addimando places Alterra's sites 3 to 5 miles from a port, interstate, intermodal hub, or airport, and puts tenant costs at 50 percent transportation and under 5 percent occupancy.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The physical spec is tight and numeric. Edison Basquez says the perfect site for most users is under 5 acres, ideally 1 to 3, with a 10,000 to 20,000 SF building on 2 to 5 acres, multi-tenant divisibility, and real infrastructure, because gravel alone fails: municipalities require paving, drainage, fencing, and lighting before tenants or owners can get business licenses [-7jTPPttg2s @ 00:00:53]. Ned of 9606 Capital buys broader. Coverage under 10 percent always works and under 20 percent can work depending on layout, with buildings of 3,000 to 50,000 SF on 3 to 25 acres. The 9606 site-work numbers run $75,000 to $125,000 per acre for light work and $175,000 to $300,000 for heavy work, with Jacksonville near $225,000 and Chicago near $125,000 per acre [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:29:59]. Matt McGregor of Industrial Advisors pegs typical capex at 9 to 18 inches of crushed rock or paving, with near-zero cost between tenants [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:01:35]. The usable-acre haircut is the number buyers forget. EdisonIndustrial shows a 2-acre site losing 0.5 acre to landscaping, retention, and setbacks, leaving 1.5 usable acres, and tenants may pay only on the usable area [Wu_IobArlaI @ 00:02:33]. Price the land you can actually rent, not the acreage on the deed.

Rent and pricing quotes vary by market, and the operators give them straight. Ryan Solberg offers Florida planning ranges, not quotes: cap rates of about 6.75 to 8 percent, with paved, secured I-4 corridor sites trading toward the tighter end, rents of $40,000 to $90,000 per acre per year, and roughly 150 to 200 basis points of compression [oYtLEutv1NQ @ 00:02:34]. Ned traces Atlanta rents from $1,200 to $1,500 to $1,800 and then $4,000 per acre per month, now about $5,000. Stabilized assets trade at a 7 cap, and Ned buys at 8.5 percent, a 150-basis-point discount, after modeling 6 to 8 months of vacancy [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:14:54]. Horowitz quotes New Jersey rents of $25,000 to $30,000 per acre per month in Newark and $15,000 to $20,000 in secondary markets, plus an entry play in Mobile at $2,000 per acre per month on a 15-year lease with 3 percent bumps [yb3YiKWxoFg @ 00:11:48]. Commercial Real Estate Now pegs primary-market caps at 6 to 6.75 percent and secondary at 6.75 to 7.75 percent. IOS leases run 2 to 5 years with 10 to 15 percent bumps at renewal, against 15-year net leases with 10 percent escalations every 5 years [dKQj8cpiqeE @ 00:03:54].

Debt is where the mechanics get exact. Horowitz underwrites to a 10 percent debt yield and 1.25x DSCR. Managed truck parking needs a 12 to 13 percent debt yield and prices in the high-6s to low-7s, roughly a 50-basis-point premium; Horowitz calls it “like a hotel.” The Elkridge, Maryland United Rentals deal went 10-year fixed at 6.8 percent, roughly 6.5 percent today, and $25 million to $200 million portfolios price at high-1s to low-2s over Treasuries [yb3YiKWxoFg @ 00:21:12]. Ned reports banks now want global cash flow. Against the banks' 1.25 requirement, Ned's DSCR sits at 2.6, and leverage across everything 9606 Capital owns is under 35 percent [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:24:12]. Solberg points buyers to specialized lenders, life companies, or debt funds at about 60 to 70 percent LTV, with SBA 504 possible for owner-users [oYtLEutv1NQ @ 00:04:59]. Addimando puts IOS borrowing costs “about 50 basis points wide” and valuation spreads to traditional industrial at 25 to 50 basis points, narrowed from 150. Addimando also forecasts borrowing costs and cap rates converging with traditional industrial within five years [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:12:36]. Rate locks swing fast: Horowitz saw a 6.29 percent lock that would have been 6.24 percent a week later and 6.40 percent two weeks later, and expects roughly 20-basis-point swings.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the Zoning Before Anything Else
   - Verify with the city that the zoning allows outdoor storage by-right, and price what it costs to make the site compliant.
   - EdisonIndustrial warns that industrial zoning may not allow outdoor storage and that buyers have bought non-compliant sites, so find out what compliance takes and costs before buying [EdisonIndustrial — Wu_IobArlaI @ 00:00:41]; the Dolphin team hires local land-use counsel on every acquisition [CREDA Global — cKtqug8KPPk @ 00:01:12].
2. Clear Environmental and Site Infrastructure
   - Order a Phase I, carry environmental insurance from day one, and hold back funds pending no-further-action letters.
   - Horowitz treats Phase I as mandatory, carries environmental insurance from day one, flags PFAS as an emerging issue, and uses holdbacks pending NFA letters [Industrial Real Estate Show — yb3YiKWxoFg @ 00:26:50]; Solberg calls Phase I non-negotiable and checks pavement thickness, drainage, and current stormwater permits [MaxLife Commercial RE MLR — oYtLEutv1NQ @ 00:04:00].
3. Measure Usable Acres and Required Improvements
   - Net out landscaping, retention, and setbacks, and confirm city-required paving, drainage, lighting, or building before closing.
   - EdisonIndustrial notes that cities may require paving, drainage, lighting, or an industrial building, which must be confirmed in diligence before closing [EdisonIndustrial — Wu_IobArlaI @ 00:01:39]; Jonathan Gonzalez warns that two 5-acre sites a few miles apart can differ entirely on zoning, usable acreage, access, and paving [Capright — p8t7CLKouHw @ 00:00:43].
4. Structure the Price and Seller Terms
   - Model vacancy into the basis, buy at a discount to stabilized pricing, and negotiate a tenant-finding window with a guaranteed floor.
   - Ned asks sellers for a 3 to 4 month window to find a tenant with a guaranteed floor price [Industrial Real Estate Show — LftC_mcL69E @ 00:22:41], and buys Atlanta at 8.5 percent against 7 percent stabilized trades after modeling 6 to 8 months of vacancy [Industrial Real Estate Show — LftC_mcL69E @ 00:14:54].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Demand is the sharpest split. CBRE's Will Pike says every major industrial allocator now runs an IOS strategy, and Leo Addimando of Alterra frames the sector as infrastructure real estate hiding in plain sight [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:20:25]. The Industrial Advisors panel pushes back hard: Matt McGregor sees more fluff and talk than actual action, and Kyle Back sums it up, saying demand has been overstated a little bit [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:03:18]. Kyle's read is size-specific. Yards of 1 to 3 acres lease, while 5 to 7 acre sites sit because institutions push rates that are hard to pencil for truck and equipment users [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:03:05]. Edison Basquez lands on the same small-yard side, putting the perfect site under 5 acres with most users needing 1 to 3 [-7jTPPttg2s @ 00:00:53]. Ned of 9606 Capital plays the other end of the size curve, buying buildings of 3,000 to 50,000 square feet on 3 to 25 acres and recently closing a 78-acre Atlanta yard with 39 usable acres [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:22:04]. The operator read: small infill yards carry the deepest renter pool, while anything above 5 acres needs a rent that pencils for the user or a patient vacancy budget.

How far along the trade sits depends on who is talking. Ned of 9606 Capital calls the sector about 85% fully institutionalized, yet still mom-and-pop the way self-storage once was, with 5 to 6 years of runway left [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:06:42]. Addimando counters that under 10% of a $200 to $500 billion universe is institutionally owned [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:15:15]. Meg Aubale argues operators are still early precisely because Aubale's deals run about $5 million each, too small for institutions and short on direct comps, which is how stabilized yields above 12% survive [-s1lJ8WxV9o @ 00:03:00]. Pricing method splits along the same line. Addimando values per usable acre, provided a functional building sits on the yard, and puts valuation spreads to traditional industrial at 25 to 50 basis points, narrowed from 150 [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:12:53]. Jonathan Gonzalez of Capright says calling two 5-acre sites a few miles apart equal because both traded at X dollars per acre misses most of the story, since one may carry environmental constraints, unusable acreage or outdoor-use restrictions [p8t7CLKouHw @ 00:01:06]. Ned prices that risk directly: stabilized assets trade at a 7% cap, and Ned buys at 8.5% after modeling 6 to 8 months of vacancy [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:14:54].

Lease term is the third fault line. Ryan Solberg treats short leases as a feature and a risk in the same sentence: 1-to-3-year terms mark rents to market fast, but many carry 30-day termination rights [oYtLEutv1NQ @ 00:03:38]. The Industrial Advisors panel sees 1-to-3-year and month-to-month deals as normal, and Matt McGregor even rents per-trailer stalls at about $250 with a 10-stall minimum, while admitting that is hard to manage [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:05:23]. McGregor also notes that typical capex is 9 to 18 inches of crushed rock or paving with near-zero cost between tenants [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:01:35]. Addimando runs the opposite book, managing Alterra to a rolling 5-year WALT and refusing months-long pop-up leases [WL25Tl_e2Gs @ 00:23:58]. Steve Azar of Ambient compares the setup to manufactured housing 20 years ago, underwrites 15-year holds, and notes that long 10-to-15-year leases leave many rents sitting below market [znP8wX2_k2w @ 00:36:38]. The exit story also splits inside one panel. Bill Condon says developers ride a roughly 5-year lease and then build, while McGregor counters that most such sites never got developed because IOS income is so lucrative [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:04:02]. Pick the lane deliberately: short paper buys mark-to-market speed and turnover risk, long paper buys stickiness and below-market rent drag.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Zoning is where buyers have actually lost. Edison Basquez reports that investors have bought non-compliant sites because industrial zoning does not automatically allow outdoor storage, and tells buyers to verify with the city, then price what it takes to make a yard compliant before closing [Wu_IobArlaI @ 00:01:02]. Chase MacLeod calls not fully confirming the allowed uses in the zoning code one of the biggest mistakes in the sector, even while marketing a $9 million Inland Empire yard with multiple by-right uses [m96MC6Qe9oM @ 00:02:16]. Steve Azar adds the grandfathering trap: a legal non-conforming use can disappear when the tenant leaves, and Southern California towns are cracking down on non-conforming sites, so Ambient runs in-house land-use work that goes deeper than the zoning report [znP8wX2_k2w @ 00:12:40]. Justin Horowitz of Cooper Horowitz calls zoning super super important and warns that a seller's municipal ties can hide zoning limits, which is why the hire is zoning counsel rather than the seller's word [yb3YiKWxoFg @ 00:15:16]. The economics check is blunt: a yard that cannot legally host the tenant pool has no rent roll, so every price conversation starts with whether outdoor storage is permitted by right, what the city will allow, and who confirmed it.

The second trap is buying gross acres and leasing net ones. Edison Basquez shows landscaping, retention and setbacks eating 0.5 acre of a 2-acre site, leaving 1.5 usable acres, and warns tenants may pay only on the usable area [Wu_IobArlaI @ 00:02:33]. Gravel alone fails in Basquez's market because municipal code requires paving, drainage, fencing and lighting before tenants can even pull business licenses [-7jTPPttg2s @ 00:01:37]. Kyle Back watches gravel yards, narrow sites and sites reached through residential roads sit, while paved, fenced, lit yards with an office pull United Rentals-type credit, and Matt McGregor adds that long, narrow strips that cannot fit a truck court stay vacant [gvn8kH2TUE0 @ 00:06:45]. Ned budgets the fix in real dollars: light site work runs $75,000 to $125,000 per acre and heavy work $175,000 to $300,000, with Jacksonville near $225,000 and Chicago near $125,000 [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:29:59]. Ned cuts material cost by taking asphalt millings at $30 per truck versus a $50 dump fee, against $800 for a truck of gravel, and pre-approves shop buildings instead of building them since a metal shop takes 4 to 5 months [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:27:08]. Run the net usable acre count and the site-work budget before the deposit goes hard.

The third trap is buying the last cycle's rent. Steve Sprenger, a former trucker turned Inland Empire broker, saw more than 25 million square feet offered for sublease [tn1QmPINHeE @ 00:19:17], overseas 3PLs proposing 10-year leases with a year of free rent and then walking away, legacy leases running 15 to 20% above market, and land values down at least 50% [tn1QmPINHeE @ 00:21:39]. Ned watched LA and Inland Empire yard rents fall from $35,000 to $15,000-$18,000 [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:31:58], and called broker claims of $8,000 to $10,000 per acre per month in Atlanta mispricing when real rent sits near $5,000 [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:13:46]. The credit side bites too: Commercial Real Estate Now flags IOS tenants as local and regional operators with weak credit, some prone to bankruptcy, and says a buyer at a compressed cap rate planning a 3-year exit is a weaker fit, even though competing operators absorbing failed sites keeps credit losses low [dKQj8cpiqeE @ 00:03:03]. Andrew Smith of Carter Jonas calls the sector simple but hard and has watched £100 million leveraged portfolio plans struggle [4dMhVDuB-hM @ 00:45:53]. Ned's counterweight is balance-sheet discipline, running leverage under 35% across everything owned and carrying a 2.6 DSCR against the banks' 1.25 requirement [LftC_mcL69E @ 00:24:12]. Stress the rent to today's comps, not the last-cycle peak.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Kill-Switches Before the Deposit Goes Hard**
>

> - Confirm outdoor storage is allowed by right, not conditional, and that it is not a grandfathered legal non-conforming use that disappears when the tenant leaves [MaxLife Commercial RE MLR — oYtLEutv1NQ @ 00:04:00].
