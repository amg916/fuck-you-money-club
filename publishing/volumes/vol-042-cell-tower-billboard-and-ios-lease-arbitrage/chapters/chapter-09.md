## Executive Summary

Operators across static, digital, mobile, indoor and programmatic formats agree the face is sold on location, rate and occupancy, not on steel. Behind The Billions ranks panels by revenue per panel, from $3-8K rural to $300K-plus digital corridors; The Bell Board Podcast's Jonathan Graviss targets 75-80% occupancy before pushing rate and puts a double-commission bounty on boards empty 90 days. Tasty Ad wins contracts by showing spec ads before signing. Street numbers: a digital face earns $144K versus $36K static in the Billboard Insider model, Blip clears around 2-6 cents per blip, LED trucks book $1,200-3,600 a day, and indoor screens carry 5-10 advertisers at $100-300 a month. Practitioners split on digital rotation counts, on whether billboards still undercut Meta and Google on CPM, and on whether beginners should start static.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Across static, digital and programmatic sellers, the face is treated as rented attention sitting on a permitted piece of ground. The Money Mindset HQ frames the business as "renting out eyeballs," priced off daily effective circulation, and states flatly that "the permission to have a board there is the asset," because the 1965 Highway Beautification Act turned legal sites into grandfathered monopolies [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:00:36] [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:09:44]. The Mister Finance makes the same point from the structure side: one structure carries two faces, each sold independently, and the operator is reselling access to the location rather than the steel itself [g_IGLl-xq-I @ 00:01:07]. Behind The Billions turns that logic into a revenue ladder: rural panels pull $3-8K, suburban $10-20K, urban interstate $50-200K, and a digital premium corridor $300K-plus [Bb-6A1zEJAc @ 00:03:26]. For a seller, the consensus is that the price ceiling is fixed before the first prospecting call goes out. Traffic count, audience fit, sight angle and legal standing decide what a face can command. The sales job is to fill that face at the highest rate the location supports, across as many weeks of the year as possible, without trading the panel away below what the location is actually worth to an advertiser.

Practitioners also agree that nobody buys a face sight unseen, and nobody renews one they cannot measure. Tasty Ad compares billboard selling to a car lot where no buyer signs before seeing the vehicle, then calls out reps who show up with a contract, a map and photos but no creative; the fix is a spec ad in front of every prospect and every renewal before the contract comes out [ivfNhpDax7I @ 00:00:53]. Adrian Boysel says 9 out of 10 billboards blow the message itself, leading with a logo when the reader wants WIIFM, what's in it for me, and points to the Somerset Assisted Living board, where the logo eats three quarters of the face and "nobody cares"; the call to action, whether a QR code, phone number or URL, has to be big [14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:05:27]. On proof, Jared VanderMeer warns that billboards are hard to track and tells buyers to run a unique URL or phone number [NtQMMoZo6dw @ 00:03:56], while Boysel stacks a dedicated landing page, QR retargeting, call tracking, an SMS short code such as "text roof to 45456," and a simple where-did-you-hear-about-us question on every inbound call [14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:09:01]. Creative and attribution are part of the sale, not an afterthought.

The third point of agreement is that a face earns on duration and fill, so occupancy discipline sits at the center of every sales operation, static or digital. Jonathan Graviss of The Bell Board Podcast sets the working target at 75-80% occupancy: above that band the team pushes rate, and below it the team pushes occupancy until the inventory fills back up [N6EyLPQ9NhA @ 00:25:03]. The Money Mindset HQ sets a higher 80-90% target and notes that programmatic fill can backstop empty time from roughly 15 cents [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:10:01]. The Mister Finance tells sellers to track occupancy face by face and to take a lower price from a long-term advertiser rather than lose weeks of revenue to an empty board, running the whole desk off a rate card, booking calendar, contracts and collections, and never promising an advertiser purchases [g_IGLl-xq-I @ 00:10:24]. Billboard Source sells on the same clock: the minimum stay is usually 3 months, 6 or 12 months is typical, and anything shorter counts as a short-term buy that carries a premium for events and promotions [gReq9TXl5BY @ 00:01:18]. Read together, the operating floor is a full calendar of paying advertisers, and the premium only gets charged once that calendar is mostly sold.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The street pitch starts with intake and spec creative. Billboard Source runs every inquiry like a hotel booking in four steps: check-in date, whether that is ASAP or a seasonal push; length of stay; room type, meaning highway boards for broad awareness or specific units for a target demographic; and budget, the step it calls most important, given as a range such as $10,000 versus $100,000 [gReq9TXl5BY @ 00:00:41]. From there it sends available boards with maps, reserves the space on contract, designs in-house for free and handles printing and installation. Tasty Ad attacks the slowest link in that chain: reps either wait 3 days for spec art or pay an outrageous fee without an in-house designer, so its tool builds ads in minutes with no design skills, backed by its own network of 23 digital displays at 95%-plus occupancy with a small sales staff [ivfNhpDax7I @ 00:01:09]. Inside the sales team, Graviss reviews unsold inventory and pays a bounty of double commission on any board sitting empty 90 days [N6EyLPQ9NhA @ 00:13:11], and uses AI to draft letters to the owners of 16-17 sites in about 30 minutes [N6EyLPQ9NhA @ 00:32:51]. Speed to creative and speed to outreach are the two levers the sharpest sellers pull.

Pricing runs on cost per impression, and the sellers who close show their math on paper before a buyer ever sees a contract. Jared VanderMeer divides a $1,000-a-month board by 50,000 viewers to land at 2 cents per impression, then tells buyers to hold that number against digital channels at the negotiating table, since billboards have stayed in the 2-5 cent band while digital costs climb [NtQMMoZo6dw @ 00:01:27]. The Money Mindset HQ works the same math: $3,300 divided by 1.4 million impressions comes out near a $2.36 CPM, inside a standard range of $2-18, with premium faces at $15-25-plus and premium digital at $30-80 [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:03:19]. Rents are typically sold in 4-week blocks, from $250 to $14K for static and $1.2K to $15K for digital, and a Geopath-certified daily effective circulation of 80K commands 40-60% more than an uncertified board [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:01:32]. Adrian Boysel prices boards at $5,000 to $35,000 a month, with Sunset Boulevard at $15K-35K, production at $250-1,500 and installation on top [14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:10:15]. The pattern is consistent: quote rate as a CPM, anchor it to the buyer's digital spend, and get the audience number certified so the rate holds up when the buyer pushes back on price at renewal.

The newer formats sell the face by the day, the slot or the six-second play. Blip, walked through by Bart Bradshaw on Local Marketing Institute, has a one-cent minimum, advertisers running $5 to $50-plus a day, and a worked example of $25 a day equaling $750 a month; the default bid is $0.35 per blip, but Bradshaw bids $0.50 because "our algorithm favors the higher" bid, and observed clearing costs run about 2-6 cents per 6-8 second blip [uoJvU0CyoBY @ 00:48:29]. Campaigns go from location, budget, schedule and artwork to review and launch, live within 24-48 hours after Blip and operator moderation [uoJvU0CyoBY @ 00:38:14]. On wheels, Cant Miss US prices route campaigns at $1,200-1,800 a day and tailgates, festivals and political rallies at $2,500-3,600 a day, so 10 event days a month grosses $12K-18K [o3KgQJFdGXQ @ 00:01:10]. Indoors, Hustle Labs places a free screen in a partner business with long dwell time, offers the owner content management plus a 20-30% revenue share, then sells 5-10 ad slots per screen at $100-300 a month each; five advertisers at $150 is $750 a month from one screen [IsQBWJcu3ro @ 00:04:53]. Each format trades permanence for speed, and each lives or dies on how many slots stay sold.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Run the four-question intake
   - Qualify start date, length of stay, board type and budget range before pulling any inventory.
   - Billboard Source books every buyer like a hotel stay: check-in date, length of stay with a usual 3-month minimum and 6 or 12 months typical, room type (highway boards for awareness, specific units for a demographic) and a budget range such as $10,000 versus $100,000, then sends available boards with maps [Billboard Source — gReq9TXl5BY @ 00:00:41].
2. Build spec creative before the contract
   - Produce a WIIFM-led spec ad with an oversized call to action for every prospect and renewal.
   - Tasty Ad shows spec ads before the contract on every prospect and every renewal [Tasty Ad — ivfNhpDax7I @ 00:00:53]; Adrian Boysel leads with what's in it for the viewer instead of the logo and makes the QR code, phone number or URL large [Adrian Boysel — 14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:05:27].
3. Quote the rate as a cost per impression
   - Divide monthly rent by audience and set the result against the buyer's digital spend.
   - Jared VanderMeer's math: $1,000 a month over 50,000 viewers is 2 cents per impression, and the negotiation compares that figure directly against what the buyer pays for digital impressions [Jared VanderMeer — NtQMMoZo6dw @ 00:04:54].
4. Wire tracking and sell a 13-week run
   - Attach a dedicated URL, call tracking or SMS short code and commit the buyer to repeat exposure.
   - Boysel tracks with a dedicated landing page, QR retargeting, call tracking and a short code like "text roof to 45456" [Adrian Boysel — 14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:09:01], and tells buyers not to expect month 1 to fund month 2, citing 13 exposures over 13 weeks minimum [Adrian Boysel — 14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:08:06].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is static versus digital, and it starts with how many advertisers one screen really carries. Behind The Billions models 8 to 12 ads per minute and treats conversion as a $200K to $500K spend that pays back in 2 to 3 years while lifting a site's value from $200K to $1M [Bb-6A1zEJAc @ 00:07:21]. Ekoniq cites Broadsign at only 4 to 5 ads per 60 seconds, yet still shows the same board jumping from $36K static to $144K digital, a 4x lift [eI1lH8lhvd8 @ 00:06:40]. Ekoniq also models a standard 14x48 conversion at roughly $200K, so that lift has to cover a large check. The Mister Finance pushes back hard: more advertising slots do not automatically mean more profit, so beginners should start with static and earn their way into screens [g_IGLl-xq-I @ 00:09:12]. Finpulse puts that warning into numbers. Eight rotating ads do not mean eight times the profit, and a six-slot face at $1,500 per slot grosses $9,000 only when every slot sells, collapsing to $4,500 when just three do [WCGRAxsks5U @ 00:07:13]. Digital multiplies inventory; it does not multiply buyers, and the rotation count each camp quotes changes the slot math before a single contract is signed.

The second fight is whether outdoor is still cheap. Jared VanderMeer runs the math as $1,000 a month divided by 50,000 viewers, or 2 cents per impression, and argues billboards hold steady at 2 to 5 cents while digital channel costs keep rising, making cost per impression the lever to pull in any negotiation [NtQMMoZo6dw @ 00:03:04]. Jonathan Graviss on The Bell Board Podcast disputes the premise: Meta and Google now match outdoor's CPM, outdoor has held roughly 5% of media share for decades, and Graviss's rapid-fire answers call the claim that digital is overpriced false [N6EyLPQ9NhA @ 00:40:41]. The same split shows up inside the sales office. Graviss sets 75 to 80% occupancy as the hinge, pushing rate above it and occupancy below it, while host Neil Bell leans occupancy-first [N6EyLPQ9NhA @ 00:25:03]. The Money Mindset HQ pushes the target higher, aiming for 80 to 90% occupancy and backfilling open slots with programmatic fill starting around 15 cents [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:10:01]. Graviss also attacks dead inventory directly with a bounty of double commission on any board sitting empty 90 days and a standing review of unsold faces. As the Bell Board conversation puts it, you have to take advantage of rate and occupancy.

The third divide is format and contract length. Billboard Source sells like a hotel booking: a 3-month minimum, 6 or 12 months typical, a premium for anything shorter, and free in-house design once the contract reserves the space [gReq9TXl5BY @ 00:01:18]. Blip's Bart Bradshaw tears that model up, selling digital faces by the blip with a one-cent minimum, advertisers spending $5 to $50+ a day, and campaigns live within 24 to 48 hours after Blip and operator moderation [uoJvU0CyoBY @ 00:38:33]. Traditional digital contracts, per the Blip team, run about four weeks to half a year at $1,000 to $20,000+, and traditional advertisers make up only about 2% of the market. On wheels, the camps split again. Jerry Teeter of Mobile Digital Billboard University pitches a $250K Legion 613 truck plus a business-in-a-box kit of manuals, contracts, website and media-kit templates [mG9J2Z6RgSY @ 00:07:05]. Cant Miss US answers the passive-income question with a flat no, calling the truck a high-tech mobile media asset instead, with route days at $1,200 to $1,800 and event days at $2,500 to $3,600 [o3KgQJFdGXQ @ 00:01:32]. Both camps sell trucks, and Cant Miss US openly pitches buying from a proven fleet for the support system, not just the metal.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures are lease and leverage failures, not bad creative. Behind The Billions walks through a buyer who paid $400M for 300 boards with 75% debt, watched ground leases come back at double rent, and went bankrupt in 2021, then a $30M digital program where 60% of leases expired, rents rose 40 to 80%, and the network sold at a loss in 2023 [Bb-6A1zEJAc @ 00:07:54]. The root paradox is that a $20K structure depreciates over 40 years while the ground lease under it renews every 20. The Money Mindset HQ quantifies the squeeze: a 10-point rent rise costs about 18% of cash flow, so lock fixed 2 to 3% escalators and a minimum rent, and know that easements sell for 8 to 15 times rent [JCnJw5eFyEE @ 00:11:14]. The Mister Finance flags the earlier trap of signing a long lease before approvals and paying rent where you are legally unable to build, and warns that grandfathered sites may not be rebuildable [g_IGLl-xq-I @ 00:03:41]. How They Make Money lists the standing risks every operator carries: sign-code changes, losing a lease, recessions and storms [eVhqvVbf0rA @ 00:03:25]. Lease cost is no rounding error either: Outfront carries about $447M in lease costs against roughly $1.39B in billboard revenue.

Digital boards fail quietly through leaks and empty slots. Ekoniq itemizes the drains: about $12K a year in electricity, percentage-of-gross leases, sales cost, maintenance and software [eI1lH8lhvd8 @ 00:07:20]. Its list of common mistakes reads like a site-selection autopsy: building a second face, road reroutes, buildings going up in front of the board, and putting a third digital board in the same mile [eI1lH8lhvd8 @ 00:08:55]. Even the small-market case Ekoniq models is modest: $80K of capital, 65% occupancy, $35,100 in revenue, $26,790 EBITDA and a 24% return. The Mister Finance shows how fast occupancy flips the sign: 10 boards and 20 faces at $1,800 per 4 weeks and 70% occupancy gross $327,600 a year and clear about $4,300 a month after $23K of monthly expenses, but at 50% occupancy the same network bleeds $3,500 a month on roughly $850K of construction [g_IGLl-xq-I @ 00:20:19]. That is why The Mister Finance insists each face be measured individually and expansion follow verified cash flow and legal rights, not excitement. Finpulse adds that permits and zoning create a barrier to entry but no guaranteed monopoly [WCGRAxsks5U @ 00:08:28]. The math lesson is identical across all three: model the face at realistic fill, not full rotation.

On the street, deals die from sloppy execution. Tasty Ad says reps walk in with a contract, map and photos but no creative, and that the bottleneck is spec art taking 3 days or costing an outrageous fee without an in-house designer, so spec ads belong in front of every prospect and every renewal before the contract [ivfNhpDax7I @ 00:01:09]. Tasty Ad backs the fix with its own network: 95%+ occupancy across 23 digital displays after nearly a decade with a small sales staff. Adrian Boysel says 9 out of 10 billboards get messaging wrong, even calling out their own Somerset Assisted Living logo, which fills three-quarters of the board, because nobody cares, and warns that a bad location can mean zero return and that expecting month one to fund month two ignores the 13-exposure, 13-week minimum [14aRU-ObgC0 @ 00:05:27]. On mobile, Jerry Teeter says most trucks are underpowered on 8, 10 or 12 kW generators, specifying 20 kW and a sealed cab wall because aging generators leak carbon monoxide [mG9J2Z6RgSY @ 00:02:56]. Hustle Labs lists the indoor version: buying 10 screens on day one, chasing trendy locations over measured dwell time, and undercharging, and assigns pitching three high-traffic businesses this week as the first move [IsQBWJcu3ro @ 00:07:00].

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Hard Stops Before Signing Ground, Steel or Screens**
> 
> Each stop below maps to a loss or trap a named practitioner reported. If any one fails, the deal waits.
