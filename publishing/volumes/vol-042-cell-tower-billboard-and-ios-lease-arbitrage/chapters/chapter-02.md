Seven practitioner channels, led by Tower Genius, converge on one point: the fine print, not the rent, sets the value of a cell site. Perpetual easements run with the land, leave the next owner an access encumbrance with zero income, and lower building NOI. ## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: ROFR, Perpetual Easements and Who Controls the Dirt

Tower Genius treats the right of first refusal as a very big deal that landlords routinely hand over for nothing. Steve Gazzella lays out the mechanic plainly: carriers and tower companies want to see your letter of intent and hold the right to match it, and they simply assume landlords will grant it in a lease negotiation, when the clause is worth tens of thousands of dollars [5MqJqF4hVEQ @ 00:00:47]. The proof came from a live Tower Genius sale: out of roughly half a dozen requests for offers on a ROFR-encumbered lease, only three buyers bothered to bid, because nobody breaks their back assembling a proposal that a tower company will just match [5MqJqF4hVEQ @ 00:01:28]. The same team calls ROFR an artificial market the tower companies love, one that drives values down and should be struck from the agreement up front whenever the landlord has leverage [H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:02:18]. The flip side is measurable: a client whose ROFR was removed during a second amendment later went to market into real competition and was offered over $200,000 on top of their best prior offer [H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:04:14]. The consensus rule is blunt: competition drives price, and the ROFR is the clause that kills competition before the first bid ever lands.

Once the rental stream is sold, it is gone for every future owner of the dirt. Steve at Tower Genius answered a Missouri reader whose late mother had granted American Tower a perpetual easement: there is nothing to undo, the easement transfers to whoever buys the house, no further income can be had from the site, and the next owner still has to give access to the tower company and its subcontractors, an encumbrance buyers price in by demanding a discount [weiPEHNdlU0 @ 00:02:07]. Tower Genius walked a North Carolina buyer, Diane, through the same trap from the other side: a buyout company is not buying or subdividing land, it is buying an easement on the lease rights, filed at the county, which a lot of people simply do not know how to spot in a title search [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:01:42]. Their rule for anyone buying a tower parcel is to run a title search and confirm the landlord is actually receiving rent, or you are buying land with an easement you will never see a dime from [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:02:56]. James Furlo, evaluating a buyout on a building being acquired, reaches the same math as an investor: selling the stream lowers net operating income, so the building itself is worth less on resale [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:10:33].

The lease is never just the square on the site plan. Vertical Consultants, fronted by a former AT&T attorney, warns that leasing 1,000 to 3,000 square feet can quietly restrict the rest of the property, limiting future development, disposition and sometimes financing, which bites hardest on commercial parcels and farmland later converted to commercial [p55_Vmt4xnk @ 00:00:56]. Their sharpest number: 95-plus percent of cell tower leases carry no relocation rights for the property owner, so when a buyer offers ten times market value on the condition that the tower's utilities get rerouted, the owner has no pathway to deliver it [p55_Vmt4xnk @ 00:01:43]. Self Storage Income, holding roughly three or four towers across its facilities, puts it more brutally: tower companies are bullies with an army of lawyers, and because the square is attached to the whole property, any future sale or change means working with, and getting permission from, a counterparty that does not care about you [bJCG2BdPq48 @ 00:02:27]. Nigel Paxton of Aries Advisors names the root asymmetry: most owners see one of these deals in a lifetime, while carriers and tower companies negotiate them every single day on contracts built to protect their interest, not yours [Yg_kM2-vltw @ 00:01:06]. Structure, not rent, decides what the site is worth.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Match Windows, Discounts, Clawbacks and Escalators

Here is how the ROFR actually fires. Once a landlord signs a letter of intent with a buyer, say a $150,000 lump sum for future rent, that bona fide offer must be presented to the tenant, whether American Tower, Crown Castle, SBA or a rooftop carrier like T-Mobile or AT&T; skip it and you are in default, and the tenant then gets 30, 45 or 60 days to match the offer or beat it by a dollar [H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:01:10]. Tower Genius does not settle for deleting the clause; Steve and Kevin Donahue swap in hybrid language they wrote on a Verizon rooftop in California 10 to 12 years ago, which Verizon accepted and which every carrier and tower company they have dealt with since has accepted on new and renegotiated sites [H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:03:22]. On the sale itself, Steve warns that easement buyers do not disclose the source of their funds, so a landlord who thinks they are running a wide auction may be bidding the same money source against itself; one landlord who booked a single 30-minute consult with Kevin Donahue closed a seven-figure sale 35 percent higher [SLOz0FJW1ew @ 00:01:39]. And read every rent reduction letter closely, because those letters are always fishing for a fresh ROFR [5MqJqF4hVEQ @ 00:03:14].

James Furlo of Furlo Capital ran the seller side live while acquiring a building with AT&T and US Cellular rooftop leases, and itemized what moves an offer. Easement buyers price the stream at roughly a 12 to 15 percent discount, effectively underwriting a 12 percent return, which made selling the lease cheaper than raising that slice of the down payment from investors who treat 12 percent as a minimum [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:04:57]. Terms swing hard by buyer: some want a 50 or 55 year easement, others 99 years; some pay more if you take installments over 10 years; some bar you from building a competing tower within a mile; and nearly all offer a 50/50 split on new tenants, which they will also cash out as a lump sum [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:13:26]. The trap: certain buyers withhold the first four months of rent as a transaction-cost clawback, so the headline number that looks higher is really the lower offer, and it only surfaces in the written letter of intent [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:16:55]. SiteBid Infrastructure frames the structural choice underneath: a master lease turns the existing tenant lease into a sublease and pays less because the buyer's rights are limited, while a 99-year perpetual easement draws the bigger upfront check [EsfznycDU6I @ 00:01:00].

On the lease side, the numbers practitioners quote set the floor for any negotiation. Vertical Consultants shows why flat co-location bonuses rot: a $400 payment per added tenant is 20 percent of a $2,000 tower rent today but only 10 percent once the tower company charges AT&T $4,000, so they push clients toward a true percentage of tower and ground revenue with an annual reconciliation right, because the tower company will shrink the bucket that share applies to [eJhWsOIzk6A @ 00:01:05]. Steve at Tower Genius reports tower companies pushing 0.99 percent annual escalators and terms like Tillman Infrastructure's 10-year initial term plus 17 five-year renewals, against a historical 25 to 30 year structure; the ask is still 3 percent, though it only lands with leverage [RMRSHif8WD0 @ 00:02:48]. The ceiling comes from carrier economics: Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile pay tower companies roughly $2,000 a month, ground rent comes out of that, and in high-supply areas landlords should expect $500 to $600 offers [RMRSHif8WD0 @ 00:03:40]. When a lease optimization letter hinted at termination, Tower Genius had the landlord reply asking when the tenant would remove its equipment, copying the carrier; within 48 hours the area director called begging them not to treat it as a termination [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:10:10].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Verify the stream before you buy or sell
   - Pull county records and confirm rent is still flowing to the landlord.
   - Tower Genius has fielded roughly 100 calls from buyers who found a recorded buyout easement after closing; run the title search and confirm rent is being paid or you own land you will never see a dime from [Tower Genius — Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:02:56].
2. Audit the option and lease clauses
   - Score market rate, escalators, utilities, taxes and expansion rights.
   - Nexus Towers checks the monthly rate and escalation cadence, who carries utilities and taxes, and whether the carrier slipped in unlimited expansion rights, because the option converts into a 30 to 35 year lease [Nexus Towers — xSsNtRvjHBg @ 00:01:56].
3. Strike or swap the ROFR
   - Replace the right of first refusal with hybrid language before any sale.
   - Tower Genius got a late-1990s ROFR removed in a second amendment and replaced with language carriers have accepted, setting up a later sale that drew over $200,000 above the best offer [Tower Genius — H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:03:05].
4. Shop, normalize and time the close
   - Collect written LOIs, net out clawbacks and line up the lender.
   - Furlo reports buyers like two months to close, can do one, and can hit two weeks with paperwork ready; a simultaneous close with the purchase lets the easement sit senior to the bank without a separate permission form [James Furlo | Furlo Capital — bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:22:41].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to cash out the rent stream at all. Steve at Tower Genius tells the Missouri heirs of a perpetual-easement grantor that there is nothing left to squeeze out of American Tower: once the stream is sold there is no other income from the site, yet the new owner still owes access to the tower company and its subcontractors, so house buyers demand a discount for that encumbrance [weiPEHNdlU0 @ 00:02:07]. Yet the same Tower Genius bench flips the script for urban rooftops facing small-cell saturation: if five or ten small cells at $270 a year each can replace a $6,000-a-month macro site, the buyout industry is still writing checks, so offload that risk and let them deal with it [mfnmRoHN1Uo @ 00:06:17]. James Furlo runs the cash-out as acquisition financing, selling the rooftop leases on a building under contract and using the lump sum as part of the down payment, because easement buyers pricing at roughly a 12% return are cheaper capital than investors for whom 12% is the floor [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:06:31]. Furlo also books the cost honestly: giving up that cash flow lowers NOI, so the building is worth less at resale unless improvements make up the gap and the spreadsheet still works [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:10:33].

Right of first refusal is where the landlord advocates and the investor-seller pull in opposite directions. Tower Genius treats a ROFR as a value killer: in one recent client sale, six requests for offers produced bids on only three, because buyers with the jitters will not break their backs on a deal the tower company will simply match, which creates an artificial market and drags the price down; Steve prices the clause at maybe 50 grand, maybe 100 grand, site by site, and says never hand it out for nothing [5MqJqF4hVEQ @ 00:01:28]. The same shop concedes the hard line bends under weak leverage: with multiple competing properties nearby, sometimes you live with the ROFR, get the tower built, and treat the site as a long-term play rather than a first-pass home run [H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:02:32]. That patience paid off when a client with a late-1990s lease had the clause struck in a second amendment and replaced with hybrid language Kevin Donahue and Steve first got Verizon to accept on a California rooftop; at retirement, a competing buyer came in over $200,000 above the best prior offer [H0Q5FZ_5RWA @ 00:04:14]. James Furlo, on the selling side of an easement, runs the reverse play and asks the buyer for a first right of refusal so the building owner gets a shot at buying the easement back if it is ever resold [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:06:59].

Even among sellers, practitioners split on which buyout structure to take and how to get paid. SiteBid Infrastructure lays out two paths: a master lease, where fresh terms are signed and the existing carrier lease becomes a sublease, keeps property rights intact but pays less because the buyer's control is limited [EsfznycDU6I @ 00:00:44]; a perpetual easement, often 99 years, transfers the tower operation rights, earns a bigger upfront check, can open the door to a 1031 exchange, and hands tenant management to the buyer [EsfznycDU6I @ 00:01:15]. Furlo found the easement menu wider than that binary: some buyers want only 50 or 55 years while others want 99, and payments can be spread over ten years for a higher total because it costs the buyer nothing upfront [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:13:26]. Almost every term sheet Furlo reviewed also carried a 50/50 split on any new tenant the buyer lands, which the buyer will even cash out as another lump sum [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:14:38]. On revenue share inside a live lease, Vertical Consultants rejects the common flat $200 to $400 per added tenant because it never tracks inflation or tower pricing, so $400 against a $2,000 AT&T rent is 20% but against $4,000 drops to 10%; the play is a percentage of what the tower company collects, with a defined bucket and annual reconciliation [eJhWsOIzk6A @ 00:01:05].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start at the title desk. Diane in Pittsboro, North Carolina bought land with a tower the previous owners had already sold; nothing was subdivided because buyers purchase an easement, not dirt, and Tower Genius recalls a Florida owner who paid $5,000 to an attorney who owned a title company and still missed the easement [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:01:24]. Tower Genius says the shop has fielded probably 100 such calls over a dozen years, and a buyer who skips the title search and rent confirmation ends up holding land with an easement they will never see a dime from [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:02:56]. The fix taught on air: have counsel go back to the seller, or politely to the cash flow buyer, for the full agreement to confirm pass-through tax and property-damage protections. Tax language is the second landmine: a church pastor called Tower Genius in tears after the congregation sold its rental stream with botched tax language and a couple of other defects, leaving a liability the church could not get out of [SLOz0FJW1ew @ 00:00:40]. The same desk warns that buyers rarely disclose the source of their funds, so a landlord who thinks the deal is being shopped to a crowd may be bidding one money source against itself [SLOz0FJW1ew @ 00:01:19].

Lease optimization letters are the volume trap. Alan in New Hampshire got an American Tower letter asking for a 50% rent cut because the two sides were supposedly partners; Alan answered that they were the landlord, not a partner, and asked when the tenant would come pick up the equipment, which Tower Genius calls exactly the right gut instinct [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:05:46]. Tower Genius principals have had heirs on the phone in tears after parents took a $500 or $1,000 reduction and signed a 90-year extension under a decommissioning threat; when the team had one elderly couple reply asking when equipment would be removed and copied the carrier, an area director called within 48 hours begging them not to treat it as a termination [Z2FKmFdZgvA @ 00:10:10]. Heading into 2026, Steve says relocations usually trace to eminent domain, not a $500 cut, yet ND7, Black Dot and other Southern California outfits still scare landlords into terrible, stinking awful deals [RMRSHif8WD0 @ 00:07:09]. New-lease paper is degrading in parallel: 0.99% annual escalators that bleed money against inflation, and Tilman Infrastructure offering a 10-year initial term with 17 additional five-year renewals [RMRSHif8WD0 @ 00:02:48].

Control is the slow-burn loss. Self Storage Income runs three or four towers, one paying roughly $3,500 a month for doing nothing, and still vows never to put up another, because selling or changing the land means asking permission from a tenant that sues, has an army of lawyers, and behaves like the mafia [bJCG2BdPq48 @ 00:02:10]. Vertical Consultants pins the mechanism: 95-plus percent of cell leases carry no relocation rights for the owner, so a buyer offering 10 times market value on the condition that tower utilities get rerouted can stall, and restrictions bleed into development, disposition and financing of the whole parcel [p55_Vmt4xnk @ 00:01:43]. On the buyout side, Furlo hit offer-sheet games: one polished buyer with a great website and friendly calls quietly withheld the first four months of rent in its letter of intent as a transaction-cost clawback, a material term never mentioned on the phone [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:16:55]. Carrier risk reprices everything: buyers paid a 24 to 25 times multiple on the AT&T rooftop lease but offered about five times on US Cellular because a pending T-Mobile consolidation might drop the site, and the equipment is not interchangeable for a replacement tenant [bk4EnTlbPdA @ 00:11:22]. Selling after closing also forces the lender to subordinate, which not every bank knows how to do.
