# Chapter 01: Cell Tower Lease Buyouts: What They Are and What They're Worth

*Lump-sum offers for rent streams, average buyout values and calculators, ranking buyout companies (TowerPoint, Airwave, Nexus), Dish lease rejections, Starlink risk, and landowner testimonials*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree a cell tower lease buyout sells the landlord's rent stream through a lease assignment and easement, not the land, and that it is priced as a multiple of annual rent. Tower Genius pegs average deals at 16 to 17.5 times, rising to 19 times for clean leases with 3% annual escalators, revenue share, and no right of first refusal; Acquisition Insider sees aggregator offers between 8 and 15 times and 18 times for competitively shopped multi-carrier leases. Tower Genius warns that ROFR, confidentiality, and consent language crush bidding, and that a handful of money sources sit behind roughly 20 buyout brands. Buyers discount at 8 to 12% and model termination risk, highlighted by Dish halting rent on about 25,000 sites. Practitioners split on timing: sell now and transfer obsolescence risk, or wait to renegotiate at renewal.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who touches this market describes the same product: a cell tower lease buyout is the purchase of the landlord's interest in an existing wireless lease income stream and its future rent payments, not the dirt and not the steel. Steve Casella of Tower Genius lays out the structure: the owner sells an easement, the lease is assigned to the buyer, and the buyer gains the right to collect future rent and renegotiate at renewal, in exchange for a lump sum or installments [pE5ehBu0C4s @ 00:00:58]. The land under the tower or the building under the rooftop site typically stays with the owner unless the deal is structured as a fee simple sale, and the buyers are tower management companies, REITs, and boutique hedge funds that specialize in digital infrastructure [pE5ehBu0C4s @ 00:01:33]. Acquisition Insider frames it bluntly: you are buying the landowner's rent check, usually a thousand to two thousand dollars a month, not the carrier rent on top, with Steel in the Air pegging the average ground lease at about $1,300 a month [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:01:13]. The Allworth Financial caller confirmed the same split: on 17 acres, only the easement in a corner of the property was for sale, not the plot [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:04:31].

Pricing is quoted the same way across camps: as a multiple of annual rent, with lease language pushing the multiple up or down. Casella's rule of thumb for an average deal is roughly 16 to 17.5 times annual cell site rent, with the exact figure driven by what the lease agreement says [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:00:32]. Acquisition Insider puts aggregator offers clustering between 8 and 15 times, cites Steel in the Air's 2026 rule of thumb of about 18 times for a competitively shopped multi-carrier lease, and states flatly that a right of first refusal drives value down [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:02:09]. Tower Genius goes further on the ROFR: Casella says he heard an executive from one of the three big tower companies call the provision a baseball bat used to beat down landlords, and warns that confidentiality and consent-to-assign language is purposely left in leases to kill any bidding environment [wpxdOZZukBY @ 00:04:41]. The shared conclusion is that the owner walks into these calls outgunned. Casella says landlords are at a complete disadvantage against the Goliaths of wireless, who are certainly not going to tell them what they should be doing, and that buyers want these streams as cheaply as possible precisely because they are valuable [kPzYR4RlrCM @ 00:03:39].

The third point of agreement is that the buyer field is far narrower and more institutional than the stack of letters suggests. Casella estimates about 20 lease buyout companies are active, but only a handful of actual money sources stand behind them, so an owner can think a bid is competitive while the same company is bidding against itself [MteawsLjdZY @ 00:01:00]. In a longer breakdown he describes a Midwest hedge fund that funds several buyout firms, meaning a landlord who solicits offers from companies A through D may pick curtain number two and discover all four sit under the same octopus [wpxdOZZukBY @ 00:12:37]. Acquisition Insider documents the institutional weight: Radius Global, parent of AP Wireless, was taken private in 2023 by EQT and a Canadian pension fund for about $3 billion with roughly 9,700 leases, while Landmark Dividend, the company that cold calls farmers, is owned by Digital Bridge with Abu Dhabi's sovereign fund holding 40% [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:01:29]. As for why buyers chase an asset some call obsolete, Casella's answer is musical chairs: buyers package the leases and sell them to a Wall Street hedge fund that sells them again, and the landlord's goal is to transfer the risk before the music stops [SQKWpJzLCTs @ 00:04:54].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Tower Genius teaches valuation as rent times a multiple, then shows how each lease term moves the multiplier. Casella's baseline is a tower paying $1,500 a month, $18,000 a year, with a 10% escalator every five years, a right of first refusal, two tenants, and 30 years left: $18,000 times 17.5 equals $315,000 [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:02:07]. Raise rent to $2,500 a month, swap to a 3% annual escalator, strip the ROFR, keep two tenants with a 10-year expiry, and the multiplier climbs to 19 on $30,000 of annual rent, roughly $570,000. Add $750 a month in collocation revenue share and the $39,000 base times 19 produces $741,000, which he ties to a client site in Utah [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:04:13]. His urban rooftop example stacks Verizon at $3,800, AT&T at $3,200, and T-Mobile at $2,750 a month, leaves Dish's $1,500 out as toxic, and lands at $117,000 times 19, or $2,223,000, adding that ROFRs in those leases would have cost about $200,000 [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:07:14]. Separately he warns that buyers treat Dish as toxic and will not touch it with a ten-foot pole [X75LHgvP1U8 @ 00:01:20].

Acquisition Insider runs the same asset from the buyer's spreadsheet. Aggregators price with discount rates of 8 to 12%, while owner-side valuations use 4 to 6%; a lease paying $18,000 a year with a 3% escalator and 25 years of term, discounted at 8%, is worth about $250,000, or 14 times, and only an assumption of perpetuity gets you to 20 times [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:02:26]. In the illustrative deal, Max buys a Crown Castle ground lease at $1,500 a month with two national carriers on the structure, pays 14 times or $252,000, and closes at $260,000 all in after title, survey, and recording. Year one nets about $17,500, a 6.7% yield growing 3% annually, and if the tenant stays the full term Max collects roughly $650,000 over 25 years for a low to mid 7% unlevered return [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:03:11]. Sourcing is off-market: the FCC antenna structure registry gives the tower, county records give the owner, a direct offer follows, and small buyers should get on landowner-side consultants' buyer lists [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:07]. Allworth Financial's host guessed buyers bake in an 8 to 10% or higher internal rate of return for the tower-pull risk [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:03:36].

On the sell side, the street work starts with knowing exactly who is paying. Casella says pay stubs that only read collocator A, B, C are not enough; owners should read the electrical meters, look for carrier labels on equipment cabinets and Sharpie marks on the meter bank, or file a FOIA request with the building department to see whether Dish pulled electrical permits [X75LHgvP1U8 @ 00:02:04]. Pricing pressure comes next. The Allworth Financial caller, collecting $11,000 a year with 2% bumps and 26 years left, had a third-party lump-sum offer of $170,000, a five-year payout at $190,000, and a ten-year payout at $225,000, while the current lessee offered about $140,000 [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:01:00]. The hosts' instruction was to push for the $190,000 already requested and split the payment, half in December and half in January, to spread the tax hit over two calendar years [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:06:46]. Airwave Advisors clients describe the competitive version: their advisor, Nick, pulled in more than eight interested buyers, threw one out immediately, pared the field to two, and ran an auction-style process that closed with a professional outfit the sellers had never heard of [ZhCVLpLy4-o @ 00:01:24].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Source the site and the owner off-market
   - Pull towers from the FCC antenna structure registry, match owners in county records, and make a direct offer.
   - Acquisition Insider says most rent streams trade off-market and a small buyer competes the same way aggregators do, while also getting on the buyer lists of landowner-side consultants who shop leases to multiple bidders [Acquisition Insider — MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:07]
2. Read the full lease and every amendment
   - Confirm who the tenant really is, the termination rights, the remaining term, and the escalator.
   - Those four terms drive the multiple before any wire goes out [Acquisition Insider — MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:24]; Casella adds that ROFR, confidentiality, and consent-to-assign language must be found before the lease is shopped [Tower Genius — wpxdOZZukBY @ 00:05:12]
3. Verify every carrier physically on the structure
   - Walk the compound, identify each paying carrier, and walk away from single-tenant sites.
   - Get an estoppel certificate and reconcile it to 24 months of bank statements [Acquisition Insider — MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:38]; Casella's field method is reading electrical meters, cabinet labels, and permit records pulled through a FOIA request [Tower Genius — X75LHgvP1U8 @ 00:02:20]
4. Run title, zoning, and the termination stress test
   - Search title for mortgages and rights of first refusal, check zoning, and model rent stopping in years five to eight.
   - Confirm zoning stops the tower company from moving next door, and if one site cannot survive the rent stopping in year five to eight, buy several or pass [Acquisition Insider — MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:38]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The single biggest split is the multiple itself. Steve at Tower Genius quotes an average buyout of roughly 16 to 17.5 times annual rent, worked through a $1,500-a-month tower at 17.5x for $315,000, and pushes clean leases with a 3% annual escalator, no right of first refusal and a co-locator to a 19x multiplier [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:03:19]. Acquisition Insider prices the same paper from the buy side: aggregator offers commonly cluster between 8 and 15 times, aggregators discount at 8 to 12% while owner-side valuations use 4 to 6%, and an $18,000-a-year lease with a 3% escalator and 25 years of term discounted at 8% is worth about $250,000, or 14 times [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:02:09] [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:02:26]. Their 20x perpetuity number is flagged as something nobody should assume. The Allworth Financial hosts land closer to the buyer's math: a caller with about $11,000 a year, a 2% bump and 26 years left was offered $170,000 cash, $190,000 over five years or $225,000 over ten, and the host guessed an eight to 10% internal rate of return baked in for the investor because of the risk of the tower being pulled [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:03:14]. Read side by side, the gap between 14x and 19x is the entire negotiation.

The second fault line is how the deal gets shopped. Tower Genius argues that a do-it-yourself auction is rigged by structure: a landlord can pull four or five offers from companies in different states and literally be holding four offers from the same money source, which Steve calls not a fair bidding situation [P2LhXcxO5KE @ 00:01:07]. Airwave Advisors clients describe the counter-playbook run by an advisor who controls the process: after two prospective buyers had extracted piles of financials, Nick took over the paperwork, surfaced more than eight interested buyers, threw one out immediately and pared the field to two outfits in an auction-type situation [ZhCVLpLy4-o @ 00:01:04]. TowerPoint pitches the opposite route, selling straight to the capital provider for an immediate lump sum, a complimentary site valuation, and a relationship framed as a long-term partner before, during and after the transaction [JX38qocLKEg @ 00:01:01]. Acquisition Insider sits on the buyer side of the same fence and treats owner-side consultants as a deal-flow channel, telling small buyers that most rent streams trade off market and that they should get on those consultants' buyer lists because those advisors shop leases to multiple bidders [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:07]. The consultant who protects the seller is the same pipe the buyer wants into.

The third split is whether to sell at all, and when. Tower Genius gives two different answers depending on the lease. On dense urban rooftops Steve calls the market a game of musical chairs, predicting that carriers will swap $6,000-to-$7,000-a-month rooftops for 10 or 20 small cells and that buyers are packaging these leases to resell to hedge funds on Wall Street, so the landlord should transfer the risk and be sitting in a chair when the music stops [SQKWpJzLCTs @ 00:04:34]. Yet in the buyout-company walkthrough Steve warns that buyers love to grab an undervalued lease right before renewal for the arbitrage spread, and that some owners should skip the sale and wait a couple of years to renegotiate [wpxdOZZukBY @ 00:03:34]. The Allworth Financial host said flatly they would sell a 26-year tower stream threatened by Starlink, since 26 years is a long time in technology, but would push on price and split the proceeds over two calendar years [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:02:51]. Acquisition Insider frames the buyer's side of that bet: multi-carrier sites bought at an 8% discount rate with the termination case modeled return a quiet 6 to 7% that escalates for decades, an asset EQT, Digital Bridge and sovereign wealth funds price like bond traders [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:06:53].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The headline failure is a tenant that walks. Acquisition Insider builds the case from real numbers: a buyer pays 15 times in 2023 for a ground lease whose only tenant is Dish, $1,200 a month on a new 5G site, $222,000 all in; the excused letter lands in October 2025, rent stops by year end after about $36,000 collected, and by June 2026 Dish is in bankruptcy and the buyer holds a claim in line with everyone else Dish walked away from, plus an easement under a dark tower worth very little [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:04:32]. Even the clean two-carrier example breaks if the lease terminates in year eight, returning about $160,000 against $260,000 invested. That is why the pros haircut single-carrier sites. Tower Genius was already treating Dish as toxic to the buyout industry in 2024, saying nobody wanted to touch it with a 10-foot pole and that buyers demand the sub-tenant names so they do not get caught holding the bag [X75LHgvP1U8 @ 00:01:00]; Steve even stripped the Dish rent out of a $117,000 rooftop valuation [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:06:34]. The Allworth Financial caller named the same exposure from the seller's chair: the carrier can tear the tower down any time and quit paying with no penalty [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:02:36].

The second trap is paper the landlord signed years earlier. Steve at Tower Genius recounts an executive from one of the three big tower companies saying on a call that the right of first refusal is like a baseball bat used to beat down cell tower landlords; the clause forces the owner to share any bona fide offer with the tenant or land in default, and when owners negotiate it out, crafty attorneys slip in confidentiality or consent-to-assign language that does the same damage [wpxdOZZukBY @ 00:04:41]. On the worked three-carrier rooftop deal, the presence of those clauses would have cost roughly $200,000, dropping a $2,223,000 sale under $2 million [pSGbaA0AbTo @ 00:07:14]. Acquisition Insider confirms from the buy side that a right of first refusal drives value down even inside an 18x competitively shopped market [MtWLazzJb9Y @ 00:02:09]. The Allworth Financial caller was living it: their lease carried a first right of refusal, and the company that already owned the lease came in at roughly $140,000 against the $170,000 lump sum from an outside buyer [L5rvTFM0pVg @ 00:06:23]. In practice the incumbent's low number is the first number most landlords ever hear, and the clause is what keeps it from being tested.

The third failure mode is process, not price, and it bites before any multiple is discussed. The Airwave Advisors client who had held a communication site since 1995 found that what buyers promised at the start was not what it ended up being in the offer, after two prospects had pulled extensive financials and documents in a cumbersome back-and-forth [ZhCVLpLy4-o @ 00:00:00]. The Nexus Towers client, a third-generation real estate owner, kept killing calls two minutes in because a high-powered buyer seemed to want the most and give the least, and nobody explained what was being received upfront versus given up [vM7__l_6wgE @ 00:00:14]. Tower Genius warns of a sharper version: some self-described landlord consulting firms advertise lease-rate and buyout advice, then try to acquire the lease for themselves once the owner calls, wolves in sheep's clothing with a built-in conflict of interest [wpxdOZZukBY @ 00:15:00]. Steve also dismisses online buyout calculators: no slick spreadsheet substitutes for checking how the site fits a tower company's grid or carrier network, what the easement will say, and whether a 1031 exchange can be completed [xfquYQeRi_Q @ 00:00:33]. A clean number on a spreadsheet, or a friendly consultant, can still hide a bad easement and a buyer on the other side.
