# Chapter 06: The Billboard Moat: Regulation, Permits and Who Owns What

*The 1965 Highway Beautification Act, state DOT permitting (TxDOT, INDOT, Iowa DOT, Colorado), 660-ft controls and spacing rules, scenic-road bans, and the landowner, structure owner and advertiser split*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Operators, analysts and the state DOT staff who police the roadside agree: billboards are a permit business with a sign on top. The 1965 Highway Beautification Act made new construction nearly impossible within 660 feet of federal highways, grandfathered existing boards and forced governments to pay just compensation for lawful removals, while a 10% federal-funding penalty keeps states enforcing hard. Operators mostly lease the dirt at roughly $400 a month per site. One static board nets about $7,000 a year and trades at eight to ten times cash flow; digital screens average about $115,000 a year versus roughly $9,000 static. The split is over fragility: a storm can permanently kill a nonconforming permit, yet only about half of 1% of such structures get removed yearly, and Austin blocked Lamar's digital upgrade through 2023.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: The Permit Is the Asset, the Dirt Is Rented

Every operator-side voice lands on the same thesis: the steel is cheap and the permission is the asset. Acquisition Insider calls the 1965 Highway Beautification Act an accidental moat, because states must police outdoor advertising within 660 feet of federal highways or lose 10% of their highway money, so the boards already standing were grandfathered and every one became a little local monopoly [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:00:00]. Cashflow Anatomy pushes harder: you can price two steel poles and a sheet of vinyl from a catalog, but along a great many American roads no new permissions are being issued, and the ones granted decades ago cannot be reissued and in many cases cannot even be rebuilt if the structure falls over [GzFGSTXAVaI @ 00:00:19]. Old School Financials puts the original squeeze on the record: a 1966 nationwide count found 1.1 million roadside signs, about 840,000 of them illegal or nonconforming, yet a lawfully built sign could not be torn down for free because the government had to pay just compensation, so old permits became scarce, protected and very hard to replace [EFxC43w3Aa8 @ 00:00:55]. The Wealth Script reduces it to one line: a billboard is a permit business with a sign on top, and whoever holds the permit gets paid the most [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:06:23].

The second consensus point is the ownership stack. The Wealth Script splits every board among three parties, the landowner, the sign owner and the advertiser renting the face a month at a time, and pegs the dirt at about $400 a month while a digital face above it can bring in more than $100,000 a year [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:00:18]. Plain Sight pulls the same structure from Lamar's filing: roughly 71,500 leased active sites at about $348.1 million in annual lease expense, around $400 per site per month and about seventeen cents of every billboard revenue dollar in 2024, with leases running month-to-month to more than ten years and about 73% expiring or up for renewal within five years [Nuf8IbOuL70 @ 00:12:54]. What They Really Sell adds that Lamar owns only about 11,200 parcels beneath its displays, and that Clear Channel Outdoor reported about $548 million of billboard permits as intangible assets at the end of 2025, not the steel and not the screens [H0bRd-9Ez2c @ 00:03:40]. Tonnage frames the arrangement as monetizing vertical space while renting the horizontal space beneath it, noting Lamar describes its land leases as commonly running 5 to 20 years, a long-lived revenue asset without owning the parcel [Jr3ddPekZXw @ 00:03:21].

The regulators confirm the moat from the inside, and their motive is money. Iowa DOT polices signs along more than 9,000 miles of primary highway, requires off-premise signs to stand on private property zoned commercial or industrial and spaced a set distance apart, leaves on-premise signs alone, and warns that non-compliance risks losing 10% of federal funding, over $50 million a year, so a sign that breaks the rules is removed [8_iv9avZiAQ @ 00:01:23]. TxDOT puts that same 10% penalty at about $361 million for Texas, which is why it commissioned research into which state rule language has actually held up when states litigate enforcement [lQ0yYTXku1c @ 00:01:30]. INDOT permit specialist Kiara Cross says $100 million of Indiana federal dollars is on the line, and if the state permits a billboard in the wrong area, the state itself pays to get it down [YWN-XPL47M0 @ 00:03:29]. TxDOT right-of-way agent John Miner spells out what that means for operators: nearly every sign sits on private land, so the department uses statewide parcel data to confirm commercial or industrial land use and to verify that whoever signed the access paper actually owns the parcel, all inside a 60-day clock per new-sign inspection [d9Le5oqu4wc @ 00:00:58].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Acquisition Insider runs the math on one two-sided static board on a decent highway: each face rents for $300 to $800 a month, call it $600, which is about $14,000 a year at full occupancy, but 80% occupancy is normal, so you actually collect around $11,500 [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:01:29]. Costs eat roughly 40%: a ground lease to the landowner at 15% to 20% of revenue, electricity if the board is lit, insurance, vinyl printing every time the ad changes, and the state permit renewal. That leaves about $7,000 a year of true cash flow, assuming you sell the ads yourself, and single boards trade at eight to ten times cash flow, so a fair price runs $55,000 to $70,000 [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:02:07]. The Wealth Script writes the revenue formula on one line, faces times price per face times how much of the year they are rented, minus land rent, power and upkeep, and on Lamar's 2025 averages an ordinary static sign brings in about $9,000 a year [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:02:19]. Economics Decoded shows in a worked example why occupancy is the swing variable: a face earning $2,000 per sold month makes $24,000 over twelve months but only $18,000 over nine, the unsold time can never be resold, and a fixed land payment still comes due [UcnVlSgvKXE @ 00:06:16].

Digital conversion is where the money concentrates. Old School Financials prices a 14-by-48 static face at $2,000 a month, or $24,000 a year from one tenant, then converts it: the screen rotates advertisers every 6 to 8 seconds, eight businesses each buy a $1,000 slot, and the same pole under the same single permit throws off $96,000 a year, four times the rent [EFxC43w3Aa8 @ 00:02:51]. The Wealth Script pulls the fleet reality: about 5,500 Lamar screens brought in roughly $632 million, about $115,000 per screen per year against about $9,000 for a static sign, roughly 13 times as much, and a digital board is usually just an old permitted sign with a new face [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:02:39]. Acquisition Insider prices a conversion at about $200,000, passes along Lamar's rule that a static face should already earn $3,000 a month before it earns a screen, and notes Lamar's chief executive has walked investors through returns above 40%; a small operator who wins one digital approval on a busy corner has minted a private annuity [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:03:21]. Economics Decoded cites Outfront's reported average initial investment of about $260,000 per digital display, and warns that owning a printed board does not settle whether a digital replacement is allowed at that site [UcnVlSgvKXE @ 00:09:42].

Sourcing is a public-records game. Acquisition Insider says there is no MLS for billboards: Billboard Insider runs classifieds of signs and small portfolios, brokers like Sign Value move most marketed deals, and Star Capital lends on nothing but signs, yet the best deals come from each state DOT's published outdoor advertising permit rolls, which you pull and use to write mom-and-pop owners directly, the same way Lamar sources its own tuck-ins, including the $147 million purchase of family-owned Verde Outdoor [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:05:06]. Diligence covers lease term, permit transferability, advertiser contracts, twelve months of real occupancy, and the DOT's traffic count, never the seller's. Cashflow Anatomy sets the inspection order: the permit first, conforming or nonconforming, then your state's position on re-erection, then stand where the traffic stands and look at what is growing, being built or planned, and only last the steel, because it is the part money can fix [GzFGSTXAVaI @ 00:06:26]. The agencies show what they will check against you. TxDOT matches whoever signed the access paper against parcel ownership data so its inspectors are not trespassing [d9Le5oqu4wc @ 00:01:25], and INDOT's Kiara Cross pulls zoning meeting minutes to catch anyone who went to the zoning board specifically to get a billboard erected, which the department treats as against the law [YWN-XPL47M0 @ 00:11:00].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the target list from DOT permit rolls
   - Pull the state outdoor advertising permit roll and mail family and one-truck owners directly.
   - Every state DOT publishes its permit rolls name by name, and the long tail of family-owned boards is where a first-time buyer can actually get a deal; Billboard Insider classifieds, Sign Value and Star Capital fill in the marketed side [Acquisition Insider — 4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:05:06]
2. Classify the permit before anything else
   - Determine conforming or nonconforming status, transferability and the state's re-erection position.
   - A nonconforming board can be maintained but not rebuilt, which makes it a different asset at a different price, and the federal rebuild exception only operates where state law permits [Cashflow Anatomy — GzFGSTXAVaI @ 00:06:26]
3. Lock the land and verify who signs
   - Confirm ground lease term, renewals and that the signer actually owns the parcel.
   - Demand 10-plus years of term with renewals or you are buying a countdown, since the landowner holds the leverage at expiry [Acquisition Insider — 4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:03:55]; the DOT checks that whoever signed the access paper owns the land [Texas Geographic Information Office — d9Le5oqu4wc @ 00:01:25]
4. Underwrite at real occupancy and gate digital
   - Price at 80% occupancy and 40% costs, then convert only proven faces.
   - A conversion runs about $200,000 and only makes sense on a proven location, with Lamar's rule that a static face already earns $3,000 a month before it earns a screen [Acquisition Insider — 4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:03:02]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split is over what a digital conversion is actually worth. The Wealth Script runs Lamar's 2025 averages and lands on roughly $115,000 per screen per year against about $9,000 for a printed or painted sign, calling the screen about 13 times as productive from the same kind of spot [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:02:39]. Old School Financials builds a gentler hypothetical: a $2,000-a-month static face becomes eight loop slots at $1,000 each, or $96,000 a year, four times the rent on the same single permit [EFxC43w3Aa8 @ 00:03:11]. Acquisition Insider prices the conversion at about $200,000 and repeats Lamar's own filter that a static face should already earn $3,000 a month before it earns a screen, citing returns above 40% on qualifying conversions [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:03:02]. Economics Decoded is the brake pedal. It refuses to multiply the printed price by the number of advertisers, because each buyer now gets a slice of a rotating schedule rather than a continuous face, and it flags Outfront's reported average initial investment of approximately $260,000 per digital display [UcnVlSgvKXE @ 00:09:42]. Its verdict: where demand is thin, the screen is expensive capacity nobody buys. The operator playbook that falls out is consistent across all four: convert only proven, high-traffic faces, and underwrite the slot price, not the slot count.

The second fault line is how hard the moat really is. Acquisition Insider sells it as absolute: buy the lease term, buy the permit, verify the traffic, and let a 1965 law keep your competition illegal forever [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:06:14]. Cashflow Anatomy, which argues the same scarcity thesis and labels it inferred, puts the counter-evidence on the table itself: one advocacy group reports only about one half of 1% of nonconforming structures are removed each year, so the rule on paper is strict and the rule in practice is slow [GzFGSTXAVaI @ 00:05:02]. One Decision Ago explains why removal stalls: the act required just compensation for any billboard taken down, and that plain cost made large-scale removal financially unrealistic, with states prioritizing road and bridge budgets over enforcement [D_1Q_8uXe9s @ 00:16:21]. Phil Edwards goes further, quoting the view that the Highway Beautification Act, riddled with zoning room and grandfathering, promoted higher, bigger, more permanent-seeming boards while consolidating the industry into the largest companies [rNWgnEqvTkY @ 00:11:14]. The practical read: the moat blocks new entrants far better than it removes incumbents, which is exactly why grandfathered inventory is the thing worth buying, and why nobody should price it as if enforcement were about to vanish or about to tighten overnight.

Practitioners also split on who actually wins at a single site. The Wealth Script is blunt: on Lamar's averages, a landowner on a 20-year lease at about $4,900 a year collects roughly $98,000 for doing nothing but owning the ground, yet once the face goes digital that rent shrinks to about 4 cents of every dollar the sign earns, so the money flows to whoever controls the permit and the screen, not the dirt [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:05:24]. Acquisition Insider flips the risk framing: this is a media business wearing a real estate costume, the passive seat belongs to the landowner collecting ground rent, and the operator owns the sales grind; it budgets that ground lease at 15 to 20% of revenue [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:04:30]. Plain Sight reads Lamar's own filing at roughly $400 a month per leased site and about 17 cents of every 2024 billboard dollar going out the door as rent [Nuf8IbOuL70 @ 00:13:16]. What They Really Sell shows the ceiling of that range: Outfront's billboard property lease expense alone represented about 24% of total company revenue in 2025 [H0bRd-9Ez2c @ 00:05:46]. Same asset class, a ground-rent burden spanning 15 to 24 cents on the dollar, and the side that holds the permit decides who keeps the spread.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The fastest way to lose a billboard is to lose the permission under it. Cashflow Anatomy walks the trap line by line: a nonconforming sign survives only in substantially the same condition, and the federal exception for re-erection covers vandalism or other criminal or tortious acts, where state law permits and the sign is rebuilt in kind; weather is none of those, and a 2006 amendment to let storm-damaged nonconforming boards be rebuilt was stripped out of the final bill [GzFGSTXAVaI @ 00:03:18]. Acquisition Insider puts the same risk in deal terms: grandfathered permits can die if the board is rebuilt, moved, or storm-damaged past a threshold, and a nonconforming sign that blows down may legally never come back [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:04:12]. The ground lease is the second countdown. Boards get torn down because the farmer's kid wanted double rent, so anything short of 10-plus years of term with renewals means you are buying a countdown [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:03:55]. Plain Sight shows even the largest operator lives inside that clock, with about 73% of Lamar's leases expiring or subject to renewal within five years, and the company removing advertising faces at the onset of a storm so structures can better withstand high winds [Nuf8IbOuL70 @ 00:13:41].

The second family of losses has nothing to do with law. Cashflow Anatomy notes a billboard is worth what it is seen from: a tree grows, a warehouse goes up, the council reroutes traffic, and income falls with no change whatsoever to the thing you bought, because nobody sells you a line of sight [GzFGSTXAVaI @ 00:06:06]. Acquisition Insider adds the reroute trap: boards all over America stare at bypassed asphalt, sold to buyers who checked the revenue but never called the DOT about construction plans, and it warns that 80% occupancy is normal while owners claim more [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:04:12]. Economics Decoded quantifies vacancy with made-up teaching numbers: a face earning $2,000 per sold month brings $24,000 over twelve months but $18,000 over nine, and that expired exposure can never be sold to the customer who calls on Friday [UcnVlSgvKXE @ 00:06:16]. The Wealth Script lists the empty face as one of three ways people lose money, since a paper board with no advertiser earns nothing that month while the rent is still due [lMUn7vCR_iQ @ 00:05:06]. Stack those together and the seller's revenue figure is the least reliable number in the deal; occupancy and future traffic are where buyers actually get burned.

The third trap is the regulator changing the rules after you buy. Plain Sight details the Austin fight: in 2017 Lamar and Reagan National Advertising applied to digitize existing off-premises signs, the city denied them, and after a reversal at the Fifth Circuit the Supreme Court held the on-premises and off-premises distinction facially content neutral in April 2022, with the sign code surviving intermediate scrutiny in March 2023 [Nuf8IbOuL70 @ 00:10:59]. Plain Sight also flags amortization, where a government argues the owner was already compensated by continued operation, a theory upheld in some instances [Nuf8IbOuL70 @ 00:09:34]. Old School Financials frames the downside plainly: cities can tighten zoning, ban new digital boards, or dim the lights, a board that loses its permit loses everything, and the landlord who overpays for a permit or borrows too much to buy one still fails [EFxC43w3Aa8 @ 00:05:15]. Acquisition Insider's own ten-board route shows how thin leverage leaves you: roughly $650,000 all in, a quarter down, about $70,000 of cash flow, call it $55,000 to the loan, and about $15,000 kept the first year, which the channel itself calls honestly thin [4PyMov-VjoE @ 00:05:41]. One digital denial or one blown-down nonconforming face can erase that cushion before the loan amortizes.
