## Executive Summary

Opposition to hyperscale AI campuses is now organized and winning votes. Polls show 65% to 70% of residents against local sites. Palm Beach County killed Project Tango’s expansion 5-1, Tulsa and the Seminole Nation passed moratoria, and 40 of 41 Ohio county ballot initiatives ban sites above 25 MW. Organizers attack water use, 97 dB noise, 16-degree heat islands, doubled power bills, construction jobs that shrink from 1,500 to three permanent roles, and land deals locked behind NDAs. The decentralized camp answers with smaller footprints: Span’s 16-GPU node at a claimed $3M per MW in six months, 20 MW sites paired with district heating to jump power queues, and 10 kW air-cooled racks that fit brownfield halls. Speakers split on whether closed-loop cooling exists at all, and on whether distributed loads strengthen or strain distribution grids.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Wherever a hyperscale campus is proposed, opposition is now the default response, and the numbers match across countries. On PBS NewsHour, Brangham reported that seven in 10 people oppose a local data center. A Quinnipiac poll put opposition at 65%: 72% of respondents cited electricity, 64% water, and 41% noise [fkB8mt1DEVw @ 00:04:45]. CBC News found the same mood in Canada, where 68% of Canadians would oppose a large data center near their home. Olds, Alberta, a town of about 10,000, faces a proposed complex 10 times the size of Canada’s largest, and residents say they were never consulted [Rj_icscgo_Q @ 00:03:14]. That sentiment is turning into votes. In western Palm Beach County, Project Tango is a 600 MW, five-building campus next to Florida’s largest power plant. Its zoning committee recommended denial, and the County Commission then rejected the 1.5M sq ft expansion 5-1. A national protest day of roughly 100 events, nine of them in Florida, was already planned [iYCYPKhdqcY @ 00:05:09]. On Latitude Media, the panel noted that 40 of 41 Ohio county ballot initiatives ban data centers above 25 MW [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:50:40]. Every operator in this chapter treats organized resistance as a standard site condition. The one factor that reliably changes the response is the size of the footprint, which runs through every section that follows.

Speakers repeat the same specific grievances. Crystal Two Bulls of Honor the Earth and the No Data Center Coalition puts facility water use at 300,000 to 2.7 million gallons per year, “even 5 million.” Two Bulls says corporate closed-loop and water-positive claims have been “dispelled,” and puts noise at about 97 dB, compared with roughly 140 dB for an LRAD: “So, 97 to 140 is not a big jump” [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:08:43]. In a second cut of that testimony, Two Bulls adds heat islands of up to 16 degrees and Montana bills that have almost doubled. Two Bulls also describes how the jobs disappear: up to 1,500 construction jobs, mostly not local, over as long as two years, then as few as three full-time jobs, as in Rapid City, South Dakota [OeYd0jErrF4 @ 00:01:42]. Asianometry estimates that a 15 MW IT facility uses about 80 to 130 million gallons a year, “as much water as three hospitals, or two 18-hole golf courses” [tJYSzc7YkY0 @ 00:00:02]. Industrial hygienists Tammy Clark and Kristen Megan Kelly focus on noise measurement. Tonal and ELF noise has to be measured in dBC or dBZ, not dBA. Vendors quote “55 dB at the fence” against an 85 dBA occupational action level that assumes 8-hour exposure, yet these sites run 24/7 [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:11:30].

Speakers also agree on how deals get made: quietly, through intermediaries, on land chosen because local rules are weak. Two Bulls says Microsoft, Google, Apple, Meta and Amazon approach tribes through subsidiaries and native-owned energy companies. The pitch opens with solar, NDAs get signed, and only then does the conversation shift to a data center. The result is tribal leadership that answers to corporations rather than to its own people [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:03:27]. Clark and Kelly describe the same approach on farmland: NDAs and leases pitched as “temporary” and “probably never going to happen,” sites picked for weak zoning, $10M offers to farmers, and one family that turned down $25M [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:39:30]. On PBS NewsHour, Michael Webber links the secrecy to unincorporated areas where jurisdiction is unclear. Webber concedes that some of the stealth is about hiding from competitors, but says companies are “very late” to engage communities, repeating the mistake of the shale era [fkB8mt1DEVw @ 00:21:11]. The decentralized camp turns these complaints into a sales pitch. Mark Bjornsgaard says smaller sites surrounded by solar can run off-grid and put less stress on the grid, and Joshua Feinberg adds that they also draw less community pushback [W92xU-lo1Gc @ 00:01:55]. The pattern holds across sources: secrecy, scale and grid load drive the backlash.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The distributed model depends on a gap in cost and build time. On Latitude Media, Span’s Xfra is presented as a 16-GPU home node, claimed at $3M per MW and six months to build, compared with $15M per MW and three to five years for a 100 MW site [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:20:50]. In that discussion, “distributed” means tens of MW or less, mostly for inference. One working definition is distribution-connected sites of 25 MW or below: “So they’re not connected at transmission voltage. They’re being connected at on distribution” [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:27:04]. The unit economics come from the same panel. Offtakes of three to five years are easy at 5 MW and above. A residential install costs about $150K, and an estimated 10% of homeowners would buy in. Shah describes 5,000 data centers of 250 kW at Crown Castle towers selling merchant tokens, which is not a long-term fixed-price offtake. Span is also offering free power to new-construction cul-de-sacs [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:43:15]. Size labels are elastic. DCSMI’s Adam Gibson notes that edge means 5 to 10 MW to a hyperscaler but 10 racks in a regional location to others, and that a 50-rack site counts as very large edge in Alaska. Providers define edge to suit their own focus [tWgTVIzYje8 @ 00:00:15]. In practice, that elasticity gives a developer room to pitch the same capacity as a set of small, quiet distribution-level nodes.

The second lever is power position. Mark Bjornsgaard calls it the race to the electron. In the UK and Europe, a 50 MW district-heating project gets ahead of a 100 MW data center in the power queue, so Bjornsgaard’s firm builds 20 MW data centers paired with district heating to move up in allocation [W92xU-lo1Gc @ 00:02:43]. Bjornsgaard also cites 6.5 TWh of curtailed UK wind, about 750 MW wasted, as the kind of stranded power that smaller sites with on-site generation and storage can absorb. Michael Webber says on-site, behind-the-meter generation solves speed-to-power when utilities quote four to five years for a grid connection, and that it can avoid August-afternoon peaks. Diesel generators, however, are outlawed in California [fkB8mt1DEVw @ 00:08:04]. SambaNova’s Rodrigo Liang tackles the problem at the rack. State-of-the-art racks draw 100 to 150 kW and are heading to 500 kW with liquid cooling, while SambaNova racks run at 10 kW, air-cooled, and fit into existing brownfield data centers [JvqqjgCu9mg @ 00:02:22]. Liang claims 10x throughput on the workload that covers 80% of inference and payback in six months, compared with 18 to 24 months. Running prefill on Nvidia and decode on SambaNova, Liang says, yields 3 to 4x throughput for 10% more power [JvqqjgCu9mg @ 00:19:17]. Taken together, low-power racks and queue-friendly pairings let compute get built without the gigawatt footprint that triggers hearings.

Opponents use their own repeatable methods, and site teams that ignore them lose. Two Bulls lists the field kit: town halls, tribal council meetings, door knocking, petitions, and coalitions with agriculture, ranchers and landowners. The results so far include Tulsa’s nine-month moratorium, a unanimous Seminole Nation moratorium, and a Muscogee resolution that was blocked [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:04:55]. Clark and Kelly go further. They advise withholding tax subsidies, noting that a Google representative testified “we’ll go somewhere else.” They also call for environmental impact studies, which they say stopped a Michigan battery plant, and for recalling officials who approve deals. They claim 50% of data center construction slated for 2026 has been stopped [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:23:26]. Their monitoring routine is simple: check diesel-generator air-permit filings every month. “So keep once a month, go on there and look” [S_6FoEr75bs @ 01:16:47]. Jeff Pierson offers counties a way out of the binary trap of general zoning or a total ban: a six-factor County Siting Posture Metric. Scores from 0 to 44 point to a conditional use permit, 45 to 79 to overlay districts, and 80 to 100 to a ban. Pierson stresses that documentation, not the score itself, is the legal shield [CmmE71lfGdw @ 00:05:01]. Developers who understand these tools can predict which posture a county will take before filing.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over footprint. Mark Bjornsgaard argues that a $1T ASI cluster would need 100 GW, about 20% of US electricity, and that the way out is decentralization: smaller sites wrapped in on-site solar and storage that can run off-grid and absorb waste power such as the UK's 6.5 TWh of curtailed wind [W92xU-lo1Gc @ 00:00:32]. Bjornsgaard's firm builds 20 MW data centers paired with district heating because, in the UK and Europe, a 50 MW district-heating project gets ahead of a 100 MW data center in the power queue, and Joshua Feinberg adds that smaller sites draw less community pushback [W92xU-lo1Gc @ 00:02:43]. Adam Gibson plays the opposite hand: tier-1 greenfield is nearly impossible to get, so capital rotates into tier-2 and tier-3 markets that still allow 100 MW greenfield builds or easy brownfield retrofits, and with demand high and capacity scarce, Gibson would put a few billion straight into new facilities [tWgTVIzYje8 @ 00:02:57]. The Latitude Media panel prices the gap: Span's 16-GPU Xfra node is pitched at $3M/MW and six months to build versus $15M/MW and three to five years for a 100 MW site, yet hyperscalers still prefer 50 MW behind 1-2 GW plants because it means one contract and protects the brand [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:20:50].

On the regulatory weapon, organizers and planners pull in different directions. Crystal Two Bulls counts moratoria as wins: Tulsa passed a nine-month moratorium, the Seminole Nation passed one unanimously, and the Muscogee blocked a resolution, all driven by town halls, tribal council meetings, door knocking, petitions, and coalitions with agriculture, ranchers and landowners [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:04:14]. Jeff Pierson calls the reflex a binary trap: counties pick between general zoning and a total ban, and the ban invites state preemption fights, takings claims and equal-protection challenges, while potentially outlawing a future project built by local farmers [CmmE71lfGdw @ 00:00:52]. Pierson's alternative is a six-factor County Siting Posture Metric scoring infrastructure capacity, agriculture, regulatory capacity, economic conditions, community alignment and cumulative regional pressure; a score of 0-44 routes to a conditional use permit, 45-79 to overlay districts, and only 80-100 to a ban, which Pierson insists must be a last resort and never the first reaction [CmmE71lfGdw @ 00:06:23]. Tammy Clark and Kristen Meghan run harder plays instead: withhold tax subsidies even when a Google representative testifies 'we'll go somewhere else,' demand environmental impact studies, and recall the officials who approve the deals [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:25:34].

Cooling claims are the third fault line. Project Tango's developers answered water, heat and noise objections with closed-loop cooling and offsite water disposal, concrete buildings, a berm and trees, on a parcel bordering the Arden community, an elementary school and sensitive equestrian horses [iYCYPKhdqcY @ 00:02:48]. Kristen Meghan rejects the label outright: 'There is no such thing as a closed loop system when it comes to water use.' Glycol mixes are 50% water, the real alternatives, dielectric fluid or mineral oil, cost more, and air cooling fails once ambient air hits 100 degrees Fahrenheit [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:43:31]. Michael Webber applies a narrower test: the question is whether a closed loop is filled once or draws water every day, and in Texas water is a more sensitive issue than electricity; Webber still holds up Meta's water-positive site near El Paso and Microsoft's air cooling near San Antonio as working models [fkB8mt1DEVw @ 00:10:45]. Rodrigo Liang sidesteps the argument in hardware: state-of-the-art racks draw 100-150 kW on the way to 500 kW and need liquid cooling, while SambaNova racks run at 10 kW, air-cooled, and drop into brownfield data centers that already exist instead of new campuses [JvqqjgCu9mg @ 00:02:22].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap fires at the kitchen table. Crystal Two Bulls reports that Microsoft, Google, Apple, Meta and Amazon approach through subsidiaries and native-owned energy companies with NDAs in hand, open with a solar pitch, then pivot to a data center once the NDA is signed, leaving tribal leadership accountable to the corporations instead of their own people [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:03:27]. Tammy Clark and Kristen Meghan describe the same script on farmland: leases pitched as 'temporary' and 'probably never going to happen,' $10M offers to farmers, one family that refused $25M, and sites picked specifically for weak zoning [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:39:30]. The transmission side is blunter. In Georgia, planned new lines jumped from 24 miles in 2019 to 1,065 miles, roughly 80% of the added 10 GW is for data centers, and Georgia Power offered Rachael Maszk $57,000 for half a backyard while representatives raised eminent domain mid-negotiation; the company's line is 'we use it less than 1 percent of the time' [e8QyJw6I-Bc @ 00:03:50]. Doug Fergus lost a quarter of a backyard and roughly 400 trees to a power line, held out for more than a year, then settled and paid down half the mortgage with the proceeds [e8QyJw6I-Bc @ 00:04:16].

The second trap is believing the jobs-and-revenue story. Two Bulls puts numbers on it: up to 1,500 construction jobs, mostly not local, over a build phase of up to two years, then as few as three full-time jobs, as in Rapid City, South Dakota, while Montana bills almost doubled and one person living alone in a trailer received a $900 bill [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:11:53]. In Alberta, residents of Olds, a town of about 10,000 facing a proposed complex ten times Canada's largest, report no consultation at all, 68% of Canadians would oppose a large data center near home, and no published papers yet prove the economic benefits [Rj_icscgo_Q @ 00:03:14]. Michael Webber names the timing problem directly: 'we feel the impacts today, the benefits come later,' and companies are very late to engage communities, repeating the shale era [fkB8mt1DEVw @ 00:17:16]. Project Tango shows what that costs a developer: the zoning committee recommended denial, the light-industrial argument failed, and the Palm Beach County Commission killed the 1.5M sq ft expansion 5-1 even after FPL said data center companies fund 100% of new generation, with a national protest day of roughly 100 events already planned [iYCYPKhdqcY @ 00:05:09].

The third trap is physical and hits operators as hard as neighbors. The Latitude Media panel warns that behind-the-meter builds can be invisible to utilities, much like cannabis grow houses; an overload trips the building rather than the feeder, and spiky inference loads create power-quality, voltage and harmonics risk that the panel calls solvable, with storage as the fix [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:24:22]. Utilization is the hidden economics check: ProLogis customers run at 25% of their 5, 10 or 20 MW interconnections, L3 chargers sit at roughly 15-30%, and EV tariffs block added load unless the site opts into a C&I tariff, while office retrofits stall on fire code, server weight, cooling and fiber [D7D1CPOoub4 @ 00:39:37]. Noise is where vendor specs break. Kristen Meghan says tonal and low-frequency noise has to be measured in dBC or dBZ, not dBA; vendors quote '55 dB at the fence' against an 85 dBA action level built on eight-hour occupational exposure, while these sites run 24/7 [S_6FoEr75bs @ 00:11:30]. Two Bulls cites roughly 97 dB at sites and compares it with an LRAD at about 140 dB: 'So, 97 to 140 is not a big jump' [Ac0-fBK0LVQ @ 00:09:45].
