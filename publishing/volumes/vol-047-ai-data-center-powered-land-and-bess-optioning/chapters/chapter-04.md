## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the interconnection queue is a request process, not a construction schedule: Stats in Motion counts 14 GW withdrawn per 1 GW connected in 2025, and Andy Knows It puts historical withdrawal at 78%. FERC Order 2023 swapped serial first-come queuing for first-ready-first-served clusters, firm deadlines, site control and impact-based cost allocation, which Keentel Engineering reduces to 'cost follows impact, not MW.' Street mechanics are cash-heavy: TVA deposits scale to $300K plus readiness money, withdrawal penalties climb to 20% after the LGIA, and ISO-NE allocations ran from $0 to $2,700/kW. Dirt to Data and GridState warn that AI campuses now face generator-style studies on the load side. Disagreement centers on automation: SPP and MISO tout 50% time savings and under-a-day costing, while PJM calls AI roughly '80% right' and not ready for prime time.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus — Readiness, Impact and the Queue as an Asset

Every practitioner who touches the numbers lands in the same place: a megawatt in the queue is a proposal, not a plant. Blueprint States frames the queue as a request process that starts studies and proves nothing about financing, construction, or operation, and warns that even a signed agreement does not mean commercial operation [v2cs7ZXTQH0 @ 00:01:04]. Andy Knows It puts the attrition at 78% of capacity that entered from 2000 to 2018 eventually withdrawing, and calls the 2,000-plus gigawatt line 'part pipeline and part graveyard' [QxDqhnba2Jg @ 00:04:12]. Stats in Motion reads the latest shrinkage the same way: in 2025, 14 GW withdrew for every 1 GW that connected, and of projects entering from 2000 to 2020 only 13% were built while 75% withdrew, which the narrator calls the first report card on Order 2023 [Z2FdaH10ZCY @ 00:00:48]. Sarah Johnson's PJM work for Environmental Defense Fund adds the split by fuel: about 85% of renewables never operate versus 68% of fossil, and a survey of roughly 40 developers ranked interconnection as the number one barrier [M62wgLYBKBw @ 00:16:15]. Blueprint States closes the loop plainly: 'a megawatt in the queue is not a megawatt on the grid.'

On the reform itself, the FERC side and the engineering side describe the same machine. Karen Herzfeld of Chairman Phillips' office laid out three buckets — first-ready-first-served clusters, speed, and technology — with costs allocated by proportional impact, site control at 90% on entry and 100% by facility study, and the old reasonable-efforts standard replaced by firm deadlines with appealable penalties [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:06:32]. Keentel Engineering compresses that into one rule: cost follows impact, not megawatts, and the real financial risk 'may be many electrical miles away from the point of interconnection' [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:03:31]. Their TVA breakdown shows the competitive dynamic has 'shifted from speed to readiness,' with 100% site control required, substation upgrades split per capita and system upgrades by proportional impact, and the blunt warning that 'your costs are coupled to your neighbors' [gGBYCEdn18o @ 00:04:44]. Andy Knows It explains why the serial model broke: the generator that triggers an upgrade pays for it, restudy dependencies made first-come-first-served 'completely unworkable,' and near-free entry let developers file on land they didn't control just to hold position [QxDqhnba2Jg @ 00:03:24]. Consensus therefore sits on readiness, controlled land, and impact-based allocation as the baseline price of admission, with deadlines that no longer bend.

For data-center land, Dirt to Data draws the line every investor needs: seeing the grid is not the same as having the right to use it, and AI campuses requesting hundreds of megawatts now raise generator-like questions on the load side [264d0wu5W4k @ 00:02:34]. Their example is 300 acres sitting near a line that can still be blocked by queued generators and other large loads, which is why queue position itself becomes the asset — 'your land hasn't changed' — and why power in 2033 against a 2028 need simply means no project [264d0wu5W4k @ 00:04:48]. GridState lands in the same spot, noting that AI data centers approaching gigawatt scale are 'the equivalent of adding an entirely new city to the grid' and face the same capacity, cost, and timing questions as generators, while interdependent projects mean 'a single project withdrawal can force studies to be rerun' [ObxvY1JRruo @ 00:05:23]. Keentel's CAISO IPE 5.0 briefing confirms the door is opening: the non-LSE affidavit no longer needs to cite sustainability, only the project's value to the organization, which opens the path for data centers and industrial operators while keeping the 25-point award and one-project-per-cluster limit [VdwOf6TKl6U @ 00:01:01]. Dirt to Data's investor checklist makes the final point: 'notice that acreage isn't on that list.'

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The cash ladder is where Order 2023 bites. Keentel's TVA walkthrough lists a $25K nonrefundable fee, study deposits of $150K under 75 MW, $200K for 75 to 199 MW and $300K at 200 MW and up, a readiness deposit at 2x the study deposit with $925K stated for the 200 MW-plus tier, and a schedule running from a January 15 window through a 60-day engagement, 150-day study, 150-day restudy and 180-day facilities study [gGBYCEdn18o @ 00:01:09]. Withdrawal penalties climb from 2x deposit to the greater of 2x or 5%, then 2x or 10%, and 20% after the LGIA, with off-ramps for no material effect, a restudy cost jump above 25%, or a facilities-study jump above 100% [gGBYCEdn18o @ 00:03:16]. Keentel adds that queue position is transferable and 'any POI change is always a material modification.' On ISO-NE's transitional cluster, the same engineers priced exposure for a 200 MW project carrying $178M of allocation at $8.9M at 5%, $17.88M at 10% and $35.6M at 20% [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:09:17]. Matthew Crosby of Cypress Creek told the WIRES panel milestone payments step to 5% then 10%, while MISO uses 10% then 20%, and pushed site control that covers the gen-tie all the way to the POI [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:44:50].

Keentel's DFAX worked example is the core screening math every POI shortlist should run. A 240 MVA line carrying 200 MVA of base flow takes impacts of 50, 30 and 5 MVA from projects A, B and C, pushing it to 285 MVA or 118%; a $40M upgrade split by megawatts lands at $17.8M, $13.3M and $8.9M, but split by impact it becomes $23.5M, $14.1M and $2.4M [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:15:28]. In the ISO-NE cluster of 23 projects and 4,826.5 MW, total cost hit $3.21B with system upgrades at $2.54B or 79%, substation $581M or 18%, and interconnection facilities just $75.5M, while 115 kV averaged about $831/kW against roughly $453/kW at 345 kV [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:07:36]. Sarah Johnson's PJM data puts the median cost near $50/kW and the 90th percentile near $400/kW against roughly $1,500/kW to install, with 13% of estimates shifting more than $100/kW between studies [M62wgLYBKBw @ 00:32:27]. Casey Cathey frames the same allocation as a highway where the last car pays for the new lane, with typical surviving costs around $7M versus the $200 to $400M figures [6zj9DYoE8-U @ 00:09:59].

Speed now comes from software and staffing. At the ESIG workshop, SPP's Swerzik credited the GridUnity-built SIMS with about 50% time savings and SUGAR 2 with automating phase 1 at roughly 8x speed, while MISO's Westfall reported Pearl Street 'has no AI,' matched 99% of constraints in benchmarking, and produces upgrades plus costs in under a day before transmission owners adjust about 20% [nFQOwcyPbbE @ 00:29:50]. PJM's Velock, by contrast, doubled staff and issued about 200 agreements in one day. Casey Cathey told Nebraska Wind and Solar that Hitachi/Nvidia GPUs run 80% faster and cut model solves from about a year to two months, with the SPP queue dashboard updated twice weekly [6zj9DYoE8-U @ 00:27:38]. In ERCOT, Keentel warns a signed SGIA is a 'false summit': the QSA cycle runs quarterly, so a missed prerequisite or failed model quality test slips synchronization a full quarter and costs millions in revenue; PSCAD EMT is mandatory for all IBRs, as-built models must match exact inverter firmware and PPC settings, and vendor data lockdown is the 'single biggest schedule risk' [YabOCr0t9pI @ 00:00:58]. Their fix is a single unified study package that satisfies both ERCOT and the TSP [YabOCr0t9pI @ 00:03:00].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Understand the node
   - Run DFAX screening on every candidate POI and test multiple sizes before committing a deposit.
   - Keentel's first rule is 'First, understand the node,' because the cluster neighbors and electrical topology set your allocated bill, not your megawatts [Keentel Engineering — TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:19:29]
2. Lock site control and the cash ladder
   - Secure 100% site control and fund study plus readiness deposits before the window opens.
   - TVA requires 100% site control with a $25K fee and study deposits up to $300K; the window opens January 15 and missing it means waiting a year [Keentel Engineering — gGBYCEdn18o @ 00:01:29]
3. Freeze vendor data and build as-built models
   - Lock inverter firmware and PPC settings, then submit PSSE and PSCAD EMT models whose MQT curves overlay.
   - ERCOT enforces strict as-built lockdowns, PSCAD EMT is mandatory for IBRs, and vendor data lockdown is the single biggest schedule risk [Keentel Engineering — YabOCr0t9pI @ 00:05:06]
4. Shadow the study and watch the off-ramps
   - Track restudy and facilities-study cost moves against the withdrawal thresholds at each phase.
   - TVA off-ramps trigger on a restudy cost increase over 25% or a facilities-study increase over 100%, 'your protection against economics the studies quietly destroy' [Keentel Engineering — gGBYCEdn18o @ 00:03:52]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Automation splits the grid operators into two camps. MISO's Westfall runs Pearl Street, a tool that “has no AI,” and reports benchmarking with 99% of constraints matching, plus upgrades and costs produced in under a day [nFQOwcyPbbE @ 00:25:02]. The catch is that transmission owners still adjust roughly 20% of those outputs, summed up in the line “Well, that ain't going to work.” PJM's Velock pushes back harder, framing AI as possibly “80% right” and “not ready for prime time yet” [nFQOwcyPbbE @ 00:46:21]. PJM's alternative playbook was brute force: staff doubled, about 200 agreements went out in a single day, the backlog was cut 63%, and the backlog clears by April. SPP sits between the two camps. It pairs GridUnity's SIMS, which delivered about 50% time savings, with SUGAR 2 automating phase 1 at roughly 8x speed, and Casey Cathey adds that Hitachi and Nvidia GPUs run 80% faster, dropping model solves from about a year to two months [6zj9DYoE8-U @ 00:27:38]. Cypress Creek's Matthew Crosby argues the unlock sits upstream: a technical conference on study criteria so automation has consistent rules to run against [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:26:19]. Automated screens move fast, but transmission owners still rewrite roughly one in five results.

The second fault line is who gets into the queue and who pays once inside. Cypress Creek's Matthew Crosby opposed any term-sheet or IRP precondition for entering the queue while backing DFAX impact-based cost allocation [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:45:40]. Keentel Engineering shows why the method moves real money: on a $40M upgrade, splitting by MW charges three projects $17.8M, $13.3M and $8.9M, while splitting by impact charges $23.5M, $14.1M and $2.4M [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:15:28]. As Keentel puts it, cost follows impact, not MW. Andy Knows It argues reform left the core economics intact, because Order 2023 “didn't change who pays,” and RMI found PJM's first reformed cluster of 2018-2020 applicants still carried long timelines and high costs [QxDqhnba2Jg @ 00:08:43]. On gating, Sarah Johnson of UW Madison, presenting for the Environmental Defense Fund, ran counterfactuals: a $900k per-generator entry fee adds about 7 GW, mostly non-renewable, while per-MW fees and later-study fees are weaker [M62wgLYBKBw @ 00:48:51]. Reprioritization adds about 8 GW total and 1.5 GW of renewables, mostly by removing small projects, and weighting renewables 3x yields 4.1 GW, of which 2.7 GW is renewable. Johnson also found raising fees still left many entrants, because wait times are so long there is a lot of true uncertainty.

Service type and shortcut strategies draw the sharpest contrasts. Karen Herzfeld, from FERC Chairman Phillips' office, called connect-and-manage worth exploring while conceding it is unclear how it works with capacity markets [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:38:00]. SPP's Casey Cathey countered on the same WIRES panel that connect-and-manage ignores stability, subsynchronous and EMT limits [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:51:06]. Herzfeld noted alternative technologies must be evaluated but need not be deployed, and Cathey called the alternative-technology cost estimates burdensome. Cathey also warned that ERIS-based affected-system studies reduce interregional transmission, while Keentel Engineering pitches the ERIS downgrade as a deliberate option for arbitrage-focused BESS [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:13:03]. LBNL's Gorman adds that ERIS and NRIS timelines diverged around 2018-2019 and the low-cost ERIS share has fallen since about 2014, while Southern Company sees little meaning in the distinction. The most aggressive playbook comes from Stats in Motion: buy old solar farms for their grid position and add batteries to skip waits of more than five years [Z2FdaH10ZCY @ 00:01:07]. The same channel predicts PJM, as FERC ordered, will require full studies for retrofits. Before reform, Andy Knows It reports, entry was nearly free and developers filed on land they didn't control just to hold queue position; Order 2023 answered with site control, which Herzfeld put at 90% at entry and 100% by facility study.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is treating a signed agreement as the finish line. Keentel Engineering calls the ERCOT SGIA a “false summit” and warns “The reality is far harsher.” The QSA runs quarterly, so a missed prerequisite or failed model quality test slips synchronization by a full quarter, costing “millions of dollars” in revenue [YabOCr0t9pI @ 00:00:58]. The engineering gauntlet answers to two masters: ERCOT checks macro stability in PSSE v36, TSAT and PSCAD, while TSPs such as Oncor, CenterPoint, ETT, LCRA, CPS and Austin Energy each apply their own relay, harmonic, inrush and RTU/ICCP rules. PSCAD EMT is mandatory for all IBRs, and if MQT curves fail to overlay across all models, the submittal is rejected [YabOCr0t9pI @ 00:05:25]. Keentel flags vendor data lockdown as the “single biggest schedule risk,” and as-built models must reflect exact inverter firmware and PPC settings. On the cluster side, the same firm warns of a “hardware penalty” from generic models, because “These upgrades can be extremely expensive” [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:17:28]. Blueprint States lays out the full path, from request to cluster studies to allocated upgrades to an agreement, and stresses that an agreement still does not mean commercial operation [v2cs7ZXTQH0 @ 00:01:18].

Study results can wreck a pro forma overnight. In the ISO-NE transitional cluster, 23 projects totaling 4,826.5 MW drew $3.21B in upgrades, with allocations ranging from $0 to $541M, or $0 to $2,700/kW [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:05:05]. Upgrades of up to 93 months meant more than seven years of delay. Keentel prices the exit on a 200 MW project with $178M allocated at $8.9M at 5%, $17.88M at 10% and $35.6M at 20% [TM7qt8CCWxk @ 00:09:17]. Sarah Johnson's PJM data shows the volatility underneath: a median cost of about $50/kW, a 90th percentile of about $400/kW, 13% of estimates shifting by more than $100/kW between studies, and costs above $100/kW making withdrawal 22 percentage points more likely [M62wgLYBKBw @ 00:33:36]. Andy Knows It cites Berkeley and RMI work showing developers walk away once interconnection exceeds 10% of capex. GridState describes the cascade: “A single project withdrawal can force studies to be rerun,” and “Suddenly, a project that looked profitable no longer works financially” [ObxvY1JRruo @ 00:04:22]. TVA's counter is a set of off-ramps for no material effect, a more than 25% cost increase in restudy, or a more than 100% increase in the facilities study, which Keentel calls “your protection against economics the studies quietly destroy.”

The remaining traps are physical and calendar-driven. Stats in Motion warns that the grid-position retrofit play carries hidden engineering: batteries are bidirectional, change fault-current behavior so relays may be miscalibrated, and push transformers sized for solar into thermal stress, so a 40 MW site could be curtailed to 20 MW [Z2FdaH10ZCY @ 00:02:49]. Its fix is to model the voltage profile and protection settings before buying. Dirt to Data frames the calendar trap bluntly: power in 2033 against a 2028 need means no project, because “Queue and timing are inseparable” [264d0wu5W4k @ 00:04:48]. The same channel stresses that 300 acres near a line can still be blocked by queued generators and other large loads, and that acreage does not appear on the investor question list. Study throughput compounds the delay. Crosby reported only about 1% of PJM facility studies on time and a simple line tap that took four to five years, while Cathey said consultants are turning away work [-BTpIv2G5-0 @ 00:59:40]. Johnson's PJM data puts facility studies at a 16-month median against a 6-month official timeline, with a 90th percentile of 35 months, and documents the downstream damage: land options expire and PPAs slip [M62wgLYBKBw @ 00:26:14]. Johnson also puts renewables that never operate at about 85%, versus 68% of fossil.
