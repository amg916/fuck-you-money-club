## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one thesis: power, not chips or buildings, is the scarce input. AI racks have jumped from about 10 kW to 120-140 kW, single campuses draw 1-2 GW, and buildings that go up in 18 months to 3 years wait 4-5 years for grid connection while large transformers sit at 3-4 year lead times. The street mechanics follow: buy grid-connected or behind-the-meter sites, order long-lead electrical gear immediately, price capacity per megawatt at $100-150/kW/month, budget $7-12M/MW for the build before roughly $25M/MW of AI fit-out, and lock take-or-pay leases of 10-15 years. Disagreements are sharp: queue sizes in Texas range from 232 GW to over 400 GW, turbine waits from 2-3 years to more than 5, and Economy Media reports over half of projects canceled or delayed while Hyper sees years, if not decades, of pent-up demand.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: Power Is the Product

Every practitioner who touched the load question lands in the same place: the bottleneck is power delivered to the right parcel on time, not chips and not concrete. The Power Engineer frames the scale bluntly: one frontier AI campus draws 1-2 GW, the output of one or two large power stations going to a single customer at a single address, and an AI rack now pulls 120-140 kW against about 10 kW a decade ago [BWtk0CSPH6g @ 00:00:00]. BUILT BENEATH puts a name on it, citing Meta's roughly 1 GW Lebanon, Indiana site at about 800,000 homes, running 24/7 rather than peaking like residential load [7L7MfK9coTY @ 00:01:13]. Break Even reaches the same conclusion from the money side, opening with the line that the most valuable thing inside a data center isn't the servers and closing with 'own the power and you own everything that runs on it' [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:14:45]. Build Core's Josh states the rural siting driver in one sentence: power availability is now more important than location [tNNIXQhntOk @ 00:03:35]. The operator read is simple. A hyperscale site is a power-delivery asset with a building wrapped around it, and every downstream decision on land, gear, tenants and financing hangs off how many megawatts can actually be energized and when.

The second point of agreement is the timing mismatch between the box and the wire. The Power Engineer puts a data center at 1-3 years against 5-15 years for a transmission line, notes that every link from plant to rack was sized for loads that arrived slowly while an AI campus needs everything on day one, and flags Texas sitting on more than 400 GW of large-load requests, over 5x its record peak, most of which will never be built but all of which must be studied [BWtk0CSPH6g @ 00:01:12]. BUILT BENEATH stacks the numbers: IEA puts the build at 2-3 years, JLL puts the grid connection wait in the largest markets at over 4 years, and DOE puts grid gear at 2+ years and large transformers at 3-4 years, summed up as 'you can't download another transformer' [7L7MfK9coTY @ 00:06:39]. Your Finance Uncle reports Northern Virginia power waits pushing projects back 3-5 years and generator lead times past 2 years [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:02:20]. The baseline prerequisite follows directly: land and capital alone do not make a project. Mark Caskey of Steel Nation sees developers with money in hand who still lack a location, a client, natural gas and generator sets [ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:00:19].

Practitioners also agree on what counts as hyperscale and what has to sit on site before a megawatt earns revenue. Eaton sizes a typical data center from about 1 MW, against 3-5 kW for a home, hosted customers at 5-10 kW, and hyperscale sites at 50 to over 100 MW, with redundant utility feeds, generators, UPS, BESS, cooling and network paths built in and most hyperscale sites running Tier IV at 99.995%, under 30 minutes of downtime a year [II6xdRkgivE @ 00:03:45]. datacenterHawk narrows the buyer pool to 5-20 cloud and SaaS/social companies operating as an oligopoly, contrasts a bank's roughly 1 MW need with a cloud provider's roughly 36 MW, and relays QTS's Tag Greason on what those buyers want: scale, speed and cost, in leases of 10-75 MW [SVLPjGiZivc @ 00:15:31]. Build Core's Josh lists the physical stack a developer has to deliver on a single campus: shell, GPU data halls, transformers, switchgear and batteries, chillers, towers and liquid cooling, diesel or gas backup, and the substation [tNNIXQhntOk @ 00:01:36]. The shared baseline: a hyperscale tenant buys energized, redundant, cooled capacity tens of megawatts at a time, and the electrical yard is the backbone of the product rather than an accessory to it.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Build cost is now quoted per megawatt, and practitioners give hard ranges for every layer of the stack. Your Finance Uncle prices construction at $7-12M per MW, so a 10 MW site runs over $100M and a campus over 100 MW runs over $1B, then layers engineering fees of 15-20% (about $30M on a $200M build) and a 15-25% contingency on top [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:03:08]. Break Even cites JLL's shell-and-core benchmark of $11.3M per MW, putting 50 MW past half a billion dollars, adds roughly $25M per MW for AI fit-out, and pegs a 1 GW AI facility at $45-55B with Stargate projected at 1.2 GW across more than 4M sq ft [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:01:10]. Within the build budget, electrical eats 40-45% and cooling 15-25%, and AI racks at 100+ kW versus 5-10 kW traditionally force liquid cooling [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:07:58]. Economy Media frames component cost differently: electrical components run under 10% of build cost, yet nothing runs without them, transformer prices have doubled in just 4 years, and sourcing is concentrated in China, South Korea and Mexico [e0Dlr07jfjA @ 00:07:34]. The operator takeaway: the electrical line items, not the shell, decide whether the capital stack and the schedule both hold.

Revenue mechanics run on the same per-megawatt unit. Break Even quotes lease pricing at $100-150 per kW per month in primary US markets and points to take-or-pay structures where tenants 'pay for what they reserved, whether they use it or not,' citing Applied Digital's May 2026 deal: 300 MW, a 15-year term, $7.5B base revenue and up to $18.2B with options [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:06:05]. Your Finance Uncle breaks the revenue stack into space and power fees, cross-connects, metered power markup, remote hands and managed services, with ancillary fees adding 10-15%, and runs the math: at about $150/kW/month plus 15% ancillary, break-even sits at 60-65% utilization [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:09:33]. The pricing rule that follows cuts against instinct: when occupancy sags, hold price and lengthen terms rather than cut the rate, because cutting price is often the worst move a struggling operator can make [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:08:20]. On the opex side, the BESS Engineer shows two sites with the same 100 MW of IT load drawing 150 MW and 120 MW; that 30 MW gap at $0.10/kWh is about $26M a year, and under a 150 MW cap, PUE 1.2 supports 125 MW of IT, 25% more, provided cooling holds at peak [MnGAkKrZF_I @ 00:01:01].

The speed-to-power playbook practitioners teach runs in three distinct moves. First, buy power that already exists: BUILT BENEATH reports developers targeting old steel mills and auto plants for their existing power hookups, because the grid connection wait in the largest markets runs over 4 years [7L7MfK9coTY @ 00:03:02]. Second, go behind the grid: Mark Caskey's Steel Nation Microgrids, launched in November 2024, has six behind-the-grid builds of 200-850 MW, mostly near Columbus, Ohio, where permits clear in about 90 days versus '90 months' in Pennsylvania, and redundant 28-40 inch gas lines put Ohio and Utah projects at power in 14-15 months [ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:16:36]. Caskey sizes 10-15 turbines for 200-300 MW, notes recip engines top out around 5-6 MW, and says buying or leasing naked turbines cuts 6-8 months off turbine waits of 2-3 years, running gas as primary supply and BESS as backup [ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:25:42]. Third, compress the build once power is secured: Hyper's Matt Caldwell names power and land as the true bottlenecks, then builds private manufacturing capacity so clients never compete for slots on shared lines, arguing repeatable factory QA beats one-off field commissioning and pulls time to tenant revenue forward [oQVb9TRp6Og @ 00:01:25].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Every practitioner agrees the grid queue is the choke point, but the numbers they quote for it do not line up, and that gap is itself the signal. The Power Engineer puts Texas at more than 400 GW of large-load requests, over five times the state's record peak, and says most will never be built but all must still be studied [BWtk0CSPH6g @ 00:01:12]. BUILT BENEATH reads the February 2026 ERCOT figure as 232,000 MW of proposed large loads, 72% of it data centers, more than double the roughly 91 GW record, with ERCOT answering through grouped studies and audits [7L7MfK9coTY @ 00:05:28]. Travis Wofford of Baker Botts explains why the totals swell: developers file five to ten duplicate applications, so the queue overstates real demand, and Wofford recalls expecting 80% of it would never be constructed [cvNO9pZAhM0 @ 00:44:06]. Timelines split the same way. The Power Engineer quotes one to three years to build a data center against five to fifteen for a transmission line, BUILT BENEATH cites two to three years for the building, and Break Even compresses the contrast into one line: building the box takes 18 months, getting the electricity can take five years [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:05:29]. Operators who underwrite on the headline queue number are reading inflated paper.

The second split is how to get electrons at all. Mark Caskey of Steel Nation runs a gas-first model: behind-the-grid builds of 200 to 850 MW, gas as primary supply, BESS as backup, diesel frowned upon, and solar dismissed as 'for show' at about 5 to 6% of supply [ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:31:23]. Caskey quotes turbine waits of two to three years and says buying or leasing naked turbines cuts six to eight months, while BUILT BENEATH puts large gas turbines at more than five years and lists modular behind-the-meter turbines, batteries, solar or wind plus storage, and nuclear as parallel workarounds [7L7MfK9coTY @ 00:07:52]. BUILT BENEATH also flags a brownfield play, with developers targeting old steel mills and auto plants for their existing power hookups. Wofford attacks the behind-the-meter thesis from the financing side: if the data center leaves, the generation asset is stranded [cvNO9pZAhM0 @ 00:42:32]. Siting advice splits too. Caskey rejects the common 'site near railroads' rule because rail vibration harms gas pipelines, while conceding rail corridors suit fiber [ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:19:27]. The grid camp waits in the queue; the gas camp buys speed but carries stranding and fuel-supply risk, and Caskey says roughly 99% of developers asked Steel Nation to source the gas.

The sharpest divide is whether the build cycle is a bubble. Economy Media opens with the claim that more than half of AI data center construction projects worldwide are being canceled or delayed, sets Nvidia's roughly 10 GW of GPUs shipped in 2025 against Goldman's count of only 7.7 GW of data centers operating worldwide, and cites Heatmap's tally of 25 contracts canceled in Q4 2025 and 99 of 770 planned sites contested [e0Dlr07jfjA @ 00:05:57]. Economy Media also reports Bloomberg's April 2026 story that Oracle and OpenAI's flagship Texas data center postponed its expansion plans. Matt Caldwell of Hyper rejects the bubble view outright, arguing that moratoriums create pent-up demand worth 'years if not decades' [oQVb9TRp6Og @ 00:04:02]. Break Even splits the difference with a timing warning: inference is expected to overtake training around 2027, which creates stranded-capacity risk [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:10:04]. The financing desk argues the same fight over tenants. Wofford walks through investment-grade underwriting at 40 to 50% loan-to-value against 10 to 15 year leases plus a 10-year renewal, while Tracy Alloway doubts that tenant diversification is real and flags a maturity mismatch [cvNO9pZAhM0 @ 00:45:16]. The bear case counts cancellations and contested sites; the bull case reads moratoriums as demand deferred rather than destroyed.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The failure that kills owners is utilization, not construction. Your Finance Uncle runs the math at about $150 per kW per month plus 15% ancillary revenue and lands break-even at 60 to 65% utilization [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:09:33]. On a roughly $200M build, the optimistic case reaches 75% utilization by year four and still pays back over more than a decade; the realistic case loses one tenant, drops to 50% utilization, and the site goes underwater [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:13:56]. The channel points to Cyxtera's Chapter 11 filing in June 2023 as the live warning and names the reflex that makes it worse: cutting price is often the worst move a struggling operator can make, so hold the rate and lengthen terms instead [BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:08:20]. Break Even shows the hyperscale escape hatch, take-or-pay leases where tenants pay for what they reserved whether they use it or not, citing Applied Digital's 300 MW, 15-year deal at $7.5B base revenue and up to $18.2B with options [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:06:05]. Build cost and contingency sit on top, per Your Finance Uncle: $7 to 12M per MW, engineering fees of 15 to 20%, and 15 to 25% contingency. Ancillary fees from cross-connects, metered power markup, remote hands and managed services add only 10 to 15%.

Long-lead electrical gear is the second trap, and the speakers who touched it converge: order early. MEP Engineer AJ warns to procure electrical gear, generators and cooling immediately, coordinate directly with power providers on substation readiness, and test soil and water early, because long-lead items cause schedule slippage [lTK_pU1_uFc @ 00:06:19]. BUILT BENEATH cites DOE lead times of two-plus years for grid gear and three to four years for large transformers, summed up as 'you can't download another transformer' [7L7MfK9coTY @ 00:06:39]. Economy Media adds the price side: electrical components are under 10% of build cost but nothing runs without them, transformer prices have doubled in four years, and sourcing runs through China, South Korea and Mexico under tariff risk that includes a threatened extra 100% on Chinese goods [e0Dlr07jfjA @ 00:07:34]. The result is stockpiling, with companies buying cooling fans and power supply systems just in case. Break Even reports transformers backordered for years and nine of ten projects running late, while electrical alone eats 40 to 45% of the build budget and cooling another 15 to 25% [gMNnJebMwzw @ 00:08:55]. Your Finance Uncle puts generator lead times past two years. Hunt's Nathan Heman lists the Suez Canal, the pandemic and Chinese New Year as recurring lessons-learned surprises on hyperscale jobs.

The last traps live in the field and in the meter. Nathan Heman, project executive at Hunt, prices waste at hyperscale: a 20-minute walk for 300 workers costs about $30K a day, across six to seven days a week and two shifts [P6AUptGZBvI @ 00:15:24]. Heman also warns that GMP contracts cap the contractor's upside but not its losses, calling it 'a fine line', and notes that on a 400-electrician site many foremen are running crews of six to ten for the first time [P6AUptGZBvI @ 00:25:55]. Caskey's admitted mistake at Steel Nation was chasing EPCMs and the 'Mag 8' instead of 20-year clients, and the lesson was to stay in your lane [ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:39:17]. On the operating side, BESS Engineer shows the cost of a loose PUE: with the same 100 MW of IT load, a site at PUE 1.5 draws 150 MW against 120 MW at PUE 1.2, and that 30 MW gap at $0.10 per kWh runs about $26M a year [MnGAkKrZF_I @ 00:01:01]. Under a 150 MW cap, PUE 1.2 supports 125 MW of IT, 25% more, if cooling holds at peak. Break Even adds that more than 60% of outages trace to power, cooling or cabling, and missing the Tier 4 standard of 99.995% triggers penalties.

- [ ] Learn grid interconnection and load forecasting so you can read a load study and talk to a utility about connection timelines [The Power Engineer — BWtk0CSPH6g @ 00:02:06].
- [ ] Screen sites for high-capacity electrical feeds, multiple fiber backbones, topography, flood risk and soil, then engage local authorities early on stormwater, roads and interconnection permits [MEP Engineer AJ — lTK_pU1_uFc @ 00:03:01].
- [ ] Target sites that can deliver more than 200 MW of power within 18 to 36 months, including rural counties with room for substations and switchyards [Build Core — tNNIXQhntOk @ 00:04:36].
- [ ] If going behind the grid, plan 10 to 15 turbines for 200 to 300 MW and buy or lease naked turbines to cut six to eight months [Data Center Sales & Marketing Institute (DCSMI) — ZcZ62ZBZTrA @ 00:25:42].
- [ ] Underwrite at about $150/kW/month plus 15% ancillary against a 60 to 65% break-even, and hold price while lengthening terms [Your Finance Uncle — BXuRdYxfF2k @ 00:09:33].
- [ ] Sequence capital as development equity, then construction loan, then take-out, and line up 10 to 15 year leases with a 10-year renewal [Bloomberg Podcasts — cvNO9pZAhM0 @ 00:20:57].
