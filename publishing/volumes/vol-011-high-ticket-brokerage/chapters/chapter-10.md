# Chapter 10: Business Valuation: Earnings, Multiples, and Deal Structure

*SDE, EBIT, EBITDA, add-backs, net profit versus cash flow, e-commerce valuations, SaaS valuation levers and exit planning, SBA financing, seller notes, earnouts, and rollovers*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Brokers, lower-middle-market advisors, SaaS specialists and a valuation professor converge on one formula: normalized earnings times a risk-adjusted multiple. Under roughly $500K-$1M of earnings, buyers price Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE) at about 2-4x; above that, adjusted EBITDA takes over and multiples climb with size, from 3-5x at $500K-$1M EBITDA to 6-10x at $5-10M and 12x-plus above $10M. Owner dependence, customer concentration, trend, recurring revenue and clean books move the multiple. Lenders cap price through SBA 7(a) rules: tax returns as source of truth, 10% buyer injection, DSCR floors of 1.25 versus a stated 1.5 lender appetite. Sharp splits remain on personal add-backs such as vehicles and phones, on juicing trailing earnings before a sale, and on whether headline price or cash at closing defines the real deal.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Main Street brokers and lower-middle-market advisors start from the same arithmetic: normalized earnings times a multiple, with risk deciding where the multiple lands. Shep Campbell of Deal Street, who values businesses every week, frames value as earnings, multiple and risk, and insists buyers purchase cash flow rather than revenue, warning that a $5M break-even company can be worth less than a profitable $1M one [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:01:08]. Campbell puts it bluntly: cash flow services debt and pays the owner [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:01:34]. Quiet Light Brokerage repeats the formula for online sellers, stating that a business's value is based on a multiple of its earnings and that buyers treat SDE as their baseline for estimating return on investment [R4jvbLHhCBg @ 00:00:26]. Ryan C. Winter reduces it to earnings times multiple, $300K at 3x for roughly $900K [tPnDBaB9oPU @ 00:01:02], and warns operators off revenue rules of thumb entirely [tPnDBaB9oPU @ 00:03:58]. Campbell backs that warning with a scar: a listing priced at 3x revenue, $15M on $5M of sales and roughly 10x cash flow, never sold [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:09:04]. Price conversations therefore open on documented profit, never on top-line volume, years in business, debts the seller owes or the seller's retirement number.

Practitioners also agree on which earnings number to multiply, and the switch turns on whether the buyer is buying a job or a managed company. Keira Hamilton notes that SDE and EBITDA both add back interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, one-time items and discretionary spending, while SDE alone also adds back owner salary [1dJeO4qEAhU @ 00:00:20]. Peter Nola labels SDE 'return to a working owner,' its New Zealand name [RoGz5NZF96I @ 00:00:38], and calls EBITDA 'managed profit' calculated after owner pay [RoGz5NZF96I @ 00:01:39], the standard in M&A for medium, large and capital-intensive firms [RoGz5NZF96I @ 00:03:16]. David C Barnett draws the line near $500K: SDE for owner-operated firms below it at about 2-2.2x and lower for service firms, EBITDA above it [Va5USDqv3-A @ 00:03:34], and EBIT or EBITDA minus capex for capital-intensive operators like trucking [Va5USDqv3-A @ 00:04:46]. Brett Cenkus places the salary add-back mainly around $1-2M deals, where buyers 'buy themselves a job,' while at roughly $20M management is retained and no salary comes back [y4u_MuL4xhw @ 00:05:11]. The size cutoff floats from adviser to adviser, but the logic holds: once a paid manager runs the business, the owner's paycheck stops counting as discretionary earnings and the buyer prices a managed cash stream.

On what moves the multiple, the risk lists line up almost item for item. Campbell names owner dependence, customer concentration around 30-40%, recurring revenue, trend, clean books, industry and size, and warns that 'the more a business depends on the owner at listing time, the more it will depend on the owner at closing time' [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:06:46]. Winter's list is nearly identical, flagging a single client at 50% of revenue as a red flag alongside owner dependence, trend, clean books and team [tPnDBaB9oPU @ 00:02:49]. Arthur Petropoulos explains why bigger companies earn bigger multiples: less key-person risk and a broader buyer pool, a dynamic labeled 'EBITDA expansion' that drives roll-ups, summed up as 'same work, different deals' [brFD1fFAYhQ @ 00:01:12]. On the SaaS side, David Newell of Quiet Light, who has sold 75-plus businesses including Drip, names key-man risk as mistake number one: document the code, hand it to a trusted developer or CTO, and step out quickly, the way Rob Walling brought in Derek as CTO and left the code after about six months [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:04:39]. Every one of these levers is about transferability. A buyer pays up for earnings that survive the seller walking out the door, and discounts every dollar that leaves with the founder.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The SDE build follows a repeatable sequence. Quiet Light's method strips non-cash expenses like amortization and depreciation plus taxes and interest, then owner benefits such as salary and home office, 'because how much you pay yourself is entirely up to you,' then true one-time expenses like building an app, then situational adjustments [R4jvbLHhCBg @ 00:01:14]. Nate Lind starts at the top line: gross sales minus chargebacks and refunds, COGS, SG&A and non-operating income such as PPP or EIDL money and lawsuit settlements [yaZZzBKh86I @ 00:01:39]. Lind then allows depreciation, interest and amortization, one-time website retrofits, the 2021 freight-cost delta and recruiter fees as add-backs [yaZZzBKh86I @ 00:04:01]. Tyler Jeffcoat of Seller Accountant runs trailing-twelve-month SDE off an accrual-basis P&L, walking $1M in sales, $340K gross profit and $100K operating profit to $185K of SDE [jkHzL8ya984 @ 00:06:26]. Jeffcoat's filter: unused rent, owner salary and a VA come back; core tools like a Skubana ERP and core contractors stay in, because 'you win every ad back battle that you don't have to have' [jkHzL8ya984 @ 00:05:06]. Brent Freeman of the CBB Group shows the stack on a real P&L: $924K plus $15K interest, $40K depreciation and $210K owner comp yields $1.189M of SDE [pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:04:53].

With normalized earnings locked in, the multiple grid is remarkably well mapped. Campbell runs Main Street SDE at 2-3.5x off comps and industry datasets, stacking $150K net, $100K owner pay and $50K add-backs into $300K of SDE that lands near $825K at about 2.75x, while nudging the multiple to 3.25x adds $150K [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:05:06]. Keira Hamilton quotes a typical 3-5x, with $500K of SDE at 4x producing $2M [1dJeO4qEAhU @ 00:01:28]. Petropoulos, whose Hillview Partners serves $1M-$20M EBITDA companies, maps the institutional ladder: $500K-$1M of EBITDA at 3-5x, $1-3M revised up to 5-9x, $3-5M at 6-9x with many $3M companies trading at 7-8x, $5-10M at 6-10x, and above $10M at 12x, with 15x and 20x printed in frothy markets [D58oCe_7BBM @ 00:04:07]. The arbitrage math explains PE appetite: buy ten $1M-EBITDA companies at 6x for $60M, then sell the combined $10M of EBITDA at 10x for $100M [D58oCe_7BBM @ 00:08:00]. Newell values sub-$1M-ARR SaaS on SDE at 3-5x, so a $500K-ARR product throwing off $200K of profit prices at $600K-$1M [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:10:22]. With the median near 4x [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:13:41], every $50K of add-backs is worth $200K of price, which is why the add-back fight matters so much [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:12:36].

The financing desk then decides whether the price survives. Deal Street's financing breakdown sets SBA 7(a) loans at up to $5M covering as much as 90% of goodwill, a buyer injection of at least 10% that cannot be borrowed [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:02:23], a 1.25 minimum DSCR as of October 1, 2026, no projections counted toward DSCR, and an independent QoE on deals of $3M-plus [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:03:08]. Campbell defines DSCR as cash flow plus owner salary divided by annual debt service, warning that if the price is so high that cash flow can't comfortably support the debt, the deal won't get financed [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:12:46]. Freeman's live structure: $3M price, 15% or $450K down, a $2.55M SBA loan over 10 years at 8.5% for $31,616 a month, producing 2.25:1 coverage with $150K of owner comp and 1.92:1 on a modified basis that charges a 20% return on the down payment, $7,500 a month [pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:12:28]. Seller notes fill the gap at 10-20% of price over 3-7 years, often amortized over 10-plus years with a 3-5 year balloon [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:05:13]; a note funding the buyer's down payment sits on full standby for the SBA loan's life [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:06:31] and can cover at most half that injection [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:07:20].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Rebuild trailing earnings
   - Reconstruct trailing-twelve-month earnings on an accrual-basis P&L before touching add-backs.
   - Start from gross sales, subtract chargebacks, refunds, COGS and SG&A, and strip non-operating income such as PPP/EIDL money and lawsuit settlements [Nate Lind - Business Broker and Beyond — yaZZzBKh86I @ 00:01:39]; run the result on TTM accrual books [Seller Accountant — jkHzL8ya984 @ 00:06:26].
2. Layer defensible add-backs
   - Add back non-cash items, owner benefits, true one-time spend and situational adjustments only where documented.
   - Follow the four-step SDE sequence of non-cash items, owner benefits, one-time expenses and situational adjustments [Quiet Light Brokerage — R4jvbLHhCBg @ 00:01:14]; expect lenders to reject meals, travel, car and entertainment and accept medical insurance only if documented [Brent Freeman  - The CBB Group — pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:05:58].
3. Pick the earnings measure and multiple band
   - Choose SDE, EBITDA or EBITDA minus capex by size and capital intensity, then place the business in its band.
   - Use SDE below roughly $500K and EBITDA above it, with EBITDA minus capex for trucking-type firms [David C Barnett Small Business and Deal Making SMB — Va5USDqv3-A @ 00:03:34]; place EBITDA companies on the 3-5x to 12x size ladder [Arthur Petropoulos — D58oCe_7BBM @ 00:04:07].
4. Stress-test price against the lender
   - Run DSCR on the proposed SBA structure before the number goes to market.
   - Compute DSCR as cash flow plus owner salary over annual debt service against the 1.25 minimum [Deal Street - Small to Lower Middle Market M&A — ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:11:50]; model the full structure the way Freeman did, a $3M price at 2.25:1 standard and 1.92:1 modified coverage [Brent Freeman  - The CBB Group — pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:12:28].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Add-back and structure moves the brokers actually run**
> 
> Small, documented adjustments compound through the multiple, and deal structure decides what the seller actually banks.
> - Apply a confirmed hosting cut retroactively: dropping from $8K to $4K a month credits $4K x 10 months, or $40K, to trailing earnings, and a one-time $10-15K website overhaul can be added back [Quiet Light Brokerage — oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:48:11].
> - Pull PPP or EIDL money and lawsuit settlements out as non-operating income, then add back recruiter fees and the 2021 freight-cost delta as one-time items [Nate Lind - Business Broker and Beyond — yaZZzBKh86I @ 00:01:39].
> - Shop lenders to the add-back schedule: SBA lenders vary in whether owner salary and depreciation come back in full, in part or not at all, so match the lender to the add-backs [Keira Hamilton — 1dJeO4qEAhU @ 00:02:40].
> - Use the seller-note trick of the trade: amortize over 10-plus years with a balloon at year 3-5 that usually gets refinanced [Deal Street - Small to Lower Middle Market M&A — a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:05:13].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The add-back fight is where brokers split hardest, and the split moves real dollars. Shep Campbell at Deal Street adds back W-2 owner salary and payroll taxes plus life insurance and family members' phones, vehicles and health plans, yet refuses to add back the owner's personal vehicle, phone or health insurance [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:02:25]. Ryan C. Winter runs the opposite playbook: salary, interest, taxes, depreciation, one-time items and the personal vehicle, phone and travel all go back in, and Winter reports SDE often lands 40 to 50 percent above tax-return profit [tPnDBaB9oPU @ 00:01:56]. Brent Freeman of the CBB Group says that inflated number dies at the bank, because lenders treat tax returns as the source of truth and refuse meals, travel, car and entertainment add-backs, accepting medical insurance only when documented [pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:05:58]. Nate Lind splits the difference: travel, meals, personal auto and phone are generally accepted, but SBA lenders rule on them case by case [yaZZzBKh86I @ 00:03:14]. The operator read: the aggressive schedule wins the listing conversation, the conservative schedule wins the financing conversation, so build both versions before quoting a seller any number at all.

Practitioners also disagree on where SDE stops and EBITDA starts, and on which multiple belongs to each. David C Barnett uses SDE for owner-operated firms under roughly $500K at about 2 to 2.2 times, lower for service firms, switches to EBITDA above $500K, and moves capital-intensive operators like trucking to EBIT or EBITDA minus capex [Va5USDqv3-A @ 00:03:34]. Brent Freeman puts the switch at about $1M and above, noting the EBITDA multiple is always higher [pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:11:00]. Brett Cenkus ties the owner-salary add-back to the $1 to 2M band, where buyers buy themselves a job, and drops it near $20M because management stays on [y4u_MuL4xhw @ 00:05:11]. SaaS breaks the pattern entirely: David Newell at Quiet Light values sub-$1M ARR deals on SDE, but over $1M ARR private equity and strategic buyers price on revenue and will accept thin EBITDA if the founder is out of the code, churn stays under 4 percent a month, and growth runs 30 to 50 percent a year [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:08:13]. Pick the earnings metric first, because it decides which multiple band a seller is even allowed to argue for, and which buyer pool will show up to the table.

Financing thresholds and pre-sale earnings tuning are the third fault line. Deal Street states the SBA 7(a) minimum DSCR moved from 1.2 to 1.25, with projections no longer allowed toward the ratio [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:03:35]. Brent Freeman says lenders want at least 1.5 in this interest rate environment, and runs a modified DSCR that credits the buyer a 20 percent return on the down payment, $7,500 a month, which drags a 2.25:1 deal down to 1.92:1 [pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:12:28]. On juicing the trailing twelve months, the 2025 Quiet Light panel split live: Tom is very anti, while John Hanstock says it depends, arguing that switching AWS to reserved pricing and showing three months of savings can justify applying them across the trailing 12 months [In6A5W0WFdY @ 00:34:26]. David Newell goes further, applying a hosting cut from $8K to $4K a month retroactively, $4K times 10 months, for a $40K adjustment, and treating a one-time $10 to 15K website overhaul as an add-back [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:48:11]. The split playbook: underwrite to the 1.5 cushion and clean books when the deal needs bank money, and save retroactive savings adjustments for the buyer who can actually pay for them without a lender's sign-off.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start at the listing price. Shep Campbell at Deal Street once listed a business at 3 times revenue, $15M on $5M of revenue, roughly 10 times cash flow, and it never sold; the same broker names selling into a downturn as mistake number two and warns that add-back-everything schedules raise red flags with buyers [ggUaT-lHV0A @ 00:09:04]. Ryan C. Winter bans revenue rules of thumb outright and treats one client at 50 percent of revenue as a red flag that drags the multiple down [tPnDBaB9oPU @ 00:03:58]. Aswath Damodaran shows why cheap-looking multiples trap buyers: the private equity rule that under 6 times EBITDA is cheap would have flagged about 10,000 of roughly 40,000 global companies in 2013 and half of all companies in 2009, so the fix is the 20th percentile or first quartile recalculated each year [xI-KTlxfNDU @ 00:04:12]. Damodaran's Ryder System case, at 2.91 times, looked cheap but carried the oldest fleet in the sector and would need debt to replace it [xI-KTlxfNDU @ 00:11:07]. The economics check is mismatch-hunting: a low multiple sitting on old assets, high taxes or heavy reinvestment needs is usually priced correctly, not a bargain waiting for a sharp buyer.

The second cluster of losses happens between handshake and funding. Brent Freeman warns that a broker ends up selling the transaction three times, to the buyer, the lender and the appraiser, and that lenders underwrite off tax returns, so add-backs that cannot survive that review die at the bank; Freeman's protection is a clawback through seller-note forgiveness if add-backs prove false [pzqh4R8Hppk @ 00:07:41]. Deal Street adds the hard SBA mechanics: a seller note funding the buyer's down payment goes on full standby, with no payments or interest for the life of the SBA loan, and the SBA will not finance earnouts at all [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:06:31]. Keira Hamilton reports SBA lenders vary on whether owner salary and depreciation get added back in full, in part or not at all, so the lender has to match the add-back schedule [1dJeO4qEAhU @ 00:02:40]. Deal Street's two-offer comparison is the economics check: Offer A at $1.2M cash, a $500K note and a $500K earnout loses to Offer B at $1.7M cash and a $200K note, which puts $500K more in the seller's pocket on day one [a5Z--B3Uhrg @ 00:13:04]. Price the structure, not the headline.

SaaS and concentration traps destroy value quietly. David Newell at Quiet Light ranks key-man risk as mistake number one, calls lifetime plans suicidal after one surfaced within 24 hours of due diligence, and lists heavy discounting and halting development for more than 3 to 6 months as value destroyers; the timing fix is to avoid selling exhausted or mid-rebuild and aim for about six months after a major release [oJsHo5vaDus @ 00:39:54]. On the 2025 Quiet Light panel, Tom walked away from a custom-data contract worth about half a team member's salary because one-off revenue is not worth anything to buyers, and flags COGS at 20 percent as a possible misclassification signal against a target near 5 percent [In6A5W0WFdY @ 00:17:52]. David C Barnett's concentration case, 60 percent of revenue from four clients, gets handled with seller notes subject to offset rather than a blunt price cut [Va5USDqv3-A @ 00:13:35]. Brett Cenkus goes harder: a business living on two customers may be unsellable or sold only at a discount [y4u_MuL4xhw @ 00:10:18]. Clean these up before listing, because diligence finds them anyway.
