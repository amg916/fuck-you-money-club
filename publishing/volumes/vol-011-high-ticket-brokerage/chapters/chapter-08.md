## Executive Summary

Brokerage owners agree that the agent makes the company. They recruit experienced producers, sell value instead of the cheapest split, and scale slowly, because brokerages fail from carrying too many costs too soon. Garth Ward seeded Park Place with a coffee-shop event where 10+ agents signed on day one, then grew to 137 agents and 4 offices. Even so, he ran at about 10% margins and rebuilt pricing from a 90/10 split with a $15K cap to 70/30 with a $500 transaction fee and a 2.25% servicing fee. Jim Remley abandoned new-agent seminars after 87% attrition and switched to targeting $2-10M producers. Joe Mendoza calls a $500 flat fee a model for going broke without ancillary revenue. Practitioners split on boutique versus franchise, where franchise fees run 3% to 8% off the top. They also split on whether social media or prospecting calls drive revenue.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who opened a shop agrees on the first prerequisite: owning a brokerage is a separate trade. Kyle Handy calls it an entirely different ball game, not agent work multiplied. It carries heavier legal and tax consequences, and the broker may answer for dozens or even hundreds of agents [QqzqH1glUuA @ 00:01:59]. The entry ticket is a broker's license. Requirements vary by state and cover education classes, industry experience and an exam, and without the license nobody carries agents under them [QqzqH1glUuA @ 00:04:17]. Ben Eger spells out what that license puts on the owner's desk. The supervising broker oversees licensed work, records, advertising and entrusted funds, and no office policy erases that responsibility. Earnest money sits in trust with an accurate ledger, separate from operating revenue. Paying office expenses out of a buyer's deposit is conversion, not merely commingling [DPj-ieo_sSM @ 00:01:40]. Dee Kumar adds the remaining baseline. Escrow funds are never commingled or converted, and every ad carries the broker ID and license number, with no blind ads. There is also a hard antitrust line against colluding, fixing prices, allocating markets or forcing tie-in agreements [V5gUCAcegsY @ 00:04:15]. Both instructors stress that commissions are negotiable and not fixed by law, so every split and fee is open to design [V5gUCAcegsY @ 00:02:43].

The second point of agreement is that agents are the brokerage's real customers, and experienced producers are the inventory worth chasing. Bob Knakal opens the commercial panel with a line the other founders echo: the agent makes the company [0u9CyKG6vj4 @ 00:00:00]. Jim Remley learned this the expensive way. The early model of monthly two-hour seminars for new licensees produced an 87% attrition rate and kept roughly 1 in 10 recruits [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:08:38]. Remley then pivoted to agents with more than two years in the business and $2 to $10 million in production. That shift carried the firm to 17 offices [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:10:00]. Garth Ward narrows the target further to the on-the-bubble agent: $1.5 to $3.5 million in volume and $50K to $120K GCI, poached from smaller independent brokerages [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:17:01]. Ward also notes where most shops stall. Most brokerages sit around 4 agents, few reach 10, and most plateau near 30 agents per office [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:03:41]. Tim Garrity's first roughly 10 agents came through relationships. After that, better splits, a cap system and a handbook co-written with agents carried recruiting [2WIql7qBDoY @ 00:11:06]. The shared lesson is to recruit people who already produce and then keep them close.

The third consensus is that owners win on value delivered, not on the cheapest split. Remley is blunt: top agents generally don't work for the lowest-cost office, and competing on lowest cost is a fallacy. That is why the model charges a $50 to $75 monthly tech-stack fee instead of racing to zero [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:34:16]. Brandon Mulrenin applies the same test from the agent's chair. Compensation has to line up with the value exchange between broker and agent, and high-fee, low-value, broker-centric firms are the ones to avoid [R_uWZH6c9lI @ 00:12:56]. Joe Mendoza paid more than $200K to the house, the broker-owner and the franchise in a GCI year north of $1M, yet spoke to the broker-owner once. He lands on one rule: if your benefits exceed the cost, that's not a bad deal [VcNeWYSFiDE @ 00:03:45]. The flip side is cost discipline inside the owner's own books. Handy warns that brokerages fail because they scale too quickly and carry too many costs. The plan has to project income, show break-even, and budget for negative cash flow for at least a few years, starting with a handful of people or even a family [QqzqH1glUuA @ 00:03:40].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Garth Ward's launch is the most detailed playbook on record. In under a week, Ward pulled market data, cold-called a couple hundred agents with low or no production, and rented a coffee shop for a $200 minimum buy-in. About 15 showed up, printed contracts sat on the tables, and over 10 agents signed on day one [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:06:27]. Co-hosted events with lenders and title reps, such as Facebook ads training, pushed Park Place to 30 agents in four to five months [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:08:28]. Every later recruit got a custom presentation and sales deck, because the team treated every agent appointment like a million-dollar listing [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:14:03]. The agent-referral incentive paid a recruiting agent a portion of the recruit's commission until cap, every year the recruit stayed, and it drove about 25% of growth [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:22:13]. Indeed ads added 20+ agents in a year [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:22:45]. Remley turns chasing into attracting. The target is 3 to 5 agents a month calling in, fed by touching the database 20 to 50 times a year, and experienced agents are never closed, only offered an open door [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:14:32]. George Pino recruits with a 100% split on the first couple of deals, education-based pitches and referral bonuses for agents who bring in hires [0u9CyKG6vj4 @ 00:20:10].

The pricing math is where launches live or die. Ward opened with a 90/10 split, a monthly fee, a $15K cap and a $150 transaction fee, and calls that model not viable [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:32:22]. The rebuilt structure ran a 70/30 split, a $500 transaction fee that includes transaction management, and a 2.25% servicing fee on every deal regardless of cap [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:33:54]. Even then, Ward reports profit margins around 10% [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:33:14]. He pegs the real economics at roughly 1,000 agents, or about 20 locations of 50 agents each [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:13:30]. Mendoza served as state broker for a flat-fee firm and calls roughly $500 per transaction a model for going broke unless ancillary title, lending and insurance revenue fills the gap [VcNeWYSFiDE @ 00:10:04]. On the franchise side, Jesse Ebner's team saw 3%, 6% and up to 8% taken off the top of every commission. That came before office splits of 70/30, 50/50 or up to 90/10 [6cCUTLOmNIE @ 00:01:16]. Keeping that money local let the boutique donate almost $60,000 to 12 nonprofits in one year [6cCUTLOmNIE @ 00:02:25]. Remley's parity list is the floor every model still has to fund: mobile website, company email, CRM and CMA/AVM, with an AI tech stack as the differentiator [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:32:59].

On the deal side, the mechanics come down to follow-up cadence and response speed. Christine McDannell runs 15 touch points, reaching out by call, text and email about five times each, every few days. McDannell holds only 5 deals at a time and averages a sale in 7 months [7jkOz9WXEh8 @ 00:22:08]. Sellers pay a $15K to $20K deposit credited toward the success fee, backed by a 90-day guarantee. If no solid LOI from a qualified, capitalized buyer arrives within 90 days flat, the deposit comes back [7jkOz9WXEh8 @ 00:09:54]. The buyer database has lived in Zoho CRM for 5 years [7jkOz9WXEh8 @ 00:39:55]. Eddie Gonzalez's team, half of whom trained at the Ritz-Carlton, holds to the 15-minute callback rule [0u9CyKG6vj4 @ 00:12:37]. The Toolnest Explained pipeline gives the board its structure: new lead, contacting, qualified, proposal sent, negotiation, won and lost. Each card moves as calls and quotes happen, so deals never go completely cold [B4H8Hy41Myg @ 00:01:38]. Tim Garrity's database of about 7,000 gets quarterly neighborhood reports covering listings, solds, days on market and price trend. It also gets a monthly newsletter with one blog post, three to four listings and one lifestyle piece, which takes an hour a month to keep as a solid drip campaign [2WIql7qBDoY @ 00:22:08].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Plan, license and budget for the dip
   - Write the business plan, size agent capacity, price the lease and infrastructure, and secure the broker's license.
   - Project income, confirm a path to break-even, and budget for negative cash flow for at least a few years before adding staff or space [Kyle Handy and AJ Mida — QqzqH1glUuA @ 00:03:08]
2. Seed the roster with a launch event
   - Pull market data, cold-call low-production agents, and host a room with contracts on the tables.
   - Ward rented a coffee shop for a $200 minimum, about 15 agents attended, and 10+ signed on day one, all set up in under a week [Mike Sherrard — OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:06:27]
3. Price for survival, not for the lowest fee
   - Set split, transaction fee and per-deal servicing fee so the brokerage profits regardless of cap.
   - Ward's working model ran 70/30, a $500 transaction fee and a 2.25% servicing fee on every deal, after a 90/10 model with a $15K cap proved unviable [Mike Sherrard — OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:33:54]
4. Install the pipeline and retention cadence
   - Track every prospect and agent through defined CRM stages and keep deliberate relationship touches going.
   - Retain through calls, lunches and handwritten notes under the rule that friends don't leave friends, and run pipeline conversations on a CRM follow-up flag [Smart Agents — YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:26:12]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split among practitioners is where the money goes after every closing. The Jesse Ebner hosts worked at two franchises and two boutiques before launching Signature Properties in 2017, and they chose not to franchise because the franchise takes 3% at one firm, 6% at another and 'all the way up to eight percent' off the top of every commission, before the office split even lands [6cCUTLOmNIE @ 00:01:16]. They kept that money local, bought tools their agents actually use, and donated almost $60,000 to 12 nonprofits last year. Joe Mendoza makes the same math from the agent chair. In his year above $1M GCI he paid more than $200K to the house, the broker-owner and the franchise fee, yet spoke to the broker-owner once, so his rule is to stay only if the benefits exceed the cost [VcNeWYSFiDE @ 00:03:07]. Mendoza also found boutique pay 'loosey-goosey' as an REO firm VP, and joined eXp in 2020. Garth Ward took the same exit after scaling Park Place to 137 agents, deciding in about three weeks and adding roughly 150 agents and 25 states on eXp revenue share [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:47:31]. Tim Garrity holds the opposite line. He frames eXp and KW as McDonald's against 'Tim's Burger Shop,' a brokerage built on customer service, paperless workflows and texting [2WIql7qBDoY @ 00:24:00].

Practitioners also split on which agents to build around. Jim Remley tried the new-agent route first. Monthly two-hour seminars produced 87% attrition and kept roughly 1 in 10 recruits [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:08:38]. He pivoted to agents with more than two years in the business and $2 to $10 million in production, which carried the firm to 17 offices. He also warns against trying to 'close' those agents; open the door and stack benefits over time instead. Garth Ward launched the opposite way. He pulled market data and cold-called a couple hundred agents with low or no production, rented a coffee shop for a $200 minimum and laid printed contracts on the tables. More than 10 agents signed on day one, and nearly all became top producers [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:06:27]. Ward's later sweet spot is the 'on the bubble' agent doing $1.5 to $3.5 million in volume and $50K to $120K GCI, poached from smaller independents [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:17:01]. Kyle Handy splits the difference. New agents are easier to recruit and more loyal, while experienced agents bring a book of business but are harder to land. So most brokerages start with a mix and use administrative services and subsidized education as the hook [QqzqH1glUuA @ 00:10:14].

The split itself is the loudest disagreement. US Realty Training calls 50/50 'no bueno,' treats 70/30 as reasonable and notes smaller shops offering 90/10, but warns that 90 of nothing is still nothing [4LntMyEuBXE @ 00:03:19]. Amanda Fesalani of Designed For Agents defends deeper splits when the support is real: the more a brokerage provides at no cost, the deeper the split. Splits run from 60/40 and 70/30 down to 50/50 on teams and as low as 40/60. Meanwhile, 100% shops charging $250 to $500 per transaction often leave a new agent alone with a 3 a.m. or 7 a.m. emergency [Vbv1TkWvBD8 @ 00:03:59]. Fesalani expects most successful agents to settle into a hybrid model long term. Brandon Mulrenin runs a 100% model and lays out the menu as 50/50 with no cap, 100%, or 70/30 with a cap. He insists compensation must track the value exchange between broker and agent [R_uWZH6c9lI @ 00:12:56]. Garth Ward shows the owner-side result. Park Place opened at 90/10 with a monthly fee, a $15K cap and a $150 transaction fee, and found it unviable. It ended at 70/30, a $500 transaction fee including transaction management, and a 2.25% servicing fee on every deal regardless of cap [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:32:22].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first economics check is that owning the brokerage may pay less than selling. Kyle Handy tells new brokers to expect negative cash flow 'for at least a few years.' He states flatly that brokerages fail because they scale too quickly and carry too many costs [QqzqH1glUuA @ 00:03:40]. Jim Remley plans for two years of reduced personal production when opening an office, because the owner's own deals carry the build [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:08:08]. Garth Ward lived the numbers at scale. At about 80 agents he hired managers, a CEO and a front desk, the raised fees barely covered staff, and margins ran around 10% [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:33:14]. By his math the model only works near 1,000 agents, roughly 20 locations of 50 agents each. Multi-state licensing would have taken about 16 years to reach 15 states. Legal exposure compounds it: two lawsuits in four years cost just under $200K combined because an entity cannot represent itself [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:38:15]. Ward also warns that Zillow leads worked at 50/50 are team income, not brokerage profit. Buyout offers that once came in at 3x net on a three-year earnout now look closer to 2x. His conclusion is blunt: a high-producing agent will make more money than a brokerage owner.

Undercharging is the most repeated pricing mistake. Joe Mendoza served as state broker for a flat-fee firm charging about $500 per transaction. He calls it a model for going broke unless title, lending and insurance revenue fill the gap; he was micromanaged there and resigned [VcNeWYSFiDE @ 00:09:32]. At Keller Williams he recruited agents and still earned 'zero' because profit share depends on the office being profitable. Garth Ward admits Park Place undercharged early and ran an undialed brand that forced a full niche rebrand. He also found that building proprietary tech cost more than the fees it replaced [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:11:48]. Jim Remley rejects the race to the bottom entirely. Parity items like a mobile site, company email, CRM and CMA/AVM sit behind a $50 to $75 monthly tech fee, and top agents generally do not work for the lowest-cost office [YKfvldXPHWw @ 00:34:16]. From the other side of the desk, Brandon Mulrenin warns agents that broker-centric firms bury costs in printing, color printing, technology, broker review, E&O, office and desk fees. Then they hit agents with surprise month-end bills [R_uWZH6c9lI @ 00:07:18]. Mulrenin also calls payouts that take weeks unacceptable; 24 to 48 hours by direct deposit is the standard.

Deal-side failures cluster around time, ego and bad numbers. Christine McDannell says the hard part is holding the whole deal together. McDannell watched a COVID lockdown kill a multi-million-dollar sale the day before closing, which is the origin of the rule that time kills deals [7jkOz9WXEh8 @ 00:12:10]. McDannell also fields sellers anchored on AI valuations. One claimed $5M on $150K net after a Claude run, a business McDannell put at about $900K max. Another seller invented $6M on roughly $50K to $120K net [7jkOz9WXEh8 @ 00:33:07]. A Bay Area salon seller took a full-price, all-cash offer from a buyer who pulled a HELOC, then balked at a three-year, 50-mile non-compete and walked. The countermeasure is a $15K to $20K deposit credited toward the success fee, which screens seller commitment, backed by a 90-day LOI guarantee. Inside the office, Garth Ward warns that skipping an early manager hire and re-routing agents to managers later damaged culture [OFzI2gK-nps @ 00:29:15]. Brandon Mulrenin reports agents losing deals while waiting a week to hear back from their broker. That support gap pushes producers out the door [R_uWZH6c9lI @ 00:04:07].
