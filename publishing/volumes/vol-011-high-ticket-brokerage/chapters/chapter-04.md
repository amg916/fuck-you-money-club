## Executive Summary

Business Broker Crowd Call operators agree that 17 states require a real estate license to sell a business, with Nevada adding a business broker permit and Illinois demanding securities registration; unlicensed operators face disgorged fees, rescinded deals, and unpaid commissions. In non-licensed states like Texas, Virginia, Maryland and DC, brokers sell personal property freely but stop at the LOI, stay out of lease assignments, and hand real estate to a licensed agent. Street numbers: 10-15% commissions with a $20,000 minimum, a Lehman-style ladder from 10% down to 6%, Rule 1017 triggered at 25% ownership change, OSJs inspected annually versus three-year branch cycles, and six-year and three-year record buckets with two years easily accessible. Disagreement centers on whether a non-required Texas license lets a broker fill purchase agreements, and how far consultant status protects unlicensed operators.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: The 17-State Map, the LOI Ceiling, and Unlicensed Exposure

Every Business Broker Crowd Call operator works from the same map: 17 states require a license to sell a business, including Washington, Oregon, California, Utah, Florida, Arizona and Colorado, while Texas does not [Wj1uuYyuyQk @ 00:01:03] [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:00:26]. The trigger is where the target business sits, not where the brokerage is incorporated, so a Delaware-based shop pulling national leads checks each listing's state first. The tool is unglamorous: search which states require a real estate license, print the Business Brokerage Press list, pin it to the wall, and check it on every inbound call. Two states break the pattern. Nevada stacks a business broker permit on top of the real estate license, and Illinois swaps the license for registration with the state Securities Commission, which is why one speaker corrects the count to 16 plus Illinois [uRVS3zWAYwI @ 00:00:47]. California splits the tiers: a broker license runs independently, while a salesperson license must hang under a broker. In Florida, Joe Shemansky, chairman of the Business Brokers of Florida, states that under Florida Real Estate Law 475 anyone transacting a business sale on behalf of another needs a valid DBPR license, checkable through the verify-a-license button at myfloridalicense.com [JkYDV-Gr_qE @ 00:00:06].

The second point of agreement is that unlicensed brokering is free until it is not. A Business Broker Crowd Call speaker compares it to doing 85 in a 65: nobody cares until a lawsuit drags you into a courtroom, where opposing attorneys lead with the missing license [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:01:55]. Depending on state law, deals can be unwound and rescinded, fees disgorged, and the unlicensed party left on the hook for damages to the other side; in California the speaker has watched a seller's attorney tell an unlicensed operator who ran the entire transaction that they were not getting paid, and to sue if they disliked it [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:03:13]. Enforcement from the field runs through complaints: a buyer or seller files with the Department of Real Estate, which issues a cease and desist, though the speaker expects most offenders keep operating. Joe Shemansky pushes the same lever, urging Florida buyers and sellers to report all unlicensed activity [JkYDV-Gr_qE @ 00:01:52]. Janis Benstock adds the licensed-side risk: a well-known agent was fined $5,000 by the real estate commission for violating billboard advertising guidelines, likely by accident, which is the cost of letting the law go stale after pre-licensure [F2USouPuthI @ 00:01:18].

Operators also agree on where the line sits in non-licensed states. A business is personal property and can be sold all day long without a license; real property is the land, and the moment the seller also wants the building sold, a licensed agent in that state has to carry that piece [CoD81HjwtQo @ 00:00:47]. The same speaker uses a strip mall to size the opportunity: twelve businesses inside, from a flower shop to an auto repair, are all business-broker inventory, but the mall itself is real estate agent work. In Virginia, Maryland and DC, which one operator says require no license for business sales, an unlicensed broker still cannot touch the lease assignment; buyer and seller, a commercial or residential agent, or a closing attorney handles it [Py7d57O-Rxs @ 00:02:04]. Paperwork has the same ceiling. Licensed-state brokers fill in the blanks on purchase agreements, while non-licensed-state brokers stop at the non-binding LOI or risk being accused of practicing law without a license, handing the purchase agreement to an attorney [paYxIXgYO0I @ 00:01:36]. A newer operator flagged the corollary: a deal bundling land, like a gas station owner who owns the dirt, needs a real estate license even in a non-licensed state [Wj1uuYyuyQk @ 00:05:01].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On fees, the Business Broker Crowd Call standard is 10 to 15% of the deal, written as a percentage with a minimum fee: a contract at 12% with a $20,000 floor turns a $100,000 sale into an effective 20% commission [_4qKlVp4-8s @ 00:00:25]. Above $3 million to $10 million the percentage drops, and the speaker lays out a Lehman-style ladder: 10% on the first million, 9% on the second, 8% on the third, 7% on the fourth, and 6% on everything after, all negotiable, against the 2 to 6% range the questioner cited for commercial transactions [_4qKlVp4-8s @ 00:01:31]. Referral money depends on the sender, not just the broker's license. In California a business broker can pay a referral fee to an unlicensed grocery-store worker who sends a seller, but CPAs cannot take referral fees from non-CPAs and attorneys cannot take them from non-attorneys under the Business and Professions Code [uRVS3zWAYwI @ 00:02:10]. Inside eXp, the mechanics are structural: business sales run through eXp Commercial with a business broker addendum to the independent contractor agreement, because eXp Realty's E&O does not cover business sales while eXp Commercial's does [Py7d57O-Rxs @ 00:02:57].

On the securities side, The Finance Lab walks the broker-dealer build in sequence. Form BD goes through the CRD, and the SEC has 45 days to grant registration or start denial proceedings; FINRA membership is a separate application that checks capital, supervisory procedures and the business plan, SIPC membership is automatic unless the firm sells only mutual funds and variable annuities, and states still need their own registrations [dKOzaPUjfHM @ 00:05:41]. The branch exclusions run on hard counts: a non-primary residence used fewer than 30 business days a year stays a non-branch, a mostly insurance storefront can do 25 securities transactions a year, and trade 26 makes it a branch [dKOzaPUjfHM @ 00:08:27]. Rule 1017 fires at 25% of assets or control; ownership changes file at least 30 days ahead and may close before review ends, while new business lines like a market-making desk must wait for approval. Form BDW takes effect 60 days after filing, and FINRA keeps jurisdiction for two years [dKOzaPUjfHM @ 00:09:44]. CertFuel adds the WSP spec: every review names who is responsible, what is reviewed, how often, and how it is documented, and a principal must hold the registration matching the business line supervised [WYhf4z4Djwk @ 00:01:59].

The money-control mechanics come from the FINOP desk. The FINOP Controller formula sums customer credits plus penalty items, subtracts customer debits after a 1 to 3% charge, and deposits any net credit into the special bank account, usually with a cushion above the calculated number [-3SAUcV8ljM @ 00:02:06]. Controls are specific: two or more finance or operations staff prepare and review, source data is imported straight into the Excel workbook to cut manual input, each line item carries a threshold that forces extra analysis, finance, operations and treasury meet before deposit instructions go out, and finance keeps timestamps, approvals and bank confirms after treasury executes [-3SAUcV8ljM @ 00:07:26]. FINRA's Sarah Kanny flags that firms averaging more than $500 million in reserve-formula credits over the last 12 month-end FOCUS reports moved from weekly to daily computation as of June 30, 2026 [BV647oU5rB0 @ 00:02:34]. Records follow VoraPrep's buckets: foundational documents like articles of incorporation for life, blotters, ledgers and customer account records for six years, correspondence, ads, WSPs and U4/U5 for three years, the first two years of both shorter buckets easily accessible, and everything electronic stored in WORM format, write once, read many [Dw1n0a_kMu0 @ 00:05:46].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Screen the Deal Location
   - Check the business's state against the printed 17-state list before quoting a fee.
   - If the state is not on the list, verify anyway: one operator gets a legal opinion from a real estate or business lawyer in a state like North Dakota before transacting there [Business Broker Crowd Call — CoD81HjwtQo @ 00:05:13].
2. Split Real Property From Personal Property
   - Separate the business assets from any land, building, or lease in the deal.
   - Sell the business yourself, but hand land sales and lease assignments to a state-licensed agent, the parties, or a closing attorney unless you hold a license in that state [Business Broker Crowd Call — Py7d57O-Rxs @ 00:02:04].
3. Price the Engagement
   - Write a percentage fee with a minimum, then ladder it down on larger deals.
   - Use a structure like 12% with a $20,000 minimum on Main Street deals and a 10%-9%-8%-7%-6% ladder by the million on bigger transactions, all negotiable [Business Broker Crowd Call — _4qKlVp4-8s @ 00:00:47].
4. Paper the Deal to the Right Ceiling
   - Fill purchase agreements only where the state license covers it; otherwise stop at the LOI.
   - In licensed states like California, brokers fill in the blanks on purchase agreements; in Texas or Montana, stop at the LOI and let an attorney draft or review the purchase agreement [Business Broker Crowd Call — paYxIXgYO0I @ 00:01:00].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how much freedom a broker really has outside the 17 license states. One Business Broker Crowd Call host calls non-license states completely unregulated and says the broker there is really just acting as a business consultant who can do whatever they want. In the same breath the host hedges the play: the safest bet is to run every deal as if it sat in your home state and follow your own license rules, so if anything blows up you can show you followed every regulation you already live under [_4qKlVp4-8s @ 00:02:06]. Inside license states the consultant label gets no respect. A dental-practice broker asking about Washington reports a rival insisting a consultant title dodges the real estate license, and the host fires back that it is not a problem until it is, like doing 85 in a 65 until a cop lights you up, because the opposing attorney leads with the missing license the moment a lawsuit starts [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:01:35] [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:01:55]. Florida takes the hardest line: Joe Shemansky, chairman of the Business Brokers of Florida, states that anyone transacting a business sale on behalf of another must hold a valid DBPR real estate license under Florida law 475 [JkYDV-Gr_qE @ 00:00:06].

The second fork is footprint. The same Crowd Call host refuses the 50-state route outright: licensing in every state, then chasing continuing ed and renewing every so many years, would be a nightmare and a full-time job in and of itself, and the host says they would never even consider it [CoD81HjwtQo @ 00:04:15]. When a lead lands in a license state, the alternative playbook is to either get licensed there or partner with a local licensee, work the deal in the background, and put that partner's name and license number on all of the paperwork [Wj1uuYyuyQk @ 00:02:00]. Ian Tucker, who brokers hard-money and DSCR loans, accepts the opposite outcome on the financing side. Because there is no federal oversight of investment real estate loan brokers, each state writes its own rules, and Tucker warns that anyone operating across the country could end up holding a lot of licenses and registrations across multiple states just to stay compliant as a business owner [VF-_WZaM0iI @ 00:01:13] [VF-_WZaM0iI @ 00:03:24]. So the real disagreement is cost tolerance: the business broker routes around the license map with a licensed partner whose name carries the paperwork, while the loan broker treats a growing stack of state licenses and registrations as the standing price of running a national book of investor loans.

The third fight is over paperwork authority and training. A caller pressed the point that a Texas real estate license should let a broker fill in a business purchase agreement the way California brokers do. The host answered no: selling a business in Texas is not licensed activity, so the license does not reach those contracts any more than it authorizes drafting an employment or consulting agreement for somebody [paYxIXgYO0I @ 00:04:09]. In California, the host adds, the state exam has nothing whatsoever on business brokering, not even one question and no continuing ed on it, so the standing advice to anyone selling businesses there is to join CAB for California-specific education on how business sales actually work [paYxIXgYO0I @ 00:05:57]. Janis Benstock, a Philadelphia broker and instructor, hits the same gap from the residential side and answers it with depth instead of association membership. CE is only 14 hours every two years, which is not enough, and the 240-hour Pennsylvania broker curriculum is worth taking even with no plan to open a brokerage, because it keeps agents out of trouble and unlocks creative structures like options, seller financing, and title workarounds that rescue deals that look dead [F2USouPuthI @ 00:02:29] [F2USouPuthI @ 00:03:20].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure is getting caught unlicensed after doing all the work. A Crowd Call host explains that, depending on state law, transactions can be unwound and rescinded just because the alleged broker was not licensed properly, leaving that person responsible for the commissions received plus damages to the other parties [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:02:33]. The host has watched California deals where the broker, or the person acting as one, handled the entire transaction and the seller's attorney simply refused to pay at the end: you are not licensed properly, we are not paying your fee, and if you don't like it, try to sue us [22hjuZT2usA @ 00:03:33]. Licensed practitioners get burned too. Janis Benstock cites a well-known agent fined $5,000 for not complying with advertising guidelines on a billboard [F2USouPuthI @ 00:01:18]. Benstock adds that the violation probably happened by accident, and admits that after roughly 15 years in real estate they only realized how much law they had forgotten once they started teaching it every day [F2USouPuthI @ 00:01:37]. That is the real trap for producers: these violations are usually accidents, which is exactly why fluency in the rules, not good intent, is what keeps the fine, the clawback, and the refused fee off the books.

The next traps hide inside deal structure, not licensing headlines. In non-license jurisdictions like Virginia, Maryland, and DC, a broker can sell the business but can only go as far as the LOI, because putting together a purchase contract counts as practicing law, and cannot touch the lease assignment without a Virginia real estate license; buyer and seller, a commercial or residential agent, or a closing attorney has to handle it [Py7d57O-Rxs @ 00:02:04]. Real property flips the rules in the other direction. A newer broker found a lead in a non-license state that included real estate, and the host confirmed that a gas station sold together with the land it sits on needs a real estate license even where business sales are unregulated [Wj1uuYyuyQk @ 00:06:04]. The same logic covers the strip mall: the shops inside can be sold by a business broker, but the building itself has to be sold by a real estate agent [CoD81HjwtQo @ 00:03:18]. Coverage is a separate failure point that has nothing to do with state law. At eXp, the realty side's errors and omissions insurance does not cover business sales; only eXp Commercial's E&O does, and only with a business broker addendum to the independent contractor agreement [Py7d57O-Rxs @ 00:03:17].

Economics checks cut across both worlds. On small Main Street deals, a contract written at 12% with a $20,000 minimum fee turns into a 20% commission on a $100,000 sale [_4qKlVp4-8s @ 00:00:47]. Run that number before signing, because on the smallest listings the minimum, not the percentage, sets the fee the seller will fight. On the broker-dealer side, FINRA's Mark Ramler flags a quiet net capital drain for M&A shops paid in restricted shares: when a portion is owed to a registered person who does not want delivery of illiquid stock, the firm ends up carrying the shares as a non-allowable asset and accruing compensation expense, cutting net worth and net capital unless a written shares-only agreement qualifies it for the new relief [BV647oU5rB0 @ 00:43:09]. The FINOP Controller stresses reconciling cash suspense accounts daily, researching credit items as quickly as possible, and reporting large credit balances up the line before they are resolved, especially for firms running daily reserve calculations [-3SAUcV8ljM @ 00:09:03]. VoraPrep closes the loop on records: six-year and three-year records must sit somewhere easily accessible for the first two years, and a third-party storage facility that takes three days to ship a box does not qualify [Dw1n0a_kMu0 @ 00:04:35].
