## Executive Summary

Yacht and aircraft brokers agree the trade runs on trust, referrals, and relationships, not credentials: aircraft sales carry no FAA license and no training path, while only Florida and California license yacht brokers. Entry means living in a yacht hub, taking any yachting job, and farming captains and managers for leads. On yachts, the street schedule pays 10% on the first $10M, 5% on the next $10M, and 2.5% per $10M after, split 60/40 toward the buyer's broker; a junior on a 20% house split nets $80K off a $1M commission. Aircraft commissions slide from about 10% on $50K planes to 0.5-1% on a $40M G550. Practitioners clash on whether 10% is fixed or negotiable, on going solo versus joining a big house, and on chasing mega-deals versus building volume pipelines.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner on this beat lands on the same foundation: the deal moves on trust, and trust compounds into referrals. Gerry Mendria calls integrity the key and says the business is strictly referral and repeat, warning that anyone whose main motive is commission would fit better in another business [5hRyQr7CMkA @ 00:00:17]. The Yacht Broker Masterclass instructor reduces it to one line, saying it really is a relationships business, and backs that up with a broker who visited an owner at home and surfaced a 70m build [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:13:10]. Wealth Pilot puts the same point in buyer terms: clients buy from people they trust and know, and the recurring money that follows a closed deal comes from management and charter commissions plus a resale roughly five years later [WWrDBEGo6gM @ 00:03:24]. On the aircraft side, GLADA chairman Phil Jordan stresses that aircraft sales has no training path and no regulation, which is why the association built an online course of 8 to 9 sections with quizzes and a certificate, plus an internal channel for planes not yet on the market and pre-buy openings [i36Xg_tsVAY @ 00:02:56]. Jordan's sharpest line on the gap: a haircut legally requires a license, while selling a multimillion-dollar jet does not, so reputation becomes the only credential a buyer can actually check.

The entry path is consistent across yacht and aircraft practitioners: get physically close to the asset and its owners before trying to sell it. The Yacht Broker Masterclass sets the first step as moving to a yacht hub such as Fort Lauderdale, Palma, or Antibes, taking any yachting-associated job even if no brokerage company hires you immediately, and networking with captains and yacht managers who become future lead sources [-7bW4uN98Hc @ 00:00:23]. David, a Northrop & Johnson broker formerly at Camper & Nicholsons, confirms what the houses actually screen for: connections, yacht knowledge, networking ability, and a sales record, with a degree nowhere on the list [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:03:08]. Gerry Mendria adds that boating experience was only a partial advantage, which fits the pattern of relationships outranking seamanship [5hRyQr7CMkA @ 00:00:46]. Aircraft entry is even lighter. Alex Bengoechea states that no FAA license is needed to broker aircraft, including at someone else's brokerage; some states require an aircraft dealer license that works like a business license with no test, Arizona and Washington add a surety bond, Florida only requires Department of Revenue registration, and the build-out is an LLC plus an EIN [p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:00:14]. The shared baseline is access, product fluency, and patience while the network matures into buyer and seller leads.

Nobody pretends the income is steady. Wealth Pilot states flatly that brokers do not earn a steady salary, pay for Monaco, Miami, and Fort Lauderdale boat shows out of pocket, earn $0 when a deal falls through, and can run 18 months with no deal before an 8-figure payday lands, calling it a feast or famine economy [WWrDBEGo6gM @ 00:01:07]. Gerry Mendria lived the seasonal version: working every single day from May until after Labor Day with no time for family or boating, then a winter that went dead, meaning no income coming in at all [5hRyQr7CMkA @ 00:02:44]. Tomic of W Yachts frames the cure as volume rather than whale hunting: ten $100k sales or twenty $50k sales can equal one $1M sale, and brokers who only chase the big fish fall into a feast-or-famine cycle that ends in burnout, because consistency trumps singularity [elBm9ZOqdsA @ 00:02:22]. David reaches the same verdict from inside a major house, calling yacht sales a high-risk, high-reward industry where the headline commission gets carved up long before it reaches a junior broker's account [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:15:06]. The working prerequisite every one of these operators implies is a cash cushion sized for long dry stretches and a pipeline deep enough that one collapsed deal does not end the year.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The yacht commission schedule the house-trained brokers quote is tiered: 10% on the first $10M, 5% on the next $10M, and 2.5% on each $10M after that, so a $30M yacht carries a potential $1.75M commission [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:05:18]. The Yacht Broker Masterclass instructor runs the same ladder on a $100M yacht: $1M on the first $10M, $500,000 on the second, then $250,000 per additional $10M [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:05:41]. That commission is then split 60% to the buyer's broker and 40% to the seller's broker, so on a $1M commission a broker on a 50/50 house split keeps $300,000 representing the buyer or $200,000 representing the seller [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:07:06]. David's worked example shows how fast the gross shrinks in practice. A yacht asking $30M sells at $25M for a $1.625M commission, but the seller insists on $24M net, which drops the commission to $1M. David defends the 60/40 tilt bluntly, since the buyer's broker can always steer the client to another yacht. After the co-broker takes that share, the house keeps $400,000; a junior broker on 20% walks away with $80,000, while a senior broker on a 50/50 split with no wage clears about $200,000 from the same sale [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:13:22]. The lesson in the arithmetic: the headline percentage is never the check.

Pay structure is where new brokers get burned or protected. The Yacht Broker Masterclass instructor lays out the menu: apprentices may get a small basic wage with a lower split, trainee splits run 30/70 or 20/80, the instructor's own first employer paid 20%, and independent contractors get 50/50 but fund their own flights and hotels, the example being trips to the Exumas. The trap is the clawback, where 2,000 to 3,000 a month in pay is treated as an advance against commission and recovered out of the first sale, so it has to be clarified before signing [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:03:06]. New builds pay differently: Lurssen and Amels deals usually pay about 5% rather than 10%, and Benetti ratchets the fee from introduction only, to introduction plus contract, to full commission for brokers who make regular shipyard visits [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:11:20]. David pegs junior house packages at a small wage plus roughly 10 to 20% of the house commission, with office space, cards, boat-show access, and a shared assistant, and notes that a higher wage always means a lower percentage [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:07:30]. Charter runs in its own lane: Behind the Gold reports charter brokers earn up to 20%, on superyachts whose running costs sit around 10% of value per year, so a EUR 100M yacht burns roughly EUR 10M annually [LBp3Cz4zFQI @ 00:01:22].

Aircraft brokerage runs on a tighter cost stack and a sliding fee. Alex Bengoechea prices the toolkit line by line: JetNet at $750 a month on a 12-month contract, Controller ads at $600 to $2,000, and a photographer at $1,500 to $2,000 or about $500 for a budget shooter, with the rule to buy subscriptions only when a deal needs them and to start in piston aircraft, which are easier to enter than jets that demand cash reserves [p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:04:13]. Commission slides with price: about 10% on a roughly $50,000 aircraft, about 6% on a $500,000 Navajo or a $1M aircraft, and 1% or 0.5% on a $40M G550, and Bengoechea always pushes for an exclusive over a non-exclusive agreement [p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:17:26]. Phil Jordan's GLADA playbook front-loads the deal team: an aviation tax adviser, finance, an escrow agent and escrow agreement, and a broker contract, plus a deposit or retainer on acquisitions and some sales so the broker is not working for free [i36Xg_tsVAY @ 00:18:24]. Jordan also warns that a general CPA lacks aviation tax knowledge; the host cited a $17,000 tax bill from a state the owner does not even live in, and when a client pushes back, the fix is having the client's own accountant call the aviation specialist directly [i36Xg_tsVAY @ 00:21:44].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Plant Yourself in a Yacht Hub
   - Relocate to Fort Lauderdale, Palma, or Antibes and take any yachting-associated job.
   - Brokerage work may not come immediately, but a yachting job puts you next to captains and yacht managers who later feed buyer and seller leads [Yacht Broker Masterclass — -7bW4uN98Hc @ 00:00:45].
2. Negotiate the Pay Structure Up Front
   - Choose between a wage and a higher split, and pin down any clawback before signing.
   - Gerry Mendria refused any wage in exchange for a higher commission percentage and hit a two-year profitability target in 6 months [Gerry Mendria — 5hRyQr7CMkA @ 00:01:32]; clarify whether 2,000 to 3,000 a month is an advance recovered from the first sale [Yacht Broker Masterclass — _Shd1I2mefI @ 00:03:06].
3. Screen Every Buyer Before Showing
   - Run Google searches, collect proof of funds, and complete KYC and AML checks.
   - David screens buyers with Google, proof of funds, KYC, and AML, then checks names on World-Check before access to the yacht [Yachts For Sale — FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:28:46].
4. Close Through Escrow and Binding Paper
   - Move from non-binding LOI to binding APA with a lawyer on retainer, and close through escrow.
   - Bengoechea always uses escrow via AIC Title or Aerospace Reports, usually splitting the fee, and says there is a way without escrow but does not recommend it [Alex Bengoechea — p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:21:23].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest fault line is whether the 10% headline is law or opening bid. David of Yachts For Sale, a Northrop & Johnson broker, works the classic tiered schedule as standard: 10% on the first $10M, 5% on the second $10M, 2.5% per $10M after that, so a $30M yacht carries a potential $1.75M commission [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:05:18]. Yacht Broker Masterclass runs the same ladder and the same 60/40 split favoring the buyer's broker, while admitting brokers often need flexibility on commission [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:05:17]. Current Yachts attacks the premise head-on. Amanda Haley and Maryline Bossar say the 10% is mostly un-negotiated, that it sometimes gets negotiated to 8%, and that fixed commissions risk being price setting; their stance is blunt: it is not set at 10% [wqPY1eV4vuQ @ 00:21:38]. They let the seller choose whether to co-broke at all, recommending at least 4% compensation but allowing two, three, four or eight. On the aircraft side Alex Bengoechea treats the percentage as a sliding function of ticket size: about 10% on a $50K airplane, roughly 6% on a $500K Navajo or $1M aircraft, and 1% or 0.5% on a $40M G550 [p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:17:26]. Operator takeaway: quote the schedule, but know which camp your counterparty sits in before you defend it.

Pay structure splits practitioners into two camps. Gerry Mendria refused any wage outright in exchange for a higher percentage of the commission, called it win-win, took over and renovated an office facing a competitor, and turned a two-year profitability projection into six months [5hRyQr7CMkA @ 00:01:32]. Yacht Broker Masterclass maps the safer path: apprentices take a small basic wage at a lower split, trainees run 30/70 or 20/80, and the speaker's own first employer kept 80 while they got 20; independent contractors jump to 50/50 but fund their own flights and hotels down to the Exumas [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:04:37]. David at Yachts For Sale prices the same trade-off from the junior seat: a small wage plus about 10 to 20% of house commission, office space, cards, boat-show access and a shared assistant, with the rule that a higher wage means a lower percentage. On going solo, David draws a hard line: it works if you hold a buyer network, but seller-side brokers need a large house for credibility, websites, legal support and show access [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:16:31]. Current Yachts sidesteps the debate entirely, shifting from a commission-based to a service-based model with a $1,500 listing prep and promotion fee [wqPY1eV4vuQ @ 00:30:02].

Entry and sourcing playbooks diverge just as hard. Yacht Broker Masterclass insists you physically relocate to Fort Lauderdale, Palma or Antibes, take any yachting-associated job, and network with captains and yacht managers until buyer and seller leads surface through patience and tenacity [-7bW4uN98Hc @ 00:00:23]. Alex Bengoechea runs aircraft prospecting from a desk: the public FAA owner database, skip tracing, cold calls and postcards, plus Facebook Marketplace, which they say works less well now [p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:16:17]. Ali Merchant on the IABI podcast says no standard sourcing software exists for charter; they built a private roster of 2,500+ operators spanning owners, central agents and captain-managers covering 40 to 400 ft yachts [Oxw138mRpnA @ 00:07:39]. Europe demands 3-day minimums while the US caps charters at 13 guests. Deal-size strategy splits too. Tomic of W Yachts argues consistency trumps singularity: 10 sales at $100k or 20 at $50k can match one $1M sale, and chasing only the big fish breeds a feast-or-famine cycle and burnout [elBm9ZOqdsA @ 00:02:22]. On a 5% kept commission, €20M in volume equals €1M, reachable as 2 x €10M, 4 x €5M, 10 x €2M or 20 x €1M. Pick the playbook that matches your geography, capital reserves, network depth and tolerance for long dry months between closes.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most-reported trap is cash flow. Gerry Mendria worked every single day from May until after Labor Day with no time for family or boating, then watched winter go dead, meaning no income coming in, which pushed them into an online business for residual income [5hRyQr7CMkA @ 00:02:44]. Wealth Pilot puts the same risk in plain numbers: brokers do not earn a steady salary, boat shows in Monaco, Miami and Fort Lauderdale come out of pocket, pay is $0 when a deal falls through, and a broker can go 18 months with no deal before an 8-figure close, which they call a feast or famine economy [WWrDBEGo6gM @ 00:02:09]. David at Yachts For Sale confesses the classic rookie blowout: after a first big sale, a top-100 yacht under NDA, they overspent on a car and hotels; the fix is to treat every commission as your last [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:23:00]. Yacht Broker Masterclass flags the hidden version of the same problem: a clawback where 2 to 3,000 a month in pay is really an advance against commission, recovered from your first sale, so clarify it before signing [_Shd1I2mefI @ 00:03:06]. Budget for dead winter months and failed deals before you budget for the next yacht show.

Overpricing is the second killer. David pitched a Monaco owner against five brokers, advising an ask of 30, serious attention to offers over 25, and an expected landing near 27; rivals promised 35 and a 33 sale, won the listing, and the yacht sat two years, was cut to 27, and sold for less [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:25:05]. Phil Jordan of GLADA reaches the same verdict from aircraft: the first offer is one of your best, and a listing that sits becomes a stinky fish that ends in a desperation sale [i36Xg_tsVAY @ 00:15:59]. Current Yachts lists the seller-side errors behind stale inventory: emotional pricing, upgrades that never return their cost, and holding costs like insurance, hull cleaning and washing while boats take four to six months to sell once listed [wqPY1eV4vuQ @ 00:27:05]. They also catch listing misrepresentation in the wild: a spec saying Volvo when the engine was a Yanmar, and Garmin units swapped for Raymarine. Jordan's own red flags for aircraft are a spec sheet with no registration or serial number and a middleman markup, illustrated as $650 versus $750. David's market rule closes it out: a boat a year on market and still unsold means the market is telling you something.

Deal mechanics fail where controls are skipped. Current Yachts reports about one in ten deals reject after survey; a $25K generator problem found at survey ends in an allowance, an escrow holdback or rejection, so the 10% deposit goes into trust before anything else [wqPY1eV4vuQ @ 00:55:01]. David keeps the seller off the yacht during showings because of cut-out risk, screens buyers through Google, proof of funds, KYC, AML and World-Check, and insists the business relies on cast iron, watertight contracts [FpmvbD9-7yc @ 00:33:11]. The IYBA broker panel shows what happens without them: one broker cut commission 10% for VAT in a side letter while a second badgered the client after contract execution; nobody sues, the business runs on trust, and a Florida tortious interference claim was not pursued because both brokers were in the Med [RPROcS6hZSE @ 00:09:41]. The panel also rules that commission is earned at contract plus deposit, and that an option holds only 48 hours unless a contract and deposit follow. Alex Bengoechea warns the aircraft market is unregulated, says the LOI is non-binding while the APA binds, keeps a lawyer on retainer, and always uses escrow [p6XXGAWrnB8 @ 00:21:23]. Every one of these failures traces back to a missing document, a missing deposit or a missing screening check.
