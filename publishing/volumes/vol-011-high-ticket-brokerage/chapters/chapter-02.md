# Chapter 02: Choosing Your Role: Business Broker, M&A Advisor, or Investment Banker

*Main Street versus lower-middle-market transactions, exit planners, buy-side and sell-side responsibilities, acquisition decisions, intermediary fees, and IBBA versus M&A Source memberships*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners split the intermediary ladder by deal size and buyer pool. Main Street business brokers sell owner-operated companies, mostly under $1 million in SDE or under $2 million to $5 million in value, to individual buyers through web listings, real-estate-style licensing and 10% to 15% success fees with nothing upfront. M&A advisors take over from roughly $5 million in value or $1 million-plus EBITDA, running structured auctions to private equity and strategic buyers, charging $5,000 to $25,000 upfront plus double Lehman success fees of 10-8-6-4-2. Investment bankers enter near $100 million with monthly retainers and $1 million to $2 million minimum success fees. Brokers lean on listing volume against a roughly 20% national close rate, while hybrid operators like Trent Lee and Michael Wildeveld import M&A-grade underwriting, CRM automation and proprietary buyer databases into Main Street deals.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who drew the map drew it by deal size first, then by buyer type. Michael Wildeveld of Exit On Your Terms, who started as a business broker before going hybrid, places the Main Street broker on owner-operated restaurants, salons and convenience stores where seller's discretionary income runs up to $500,000 to $700,000 and usually stays under $1 million, typically closed as an asset sale rather than a stock purchase [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:00:49]. Nate Grant of CompassPoint sets the same floor: a couple hundred thousand up to half a million in earnings, on roughly $1 million in revenue, is where business brokers are best suited to sell [2KA0GQ8fBkc @ 00:00:20]. RareBrain draws the broker ceiling at $5 million in business value and opens the M&A advisor lane from a $5 million transaction size up to about $150 million [ntmKBtSOxhU @ 00:01:06]. Justin Goodbread runs the identical price-tag test: under $5 million points to a broker, $5 million to $30 million points to an M&A advisor, and above $30 million the seller may still use an M&A advisor or bring in an investment banker depending on circumstances [gDql0VQct6A @ 00:14:19]. The operator takeaway is blunt: the size band you can credibly serve decides which title you should be wearing, because the client pool, the fee model and the buyer universe all follow directly from it.

Buyer pool is the second hard line, and the speakers agree on it almost word for word. Gary Weinmann of American Business Intermediaries says sell-side M&A advisors typically market only to public companies, large private companies and private equity firms and rarely market to an individual buyer, while business brokers typically only market to individuals [LRGkOAsiUdQ @ 00:03:22]. Ryan C. Winter lists the broker's buyer mix as first-time owners, career changers, employees, competitors and private investors, versus an M&A advisor who takes fewer engagements, goes deeper on financial analysis and reaches private equity groups and corporate buyers directly through a formal bid process that puts them in competition [If4qHRijbWg @ 00:00:18]. Gill Agency adds that M&A advisors carry relationships with strategic buyers, private equity firms, family offices and holding companies, and that the yardstick flips from SDE on the broker side to EBITDA on the M&A side [UdqBDl4moqs @ 00:00:46]. Wildeveld supplies the sharpest warning for anyone positioning as an M&A advisor: below roughly $3 million to $5 million in EBITDA most private equity groups are not interested, so an advisor whose only playbook is the structured auction cannot create a market where none exists [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:03:26]. Pick the role by the buyers you can actually reach, not by the title that sounds bigger.

Licensing and skill floors complete the baseline. Wildeveld counts real-estate licensing for business brokers in roughly 17 to 20 states, naming California, Nevada, Arizona, Hawaii and Florida, while M&A advisors may carry real-estate broker licenses or FINRA registration under SEC regulation, and investment bankers are certainly FINRA-licensed with a far heavier expense structure [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:07:13]. Goodbread flags the gray area in between: depending on transaction structure, many lower middle market advisors are not required to be licensed at all, so an M&A advisor answering no to the license question is not automatically operating out of bounds [gDql0VQct6A @ 00:08:17]. Trent Lee describes the actual broker job as reading financials like a quasi-CPA, carrying enough legal background to fill in templated contracts, and handling marketing, negotiation and sales, which is exactly why nobody walks into it straight out of college [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:02:08]. Lee's entry rule is just as hard: do not even think about the industry until you have that baseline experience plus 12 to 18 months of runway to go with no income, still support your family, and avoid getting desperate enough to do anything stupid along the way [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:16:30]. Every rung on this ladder rewards the operator who shows up licensed, credentialed and capitalized before the first listing appointment ever lands on the calendar.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Fee architecture is where the three roles separate in hard cash. Nate Grant of CompassPoint calls 10% at close the standard broker fee on companies priced under $1 million, with no upfront money because most states regulate brokerage through the department of real estate, which bars upfront charges unless the broker delivers something beyond normal listing services, such as an executed marketing plan or a valuation from an accredited appraiser [bjJIoxsFNY8 @ 00:00:19]. Grant's M&A tier adds an upfront fee and a double Lehman success fee, 10% of the first million, 8% of the second, 6% of the third, then 4% and 2% on the remainder, with contingent notes and earnouts on bigger deals reopening the fight over what gets paid and when [bjJIoxsFNY8 @ 00:01:33]. Wildeveld defends M&A retainers and monthly work fees as infrastructure coverage for offices, payroll and industry subscriptions that run $15,000 to $25,000 apiece and over $100,000 a year for the firm [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:05:49]. Francis Walsh of The Digital Merchant prices those rarely published M&A upfront fees at $15,000 to $25,000, usually deducted from the success fee at closing but never refunded if the business fails to sell [NTugAc75tOg @ 00:04:28]. The broker eats the timing risk on contingency; the advisor bills part of that risk to the client up front.

Marketing mechanics follow the same fault line. Gary Weinmann says most business brokers list on web sites, wait for the inbound call and deploy little or no outbound marketing, against a nationwide broker success rate of about 20%, which is why the winning broker model is to list as many businesses as possible [LRGkOAsiUdQ @ 00:04:06]. Grant supplies the capacity math behind that volume: a solo broker typically needs seven to ten listings at a minimum to make a living, leaving little time per client and pushing marketing toward one-page flyers or fill-in-the-blanks templates on the web [2KA0GQ8fBkc @ 00:00:39]. Wildeveld's counter-model for deals under about $3 million in EBITDA is proprietary reach: a 40,000-person database of individual buyers and a 4,000-person family office database that sit completely off the M&A and investment-banking radar [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:09:21]. Luke Fox, formerly in private equity, lays out the sell-side auction the M&A tier runs instead: define the process, attach an early value, select a buyer universe, collect non-disclosure agreements, send a polished information memorandum, open a data room, then pull first-round bids before narrowing the field [7ABdy0hf9u8 @ 00:02:49]. Each model is a different machine, and the operator has to staff, finance and feed whichever one gets chosen.

Trent Lee, who has closed more than 600 transactions mostly on Main Street, built a niche by importing M&A-grade pitch decks, valuation, systems, CRM, outreach, automation and AI into small deals [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:06:08]. Before a listing goes live, Lee builds a personal credit memo, runs DSCR and factors in the buyer's livable wage, backed by certification as a business appraiser and SBA-underwriting training, so the price and the deal structure survive a lender and not just the seller's retirement number [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:08:53]. Buyer screening is just as hard-nosed: an unsigned inquiry from a throwaway Hotmail address gets judged on sight, and callers are tested on whether they know recast, EBITDA versus SDE, add-backs and DSCR before they earn real attention [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:13:48]. Winter hands sellers the mirror-image filter for vetting any intermediary: a price quoted before anyone has seen the returns is a sales pitch, not a valuation, so ask how the firm will value the business, how it will find and screen buyers, and how often it will report [If4qHRijbWg @ 00:01:59]. For a new operator, those two checklists are effectively the job description of a credible intermediary, whether the business card says broker, M&A advisor or something grander.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Size the deal and pick the lane
   - Run the price-tag test and the buyer-type test before choosing which title to operate under.
   - Over $100 million in sales price points to an investment banker, while an individual mom-and-pop buyer not using corporate money points to a broker and buying syndicates, hedge funds or international buyers point to M&A advisors or investment bankers [Justin Goodbread — gDql0VQct6A @ 00:14:47].
2. Lock the license and the credentials
   - Secure the state license your lane requires and add valuation credentials before the first listing.
   - Licensed states such as California, Nevada, Arizona, Hawaii and Florida require broker-side licensing [Exit On Your Terms — Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:07:13], and Trent Lee got certified as a business appraiser and trained as an SBA underwriter before selling a first business [Beau Eckstein, Business Ownership Coach — svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:08:37].
3. Pre-underwrite every listing
   - Build the lender's view of the deal before the business goes to market.
   - Draft your own credit memo, run DSCR and factor in the buyer's livable wage, then align price with deal structure so the listing is actually marketable rather than priced to the seller's retirement wish [Beau Eckstein, Business Ownership Coach — svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:08:53].
4. Publish the fee waterfall and build the buyer channel
   - Set a tiered success fee and pair it with proprietary buyer lists, not just inbound web listings.
   - Run 10% of the first million, 8% of the second and 6% of the third on a waterfall that steps down with deal size [Exit On Your Terms — Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:05:08], and feed listings into owned databases of individual buyers and family offices [Exit On Your Terms — Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:09:21].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The size lines that decide which seat you occupy do not match from desk to desk. RareBrain [ntmKBtSOxhU @ 00:00:47] puts business brokers on transactions under $5 million in business value at ten to twelve percent commissions, and hands everything from $5 million to roughly $150 million to M&A advisors. The Digital Merchant [NTugAc75tOg @ 00:00:50] draws the broker ceiling much lower, at deals worth less than $2 million, with M&A advisors working seven, eight, and nine figure sales, while admitting some brokerages run well past $2 million and some advisors take smaller deals. Gill Agency [UdqBDl4moqs @ 00:00:18] measures on revenue instead of price: brokers handle Main Street companies at $5 million in revenue or lower, and M&A advisors take $5 million to $50 million or higher. Justin Goodbread [gDql0VQct6A @ 00:15:18], who otherwise sorts the three players by transaction size, flags the overlap directly: a seller in the five, six, or seven million dollar range who thinks the deal is simple may still need an M&A firm, so the size of the business should not dictate the hire. The working takeaway is that no two practitioners publish the same cutoff, so buyer type and complexity settle the borderline cases.

Fee math is where the desks split hardest, and it shapes how each role gets paid. CompassPointCapital [bjJIoxsFNY8 @ 00:01:33] calls the double Lehman the industry standard for M&A advisors: 10 percent of the first million, 8 of the second, 6 of the third, then 4 and 2 on the remainder. RareBrain [ntmKBtSOxhU @ 00:02:06] teaches a different double Lehman, 10 percent of the first million, 9 of the second, 8 of the third, averaging out to roughly four to five percent on a $20 million deal. The Digital Merchant [NTugAc75tOg @ 00:03:56] describes broker schedules starting far higher, an illustrative 15 percent on the first million, 10 on the second, and 7 after that. The upfront-fee question splits the same way. Ryan C. Winter [If4qHRijbWg @ 00:02:16] charges a pure success fee at closing, nothing upfront, landing between 8 and 15 percent depending on deal size, while CompassPointCapital says the M&A tier earns its upfront fee because custom marketing plans, mailings, calls, and travel put more cost into executing the plan. RareBrain counters the sticker shock directly: an M&A advisor can work a year of their life for free with no guarantee. Choose a role and you are choosing which of these schedules your clients will accept.

The sharpest split is over how much selling a broker actually does and whether buy-side work pays. American Business Intermediaries [LRGkOAsiUdQ @ 00:04:06] describes brokers listing many businesses on websites and waiting for the inbound call, deploying very little outbound marketing. The Vant Group [fAVVcCIVQeE @ 00:00:53] calls that picture a misconception, rejecting the broker who puts two pages together, posts them on a website, and does minimum work, and claims to rival investment bankers by bringing white glove treatment to Main Street. Exit On Your Terms [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:09:07] says that below roughly $3 million in EBITDA buyers must be reached directly, which is why it keeps a 40,000-person individual buyer database and 4,000 family offices that sit completely off the radar of M&A advisors and investment bankers. On the buy side, Trent Lee, interviewed on Beau Eckstein's channel [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:25:00], says representing buyers to go find a business is not worth it without a central MLS, so buyers in a free school community source their own deal, then pay for representation because the listing broker hardly ever co-ops. The operator choice is whether your role waits, markets, or builds a buyer list before the first listing ever goes live.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive mistake Exit On Your Terms [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:06:16] reports is a seller too small for the advisor's marketplace paying upfront fees anyway, because the advisor still benefits from those fees and may take the client while not being geared to deliver the outcome; from the mid market up to investment banks that can mean $150,000 to $250,000 plus just to get going. The same firm warns that shops used only to structured auctions fail companies under $3 million to $5 million in EBITDA because most private equity groups will not be interested, and that putting a category A business in front of a category C advisor is shooting yourself in the foot. The Digital Merchant [NTugAc75tOg @ 00:04:45] flags the sibling trap: an M&A upfront fee in the $15,000 to $25,000 range that is not refundable if the business never sells, unlike the no-win, no-fee broker arrangement. CompassPointCapital [bjJIoxsFNY8 @ 00:02:11] puts investment-banker retainers at $5,000 to $10,000 a month with success-fee minimums of $1 million to $2 million at large banks. Justin Goodbread [gDql0VQct6A @ 00:04:00] notes even brokers sometimes charge an upfront fee when the seller has a mess that needs positioning.

The broker economics check is brutal. American Business Intermediaries [LRGkOAsiUdQ @ 00:04:28] puts the nationwide success rate for selling through a business broker at about 20 percent, which is why the surviving model is to list as many businesses as possible because many will not sell. Trent Lee [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:07:02], with over 600 transactions closed, blames two parties for the unsold pile: the broker who mispriced the listing so it fails basic market multiples and DSCR, and the seller who built a business dependent on the owner. Trent Lee [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:30:32] estimates about 50 percent of listings will not qualify for an SBA loan, and the number one reason is sellers being dishonest on their tax returns; the math is that $100,000 of unreported income or stuffed personal expenses at a 28 percent bracket saves $28,000 in tax but costs $350,000 in valuation at a three and a half multiple. CompassPointCapital [2KA0GQ8fBkc @ 00:00:39] adds the capacity constraint: a solo broker needs seven to ten listings at a minimum to make a living, which leaves little time on each client and pushes marketing toward one-page flyers and fill-in-the-blanks templates. Price it wrong or carry dirty books and the listing joins the unsold majority.

The ramp into the role itself is a failure point. Trent Lee [svRg7_BrwXY @ 00:16:30] tells would-be brokers not to enter until they have basic experience plus 12 to 18 months of runway to go with no income without getting desperate, and says a 24-year-old lacks the credibility to guide a 65-year-old putting an entire retirement on the sale; Trent Lee got certified in business appraisal and trained as an SBA underwriter before the first deal. Luke Fox [7ABdy0hf9u8 @ 00:04:58] reports sell-side work running 100-plus hour weeks under time pressure with lawyers and stakeholders scrutinizing every step, and says bids must be screened for whether the bidder can actually complete or will fall over at the last minute because they cannot raise the money. On the buy side, POTOMAC M&A [-mNiPaYyDro @ 00:09:34] recounts Jennifer, a SaaS founder who bought a competitor 70 percent of her size, expected the legacy customers to migrate, and instead ended up running two separate businesses with a culture clash, a living dumpster fire that ate a couple of years of management time. Objective, Investment Banking & Valuation [Ru1cmBnC5Rc @ 00:00:51] backs the warning: companies that acquire for the sake of acquiring are not successful.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Checks Before You Sign an Engagement or Take a Listing**
> 
> Each check traces to a failure a practitioner watched happen firsthand; clear all four before the engagement letter goes out.
> - Confirm the company fits the buyer pool you can reach: under $3 million to $5 million in EBITDA most private equity groups will not be interested, so a structured auction alone will not sell it [Exit On Your Terms — Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:03:26].
