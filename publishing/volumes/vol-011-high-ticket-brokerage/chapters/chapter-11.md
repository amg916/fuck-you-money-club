## Executive Summary

Practitioners in four specialist lanes converge on one rule: price the paper, not the pitch. Digital operators favor specialist brokers like Quiet Light, which claims to reject 90% of applicants and charges 10% of the first $1 million, over open marketplaces like Flippa, where unserious buyers and scammers roam. FE International closes 94% of listings but publishes no upfront pricing and bills buyers up to $1,000. Tower Genius pegs cell tower buyouts at 16 to 18 times annual rent depending on a right of first refusal, while SiteBid favors perpetual easements for bigger checks and 1031 treatment. Data center teams start with a utility load ramp, and Andrew Dubinsky warns 6418 credit buyers carry the IRS risk. Disagreements center on Quiet Light: Francis Walsh is a huge fan, while Colin Matcha found its team distant and cold.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Across the digital-business side, the loudest agreement is that specialization beats generalists once a deal gets serious. Chris, a long-time buyer who has worked with franchise and independent brokers, says the single lesson they learned without doubt is to go with a specialized broker rather than one that lists pizza shops, liquor stores, and auto service in the same book [NSNpSgvzAyA @ 00:00:05]. Francis Walsh of The Digital Merchant lands in the same place, saying they are a big fan of specialization and steering non-SaaS sellers away from Acquire.com because it is built around SaaS startup assets [yd4nlCQanq4 @ 00:09:17]. The market splits into three operating models. Full-service brokers such as FE International and Quiet Light find buyers for you, gate access, and broker the deal; open platforms such as Flippa let buyers and sellers deal directly with little vetting; and acquirers such as Onfolio buy the business outright, but only above $500,000 a year in profit [yd4nlCQanq4 @ 00:10:32]. Colin Matcha, an SEO operator of seven years who brokers digital assets for clients, frames the buy side the same way: you will end up shopping both brokerages and marketplaces, but only the brokerage model gives you a dedicated broker, a prospectus, and a gatekeeper between you and the seller [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:03:04].

The second consensus point: curation is the product, and the broker works for the seller. Colin Matcha reports Quiet Light claims to reject 90% of the sellers who want to list, which is why they rate its listings the highest quality in the space [l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:09:58]. Business Solution describes Empire Flippers only accepting established businesses clearing roughly $1,500 to $2,000 a month in net profit over the trailing 12 months, with every listing reviewed before it goes live [zcYOmZ8cAGE @ 00:00:55]. Chris adds that Quiet Light pre-vets its buyer list too, running NDAs and proof of funds before anyone sees confidential data, and that the buyer pool is already networked: individual tech investors, search fund operators, family offices, and smaller private equity groups [NSNpSgvzAyA @ 00:08:27]. The flip side, which Colin Matcha hammers on both reviews, is that these firms represent the seller and are paid a percentage of the sale price, so every extra dollar of price is extra commission for them; at FE International that cut is 15%, and the brokers 'are not really on your side' when you negotiate [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:14:04]. Operators who internalize that alignment treat the broker as the seller's agent, use them for information flow, and run their own diligence regardless of how curated the feed looks.

In the hard-asset and tax-credit lanes the consensus is the same instinct applied to different paper: the document behind the asset is what gets priced. On cell towers, Mike Travis of Nexus Towers says property owners misread the deal: a tower easement sells only the specific area the tower uses, not the building or rooftop, and works like a deed that lets the buyer securitize that little section while the owner keeps everything else [YjzN5Uo5yf8 @ 00:00:14]. On data centers, Data Center Build Academy says power is factor number one 'and it's not close', so the first call goes to the utility rather than a real estate broker [8UjhKryCNbQ @ 00:00:44], and Reed Smith calls 'powered land' a marketing term because a utility letter of intent is very different from a signed agreement [HXjpQWcZ6F8 @ 00:00:30]. On transferable tax credits, Andrew Dubinsky stresses that a Section 6418 credit is 'just an idea' until the project is placed in service and the IRS issues a registration number, and even then the number is a claim check that does not confer validity on the credit [h0qEAIoQ4Jo @ 00:08:50]. Every one of these practitioners prices the paper, not the pitch.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The fee math is where the platforms separate. Colin Matcha lays out Quiet Light's ladder: 10% of the first $1 million, so a $500,000 deal pays $50,000, and a $1.5 million deal pays $145,000 once the second million drops to 9%; they note FE International and Empire Flippers both charge 15% [l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:08:09]. Business Solution breaks down Empire Flippers' blended structure: a $7,000 minimum, 15% on the portion up to roughly $700,000, 8% between $700,000 and $5 million, and 2.5% above that, with nothing paid upfront to list [zcYOmZ8cAGE @ 00:01:36]. Flippa runs the opposite model, charging listing fees starting around $29 to $49 plus tiered success fees up to about 10%, higher if you buy its partner broker service, while Investors Club charges sellers nothing and monetizes buyer memberships instead [zcYOmZ8cAGE @ 00:04:38]. On the buy side, Colin Matcha flags FE International as the one brokerage charging buyers a fee, capped at $1,000, plus escrow.com costs including a $75 domain holding fee; pay by wire transfer for the smallest fee and skip credit cards because the points will not cover the difference [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:14:38]. Model these fees before listing, because Business Solution notes sellers bake commissions into asking prices.

On the buy side, Colin Matcha's workflow at FE International is concrete. Sign the NDA once, request the prospectus, and expect it within one to two business days; the PDF carries the P&L, analytics traffic, growth strategies, and seller Q&A [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:07:21]. Read the asterisk: listing figures are often annualized off the last three months, which Colin Matcha calls really short when six months is the usual floor. FE International generally will not hand over Google Analytics or Search Console access, so ask for specifics instead: the last 30 days versus the previous 30, year-on-year traffic, and the top 25 pages by traffic [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:09:08]. When a broker stalls, a non-binding letter of intent signals seriousness and tends to unlock more data and faster replies. At Quiet Light the NDA unlocks a business summary, a seller interview in video and audio, and a monthly P&L, and listings flag whether lenders are pre-approved or the deal is SBA eligible [l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:04:49]. Both firms keep the buyer off the negotiating table: offers go through the broker by email, and Quiet Light accepts all-cash offers at listing price by default [l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:05:41]. Price itself is a multiple of profit, which FE International quotes in years.

Tower and credit deals have their own street math. Tower Genius says buyout calculators do not exist, but gives a rule of thumb: with a right of first refusal in the lease, offers land around 16 to 17 times annual rent; without one, 17 to 18 times, and a second opinion can surface money left on the table [V2LGinURueI @ 00:01:03]. SiteBid Infrastructure ranks easement scopes: specific easements match the leased footprint and are least preferred because they block new tenants, general easements over a full rooftop draw the highest valuations and can carry a 50/50 revenue share, and expanded easements add roughly 500 square feet [fLebm5BppxY @ 00:01:04]. For data centers, Reed Smith says diligence starts with a load ramp from the utility stating megawatts and timing, such as one gigawatt in five years, which is valuable but not now [HXjpQWcZ6F8 @ 00:01:09]. Andrew Dubinsky's 6418 sequence is: place the project in service, register on the IRS energy credit portal, wait two weeks to two months for the registration number, sign the transfer agreement, then the buyer claims the credit on Form 3800 with that number while the seller reports the transfer [h0qEAIoQ4Jo @ 00:07:16].

   - Colin Matcha says Quiet Light is not great for acquisitions under $150,000 and points smaller buyers to Empire Flippers, Flippa, or smaller marketplaces, while rating FE International the best profitable-SaaS brokerage across all levels [Colin Matcha — l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:11:42].
2. Get the paper under NDA
   - Put an NDA on file, pull the prospectus or business summary, and log the seller interview and monthly P&L.

   - De-annualize three-month figures and request targeted traffic pulls instead of waiting for analytics access.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the digital-business lane is Quiet Light versus FE International, and practitioners who have worked both sides do not land in the same place. Francis Walsh of The Digital Merchant says Quiet Light gets the first call at the mid-market level, because FEI hides its pricing, serves the same revenue band, and does not cater to any segment better than its competitors [yd4nlCQanq4 @ 00:13:55]. Chris, shopping for online businesses from the buy side, backs that read: every Quiet Light advisor has started, bought, or sold an online business, a whole-org-chart model Chris never saw in years of dealing with franchise and independent brokers [NSNpSgvzAyA @ 00:03:25]. Colin Matcha pushes back on the relationship side, reporting that the Quiet Light team came across as distant and cold at a happy hour [l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:01:45]. Matcha credits FEI with in-person events and email follow-up no other online broker comes close to, and routes deals by asset class: profitable SaaS at any size goes to FEI, called probably the best profitable SaaS brokerage across all levels, while unprofitable SaaS goes to MicroAcquire [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:18:44]. The operator move is picking the house by asset class and deal size, not by brand reputation.

Curated versus open marketplace is the second fault line. Business Solution names Empire Flippers its top pick for a curated deal flow, but The Digital Merchant would still send a mid-market seller to Quiet Light over Empire, arguing the consultants carry better expertise, the vetting is less painful, and the value for money is stronger [yd4nlCQanq4 @ 00:11:52]. On Flippa the two channels agree on the facts and split on the verdict. The Digital Merchant says four- and five-figure sellers can do good business there, but anything more serious drowns in time-wasters, unserious buyers, and even scammers because buyer vetting is thin [yd4nlCQanq4 @ 00:08:33]. Business Solution frames the same openness as an edge: lots of deal flow, smaller listings bigger brokers ignore, and a good fit for buyers who like hunting deals and are happy to verify everything themselves [zcYOmZ8cAGE @ 00:05:43]. A third playbook flips the fee model entirely. Investors Club charges sellers no listing or success fee, so sellers keep 100% of proceeds, and monetizes through buyer memberships instead, backed by an exclusivity agreement that keeps the listing from being blasted everywhere [zcYOmZ8cAGE @ 00:07:04]. Choose the venue by whether you are paying with commission, membership dues, or your own diligence hours.

In cell tower deals the split is structure: keep the land clean or take the bigger check. SiteBid Infrastructure lays out the master lease as the simpler path, where a new lease is written with fresh terms and the current tenant becomes a sublease, keeping property rights intact but usually paying less because the buyer's control is limited [EsfznycDU6I @ 00:00:44]. The perpetual easement, often 99 years, pays a bigger upfront number, hands lease management to the buyer, and opens the door to a 1031 exchange into other real estate [EsfznycDU6I @ 00:01:30]. Mike Travis of Nexus Towers works the opposite angle for owners who hate the word easement: when a lender would give an owner a hard time over an easement shown on the property, Nexus has structured the same deal as a master lease so it records as a lease and never shows as an easement [YjzN5Uo5yf8 @ 00:01:35]. Pricing philosophy splits too. Tower Genius says no buyout calculator exists and every site is priced on location, tenant, and buyer appetite, then still gives a rule of thumb: annual rent times 16 or 17 with a right of first refusal in the lease, 17 or 18 without one [V2LGinURueI @ 00:01:03]. Treat that multiple as an opening anchor, not a ceiling.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first buyer-side trap is reading broker numbers at face value. Colin Matcha flags that FE International listing figures carry an asterisk: revenue and profit are annualized off the last three months, meaning those months multiplied by four, and Matcha calls three months really short when six is the usual floor [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:06:30]. On Quiet Light, the stale-data problem shows up differently: listings that sit longer than a month can carry a prospectus still showing August numbers in October, even though September is fully closed [l-9E6P1CSEc @ 00:10:33]. The second trap is forgetting who the broker works for. Matcha is blunt that brokers represent the seller and earn a cut of the sale price, 15% at FEI, so they are incentivized to sell for as much as possible and are not really on your side when you negotiate [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:14:20]. Then the fees leak at closing. FEI is the only brokerage Matcha knows that charges buyers a fee, capped at $1,000, and escrow.com adds a $75 domain holding fee plus a percentage fee by transaction size, with the smallest fees near 0.89 on a wire transfer; Matcha warns that credit card points will not make up the difference [r4fmjknWB7o @ 00:14:57].

Infrastructure buyers get burned by power promises. Data Center Build Academy opens with the nightmare: a company buys two hundred acres of flat, fairly priced land, calls the utility, and learns the needed power will not be available for six years, a scenario described as real and happening across the industry right now [8UjhKryCNbQ @ 00:00:00]. Reed Smith LLP treats the phrase itself as the trap, calling powered land a marketing term because power commitments come in very different forms, and a letter of intent is very different from a signed agreement; that has to be pinned down early, before clients burn time, money, and resources chasing a site with no contracted power [HXjpQWcZ6F8 @ 00:00:30]. The Reed Smith fix is contractual: off ramps and flexibility to terminate if power is not or cannot be contractually committed in the client's timeframe, plus walk-away or renegotiation rights if a moratorium blocks the build [HXjpQWcZ6F8 @ 00:02:17]. Zoning is the second killer. In the Academy's three-site case, Site C had power in two years but strong local opposition to rezoning, and the team passed because a rezoning delay can cost more than the land itself, while Site A's six-year power timeline made it almost worthless for now [8UjhKryCNbQ @ 00:04:59].

Tax credit buyers carry the downside after the wire. Andrew Dubinsky is explicit that once a 6418 credit is transferred, the buyer is liable for anything the IRS objects to and has to collect from the seller; Dubinsky pegs an IRS invalidation at about a one in 20,000 chance, but the buyer is effectively on the hook for the money anyway [h0qEAIoQ4Jo @ 00:05:27]. The pitch trap is the registration number. Dubinsky warns that a seller saying the IRS registered the credits means nothing, because the number is a tracking number, a claim check that confers no validity or blessing [h0qEAIoQ4Jo @ 00:08:50]. Supply is also less ready than it looks: most credits are not ready to transact when they hit the market, either too early, still under construction, or, on smaller credits, short on information for a comprehensive transaction, much like a house that needs work before listing [Z0ATgOouiIM @ 00:03:08]. On the tower side, the cost of going alone is money left behind. Tower Genius says every site must be looked at individually and a second opinion pays because owners can leave a little or a heck of a lot of money on the table against big wireless [V2LGinURueI @ 00:01:21].
