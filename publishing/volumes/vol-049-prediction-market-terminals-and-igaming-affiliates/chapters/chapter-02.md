## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the cross-venue trade is simple: buy YES on one prediction market and NO on another for a combined cost under $1, favoring Kalshi and Polymarket because peer-to-peer, fee-funded exchanges do not limit arbers the way sportsbooks do. The live disagreement is whether scanner gaps are real: OngPredicts traced every top gap they rechecked to a dead or already-moved venue price.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every arbitrage operator here runs the same core trade: find one event listed on two venues, buy YES on one and NO on the other, and confirm the two prices add up to less than $1, because exactly one leg settles at $1 and the gap is locked profit. Taf frames it with a 35-cent YES on Polymarket plus a 40-cent NO on Kalshi for 75 cents total, leaving a 25-cent spread that pays no matter which side resolves [GLvhRHB7n2U @ 00:02:45]. Juiced Bets runs the J.D. Vance illustration: YES at 60 on Kalshi, NO at 35 on Polymarket, 95 cents in for a guaranteed 100 cents back, a 5% return with zero view on the election [PaQjSUCZVb0 @ 00:03:05]. OngPredicts uses the same 40-plus-55 structure and stresses they do not care who wins as long as both legs cover every outcome and resolve the same way [to-RcU1_UCQ @ 00:01:13]. Scrape Atlas pitches 42 cents YES against 53 cents NO for 95 cents total and warns that platform take rates and likely payment processing fees must come out of that spread before it counts [OPiw3RZFInQ @ 00:01:54]. The shared prerequisite is blunt: price both legs, subtract fees, and only fire when the combined cost still clears under the $1 payout.

The second point of agreement is why arbers left sportsbooks. Taf says books clock arbitrage activity and limit accounts until you cannot bet anymore, which happened to Taf and many other bettors, while prediction markets carry no bans, no limits, and prices Taf calls extremely inefficient [GLvhRHB7n2U @ 00:00:50]. Clark Knows Ball explains the business model: Kalshi is peer-to-peer, you are filling another user's open order, and the exchange earns on every transaction, so it has no reason to cut a winner from hundreds or thousands per play down to five or ten dollars the way FanDuel or DraftKings do [ghCIWcXbadk @ 00:01:02]. Bet The Math says Kalshi and Polymarket never limit, never cut bet sizes, and actually love arbitrage bettors, so the strategy scales until it hits available liquidity, with arbs showing up both between two prediction markets and between a prediction market and a sportsbook [nOjpng5gNGo @ 00:07:14]. Juiced Bets confirms the platforms have no issue with arbitrage because they are not making money when you lose, only a fee on every trade [PaQjSUCZVb0 @ 00:00:46]. The caveat travels with the praise: that fee is the venue's revenue, so the no-limits pitch always arrives paired with fee math on each leg.

On live markets, the consensus is blunt: the retail viewer is the slowest node in the chain. Part Time Larry watched ESPN's Gamecast log a Walker touchdown while the TV stream still showed Seattle scoreless, cited a 5-to-15-second Gamecast lead over broadcast and a 15-to-30-second lag on free data versus pro feeds that might run $5,000 a month, and concluded that tapping bets on a phone mid-game gets you 'the worst odds you could possibly have' [bA_NUrMJuw4 @ 00:16:52]. Whale Slots puts consumer stream delay at 7, 10, even 30 seconds against bookmaker feeds piped over private satellite links and dedicated stadium fiber, so a goal can already be priced 15 to 20 seconds before the viewer even sees the breakaway [QpjzafRRmjQ @ 00:01:54]. OddsJam's live-betting host reinforces the speed tax from the arb side: live plays get pulled mid-click, 'boom it gets suspended,' and you will miss opportunities even with the play loaded and nothing done wrong [36K9qpRjKXQ @ 00:01:03]. Unabated's Jack frames the same gap as adverse selection: a resting offer in a close fourth quarter only gets filled after a big play turns it into a bad bet [rrKRhjye1sw @ 00:08:09]. Nobody in this group claims a phone-and-stream operator wins the live race on reaction speed alone.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Filter the scanner list
   - Set closing time under 30 days and liquidity above $100,000, then skip rows marked ended or showing a 0-cent side.
   - A 0-cent side usually means that venue has no real price at all, and the filter keeps cash from being tied up for months in thin venues [OngPredicts — T6thRudYjsg @ 00:01:48].
2. Verify both rule sets settle identically
   - Open the full rules on both venues and compare event, deadline, candidate, and resolution source.
   - For nomination markets, check how each venue handles a candidate dropping out, a replacement nominee, a delayed convention, or a cancellation; if the wording is unclear, skip the pair [Chen Trading — lYFPEQllQOw @ 00:04:33].
3. Size to the order book, not the headline
   - Enter your intended position size and watch average price, fees, and net profit update on both legs.
   - A deep book on one side does not fix a thin book on the other, and if the expected edge disappears at your intended size, pass on the trade [Chen Trading — lYFPEQllQOw @ 00:04:15].
4. Fire both legs and hedge any miss
   - Keep enough funds in both wallets, place both sides, then inspect fills before walking away.
   - If Polymarket fills and the Kalshi price moves before your second leg, the tool tells you how much to place on the Kalshi side to stay hedged rather than exposed to one outcome [Juiced Bets — PaQjSUCZVb0 @ 00:10:30].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split among these operators is whether a retail operator can win on speed at all. Part Time Larry wired the unofficial ESPN Game Cast feed into a Kalshi websocket bot and concluded that the professional algorithmic traders on Kalshi "probably have the fastest data feed and are moving first," confirming on a second game that Kalshi odds moved before his signal [bA_NUrMJuw4 @ 00:03:57]. His verdict is blunt: anyone betting off what they see on TV is "basically wasting their time," so either own the fastest data or a better pre-game model and stop competing on speed [bA_NUrMJuw4 @ 00:17:23]. Clark Knows Ball pitches the reverse thesis for live Kalshi action, arguing in-game opportunities get "even more sporadic" and bigger because you are up against real people in the heat of the moment, not FanDuel and DraftKings conglomerates running algorithms every second [ghCIWcXbadk @ 00:03:15]. Unabated's Jack cuts against that: Kalshi, Polymarket and others use institutional market makers whose algorithms and automated systems essentially act as the bookie, so "if you think it's always going to be you versus some bro on Robin Hood, you're mistaken." He does concede that prime-time screens fly by with recreational tickets mostly under $100, which is why big events can price slightly better than a sportsbook [rrKRhjye1sw @ 00:09:17].

The second fault line is what to do with capital once both legs are on. Juiced Bets refuses to sit in long-dated arbs: on a J.D. Vance election market he does not want money "tied up for the next 2 years," so he waits for Kalshi and Polymarket prices to cross over and cashes out both sides early, citing an aliens-before-2027 pair bought at 14 and 76 cents that crossed and closed for 10% a couple of hours later [PaQjSUCZVb0 @ 00:03:59]. He even rates a Taylor Swift top-Spotify-artist market that crossed roughly every two weeks as a fantastic candidate, because that overlap history makes another cross likely [PaQjSUCZVb0 @ 00:08:30]. Bet The Math's Alex runs the opposite rhythm: pair a Kalshi leg with a sportsbook leg on a single game, and "the second the game ends, you get your money back," which he calls daily compounding [nOjpng5gNGo @ 00:06:57]. OngPredicts enforces the same discipline mechanically, filtering closing time under 30 days and liquidity above $100,000 so cash is not locked for months and thin venues drop out [T6thRudYjsg @ 00:01:48]. Juiced Bets' bet is that these markets "are not efficient" and will keep crossing; the short-dated camp simply collects at the final whistle.

Tooling splits into build and buy camps. Odds Breaker, who tells viewers to stop paying hundreds a month for scanners, builds a free one on the Odds API's 500 free monthly requests with four commands to an AI coder, and it surfaced five live arbs including a 2.3% MMA margin [aZvtaObt8Gw @ 00:00:40]. Scrape Atlas builds the Kalshi-Polymarket monitor inside ChatGPT through the ScrapeAtlas MCP plugin and argues against stretching thin across 50 bets: find one deviating bet and "literally just spam it over" until it materializes [OPiw3RZFInQ @ 00:04:50]. He then pushes serious operators off ChatGPT onto a Mac Mini or a Hetzner server for a faster scraping clip, still routed through ScrapeAtlas because generic scrapers get blocked [OPiw3RZFInQ @ 00:05:03]. The buy camp runs the other way. OngPredicts pays $48.88 a month for Predictify because manual tab-hopping across Polymarket, Kalshi and Opinion means "doing the job of a software myself," yet he insists "I'm not using this to blindly click on every trade that shows up" — the scanner only nominates candidates for research [to-RcU1_UCQ @ 00:03:42]. His filter is simple: a fat spread with basically no liquidity gets skipped, while a smaller spread with real size is potentially more interesting.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Part Time Larry is candid: he ran the ESPN-to-Kalshi bot live during Seahawks versus 49ers and "lost money, but I'm showing it anyway" [bA_NUrMJuw4 @ 00:05:01]. The trade log shows why. His config simulated a YES buy on Seattle after a 3% win-probability spike on a Sam Darnold completion, with two exits — 95 cents or a time stop — and the position auto-exited at break even, where "maybe we even lost money on a commission" [bA_NUrMJuw4 @ 00:15:32]. He also polled ESPN with a 2-second delay to avoid rate limits, on a free feed that "might be 15 seconds, it might be 30 seconds" behind courtsiders and the Sportradar-partnered feeds he figures top Kalshi traders pay up to $5,000 a month for. Whale Slots frames the same trap from the bettor's chair: streams run 7, 10, even 30 seconds late, and a goal can hit the book's feed 15 to 20 seconds before you even see the breakaway [QpjzafRRmjQ @ 00:01:54]. They also flag latency arbitrage, where provider algorithms accept a delayed bet just as the odds shift against you or reject it outright, and their fix is to drop pure reaction betting because by the time you see it, the edge is probably gone [QpjzafRRmjQ @ 00:02:58].

Phantom spreads are the first trap scanner users report. OngPredicts opened a Dodgers World Series row showing 7 cents against 29 cents, a 22-cent gap, and found it was "really about a third of a cent" once the compare tab pulled live prices [T6thRudYjsg @ 00:02:51]. His rule: trust the venue with the most money traded and treat a price far from everyone else as a reason to check, not a reason to buy. Re-running three more top rows about four hours later went "three for three": every gap came from a venue with no live price or a price that had already moved [T6thRudYjsg @ 00:04:11]. Even real spreads shrink with size. Taf's AI-benchmark pair, 6 cents YES on Kalshi against 78 cents NO on Polymarket, returned about $31 at $205 to $220, and "the higher you put, you're actually going to make less money" because the book is not deep enough [GLvhRHB7n2U @ 00:09:16]. Chen Trading spells out the arithmetic: if the first ask is 2 cents with only 100 shares and you need 500, that level covers one-fifth of the order and the rest climbs the book or stays unfilled, so average every level before trusting the two-leg estimate [lYFPEQllQOw @ 00:03:36].

The last traps are contract mismatch, leg risk, idle cash and fees. Taf warns that markets "can be titled the same, they might resolve totally differently, and you will end up losing your money on one of the legs" [GLvhRHB7n2U @ 00:05:51]. Chen Trading's nomination checklist, covering a candidate dropping out, a replacement nominee, a delayed convention or a cancellation, exists because any uncovered case leaves a gap in exposure, and execution adds its own risk: one leg may fill while the other moves or only partially fills [lYFPEQllQOw @ 00:05:14]. His countermeasure is to set maximum total cost and size in advance, then inspect filled shares and average price after each order and recalculate the remaining leg. Odds Breaker names the logistics risk plainly: without cash already sitting in six betting apps ready to fire instantly, the lines can move against you pretty quickly [aZvtaObt8Gw @ 00:01:49]. Unabated's Jack ranks fees as the number-one caveat: a 3% commission can turn a price 5 cents better than DraftKings into a worse bet than you would have had at DraftKings [rrKRhjye1sw @ 00:10:27]. Fees may come off winning wagers, off takers only, or off makers and takers alike, and either way they have to be factored in.
