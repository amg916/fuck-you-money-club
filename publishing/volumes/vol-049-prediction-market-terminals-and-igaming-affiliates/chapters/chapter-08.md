## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus — Affiliates Are the Permanent Acquisition Layer and Relevance Beats Volume

Every operator-side and affiliate-side voice starts from the same premise: the affiliate channel is structural, not optional. Levon Nikoghosyan, CEO of AffPapa, says flatly that running the industry without affiliates is “impossible” because they hold the largest share of traffic [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:03:37]. CasinoRank+Insider puts hard numbers on it: over 74% of operators use affiliates, those partners bring in 30 to 50% of all new depositing players, and the channel is worth over $7B in 2025 [XU9tdzKAxdU @ 00:00:33]. The SlotCatalog team says the affiliate model will stay a large part of its revenue but is no longer enough on its own [GM2_yRmV_Bw @ 00:03:19]. Erica, VP of Marketing at Income Access, frames the whole game in one line, “It's a relationship built industry,” and the follow-up advice is to find what performs, then ask affiliates directly what they need from the operator [H4Pub14VDXA @ 00:03:49]. The operator takeaway is blunt. Affiliates are the acquisition backbone, but the partnership is a living, day-to-day relationship, not a tracking link handed over and forgotten. Erica names that exact pattern, setting affiliates up, handing them data, and never engaging on what isn't working, as the single biggest mistake operators make.

The second point of agreement is that raw volume is finished as a selling point; relevance and downstream value are the new currency. Nikoghosyan says tier-1 affiliates choose brands on traffic relevance, conversion, and fairness with no shaving, summed up as “It's about how relevant your traffic is to them” [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:07:57]. On the Affnook webinar, Joe Hatches argues an FTD is no longer the end of the funnel and that affiliates must own their data and attribution across the whole pipeline, while Brendan adds that big publishers now maximize value per FTD instead of pushing CPA volume [jE9PBJOzw_E @ 00:00:17]. Daniel, on the same panel, agrees with both and says regulation shifted attention to click-to-deposit drop-off, because it is “not... just a volume game” [jE9PBJOzw_E @ 00:01:39]. Erica reports the identical shift from the Income Access side: quality versus quantity is the hot topic, operators entering new markets do not want “mass traffic,” and they want partnerships balanced around “high value KPIs” [H4Pub14VDXA @ 00:00:43]. Put together, the consensus brief for an operator is simple. Buy relevance per GEO, measure past the first deposit, and judge every partner on what converts after the click rather than how many clicks arrive.

The baseline prerequisites for a partnership are just as consistent across speakers. Paul, CMO at Mr Gamble, works with licensed casinos only, tests deposits and landing pages as a player, checks forums for late payments, and treats localized payments as mandatory: Canada without Interac is a “no go” [R-_FHgwDEFQ @ 00:12:03]. Paul runs this screen across the UK, Canada, New Zealand, and the Nordics. Nikoghosyan's first red-flag check is operating history. Ask how long the brand has been live and confirm with other affiliates, because operators running only a couple of months can vanish [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:12:00]. On the Peaky Ads episode, Nikoghosyan lists why deals actually die: a mismatch on payments, localization, language, games, or odds, followed by term changes or shaving, and finally a plain lack of trust [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:08:51]. The sharpest single tell is an operator pitching “we work worldwide” with no defined GEO; those brands pay listing fees, fail to convert, and get delisted [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:15:02]. Stack those together and the shared checklist writes itself: a licensed brand, local payment rails, a track record other affiliates will vouch for, a defined GEO list, and terms that are never rewritten or shaved once the traffic starts flowing.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers — Vetting Scripts, Deal Pricing, GEO Segmentation & Execution Controls

The vetting script Paul runs at Mr Gamble is concrete. Ask the operator for its top 10 GEOs and its conversion numbers, then benchmark those figures against data on “100 thousand casinos” before committing a single click of traffic [R-_FHgwDEFQ @ 00:12:03]. The tool stack is deliberately basic; Paul names “Sim rush or offers” as the plain SEO tools in use at Mr Gamble [R-_FHgwDEFQ @ 00:15:34]. Nikoghosyan puts a price on skipping that homework: spending 10K to 20K on listings is wasted when the traffic is not relevant [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:16:36]. Segmentation by GEO is the next lever. Nikoghosyan tells affiliates to split traffic by GEO, naming Morocco, the US, and LatAm, because without it a business that should earn 100K a month ends up “earning like ten K and burning the other 90 K” [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:20:12]. The working sequence follows directly. Pull the operator's top-GEO and conversion data, benchmark it against an existing operator dataset, accept listing spend only where the GEO matches, and report results country by country so a leak shows up before it compounds. The 90K figure is what running that sequence loosely, without GEO splits, costs every month.

Deal pricing in the US runs hot and the figures conflict. The Christian Strutt channel quotes US sports-betting CPAs running “well up from $250” to as high as “$1,000,” hybrid deals included, for mobile-first, high-value players under very strict regulation [MKTk-Y-xt1I @ 00:02:12]. CasinoRank+Insider reports US sports CPAs “over $3000,” three times Strutt's ceiling, and lists LatAm as rising through Brazil and Mexico [XU9tdzKAxdU @ 00:01:27]. Daniel at Push House lays out the full menu for sports betting: CPA is “safest but not the most profitable,” CPL comes as SOI or DOI and is rare in the niche, CPS pays a preset percentage once per unique user after first deposit, RevShare pays on every deposit, and hybrids pair CPA with RevShare or CPL with CPA, with payouts scaling on traffic volume and quality [Qygo77RCGlw @ 00:03:12]. Nikoghosyan refuses to crown a winner: no CPA or rev-share model is best, the match is what matters, rev share is less risky for operators, and both models can be swindled with bonus-abuser traffic [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:04:43]. The practical rule is to price each deal to its traffic, not to a template, and to treat bonus-abuse exposure as a risk that no payout structure removes on its own.

Execution on the page and inside the ad account is where the margin is protected. For US state traffic, the Strutt playbook is to geo-target so only licensed books show in each state, using pages such as /NY and /NJ, with 18+ notices, gambling-aware links, RG messaging, and T&Cs in place [MKTk-Y-xt1I @ 00:03:51]. The attached warning is severe: showing a bonus in a state where it is not offered “will just completely kill it,” so state promo codes are mandatory [MKTk-Y-xt1I @ 00:04:46]. On paid traffic, Alexander Klosa of Pin-Up Partners runs no fixed 70/30 portfolio split; EPC is the trigger. When EPC shifts, ask the offer owner, slow or stop traffic, and move volume into tier-2 and tier-3 GEOs while tier 1 recovers [qOL8mMQPJyI @ 00:11:01]. Relationship mechanics are timed just as tightly. Paul books 30-minute meetings at conferences, keeps ad hoc ones to 10 to 15 minutes, expects affiliate managers to send memos after every meeting, and caps attendance at 3 to 4 conferences a year [R-_FHgwDEFQ @ 00:27:32]. Each of these is a small, repeatable control an operator or affiliate can install: per-state landing pages, state-specific codes, an EPC tripwire with a tier-2 fallback, and a written memo after every face-to-face meeting.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

There is no single correct deal structure, and the people living inside these contracts say so bluntly. Levon Nikoghosyan of AffPapa refuses to crown either CPA or revenue share: the right match between affiliate and brand is what matters, rev share carries less risk for the operator, and both models can be swindled by an affiliate pushing bonus-abuser traffic [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:04:43]. Daniel at Push House argues from the media buyer's seat instead, calling CPA the safest model but not the most profitable, with RevShare paying on every deposit and hybrids such as CPA plus RevShare or CPL plus CPA scaling with traffic volume and quality [Qygo77RCGlw @ 00:03:12]. The number fight gets sharper on US sports. Christian Strutt quotes CPAs running well up from $250 and reaching around $1,000, hybrid deals included, while pitching US players as mobile-first and high value [MKTk-Y-xt1I @ 00:02:12]. CasinoRank+Insider puts US sports CPAs over $3,000 in the same market and says some US casinos already pay over $300 per player [XU9tdzKAxdU @ 00:01:27]. Same country, same year, a threefold gap between two published ceilings: whichever figure lands on the table in a negotiation is one camp's number, and the other camp's number contradicts it.

The regulated-versus-grey split is where playbooks diverge hardest. Alexander Klosa of Pin-Up Partners argues regulated markets simply perform better because a team can optimize one ad account instead of burning through 100 to 300, while admitting some teams skip regulated markets entirely to work grey zones [qOL8mMQPJyI @ 00:07:53]. Klosa's trade-off line: clear rules raise costs but avoid bans. Daniel at Push House lays out the grey side without apology: unofficial offers carry better odds, get blocked, and survive on mirror domains, while official offers hold Curaçao or Malta licences [Qygo77RCGlw @ 00:05:25]. Erica, VP of Marketing at Income Access, reports the buy side moving the other way, with operators demanding regulated partners who stay out of unregulated markets and refusing mass traffic when entering new countries [H4Pub14VDXA @ 00:01:11]. Power dynamics flip by continent too. Levon Nikoghosyan says that in the US only licensed brands are in play, so affiliates chase operators and make the compromises, whereas in Europe operators chase affiliates [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:20:03]. That single fact changes negotiating posture: a European publisher can hold out for terms, while a US publisher fighting for a slot with a licensed sportsbook is the one making the compromises Levon describes.

Practitioners also split on how wide a portfolio should spread. Michael Daly, CEO of Catena Media, names an ideal geographic split of 40% Europe and 40% US while building into the Netherlands, LatAm, Japan, and Australia [DmXIrgbAw-Y @ 00:10:27]. Alexander Klosa rejects fixed ratios outright: there is no set 70/30 split, the affiliate watches EPC shifts, asks the offer owner what is happening, slows or stops traffic, and moves to tier-2 and tier-3 GEOs while tier 1 recovers [qOL8mMQPJyI @ 00:11:01]. Klosa adds that survival means running multiple traffic sources and staying in contact with the affiliate manager for forward-looking information. Levon Nikoghosyan pushes the opposite extreme for LatAm entry, telling newcomers to pick Chile, Peru, Colombia, or Mexico, avoid Brazil, and focus on a single GEO like sunlight concentrated through a lens [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:42:32]. On product scope, SlotCatalog says the affiliate model will remain a large part of revenue but is not enough on its own anymore, which is why the company now sells an intelligence layer to operators and studios on top of its B2C listings [GM2_yRmV_Bw @ 00:03:32]. The resulting choice is concrete: Daly's continental balance, Klosa's EPC-triggered rotation, or Levon's single-market focus, each defended by someone running real traffic.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is partnering with an operator that disappears. Levon Nikoghosyan warns that scam brands drain affiliates within 6 to 12 months, even as spend campaigns and upfront, listing, and annual fees keep growing across deals [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:48:53]. On the Odys podcast the same practitioner gives the screening rule: check how long the operator has been live and confirm with other affiliates, because brands running only a couple of months can vanish [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:12:00]. A second red flag is the operator who says 'we work worldwide' with no defined GEO; those brands pay listing fees, fail to convert, and end up delisted [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:15:02]. Paul, CMO of Mr Gamble, runs the affiliate-side due diligence as a player would: licensed casinos only, test deposits and landing pages personally, search forums for late-payment complaints, and demand localized payments, with Canada minus Interac treated as a flat no-go [R-_FHgwDEFQ @ 00:12:03]. Paul also asks operators for their top ten GEOs and conversion numbers and benchmarks them against the wider casino set before listing anything. Every one of these checks costs hours; skipping them is how affiliates end up inside Levon's 6-to-12-month drain.

The economics checks are blunt. Levon Nikoghosyan says an affiliate that fails to segment traffic by GEO, separating Morocco, the US, and LatAm, ends up instead of earning 100K a month, earning like ten K and burning the other 90K [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:20:12]. Michael Daly exposes a margin trap inside Catena Media's own books: sport delivered 28% of revenue but only 9% of EBITDA, the company had under-invested in sport, and casino carries the higher lifetime value [DmXIrgbAw-Y @ 00:27:02]. Daly also calls paid search low-margin and best avoided by affiliates, with organic SEO plus content coming first. On the publishing side, Christian Strutt warns that showing a bonus in a state where it is not offered will just completely kill it, so every state page needs its own promo codes and geo-targeting so only licensed books appear [MKTk-Y-xt1I @ 00:04:46]. Operators burn money too: Levon says spending 10K to 20K on listings is wasted without traffic relevance [ohEZ5-gHckQ @ 00:16:36]. The common thread is that headline volume hides the leak, whether the leak is an unsegmented GEO, a low-margin vertical, a mismatched state bonus, or a listing fee paid to the wrong audience.

Regulatory reversals wipe out whole books overnight. Levon Nikoghosyan calls the Brazil ban a business killer: an SEO site with 10,000 players a day has no legal outlet, rerouting that traffic risks jail, and switching players to gray brands will also be banned, while Brazil-only operators shut down [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:27:51]. When the Curaçao files broke, brands stripped license details from their footers within a day [JCr5X66vWg4 @ 00:31:35]. Michael Daly watched Germany fall 50% year on year to well under 10% of Catena Media revenue once the new tax took effect and revenue share was cut off [DmXIrgbAw-Y @ 00:04:19]. The softer failure is self-inflicted. Erica at Income Access names the biggest operator mistake as setting up affiliates, handing them data, and then never engaging on what is not working, and warns that large traffic sources carry risk when the operator does not understand their models [H4Pub14VDXA @ 00:03:34]. The fix offered is a day-to-day relationship: find what performs, then ask affiliates what they need. Combine a market that can close in a day with a partner nobody talks to, and the operator learns about the damage from the revenue report rather than from the affiliate.
