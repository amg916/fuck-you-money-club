## Executive Summary

Private lenders and JV operators converge on one rule: the promissory note is the IOU, but the recorded deed of trust or mortgage is what lets you take the house. Hard Money Mastermind runs about 80 pages per loan, puts personal guarantees on 98% of deals, and treats the closing instruction letter as the single most important document. The Real Estate Lender pegs recording at $38, the lender title premium near $1,850, and insurance around $2,400 a year. Usury is the kill switch: Lender Link fixes California at a 10% APR cap with points included, Scott Horne pegs Texas at 18% individual and 24% corporate, and fees can turn a six-month loan into 50%. The split comes on JVs: Clint Coons calls JV agreements lawsuit generators, while Molly Tennant teaches them for quick $20,000 closing money.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every lender in this lineup splits the paper the same way: the promissory note is the IOU, and it cannot touch the house. Investor Army frames the note as the borrower's promise at 5, 10 or 12 percent for six months to a year, while the deed stays with the investor and the deed of trust is the instrument that lets the lender foreclose [ltZrMvVSfdw @ 00:02:46]. Manuel Angelus of The Real Estate Lender says it bluntly: the note alone cannot reach the property, and an unrecorded lien is a secret between friends, so recording for about $38 is what sets priority, with property taxes still ranking ahead of everyone [Bp3FsnPecRY @ 00:02:26]. Jason of Hard Money Bankers, lending since 2007 across 4,000-plus loans, keeps the note unrecorded but insists on holding the original, while the mortgage or deed of trust goes on record [bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:03:09]. Angie Diarth of Inland Professional Title runs the same three-document stack on seller-financed closings: the warranty deed passes title, the note sets the terms, and the recorded deed of trust forces the buyer to carry insurance and pay taxes [JExnsYGsNd8 @ 00:01:57]. The operator takeaway is simple: money moves on the note, but leverage lives in the recorded lien.

The second consensus point is that the borrower's personal balance sheet sits behind the collateral. Trevor Calton of Real Estate Finance Academy states that loans are typically made with recourse, and non-recourse only exists if the documents define it; the lender can both sue on the note and foreclose on the mortgage [Z-MpltBp8qA @ 00:00:19]. Jason of Hard Money Mastermind backs that with hard numbers: 98% of the shop's loans carry personal guarantees, and the one exception, self-directed IRA loans, gets underwritten tighter with LTV dropped from 65% to 50% [bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:05:23]. William Dolan of Esquire Advice explains why guarantees matter in a blowup: in bankruptcy unsecured creditors get little and 'survives discharge' clauses are unenforceable, but the guarantor stays on the hook, so the guarantor needs a strong balance sheet because retirement funds are hard to reach [hy-UeKXcTUA @ 00:05:55]. Even on the JV side, Clint Coons builds the guarantee into the structure, with the gap funder and the operator both signing personal guarantees for the hard money lender, pitching it to a reluctant funder as protection through default notices and real ownership that keeps them committed [6bibMzU1L3U @ 00:04:18]. The pattern across all four: collateral first, a named human signing second.

The third consensus point: usury is measured on the all-in cost, not the note rate, and the paper must be clean enough to enforce and sell. Scott Horne lists the fees that count toward APR, points, origination, processing, appraisal and lender-required surveys, while title closing costs and buyer inspections stay out [XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:03:10]. Nema Daghbandan of Geraci Law Firm, speaking on Lender Link, lands in the same place for California: a 10% cap on an APR basis with points and fees included, so a 9.99% note rate is not automatically safe [W1fiiFnsFhE @ 00:01:20]. Ayala Law PA warns from a live trial that the combined rate has to stay under the Florida limits, and a lender that blows past them risks being unable to enforce even the principal [0cBnXayz9w4 @ 00:04:24]. On the instrument itself, Damen Capital Fund draws the hardest line in the room: always use a promissory note with a deed of trust or mortgage, never a land contract, because land contracts drag in seller liability, title policy loss, double transfer tax and forced foreclosure after thresholds like 5 years or 20% paid, which is why the fund pays significantly less for them [mrt2bJ7XfNU @ 00:00:21]. Clean paper and honest APR math are the two prerequisites these lenders share.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Jason's hard money stack runs about 80 pages per loan, and the build order is worth copying. On top of the note and recorded mortgage or deed of trust sit an assignment of rents, the LLC documents and corporate resolution signed by all members, a commercial-purpose affidavit, a construction security agreement, an AML declaration and an ACH authorization; powers of attorney and remote online notaries go on the avoid list [bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:09:55]. The document Jason calls most important is the closing instruction letter, three to four pages the title company must sign, because the title company does not represent the lender; the commitment gets verified directly with the insurer, sticking to Chicago, Fidelity, First American or Old Republic, and money wires only after the HUD-1 and the signed letter are in hand [bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:11:31]. William Dolan layers on an assignment of contractor agreements and a lender's title policy with riders [hy-UeKXcTUA @ 00:02:41]. Manuel Angelus tells lenders to actually read the policy exceptions on a premium of about $1,850, then name themselves mortgagee and loss payee on insurance running about $2,400 a year, and reverify during the loan because vacancy after 30 to 60 days, builder's risk and flood are the gaps that bite [Bp3FsnPecRY @ 00:05:58].

The underwriting math is stated in plain dollars. Investor Army's flip runs a $40,000 purchase plus $30,000 of repairs against a $100,000 ARV, with the lender advancing $70,000 and leaning on a 30% leverage position for protection [ltZrMvVSfdw @ 00:00:44]. Manuel Angelus works a bigger deal: $285,000 purchase, $65,000 rehab, $460,000 estimated ARV and a $200,000 loan, which pencils to roughly 70% LTV, about 57% LTC and about 43% of ARV, paying about $1,666.67 a month at 10% interest-only [Bp3FsnPecRY @ 00:04:04]. Clint Coons, walking through the JV agreement on Toby Mathis's channel, routes funder money to escrow instead of the investor: $50,000 within 5 days for acquisition, $20,000 within 20 days for rehab and an interest reserve booked at $8,000 even though $800 a month over 8 months computes to $6,400, for $78,000 total secured by a second deed of trust behind Green Turtle LLC [FiBdpAVfzMo @ 00:15:41]. Scott Horne then shows how fees wreck the headline rate: a $50,000 fee on a $200,000 six-month loan works out to 50%, and $2,500 charged on $10,000 of 30-day earnest money works out to 300% [XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:04:46]. Every one of these operators runs the full-term APR before quoting a rate to anyone.

Usury compliance is engineered, not hoped for. In California, Daghbandan identifies two exits from the 10% cap: a loan arranged by a licensed real estate broker who earned a fee and secured by real property, or a loan made by a California Finance Lender; the exemption is an ongoing requirement, so an unlicensed holder adding rate, points or even an extra $50,000 advance at the same rate on a modification loses it, while a pure 12-month time extension is fine and late charges, default interest and prepayment penalties sit outside usury entirely [W1fiiFnsFhE @ 00:02:48]. In Texas, Scott Horne points to the 60-day cure available through a usury savings clause, while warning that borrowers can still sue for up to about 4 years [XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:07:09]. On equity-style deals the payout is scripted in advance: Clint Coons's LLC example nets $100,000, returns the funder's $75,000, and splits the remaining $25,000 at $12,500 each [6bibMzU1L3U @ 00:08:35]. The JV agreement waterfall pays sale costs, a first lien capped at $150,000, any other second lien of $20,000 to $30,000, Party A's $78,000 plus $12,000 of Exhibit B additions for $90,000, Party B's receipted costs, then a 50/50 split [FiBdpAVfzMo @ 00:19:36].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock Down the Borrowing Entity and Signing Authority
   - Pull the borrower's LLC documents, corporate resolution and operating agreement and confirm who can bind the entity.
   - Jason requires LLC docs and a corporate resolution with all members signing, and refuses powers of attorney and remote online notaries [Hard Money Mastermind — bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:07:02]; William Dolan warns to watch for stale managers still listed in operating agreements [Esquire Advice — hy-UeKXcTUA @ 00:04:29].
2. Run the All-In APR Against the State Cap
   - Add points, origination, processing, appraisal and lender-required surveys to interest over the actual term and compare to the cap.
   - Scott Horne shows a $50,000 fee on a $200,000 six-month loan hitting 50% [Scott Horne's Owner Finance Network — XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:04:46]; in California the cap is 10% APR unless a licensed broker arranges the loan or a CFL makes it [Lender Link — W1fiiFnsFhE @ 00:01:48].
3. Assemble the Stack and Close Through the Instruction Letter
   - Build the note, deed of trust, guarantees, assignment of rents and affidavits, then deliver the closing instruction letter to title.
   - The roughly 80-page set includes a commercial-purpose affidavit, construction security agreement, AML declaration and ACH authorization, with the title commitment verified directly against Chicago, Fidelity, First American or Old Republic [Hard Money Mastermind — bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:11:31].
4. Record, Insure and Reverify Through Payoff
   - Record the deed of trust, get named mortgagee and loss payee, and recheck coverage gaps during the loan.
   - Manuel Angelus records for about $38 to set priority, carries about $2,400 a year of insurance naming the lender, and rechecks vacancy, builder's risk and flood gaps before issuing reconveyance at payoff [The Real Estate Lender — Bp3FsnPecRY @ 00:05:27].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over the joint venture paper itself. Clint Coons tells investors to skip the 'JV agreement garbage' entirely. In his view a JV agreement 'ensures a lawsuit': a gap funder can wire $20K, walk away with the project $25K short, and the hard money lender forecloses before anyone gets to court [6bibMzU1L3U @ 00:01:36]. The Coons playbook is to pre-form at least three Wyoming single-member LLCs owned by the Corp and make offers through them. Once an offer is accepted, the gap funder and the investor personally become 50/50 members, and both sign personal guarantees for the hard money lender [6bibMzU1L3U @ 00:04:18]. Coons's own walkthrough on the Toby Mathis channel still calls the LLC the best vehicle, but it treats a written JV agreement as the fallback. That agreement is never signed in your own name, because it can be treated as a general partnership. It is armored with escrow funding, a second deed of trust, and a recorded, notarized copy in the property's county [FiBdpAVfzMo @ 00:01:57]. Molly Tennant runs the opposite direction for small checks. Her JV agreement covers someone bringing $20,000 to close and getting paid back, or short-term lending for a share of an assignment fee. It is capped at three parties, and past that an LLC becomes the smarter wrapper [TTA8OtC6qJs @ 00:00:56].

Security paper splits along two lines: land contract versus a note plus a recorded lien, and mortgage versus deed of trust. Damen Capital Fund's rule is absolute: always use a promissory note with a deed of trust or mortgage, never a land contract. Land contracts drag in seller liability, double transfer tax, title-clouding memoranda, liens filed by upset buyers, and forced foreclosure after roughly five years or 20% paid [mrt2bJ7XfNU @ 00:00:21]. William Dolan of Esquire Advice frames the choice by state. Mortgage states force judicial foreclosure, while deed of trust states give the trustee a power of sale. When the collateral is stock or LLC interests, the playbook flips to a pledge, possession of the certificate, and a UCC-1, with an Article 8 opt-in and a commercially reasonable sale [hy-UeKXcTUA @ 00:01:49]. Angie Diarth at Inland Professional Title adds that a seller-financier can choose judicial or non-judicial foreclosure and take either the property or just the money [JExnsYGsNd8 @ 00:03:40]. Guarantee policy splits too. Jason at Hard Money Mastermind puts personal guarantees on 98% of loans. Self-directed IRA loans are the exception, so the cushion comes from dropping LTV from 65% to 50% instead. Dolan's counterweight is that a guarantor is only worth the balance sheet behind them [bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:05:23].

Usury is where the rate math and the jurisdictions refuse to line up. Scott Horne works with Texas caps of 18% for individuals and 24% for corporate entities, and believes Arizona has none. He counts points, origination, processing, appraisal and lender-required surveys toward APR, while excluding title closing costs and buyer inspections [XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:00:32]. In California, Nema Daghbandan of Geraci Law Firm says business purpose does not create an exemption. The cap is 10% on an APR basis, so a 9.99% note rate loaded with points is not automatically safe. The escape hatches are a licensed broker arranging a loan secured by real property, or a California Finance Lender making it [W1fiiFnsFhE @ 00:01:20]. Daghbandan carves late charges, default interest and prepayment penalties out of the usury math as borrower elections. Ayala Law, by contrast, argued in a live trial that a lender cannot double-charge late fees and default interest at all [0cBnXayz9w4 @ 00:03:04]. TownCenter Partners pushes a separate line for litigation finance. If repayment is contingent on recovery, there is no loan and no usury. Some courts, however, look past the contract language and ask whether the litigant is virtually certain to win [YahUsg3anlA @ 00:01:34].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first failure mode is the money partner who stops funding mid-deal. Clint Coons describes a gap funder who wires $20K, leaves the project $25K short, and lets the hard money lender foreclose before a breach suit can even be filed. Time pressure compounds it. A Florida LLC was still incomplete on day 6 of a 10-day close and had to be replaced the same day by a Wyoming LLC, which carries $50 a year in costs [6bibMzU1L3U @ 00:10:04]. The second failure mode is a runaway operator. Coons's walkthrough cites a $400K demolition horror story. The responses are to bar Party B from exceeding the budget by $8K without approval, and to force a listing within 7 days of completion. Price drops are scripted at 8%, then 5% at 60, 90 and 120 days, down to a $490K break-even floor, after which the property converts to a rental [FiBdpAVfzMo @ 00:25:12]. Scott Horne flags the structural trap: a JV built only to dodge usury limits is a sham. The funder has to genuinely participate or hold real ownership in the LLC or on the deed [XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:08:27]. Molly Tennant adds that breach is pursued separately, so breach disclosures belong in the agreement up front [TTA8OtC6qJs @ 00:01:56].

Closing-table failures are where private lenders lose principal without any borrower default. Jason at Hard Money Mastermind refuses powers of attorney and remote online notaries, and reminds lenders that the title company does not represent them. He verifies the title commitment directly with the insurer. He wires only after the HUD-1 and a signed three-to-four-page closing instruction letter are in hand [bNJYDcDSFxU @ 00:17:38]. Manuel Angelus at The Real Estate Lender puts it bluntly: an unrecorded lien is a secret between friends. A $38 recording fee is what sets priority, and property taxes still jump ahead of everyone [Bp3FsnPecRY @ 00:02:26]. Angelus also warns that insurance quietly fails mid-loan. Vacancy exclusions kick in at 30 to 60 days, builder's risk may be missing, and flood leaves gaps. The lender has to be named mortgagee and loss payee and must reverify coverage during the term, not just at closing [Bp3FsnPecRY @ 00:05:58]. William Dolan of Esquire Advice raises the authority trap. Operating agreements often list stale managers, so the person signing for the borrowing LLC may have no power to bind it. And once bankruptcy hits, unsecured lenders collect little [hy-UeKXcTUA @ 00:04:29].

Rate and default math is the third trap, and it cuts both ways. Ayala Law's live trial involved a $5M balloon loan. Delayed refinancing let 21% default interest accrue daily, and a new lender retroactively assessed default interest on past late payments under a no-waiver clause. The balance swelled to about $6.4M, and the lawsuit itself then blocked the refinance [0cBnXayz9w4 @ 00:03:19]. On the lender side, Nema Daghbandan warns about modifications of California loans by an unlicensed holder. Adding rate or points, or even advancing an extra $50,000 at the same rate, loses the exemption because no licensed party is involved anymore. That turns an exempt loan into usury exposure [W1fiiFnsFhE @ 00:04:14]. Scott Horne shows how fees blow up APR. A $50K fee on a $200K six-month loan works out to 50%, and $2,500 on $10K of 30-day earnest money works out to 300%. Texas borrowers can sue for up to about four years for interest, principal, fees and possibly treble damages, even after a 60-day savings-clause cure [XdrwPF-D1FI @ 00:06:38]. Quad's Mississippi case shows the borrower's audit: a 33.04% APR against a 14% cap on loans above $5,000 is a 19-point excess [eFyovu8iZIA @ 01:02:26].
