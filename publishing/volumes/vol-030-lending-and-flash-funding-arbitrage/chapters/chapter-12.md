## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Credit stacking, as the operators run it, is a sequenced blitz: you fire business card applications at several banks inside a tight window so each lender underwrites before the others' inquiries and new accounts show up. Radikal Marketer frames it as submitting applications 'in a specific order with multiple banks' to unlock large amounts of unsecured capital [Oa3bugxGPHE @ 00:00:17], and This Will Make You Rich names the engine outright: the reporting delay 'creates a very short window of time' [6D6yJ60Vi0k @ 00:01:39]. The reason operators chase cards instead of term debt is underwriting speed. Stephen Smith points out that loans want one to two years of revenue while cards run on stated income [UuM0MLm-2DU @ 00:00:42], and Jack McColl on BetterWealth puts it bluntly: lenders need good credit, no tax returns, and 'the business can be a week old' [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:03:41]. The payoff is real working capital at 0% for a promo window, typically 12 to 18 months on the cards the crew rates highest, with personal guarantees tying every balance back to the founder's SSN. That guarantee is the price of admission: a brand-new LLC borrows on the owner's personal file, so the person, not the entity, is what the bank is actually pricing.

The crew agrees the personal file, not the business, decides the size of the round. Marvin Francois's checklist is no negatives, utilization at 10%, two to three inquiries or fewer, two-plus years of history, and three to five cards carrying $5K-plus limits, because lenders underwrite on comparable credit: $1K personal limits produce $2-4K business approvals [6xdE38E2F8Q @ 00:02:42]. Naam Wynn sets the floor at 680 with no negatives, utilization under 30% and ideally under 10%, seven to ten primary accounts, and one tier-1 card at $10K from Chase, BofA, Citi or Wells [bdodeCpS6JI @ 00:09:13]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth wants no more than two inquiries in six months and four or more personal cards totaling at least $15K, ideally $40-80K [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:04:41]. Radikal Marketer's co-host compresses all of it into one line operators repeat: 'your profile is your resume... your score is your reputation,' and a 740-800 score alone does not carry the file [Oa3bugxGPHE @ 00:09:28]. The pattern across every speaker is the same: thin files, high balances, and fresh inquiries crush limits long before the score itself becomes the problem, so profile repair comes before the first application.

Two more rules hold across nearly every playbook: spread pulls across the three bureaus, and warm the bank up before you apply. Marvin Francois illustrates the bureau rule with Person A, who hits three TransUnion-pulling banks and watches approvals slide from $25K to $15K to $7.5K, versus Person B, who splits Experian, TransUnion and Equifax [6xdE38E2F8Q @ 00:01:02]. Colin Matthew states the hard ceiling: most banks 'flat out deny you' at three or more hard inquiries on any single bureau [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:01:42]. On relationships, Naam Wynn puts the gap at $20K versus $150K-plus, with Chase paying $75K to a known customer against $5-10K cold [LktXzMB-Sis @ 00:05:04]. Christopher Grant's guest Sadik routed Amazon deposits into a Chase checking account, seasoned it 30 days, and pulled $72.5K in one day [-M4ZoYEoh3k @ 00:24:54]. Chase leads the order for the same reason everywhere: it is the most inquiry-sensitive lender, so it gets the clean file before anyone else touches it. Cal Barton puts Chase alone in his S tier, Colin Matthew's bankers say Chase is the only bank to apply to before US Bank, and Jack McColl runs Chase first, then US Bank.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The build starts before a single application. Naam Wynn says proper setup is the difference between a $5K and a $50K limit, and walks it step by step: a virtual business address through iPostal, Opus, Regus or Alliance instead of a PO box or UPS store, an 800 number with a 411 listing, a domain email at about $10 a month, and an EIN that takes about five minutes, with the address matching everywhere [bdodeCpS6JI @ 00:01:03]. He adds that nine of ten DUNS denials trace back to mismatched details, and that credit repair, cannabis, car dealers and travel are high-risk NAICS codes while consulting and B2B read low risk [bdodeCpS6JI @ 00:07:20]. Marvin Francois prices the full business checklist, name, EIN, operating agreement, address, email, phone and website, at under $300 [6xdE38E2F8Q @ 00:03:38]. Colin Matthew's banned-industry list runs trucking, cannabis, restaurants and crypto [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:01:29]. Documentation rarely bites on the first round: Cal Barton says limits under $25K usually need none, and Jack McColl says Chase extends up to $150K before asking for tax returns. Every detail must match identically across every application.

Sequencing is where the money gets made. Colin Matthew's round one opens a Chase business checking account with at least $4K, ideally $10K, lets it sit five business days, then takes the Ink Unlimited at $30-40K and the Ink Preferred at $40-60K, moving the Preferred limit onto the 0% card for $70-100K at Chase alone [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:02:27]. US Bank follows with a $2-4K deposit and a seven-day wait through a relationship manager, then Truist, Citizens, PNC, Wells Fargo and Amex. Christopher Grant runs the same Chase move under the 2/30 rule, two cards per 30 days, turning a $20K Ink Cash plus a $40K Ink Preferred into $60K at 0% [-M4ZoYEoh3k @ 00:20:42]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth confirms limit moves work at Chase, BofA and Amex but not US Bank, citing $47K plus $5K into $52K, and says never close those cards [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:16:41]. Naam Wynn sizes the round at two to four approvals per bureau, six to twelve per round at about $10K each [LktXzMB-Sis @ 00:02:13]. For California applicants he runs Chase and Amex on Experian, US Bank on TransUnion, and Citizens on Equifax, capped at two inquiries per bureau.

Turning limits into deployable cash has its own price sheet. Naam Wynn runs vendor payments through Plastiq or Melio at about 2.9%, so $30K costs about $900, and quotes MF Capital liquidation at about 6% for up to 90% of limits, against 10-20% loans [bdodeCpS6JI @ 00:12:29]. Andrew Kraft prices Plastiq-style processors at 2.5% to 4% [S_u_YpirRNM @ 00:04:28], and Gap Funding pays down payments, closing costs and contractor invoices through Plastiq by check, ACH or wire, then uses Kashoo to land cash in an account for proof of funds [ItOMPgJwT_4 @ 00:03:39]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth says liquidators run about 3% plus 2-3%, so the deal has to return well over 10% to justify it. Rounds repeat on a cycle: Naam Wynn reports Experian inquiry removals in about 48 hours and Equifax and TransUnion in one to two weeks, then a 90-day seasoning wait and manual limit reviews every 90 days, $30K to $60K to $100K [LktXzMB-Sis @ 00:10:02]. Christopher Grant staggers due dates weekly across nine cards using ChatGPT or Claude, and pockets the $750 Ink bonus on $6K spend before moving the limit.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split on the street is timing. Gap Funding's Mick Wadley teaches the same-day stack: submit four to five applications in one sitting so no issuer's inquiry posts before the next goes in, and calls applying for one card then going back three days later for another the wrong way to do it [ItOMPgJwT_4 @ 00:01:26]. This Will Make You Rich frames the whole play around that gap, describing stacking as applying to several lenders before they can see each other's approvals, because the reporting delay creates a very short window of time [6D6yJ60Vi0k @ 00:01:39]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth runs a deliberate order instead: Chase first, then US Bank, with Bank of America handing out better limits even without a relationship manager [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:24:19]. Colin Matthew backs that ordering with banker intel, saying his bankers told him the only bank you can apply before US Bank is Chase, and builds a pause into Round 1: if the first Chase approval lands under $30K, wait 30 days [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:03:55]. The practical read for an operator is that same-day blitzes maximize inquiry blindness across issuers, while sequenced rounds let early approvals and relationship deposits lift the limits on every later application in the chain.

Practitioners also split on how wide to go. HowToCredit opens with 'Stop collecting 0% interest business credit cards,' arguing one card with a long enough runway can outbeat five short ones, and that picking the card is maybe 20% of the job while getting the limit is the other 80% [0jZD4_NoCTs @ 00:00:00]. Marvin Francois keeps the spread tight: three to four banks max gets $50K to $100K, and Francois holds two $50K cards from Chase alone [6xdE38E2F8Q @ 00:08:25]. Radikal Marketer puts the ceiling at four to seven banks with three to six as the sweet spot, built from two in-house underwriters like Chase and Amex, two regionals like Citizens and PNC, and two smaller banks or credit unions using third-party underwriters, then double-dipping at one bank to reach about 12 lines [Oa3bugxGPHE @ 00:04:19]. Colin Matthew runs the opposite extreme, an 11-bank, two-round sequence that adds First Citizens, BofA, Elan and TCM after Chase, US Bank, Truist, Citizens, PNC, Wells Fargo and Amex, landing a conservative total of $258K-plus [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:09:41]. Wide stacks chase total exposure; narrow stacks chase runway and cleaner repayment math for operators who would rather service one balance than juggle many.

Inquiry removal is where the playbooks clash hardest. Naam Wynn teaches that hard inquiries from business applications can be disputed and removed from the personal report, with Experian clearing in about 48 hours and Equifax and TransUnion in one to two weeks, followed by a 90-day seasoning wait before the next round [LktXzMB-Sis @ 00:10:02]. Colin Matthew bakes the same wipe into the gap between rounds, putting TransUnion and Equifax at roughly 14 days and calling Experian typically easy [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:07:02]. Stephen Smith draws a hard line: if the card reports to personal credit, keep that hard inquiry; only when the account does not report is it okay to remove it [UuM0MLm-2DU @ 00:05:31]. Christopher Grant's guest Sadik reports the downside directly, warning that fraudulent disputes have gotten accounts closed [-M4ZoYEoh3k @ 00:31:04]. Seasoning windows diverge just as widely: Colin Matthew lets a funded Chase checking account sit five business days minimum [WAqBw-d5K44 @ 00:02:39], while Sadik routed Amazon deposits into Chase checking for 30 days before landing $72.5K in a single day [-M4ZoYEoh3k @ 00:24:54]. Operators pick a lane based on how many inquiries they already carry and how much calendar time they can afford to burn.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure is the runway running out. HowToCredit warns that picking the wrong card means paying 20%-plus APR by month eight, and that a balance transfer onto a purchase-only card accrues full interest from day one, because the banks are betting on balances that never get cleared [0jZD4_NoCTs @ 00:02:00]. Zach Siegel's rule is blunt: never let the 0% periods expire with significant balances, since post-promo rates run 16% to 28%, and late payments can cancel the promo outright [Fs38E_kSwxE @ 00:08:14]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth pegs the post-promo range at 18% to 25% and reminds operators that default lands on personal credit because the cards are personally guaranteed [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:41:53]. Gap Funding names the strategy-breaker directly: it fails when there is no equity play, and none of it works if balances are unpaid when the 0% period ends or a mortgage application goes in while the personal double stack is still maxed [ItOMPgJwT_4 @ 00:05:05]. The economics check is simple: map the sale, refinance or fixed repayment date before the first swipe, then size total exposure to what that exit can actually clear inside the promo window.

Turning card limits into cash carries its own drag. Naam Wynn quotes Plastiq or Melio at about 2.9%, roughly $900 on a $30K payment, and MF Capital liquidation near 6% for up to 90% of limits, pitched as cheaper than 10% to 20% loans [bdodeCpS6JI @ 00:12:42]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth stacks the liquidator cost at about 3% plus another 2% to 3%, concluding the opportunity has to return well over 10% to justify it [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:47:10]. Radikal Marketer adds that the systems are starting to get tighter for credit card liquidation, so the exit door itself is narrowing [Oa3bugxGPHE @ 00:01:26]. Balance transfers bleed too: HowToCredit flags that a 3% versus 5% transfer fee is a $400 gap on $20K, money straight out of savings, and that US Bank Shield transfers must land within 30 days [0jZD4_NoCTs @ 00:07:31]. Stack the fees together and the real cost of so-called free capital climbs fast: a 2.9% processor charge, a 3% to 5% transfer fee and a liquidator spread can eat a large slice of a deal's margin before the inventory, rehab or ad spend ever produces a dollar of return.

Shutdowns and decay hit operators who push past a bank's tolerance. Cal Barton rates Amex A-tier with limits up to $50K but warns of financial reviews and shutdowns for cardholders who liquidate too early [Eh5z4F-toEI @ 00:06:08]. Christopher Grant's guest Sadik restricts 0% lines to proven inventory only, nothing for testing or lifestyle, after spending four months stuck at $20K to $30K a month on cash before $110K across two cards pushed the business to $100K a month [-M4ZoYEoh3k @ 00:19:54]. Jack McColl on BetterWealth cautions that a second round of funding is almost never as successful as the first, and wants a same-bank balance paid down to 50% before reapplying [ojWjbXNf-HI @ 00:42:14]. Stephen Smith reminds every startup founder that the personal guarantee means the balance is owed even if the business never earns a dollar [UuM0MLm-2DU @ 00:01:10]. The pattern across these reports is consistent: first-round approvals are the easy part, while repayment discipline, deployment into cash-producing assets with an exit already mapped, and patience between rounds decide whether the stack compounds or collapses into high-interest personal debt.
