# Chapter 01: The Double Close Playbook

*Back-to-back, simultaneous, and same-day double closings; A-B/B-C and ABC closings; triple closings; flash cash; double-escrow contracts; REO simultaneous closings; the end buyer funding both sides; and real no-money deal walkthroughs*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Wholesalers, title operators and transactional lenders converge on one structure: two contracts, two independent back-to-back closings, so the seller never sees the resale price and the end buyer never sees the buy price. They reach for it when a spread crosses roughly $10,000 to $20,000, when REO, HUD, hedge-fund or no-assignment paper blocks assignments, and as states and title underwriters shut down pass-through funding and blind HUDs. Funders price at 1% to 3% of purchase, flat $850 to $2,000, or 1% plus $100, wire only after the end buyer's cash sits in escrow, and get repaid on the B-to-C leg, often within an hour. The split: Kevin Tacher and the funders insist leg one closes with outside cash, while Jerry Norton, Luigi Ontiveros, Travis Stagg and Rick use the end buyer's wire to fund both legs.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner here defines the double close the same way: two separate contracts, two separate closings, same property, run back-to-back. Kevin Tacher of Independence Title says the art is making closing A and closing B close independently, so a title claim on one never touches the other, and the shop records deed one by e-recording, typically same day or next, before following with deed two [lLbfcHAX30o @ 00:01:09]. Ryan Schrope, who has run over 135 lending transactions in a year, draws the hard line: an A-to-B contract plus a B-to-C contract closing on the same day, and if only one contract exists, it is just an assignment, not a double close [j30R1Cef6PE @ 00:07:19]. Pete Russell of Velocity Capital frames the flow in plain numbers: seller contract at $100,000, separate end-buyer contract at $150,000, the funder wires the full $100,000 to the attorney or title company, the first closing records, the second closing settles, and the closing company disburses profit to the wholesaler and principal plus fee to the funder [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:05:21]. Because the seller only signs closing one and the end buyer only signs closing two, usually remotely and at separate times, neither side ever sees the other's number, which is the entire point of running the structure.

The consensus driver is protecting the spread. Russell has had deals die at the table: an attorney and seller went line by line through a HUD, saw the wholesaler's business name next to $30,000, decided it was unearned and walked out, while savvy buyers who spot a big assignment fee come back weeks later claiming mold in the crawl space and demanding $10,000 to $15,000 off [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:09:34]. Ricky of Flip Lab puts the tolerance line near $20,000: above that, plenty of sellers and buyers start 'looking in your pockets,' so on a big spread paying $5,000 to $7,000 to double close is worth it because less can go wrong [Qk7hhsytc9Y @ 00:04:42]. Jerry Norton of Flipping Mastery TV sets the comfort zone lower, saying most cash buyers shrug at fees under $10,000 or $15,000 but balk at $20,000, $25,000, $30,000 or higher, and adds the forced case: bank REOs and short sales carry no-assignment clauses, so the double close becomes mandatory [Jfl-5LgDTaM @ 00:04:32]. Dave Dinkle of LearnREInvesting has watched sellers refuse to close over an investor profit of only $2,500, so 'too large' sits squarely in the eye of the beholder [_QXnSUQsTNw @ 00:02:41].

The baseline prerequisites repeat across every channel. First, a closing company that genuinely handles double closes: Norton warns the wrong title company will kill the deal, so he hard-codes the investor-friendly closer into both the seller and buyer contracts [Jfl-5LgDTaM @ 00:09:40], and Schrope asks 'do you understand the double close' on the very first call, because some title shops have never done one and some refuse outright [j30R1Cef6PE @ 00:12:42]. Second, an end buyer whose cash is actually sitting in escrow before anyone wires leg one, a condition every funder in this group treats as non-negotiable. Third, regulation is herding wholesalers into this structure. Russell reports Tennessee closers shutting down pass-through funding and blind HUDs, yet says not one attorney, escrow or title company nationwide has told clients no on a straight double close, and David Olds, whose company owns a title operation in six states, notes closers like it because they book two sets of fees, roughly an extra $300 [KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:07:54]. Dinkle sees the same shift from the funding side: as more states ban assignments, sales of contracts and joint venture agreements, investors are adopting double closings as standard operating procedure rather than a last resort [_QXnSUQsTNw @ 00:03:58].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the capital side, transactional funding, which Tacher also calls flash funding, covers the A-to-B purchase as an unsecured loan with no mortgage or note signed, repaid as an unsecured payoff on the B-to-C closing, and the closer issues title insurance on both legs [lLbfcHAX30o @ 00:02:17]. Pricing varies by shop. Ricky's Flip Lab Capital charges one to three percent of the purchase price, and Ricky runs the math openly: a $200,000 buy at 2% is $4,000, plus about $3,000 in closing costs, so $7,000 in fees comes off what would have been a $20,000 assignment [Qk7hhsytc9Y @ 00:02:49]. Jasper Cool's Bright Closings charges 1% of the purchase price plus a $100 processing fee above $75,000, so $1,100 on a $100,000 house, and a flat $850 below $75,000, all paid from the second closing; the shop funds every state except New York, Alaska and Hawaii, answers submissions within 5 to 10 minutes and can fund the same morning [EUE6UWl2qBA @ 00:03:19]. Russell's Velocity Capital prices off the amount funded, asks for no 10% to 20% down, and collects nothing upfront except a $250 initiation fee credited back at closing, a fee added only because heavy volume brought too many deals that were submitted and then abandoned [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:14:38].

Underwriting is the deal, not the borrower. Russell says Velocity ignores credit score, experience and profit size; if the deal reaches the closing line and everything lines up, it funds, and more than 50% of monthly clients are repeat users who switched every deal over after the first one [KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:13:29]. Intake is a short form: Bright Closings asks for the borrower name exactly as it appears on the seller contract, phone, email, property address, A-to-B purchase price, closing date, the closing attorney or title company with contact details, whether that closer already knows it is a double close, and uploads of both signed contracts [EUE6UWl2qBA @ 00:00:49]. Schrope's lending workflow then runs entirely through the title officer: confirm the A-to-B HUD to learn the true wire amount, send a payoff letter on letterhead committing title to hold the money in escrow and return principal plus fee, approve the B-to-C HUD showing the lender's LLC payoff, and wait for the end buyer's funds [j30R1Cef6PE @ 00:15:20]. Every funder here wires last. Ricky funds only after title confirms the end buyer's cash is on hand, which leaves the lender exposed mainly to bad wires, disputes or a bad title company, and Flip Lab passes on hedge-fund deals needing 30-day gaps between closings [Qk7hhsytc9Y @ 00:06:50].

The wholesaler-side mechanics center on paper, timing and earnest money. Norton's closing-in-escrow run sheet on a $220,000 buy and $250,000 resale: seller signs at 10 a.m., the wholesaler signs both sides at 11 a.m. bringing no money, the cash buyer signs and wires $250,000 at 1 p.m., the wire lands at 2 p.m., title pays $220,000 to the seller and $30,000 to the wholesaler, then records closing one before closing two, leaving the wholesaler around $300 to $500 in fees [Jfl-5LgDTaM @ 00:08:20]. Norton's buyer contracts demand at least $2,500 non-refundable, preferably $5,000 to $10,000, plus a clause instructing escrow to release the deposit as damages with no further instruction needed. Luigi Ontiveros's double-escrow resale contract closes in seven to ten days, requires $10,000 non-refundable by 2 p.m. the next day, and charges the buyer $200 per day once funding slips past 48 hours after escrow requests funds [UpxkrBIxTtQ @ 00:06:05]. Russell keeps the seller-side deposit near $500 in attorney escrow behind an inspection period and clear-title contingency, wants $3,000 to $5,000 going hard from the end buyer, and reuses the same one-page seller contract with roles flipped for the B-to-C side [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:18:00].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock both contracts
   - Sign the A-to-B seller contract and a separate B-to-C contract with the end buyer before requesting funds.
   - Funders will not spend time until both signed contracts exist; with only one contract it is an assignment, not a double close [Ryan Schrope — j30R1Cef6PE @ 00:09:00]
2. Submit the deal and introduce the closer
   - Submit contact info, address, amount needed and closing date, then introduce the funder to the title company or attorney.
   - Velocity's team then works directly with the closing professional on delays and final amounts instead of chasing the wholesaler [David Olds REI — KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:11:44]
3. Paper the payoff
   - Confirm the A-to-B HUD amount, deliver a payoff letter, and approve a B-to-C HUD showing the funder's payoff.
   - Title signs a letterhead payoff agreeing to hold funds in escrow and return principal plus fee; expect corrections because title rarely gets the HUD right on the first try [Ryan Schrope — j30R1Cef6PE @ 00:16:22]
4. Fund last, then disburse
   - Wire leg-one money only after all docs are signed and the end buyer's funds sit in escrow.
   - Velocity is the last piece of the puzzle, wiring once closing docs are signed and the end buyer has fully committed money into escrow [David Olds REI — KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:12:46]

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — How the operators squeeze cost and friction out of the double close**
> 
> Small moves the practitioners use to keep fees down and keep both legs closing on schedule.
> - Keep B-to-C closing costs on the end buyer as if it were an assignment, then ask the title company for a break on A-to-B costs since they earn double in about an hour [Closer Control — qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:13:04]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the double-close game is whose money hits closing A. Kevin Tacher of Independence Title draws a hard line: both legs close independently, the end buyer's money never funds the first closing, and closing A must close cash, so their shop arranges transactional or flash funding as an unsecured loan with no mortgage or note, repaid as an unsecured payoff on closing B [lLbfcHAX30o @ 00:02:01]. Jerry Norton of Flipping Mastery TV runs the opposite play: every party signs in escrow, the end cash buyer wires $250,000, and title uses that wire to fund closing one, paying the seller $220,000 and the wholesaler $30,000 before recording both deeds in order, with the wholesaler hiding the fee and skipping transactional funding costs entirely [Jfl-5LgDTaM @ 00:08:37]. Travis Stagg of Forefront Enterprises runs the same mechanic on a $20,000 buy and $25,000 resale: title pays the seller out of the buyer's funds and sends the $5,000 difference, so the only skin in the game is a $500 to $2,000 deposit, letting an operator run ten or twenty times as many deals [Qe97wvpylvo @ 00:01:29]. William Bronchick calls it pure sequencing: deeds and funds sit in escrow, either side can sign first, and nothing records until every piece is complete [Z9NzpNmbuu8 @ 00:02:39].

Whether end-buyer money can still touch the A leg depends on who holds the pen. Pete Russell of Velocity Capital reports wholesalers calling because blind HUDs and pass-through funding are being phased out, and host David Olds, who owns a title company in six states, says underwriters like First American, Old Republic, WFG and Fidelity see pass-through as something that just looks like fraud [KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:07:27]. Jason Gaston calls the end-buyer-funded simultaneous closing frowned upon though still done in many places, and routes around it with transactional funders charging a flat one to two thousand dollars [JbOkaP42JKo @ 00:01:47]. Dave Dinkle of LearnREInvesting says most closing agents and title insurers refuse these transactions, and the disclosure needed to make them work is usually a deal-killer because seller and end buyer then see the profit [_QXnSUQsTNw @ 00:03:26]. Rick of Flip With Rick refuses to concede the point: have the title company prepare a directive the cash buyer signs, authorizing part of a $150,000 purchase to fund the $110,000 A leg with the $40,000 balance going to the wholesaler, or accept a blind or split HUD when a friendly title company offers one, which Rick says most will not [4Ueirfxr5Ww @ 00:10:54].

Operators also split on when a spread justifies paying for two closings. Luigi Ontiveros draws a hard line at $10,000: anything above it, even $11,000, goes through a double escrow that costs only three to five hundred dollars extra, because buyers who see big profits kill deals [UpxkrBIxTtQ @ 00:01:10]. Ricky of Flip Lab sets the pain point higher, saying many buyers and sellers start looking in your pockets once an assignment fee passes roughly $20,000, and that on big spreads paying five or seven thousand dollars to double close buys security and less that can go wrong [Qk7hhsytc9Y @ 00:04:42]. Rick flips the logic on buyers entirely: never switch to a double close because a cash buyer resents the fee, drop that buyer and find another, and save the double close for sellers spooked by a realtor sister, lawyer or other outside party [4Ueirfxr5Ww @ 00:13:39]. Pete Russell takes the opposite stance, telling wholesalers clearing $20,000 to $50,000 that an extra couple thousand in A-to-B costs is cost of business next to a buyer demanding a $5,000 to $10,000 reduction or a seller walking out at the table, and recommends asking the title company for a break since it earns double fees in about an hour [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:14:04].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start with a visible spread. Pete Russell had a seller and their attorney go line by line through a HUD, spot the business name beside $30,000, decide the fee was unearned and walk away from the closing table, even though the seller had been prepped that the contract would go to a partner at the agreed price [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:09:34]. Russell has also been stung by savvy buyers who spot a large assignment fee weeks before closing, then return claiming mold in the crawl space and demanding $10,000 to $15,000 off, so even a negotiated $5,000 cut comes straight out of the wholesaler's pocket [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:10:18]. Rick warns that a realtor relative can show up at closing vowing to bust the assignment fee, and that lawyers, title companies or the seller can blow a deal up the same way, especially when the wholesaler hid the profit instead of telling the seller up front that they are an investor out to make money [4Ueirfxr5Ww @ 00:03:52]. David Olds sees the same pattern nationally: title companies tightening policy, states regulating assignment fees and buyers reading deal structure, so transparency on big fees kills deals that once closed easily [KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:00:21].

The second trap is the closer itself. Jerry Norton says flatly that the wrong title company will kill your deal, so the investor-friendly closer should be named in both the seller and buyer contracts with a clause binding each side to use that company [Jfl-5LgDTaM @ 00:09:40]. Dave Dinkle watched a closing agent tell a new investor that the attorney-owned firm could not legally do an ABC closing, because the term meant something different to that agent; moving the file to a high-volume investor closer worked, but the two-day deadline had to be extended [_QXnSUQsTNw @ 00:02:09]. Ryan Schrope, with over 135 lending transactions in a year, screens title on whether they wire at all, since a check-only shop can lock up $100,000 for five to seven days plus deposit holds, and reports title rarely gets the B-to-C HUD payoff right on the first try [j30R1Cef6PE @ 00:11:46]. Pete Russell fields panicked calls from wholesalers whose title company announced at the last minute that the old pass-through method was dead, asking for funding tomorrow or in two days, and needs extra lead time on double closings over a million [KG3yn1b1Yr4 @ 00:09:27].

Then run the math before celebrating any spread. Ricky of Flip Lab prices a $200,000 purchase against a $220,000 resale: a 2% transactional fee of $4,000 plus about $3,000 in closing costs strips $7,000 out of a $20,000 spread, money the wholesaler simply keeps on a straight assignment [Qk7hhsytc9Y @ 00:02:49]. Rick says a $10,000 double close can net as little as four grand once $5,000 to $6,000 in closing costs hit the HUD, and that taking title means owning a house you never planned to own if the cash buyer fails, plus a whirlwind of IRS paperwork from stacked HUDs [4Ueirfxr5Ww @ 00:05:40]. Jerry Norton learned the deposit lesson the hard way when a cash buyer flaked on closing day and refused to let escrow release a $5,000 non-refundable deposit, a big fight that produced a release clause letting escrow pay the wholesaler without further instruction [Jfl-5LgDTaM @ 00:12:19]. Pete Russell got burned from the other side, posting non-refundable money with sellers who took it and ran, and now parks about $500 in escrow behind an inspection period and clear-title contingency [qtTEzFSMqJg @ 00:18:44].
