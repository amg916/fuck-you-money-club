# Chapter 05: Earnest Money Deposits: Holding, Protecting & Losing Them

*What EMD is and where it's held, contingencies and inspection periods, state-specific rules (Florida, Connecticut, Idaho, New Jersey), wholesaler EMD practices, cancellation and refund scenarios, and e-tender EMD escrow*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Realtors, attorneys, a mortgage lender and a wholesaler converge on one rule: the earnest money deposit sits with a neutral holder (title, a brokerage trust account, or attorney escrow) and becomes a closing credit. It is only recoverable through a live, written contingency. Inspection, loan, appraisal and title windows are the exits. Miss a date or waive a clause and the money is exposed. Street numbers run from a $1 consideration floor and a 1% Idaho resale reference to the 3% scripted benchmark, plus 10% deposits in Greenwich and on custom builds, and builder deposits that go hard at signing. The mechanics are three-day funding, wires sent only on title-direct instructions, 60-day fund seasoning, and daily deadline calls. Practitioners split on who holds the money, on whether release needs both sides to sign, and on wholesalers skipping deposits entirely.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus: Neutral Escrow, a Credit at Closing, and Contingencies as the Only Shield

Every practitioner who addresses custody agrees on one point: the deposit does not land in the seller's pocket. Katie Anderson in Pensacola sends it to the title company or the closing attorney. They park it in an escrow account tagged with the property address until the contract either cancels or closes [NMjK2yG_6ys @ 00:00:31]. Charles Nedder runs a different pipe in Greenwich, Connecticut. The buyer wires the deposit to the buyer's attorney, who forwards it with the signed contract to the seller's attorney. It then sits untouched in that attorney's escrow account [xyiyeiBkCqQ @ 00:00:47]. The Treasure Valley Home Show team in Idaho says the holder is always a neutral third party, either a brokerage trust account or title, and that title is now the usual landing spot [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:08:43]. On a clean close the money is neither a fee nor consumed. Lender Chase Hodson tells buyers to read it as a line on an accounting ledger: a credit that offsets closing costs or down payment [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:03:35]. Alec Osland of Yanowitz Law Firm states the same mechanic plainly. The deposit stays in a neutral escrow until closing and then gets applied toward the down payment or closing costs [D8jafo21-34 @ 00:00:57]. The operator takeaway is simple: the deposit is cash-to-close parked early, not a payment to the other side.

The second consensus is that contingencies are the only reliable way out with the money intact. Ed Kaminsky names three core protections: inspection, loan approval and appraisal. He says a buyer who is not satisfied with the outcome of any of them can cancel and take the deposit back [p5RGCN-UQL8 @ 00:00:46]. Daryl Williams of Win-Win Realtors adds a fourth, the preliminary title report contingency. He says a buyer who finds something they disapprove of during any of those windows is entitled to the refund [y9awo1XfRU4 @ 00:01:28]. The scripted agent pitch delivered by James Brinkman and his peers carries the same warning. Back out without a valid reason such as a contingency, and the seller may have the right to the deposit for breach of contract [NqrtD90l6pk @ 00:00:53]. Andrew Finney goes further and tells buyers to always leave the inspection contingency in place. That clause is what lets them walk and recover the deposit when a seller refuses to repair real defects [ND7J57hzroE @ 00:01:47]. JAG Real Estate's walkthrough of the California form shows how deep the exit menu runs. The loan, appraisal, property investigation, insurance, seller documents and title report all work as ways out that return the deposit [E9HjXJUTsZc @ 00:03:18].

Third, every speaker treats the calendar as the real battlefield. Charles Nedder says contract dates are not suggestions. They are the lines that decide whether the deposit is protected or exposed, and missing a deadline or waiving a contingency without understanding the risk leaves a buyer exposed [xyiyeiBkCqQ @ 00:03:38]. Katie Anderson gives the concrete version. A buyer with a 15-day right to inspect and cancel who changes their mind on day 20 has acted outside the contract, and that deposit should be released to the seller [NMjK2yG_6ys @ 00:01:41]. The Idaho agent says that as each contingency drops away, the earnest money moves deeper into jeopardy. A contingency can clear simply because the date passed [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:12:00]. The practitioners also agree that escrow holders will not pick a winner on their own. Robert Greenblatt in New Jersey says the escrow holder is not allowed to release the money unless both parties agree. A buyer who simply wants out after the inspection cannot just walk away with it [CCWuS-x3COc @ 00:01:39]. Anderson describes the endgame when nobody signs the release form. The funds sit in the title or attorney escrow account while the sides try to mediate, and in very rare cases the fight goes to court [NMjK2yG_6ys @ 00:02:41].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools and Numbers: Sizing, Funding, Sourcing and Deadline Control

Sizing runs from a token dollar to a fifth of the price, depending on market and product. Robert Greenblatt says a New Jersey contract only needs consideration, so a deposit could technically be $1. In practice $1,000 often is not enough and some sellers want 10%. His test is behavioral: a buyer with $1,000 at risk may walk, while a buyer with $20,000 in escrow probably will not [CCWuS-x3COc @ 00:00:58]. Ed Kaminsky says the deposit is typically 3% of the purchase price. That figure is common because of the liquidated damages clause [p5RGCN-UQL8 @ 00:01:58]. The Idaho team uses roughly 1% as a reference point on resale homes, or $5,000 on a half-million-dollar house [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:20:58]. Builder contracts on custom homes are another story. They can demand 10% on a $600,000 to $1 million build, and the agent has heard of 20%, because the builder has already spent cash on a personalized product [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:34:22]. In Greenwich, Charles Nedder says local custom is 10% at contract signing. On a $2 million home that means wiring $200,000 to your attorney, often within five days of the accepted offer [xyiyeiBkCqQ @ 00:01:49]. At either end of that range, the cash has to be staged before the offer goes in, not scraped together after acceptance.

Delivery runs on a tight clock. Katie Anderson says page one of the contract states where the deposit goes and how many days the buyer has. Typically that is three business days to get it into escrow [NMjK2yG_6ys @ 00:00:15]. The Idaho agent says the state form defaults to three days, counted from the day after full ratification. An offer accepted on Monday means Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. The buyer must still fund on time even after spotting a problem, because funding is a contractual commitment [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:09:36]. Payment rails are narrow. Kim Haber says title companies take a wire transfer and some still take a cashier's check, but most now require a wire. Cash, debit and credit cards are not accepted [1ysRoNF77u8 @ 00:01:31]. Sourcing is the lender's hidden gate. Chase Hodson says cash pulled from a wall safe or mattress and deposited the day before is non-sourceable, because the funds must show in the account for 60 days. Gifts must be documented as non-repayable [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:19:20]. If the deposit cannot be sourced, the lender backs the credit out and the buyer has to replace it with sourceable funds [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:23:46]. JAG Real Estate says most parties want the deposit wired precisely so it can be tracked [E9HjXJUTsZc @ 00:02:39].

The operators who play offense work the contract terms themselves. In One Percent's wholesaling playbook, the investor funds the deposit to title only when no end buyer is lined up. Otherwise the end buyer sends it, and the investor gets the money back with the assignment fee. Sections four and five of his purchase agreement let him cancel by written notice, even a text or email, inside the inspection period, and the escrow agent then releases the deposit with no further written instructions [YO8RBYT_lQ0 @ 00:01:31]. He also scripts a no-deposit pitch to sellers: a deposit delays the process and limits the cash offer [YO8RBYT_lQ0 @ 00:03:19]. The Idaho agent cuts the all-contingencies-cleared clause from the seven-day default to one day before close to protect financed buyers [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:17:01]. On a 100% VA loan where the buyer cannot source cash, he writes a $500 deposit rather than let $5,000 appear out of nowhere [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:22:02]. Larry Mastropieri's team runs around 40 active deals and reads every contractual deadline aloud on an 8 a.m. operations call. When a seller asks for a 14-day loan commitment, his team gets the lender on the phone and makes them confirm it two or three times [CSHBzTZo3Yc @ 00:02:51]. JAG Real Estate says setting these timelines is how they have beaten multiple all-cash offers [E9HjXJUTsZc @ 00:10:51].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Stage and season the cash
   - Move deposit funds into a bank account well before going under contract.
   - Cash in a wall safe should go into the bank 60 days before going under contract, so the lender can source it and the deposit credit is not backed out [The Treasure Valley Home Show — 5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:24:01]
2. Fund by wire inside the delivery window
   - Wire the deposit to the named escrow holder within three business days of acceptance.
   - The deposit is due within three business days of the accepted offer, and most parties want it by wire transfer so it can be tracked [JAG Real Estate Lifestyle — E9HjXJUTsZc @ 00:02:39]
3. Run a daily deadline call
   - Put every contingency date on a shared calendar and review it every morning.
   - Read off every contractual deadline on the 8 a.m. operations call and flag each one good, bad or needs action, so no date slips [Larry Mastropieri — CSHBzTZo3Yc @ 00:01:41]
4. Extend or cancel before the window closes
   - If a contingency cannot be satisfied on time, ask for an extension in writing, and cancel if it is refused.
   - If the loan is not approved within the 21-day window, ask for an extension. If the listing side refuses, cancel to get the deposit back without drama [Win-Win Realtors TV — y9awo1XfRU4 @ 00:03:31]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The deposit number itself is where operators split hardest. Kristie Dimarakis runs a flat 3% script, pegging a $650,000 offer at a $19,500 good faith deposit as the default way to show the seller you are serious about buying their home [NwZ7Oa8GUXQ @ 00:00:38]. The Treasure Valley team in Idaho works off roughly 1% on resale and spec homes, about $5,000 on a half-million-dollar house, and will deliberately write just $500 on a 100% VA deal when the buyer's account does not show the cash, rather than letting a borrowed $5,000 suddenly appear and trigger a sourcing fight later [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:22:02]. Charles Nedder sits at the opposite pole: Greenwich custom is 10% at contract signing, which means a $200,000 wire on a $2 million home, often within five days of an accepted offer, and the deal is not secure until that wire lands [xyiyeiBkCqQ @ 00:01:49]. Robert Greenblatt argues the dollar figure is legally trivial, since a New Jersey contract can technically be held with $1 of consideration, but psychologically decisive: a buyer with $1,000 at risk may walk the moment nerves hit, while a buyer with $20,000 sitting in escrow is far less likely to bail, so the amount itself sends the seller a commitment message [CCWuS-x3COc @ 00:00:58].

Operators also split on who sits on the money and what it takes to pry it loose. Katie Anderson routes Pensacola deposits to the title company or closing attorney within three business days, and warns that if the parties will not sign a release form even when the contract is clear, the funds sit in that escrow account through mediation and, rarely, court [NMjK2yG_6ys @ 00:02:41]. Charles Nedder runs it attorney-to-attorney: the buyer's attorney forwards the deposit with the signed contract to the seller's attorney, and once contingencies lapse both attorneys must agree before a cent moves [xyiyeiBkCqQ @ 00:03:07]. Robert Greenblatt states the escrow rule bluntly: the holder cannot release unless both parties agree, and a buyer who just wants out after inspection under the standard agreement of sale does not get an automatic refund, it becomes a negotiation [CCWuS-x3COc @ 00:01:39]. The One Percent wholesaling crew writes around that problem in their own paper: section five of their purchase and sale agreement says that if the buyer cancels during the inspection period, no further written instructions are required for the title company or title attorney to release the deposit back, no questions asked, which turns the refund into a paperwork formality [YO8RBYT_lQ0 @ 00:02:47].

The sharpest strategic split is whether to stretch contingency windows or compress them. The Treasure Valley team notes Idaho's default clears all contingencies seven days before close, so on a 30-day escrow a loan problem discovered on day 22 leaves the buyer exposed; their fix is to collapse that window to one day in their offers, while admitting a listing agent may push back because sellers want to know sooner [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:17:01]. Daryl Williams works the default 17-day review and 21-day loan periods, and if financing is not approved in time, asks for an extension and cancels outright if the listing side refuses [y9awo1XfRU4 @ 00:03:31]. JAG Real Estate Lifestyle plays the other direction: tighten every timeline using trusted vendors so the offer reads as good as cash, a method they say has beaten multiple all-cash offers, including on their own purchase [E9HjXJUTsZc @ 00:10:51]. Larry Mastropieri splits the difference when a seller demands a 14-day loan commitment: the team gets the lender on the phone, confirms the buyer can deliver documents by tomorrow, and makes an outside lender repeat two or three times that the deadline is a non-issue with no excuses [CSHBzTZo3Yc @ 00:02:51].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common way practitioners watch deposits die is a blown clock rather than a bad house. Katie Anderson lays out the textbook loss: a buyer with a 15-day right to inspect and cancel who changes their mind on day 20, or simply gets spooked by the inspection report, has walked outside the contract and the money should go to the seller [NMjK2yG_6ys @ 00:01:20]. The One Percent crew gives wholesalers the same math on a 14-day inspection period: let day 15 or 16 pass without canceling or finding a buyer and you are technically liable to hand that deposit to the seller, and you are not getting it back [YO8RBYT_lQ0 @ 00:02:05]. Daryl Williams adds the post-removal trap, warning that once you and your agent remove contingencies, a change of heart about the property for whatever reason means you say bye bye to your deposit [y9awo1XfRU4 @ 00:04:16]. The Treasure Valley team stresses that contingencies do not only clear when you sign them off; they clear just by missing the date, which is why their team runs a hyperdetailed, calendar-driven transaction coordinator so no deadline slips and the earnest money stays out of jeopardy [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:36:30].

The second killer is stripping protections in the heat of a bidding war. Andrew Finney has watched buyers in multiple-bid fights drop the loan contingency, then the inspection contingency, then everything, which leaves them on the hook for the house whether they can afford it or not; lose a job or get caught in a one-to-two-month transfer gap and the lender treats them as unqualified [ND7J57hzroE @ 00:03:05]. Finney also prices the appraisal waiver: on a $400,000 list price appraising at $390,000, waiving means agreeing upfront to pay $10,000 over appraised value instead of renegotiating or splitting the gap five and five [ND7J57hzroE @ 00:04:27]. Chase Hodson, the Treasure Valley lender, describes a colleague whose verbal verification of employment within 24 to 48 hours of signing revealed the borrower had lost their job, before the employer had even told the borrower, and the lender had to break the news at the signing table [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:14:36]. Ed Kaminski shows what an intact appraisal contingency buys: on a $1 million purchase appraising at $900,000, the buyer can cancel and recover the deposit, renegotiate the price, or bring more down payment and still close [p5RGCN-UQL8 @ 00:01:06].

The last cluster of losses happens outside the contingency calendar entirely. Chase Hodson warns that cash pulled from a wall safe or mattress and deposited the day before the earnest money wire is still nonsourceable, because lenders on conventional, FHA, USDA and Fannie or Freddie backed loans want 60 days of history; if the deposit cannot be sourced, that credit gets backed out and must be replaced with funds under the same scrutiny, so the fix is to bank the cash 60 days before going under contract [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:24:01]. The Treasure Valley host flags wire fraud: scammers send emails that look identical to title, so wire only on instructions straight from the title company, call them yourself, and know that once five grand is intercepted it can kill the deal [5lKK7iczAKk @ 00:38:50]. Charles Nedder notes that a disputed release after contingencies expire keeps the deposit frozen in escrow until both attorneys agree or a court decides, a process that can take months [xyiyeiBkCqQ @ 00:03:22]. JAG Real Estate Lifestyle hands every buyer a wire fraud advisory alongside the investigation advisory for exactly that reason, with an instruction to call the team if anything about transferring money is unclear [E9HjXJUTsZc @ 00:07:19].
