# Chapter 03: Understand Inventor Royalty Economics

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree that licensing means renting your rights to a company that already owns manufacturing and distribution, then collecting royalties without running a business. The rate band is tight. UpCounsel cites 3 to 6% of gross sales. Stephen Key and Andrew Krauss see about 5% most often, seven or eight when CAD, testing, or tooling is done, and over 10% only for Disney-level brands. The street mechanics are cheap: a $25 Kinko's prototype produced a contract in three days and $100,000 in first-year royalties, a three-hour toy built from shop parts generated about $10 million, and Key files provisional applications for $65. They split on tactics. Sef Chang lets the company crunch its numbers before naming a percentage. J.D. Houvener walks in with written walk-away terms. Jeff Schell trades royalty points for licensee-funded enforcement.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner in this lane describes the same core trade: the inventor keeps the idea and rents the rights to a company that already owns manufacturing and distribution. Brian Fried has collected royalty checks for over 18 years. Fried first pushes an idea through a search, a patentability opinion, a prototype, and 3-D CAD. Then the intellectual property goes to a company that will manufacture it, sell it through internal or third-party sales reps, and pay a royalty [UnGdjQb467o @ 00:00:59]. Patent attorney Jeff Schell frames the same deal as a permission grant in exchange for a fee or royalty. Schell calls it a win-win, because the licensee gets to sell the patented invention legally [cfPpWt8cw64 @ 00:00:33]. J.D. Houvener explains why the model beats building a company for most inventors. Raising money, hiring employees, taking manufacturing risk, selling to strangers, and carrying HR paperwork is a ton of effort, and many inventors already have full-time jobs and children [2yLgmvphmBw @ 00:03:23]. Stephen Key pushes the risk point hardest: there is no financial risk and you do not have to quit your day job. Key's wife Janice took a full-time job for the health insurance while Key learned to license, and the family moved from simple ideas to big ones that built a large revenue stream [hbfKyomYp6U @ 00:02:59].

On the headline number, the speakers cluster tightly in the low single digits. UpCounsel puts typical patent royalty rates at 3 to 6% of gross sales. The rate swings with industry, the uniqueness of the invention, and the strength of the patent [mtxmo_OkhSw @ 00:00:30]. Stephen Key and Andrew Krauss have coached students for years. They peg the most common consumer-product rate at about 5% and drop it toward three for huge-volume consumables. The rate climbs to seven or eight when the inventor has already done CAD drawings, testing, a prototype, or even tooling. They also warn that over 10% is Disney territory, citing a secondhand report of a 20% rate on The Lion King at its peak [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:05:09]. Sef Chang, interviewed by The Patent Professor, gives a generic range of one or two up to five percent. Chang acknowledges that some licensors land 14, 15, or 20, but says the companies themselves often do not know the right number until they do the math [bO09tLv7ysM @ 00:00:20]. Brian Fried specifies the base: the royalty is typically a percentage of the wholesale price the licensee charges the retail buyer. Fried is paid quarterly and gets sell-through reports showing units sold by store and by product variation [UnGdjQb467o @ 00:01:58]. Double digits show up on the record only as outliers or big-brand deals. The practitioners never treat them as the opening position for an independent inventor.

The bigger consensus is that the rate alone is a vanity number. Krauss argues that a half-percent royalty on a bazillion units can beat a fat rate on thin volume. Krauss adds that 5% on half a million units a year may be extremely generous. Key's formula has three inputs: volume, royalty rate, and price point, whether the product sells for $500, $19, or 99 cents. Key runs all three through a calculator before signing anything [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:07:09]. Exclusivity is the second lever everyone names. Schell ranks the payouts this way. Exclusive licenses usually return the highest royalties. Sole licenses sit in the middle, because the inventor keeps the right to use the invention. Non-exclusive deals pay less per licensee but let multiple payers stack [cfPpWt8cw64 @ 00:01:08]. Houvener calls the exclusive versus non-exclusive choice a huge price negotiation. Houvener also stresses that the license term is rarely the full twenty-year patent life, because both sides want to return to the table if the market takes off or stalls [ZbJhX8I0koA @ 00:04:12]. The third shared truth is patience. Key says money does not start at signing. The company must make the product, ship it, and get it on the shelf, and then distribution has to grow. Royalties really add up after a year, a year and a half, or two years [Se-TzTVqhfY @ 00:06:40]. Operators who need cash next quarter should keep a day job funding the pipeline.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Key's street method is to spend almost nothing until a company says yes. For the Michael Jordan Wall Ball, Key made the prototype at Kinko's using a $15 Ohio Art backboard and a $10 poster, roughly $25 total. Key had a licensing agreement three days later, and the product earned about $100,000 in first-year royalties and sold for 10 years [gxeuCPaGeHY @ 00:04:08]. The Sweet Darts arrow needed no prototype and no intellectual property filing at all. It paid about $10,000 for one Valentine's season and never sold again [Se-TzTVqhfY @ 00:02:53]. Brian Walker built the original Light Chaser in three hours from parts already sitting in the shop. Walker assumed the $5,000 advance would be the only money ever seen. Instead the toy reached an estimated seven to eight hundred million dollars at retail across more than a hundred versions [WEVVJ111b8o @ 00:04:13]. Key's current cost stack is even lower. An AI prototype costs nothing, and 3D printing lets you Frankenstein a model together. Key uses AI to write a provisional patent application and file it at the USPTO for $65 [hM7gpPqIDoQ @ 00:17:40]. The workhorse tool is the sell sheet, a one-page advertisement that sells the benefit. Key uses it to gauge company interest before any real money moves on prototypes or patents.

In the negotiation room, Sef Chang refuses to name a percentage first. Chang asks only whether the company is interested. The company then calls manufacturers, gets quotes, and crunches its own profit. Next Chang asks for the cost rundown and moves the needle back and forth. If the percentage lands low, Chang negotiates for something else that compensates [bO09tLv7ysM @ 00:01:06]. Krauss names those other levers. If the company offers three but you wanted four, push the minimum guarantees higher. Require guaranteed sales figures with the rights coming back if they are missed, or have the company pay for your patents [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:03:20]. Houvener tells inventors to walk in with two plans: the perfect deal and the walk-away terms, such as at least four percent royalty or an exclusive grant. Holding those terms in hand gives you posture and keeps you professional instead of emotional [ZbJhX8I0koA @ 00:02:26]. Schell offers a trade most rookies miss. Under an exclusive arrangement, accept slightly lower royalties in exchange for putting the expensive obligation to enforce the patent on the licensee [cfPpWt8cw64 @ 00:02:52]. Houvener also carves deals surgically. One option is licensing claims one through ten to one party and claims eleven through twenty to another. Another is splitting make and sell rights across Canada, the US, and Mexico.

The unit math is simple and brutal. Key's chart starts with the number of stores the licensee is in. Assume each store sells one unit a week, because anything slower gets kicked to the curb. Then run the result at 3, 4, 5, and 6% across chains like Walmart, Target, Lowe's, and Home Depot [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:02:17]. Krauss contrasts the do-it-yourself alternative. You could sell 5,000 to 20,000 units yourself at a 20% profit margin while working 60 to 80 hour weeks. Or a licensee could move half a million units a year while you do no work [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:04:09]. At the top end, Key's rotating label paid royalties for over 15 years and made about $10 million. It ran on Nescafe, Jim Beam, and Lawry's, with sublicenses in Japan and Ireland [hM7gpPqIDoQ @ 00:13:06]. Walker's Light Chaser checks climbed from $75,000 to $180,000, $260,000, and $340,000 in the breakout year. Later checks reached $250,000 to $350,000 per quarter, but a third of roughly $10 million went to an agent [WEVVJ111b8o @ 00:05:41]. Splits cut the take too. The Wall Ball was a split royalty, with partner Russell Hicks taking half. Its final check, for 53 cents, was never cashed.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Validate with a sell sheet before spending
   - Build a one-page advertisement selling the benefit and send it to target companies before any prototype or patent spend.
   - Gauge interest first, and build a prototype only when a company wants proof of concept, because prototyping every idea burns too much time and money [inventRightTV — INuH7fr5H40 @ 00:04:26]
2. File a well-written provisional application
   - Draft and file a provisional patent application that includes manufacturing notes, workarounds, variations, materials, and good drawings.
   - A provisional with enough detail to show how the product could be made carries value with companies, and it costs far less than filing full patents on every idea [inventRightTV — INuH7fr5H40 @ 00:07:33]
3. Run the royalty math on the licensee
   - Multiply projected annual units by price point by royalty rate, and test 3% through 6% against the licensee's real store count.
   - Get a calculator out and model the units at that price and rate. The numbers may reveal that the licensee lacks the volume to be worth an exclusive [inventRightTV — 5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:07:23]
4. Negotiate from a written walk-away list
   - Enter the meeting with your perfect-deal terms and your deal breakers written down. Then trade on minimum guarantees, term, territory, and enforcement.
   - Having goals and walk-away terms in hand gives you posture and keeps you professional and unemotional across the negotiation [J.D. Houvener / Bold Patents Law Firm — ZbJhX8I0koA @ 00:02:26]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The number everyone wants is the royalty rate, and the operators do not quote the same one. UpCounsel pins the average at 3 to 6% of gross sales, with the figure moving on industry, uniqueness of the invention and strength of the patent [mtxmo_OkhSw @ 00:00:30]. Sef Chang, interviewed on The Patent Professor, gives a lower generic band of two percent, maybe even one, up to five, while noting that some people have landed 14, 15 or 20 and warning that even the companies themselves do not really know the right number until they do the math [bO09tLv7ysM @ 00:00:20]. Stephen Key and Andrew Krauss at inventRightTV call about 5% the most common rate they see, drop high-volume consumables toward three, push it to seven or eight if you have done CAD drawings, testing, a prototype or even tooling, and say it is hard to get over 10 because Disney gets over 10 and you are not Disney [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:05:24]. The base also differs: Brian Fried says his royalty is typically a percentage of the wholesale price the licensee charges the buyer, paid quarterly with sell-through reports by store and variation [UnGdjQb467o @ 00:01:58]. A quoted percentage means nothing until you pin whether it rides on gross sales or wholesale, and how much development you already absorbed for the company.

Negotiating posture splits the camp. Sef Chang refuses to open with a number: he asks whether the company is interested, lets it contact manufacturers, pull quotes and compute its own profit, then demands the cost rundown and moves the needle back and forth until both sides are comfortable, trading for something else of value if the percentage lands low [bO09tLv7ysM @ 00:01:24]. J.D. Houvener of Bold Patents takes the opposite stance and walks in armed: a perfect-deal target plus hard walk-away terms such as at least four percent royalty or an exclusive grant, which he says gives you posture and keeps you professional and unemotional at the table [ZbJhX8I0koA @ 00:02:45]. Stephen Key and Andrew Krauss land in between, warning that the rate is only one lever; if the company says three and you want four, you can recover it through higher minimum guarantees, guaranteed sales figures that return the rights if missed, or having the licensee pay for your patents [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:03:20]. Jeff Schell adds a fourth trade the others do not price: under an exclusive arrangement, settle for slightly lower royalties if it moves the expensive obligation to enforce the patent onto the licensee [cfPpWt8cw64 @ 00:02:52]. Across all four, the percentage is currency to spend, not the scoreboard itself.

Exclusivity and exit route are where the playbooks fork hardest. Jeff Schell ranks the grants cleanly: an exclusive license locks out everyone including the patent holder and usually pays the highest royalties, a sole license leaves you and one licensee in the market for a middle rate, and a non-exclusive license lets multiple companies pay you at less per licensee [cfPpWt8cw64 @ 00:01:08]. Sef Chang treats exclusivity as a walk-away issue: if he knows one company does a good job on one side of the market and another owns the other side, he will refuse an exclusive and leave rather than give it up [bO09tLv7ysM @ 00:02:51]. On building versus licensing, Andrew Krauss frames it as pure arithmetic, a 20% profit margin on 5,000 to 20,000 units while working 60 to 80 hour weeks against a licensee moving half a million units a year while you do no work [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:04:09]. J.D. Houvener describes the third route, an outright sale, as freeing you from monitoring the licensee but warns the buyer may sit on or shelve the patent, so the product never ships and you may have negotiated away your own ability to iterate in that space [2yLgmvphmBw @ 00:07:18]. Choose the route by volume, by control, and by how much business you actually want to run.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Stephen Key is blunt that not every license pays. His novelty run produced Sweet Darts, a plastic suction-cup arrow that sold during one Valentine's season for about $10,000, plus back-to-school spinner pins, Cool Pals lunch-box ice packs and roughly 30 plush characters that each landed around $10,000 or less [gxeuCPaGeHY @ 00:00:36]. He says the Sweet Darts money never repeated and that seasonal deals felt too much like work, which is why he did not like that industry very much [Se-TzTVqhfY @ 00:03:14]. Even his winners carried haircuts: the Michael Jordan Wall Ball was a split royalty where his partner took half [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:05:09]. Brian Walker on inventRightTV shows the other end of the curve. His light chaser, built in three hours from parts already in his shop, nearly disappeared after he licensed it to SRM Entertainment because it sat unexplained in little boxes on novelty shelves until it moved into Barnum & Bailey, Universal and Disney. He expected his $5,000 advance to be all the money he would ever see, yet it generated about $10 million in royalties, a third of which went to his agent [WEVVJ111b8o @ 00:04:33]. The economics check: price the season, the split and the agent cut before celebrating any headline royalty number, and do not write off a slow starter too early.

Time is the silent cost line. Key says royalties do not start the minute you sign: the licensee has to make it, ship it and get it on the shelf, then you wait a couple of months to collect and longer for distribution to grow, so you are talking a year, a year and a half, or two years before checks really add up [Se-TzTVqhfY @ 00:06:40]. Brian Walker has seen licenses produce royalties in six or seven months but says a year or more is more common, and the drag scales with tooling: his light chaser line needed a roughly 15-cavity mold and about 30 components, while his six-part animating product can reach production far faster [WEVVJ111b8o @ 00:12:56]. Walker also burned his own protection, filing about six provisional patents and letting every one expire because he was too lazy to push them within the one-year window [WEVVJ111b8o @ 00:11:22]. Key's counter-discipline is to never pour serious money into one favorite idea, and he warns that choosing the wrong idea means years go by without income; if you honestly count up all the time spent, you are not saving money on a budget, you are losing it [hM7gpPqIDoQ @ 00:19:05]. Budget the calendar as hard as the cash.

Most self-inflicted losses happen at the table or after the ink dries. Krauss says many students arrive after toploading a deal with demands like $100,000 upfront plus a 20% royalty, asks that are just not practical and that come from not knowing what the other side is doing [5pNsi_2H-J4 @ 00:01:06]. J.D. Houvener warns newcomers who assume the license runs for the full twenty-year patent life; both sides want to return to the table after a commercially reasonable period, and since there are no patent police, the owner must hunt infringers and get an exclusive licensee contractually on the hook to help [ZbJhX8I0koA @ 00:06:10]. In a separate briefing he flags the version-two trap, where the manufacturer develops an improved variant during tooling or fabrication and tries to claim it unless the contract settles who owns what, alongside terminating if the licensee sits on the product [2yLgmvphmBw @ 00:05:03]. Key admits he dropped the ball himself after traveling for six months and coming home to find things with his licensees not quite right, and he insists on a recap email after every conversation and never going around the person you work with [hbfKyomYp6U @ 00:05:56]. A signed license is a business you still have to run.
