Adam Diament and Patents Demystified mine assignment records for owners, collateral, and licensing contacts. The split: Edel dismisses Quick Search that Erinnae Baker teaches, while inventRightTV's Andrew hedges on going fully patent-free.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Across the licensing camp the baseline is blunt: an issued patent is not the entry ticket. inventRightTV states that 99% of the students taught over the last 15 years licensed product ideas without a patent [CS31715RNQk @ 00:01:31], and the same channel says flatly that you do not need a patent to license an idea, because companies will not defend one in federal court and 'you're not going to sue everybody' [fyUYT-gfzfU @ 00:00:36]. What replaces the patent, in the same camp's words, is perceived ownership, built with a provisional patent application that buys one year of patent-pending status. The USPTO's own outreach talk confirms the mechanics behind that strategy: a provisional lasts 12 months and is never examined [binARAl4AK8 @ 00:28:18]. John Ferrell frames the same discipline from the deal side, insisting you never pursue a patent without a business reason and that you walk into any manufacturer meeting already knowing your price, or you end up inside someone else's agenda [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:01:28]. The common thread is that protection is a commercial lever, sized to the deal, rather than a trophy filed for its own sake before any company has shown interest.

The second point of agreement is that research comes before filing, and that it starts cheap. inventRightTV's first requirement is a genuinely new idea, checked against Google Image and Google Shopping until a clear point of difference shows up [fyUYT-gfzfU @ 00:01:25]. Erinnae Baker positions the USPTO database for exactly the same job: pulling technical specs, seeing whether an idea has already been done, and judging how similar existing patents are, with full text running from 1976 and partial data reaching back to 1790 [Awpsichbouw @ 00:02:37]. Edel Patents adds the mindset that separates a useful search from a wasted afternoon: 'search for the concept not the word,' because the document you need 'lies at the intersection of a few concepts' [SDXhhmDKGJo @ 00:03:09]. Intellectual Property Ontario's analyst Dilshay Singh draws the same line between modes, reserving basic search for known items, names and dates, and pushing anything conceptual into the advanced command-line syntax documented under the Help tab [2L5qDLM_EkY @ 00:02:30]. Put together, the practitioners treat search as the cheapest insurance in the whole chain: it tells you whether a point of difference exists before a single dollar goes into a provisional or a pitch.

The third consensus is about who actually owns the asset you are about to license. Patents Demystified starts from the default: inventors own the rights in a patent or application, with no work-for-hire rule like copyright, so ownership only moves through employment, partnership or collaboration agreements [zel57Kej5nY @ 00:01:05]. Idea2PatentAI repeats that rights initially belong to the inventors and treats recording an inventor assignment through Assignment Center as the optional last step of a provisional filing [ixENhsfAdEA @ 00:05:52]. Adam Diament explains why that optional step matters: recording is technically optional but highly recommended, because the first party to record usually wins a dispute, and unrecorded company assignments create headaches during fundraising and acquisitions [k0S01jxvk6M @ 00:03:00]. John Ferrell closes the loop on the licensing side with a hard rule against permanently assigning patents, copyrights, trademarks or trade secrets to a manufacturer, since a bankrupt licensee would then own your rights; an exclusive license, he says, is fine [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:02:24]. The operator takeaway is consistent across all four: know exactly whose name sits on title, record every transfer you make, and license rights out rather than selling them.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the search engine itself, the mechanics get specific. Edel Patents tells beginners to skip Quick Search entirely, build each concept from OR-joined synonyms, stack concepts with AND so that every AND shortens the list, wrap niche phrases in quotes, and lean on grouping because 'the search engine really likes parentheses' [SDXhhmDKGJo @ 00:04:08]. USPTO examiner Edward puts a number on that advice: the same dog-and-bowl example returns 316 hits without parentheses and 46 with them [np8RKbVMSLg @ 00:23:30]. In Patent Public Search Advanced the index sits at the end wrapped in periods, as in (dog and bowl).TI., legacy syntax will not retrieve accurate results, and document-number fields need seven characters, so you pad with leading zeros because the PN button will not add them for you [np8RKbVMSLg @ 00:14:03]. Edward's design-only filters, S.AT. or S.KD., each cut his example down to 31 hits, and date windows run in formats such as @AY>=2014. Dilshay Singh of Intellectual Property Ontario works the same grammar from the analyst side, using .ab. for abstract, .as. for assignee, @pd>= for a date range, NEAR n for bidirectional proximity and * as a wildcard [2L5qDLM_EkY @ 00:03:34].

Finding a specific application is its own minefield. Edel Patents breaks the number into a two-digit series code plus a six-digit serial written as 2 digits, slash, 3 digits, comma, 3 digits, and warns that the filing receipt carries two eight-digit numbers at the top, so grabbing the wrong one means 'looking for a patent application that doesn't exist' [oOln2IDvhqE @ 00:01:47]. The USPTO search rejects full application numbers outright, so his reliable path runs from the USPTO site to Patents, Find It Fast and Public PAIR, adding a slash after the first two digits if it fails and simply retrying on errors; PatentScope is fast, while Google Patents is fast but fails more often [oOln2IDvhqE @ 00:06:09]. Once inside, the Image File Wrapper holds the prosecution history, and the move he singles out is comparing claim 1 of the pre-grant publication against the granted claim, highlighting the added language, because 'there's a really good chance you just ran your highlighter over one of the most important weaknesses in the patent' [oOln2IDvhqE @ 00:07:21]. Adam Diament notes provisionals are harder still, requiring the application number to surface at all [k0S01jxvk6M @ 00:06:58].

The filing and deal numbers are where the economics land. Idea2PatentAI says the provisional upload itself takes under 30 minutes in Patent Center, using a free USPTO account with identity verification, the specification, drawings, an Application Data Sheet it calls the most important form, and a micro entity certification, with fees paid the same day: $130 small or $65 micro, on Eastern time [ixENhsfAdEA @ 00:05:22]. The USPTO's Marilyn Nash lists the full ladder: provisional fees of $325 large, $130 small and $65 micro, nonprovisional fees of $400 to $2,000, and attorney fees of roughly $6,000 to $18,000 [binARAl4AK8 @ 00:29:33]. inventRightTV prices a full patent at about $15k against products that mostly sell for 2 to 3 years while examination runs 1.5 to 4 years, then hands over the contract mechanics: open up the grant of license to fold in improvements, keep filing new provisionals to stay patent pending, and tier the royalty at 7% if granted, 5% if pending and 1% if it never issues [CS31715RNQk @ 00:11:10]. John Ferrell's example royalty is 5 cents per widget, backed by an annual minimum that ends the contract and returns all rights to the inventor if missed [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:03:02].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Validate the point of difference
   - Run Google Image and Google Shopping checks, then a concept-based USPTO search before spending on any filing.
   - Confirm a visible point of difference on Google Image and Google Shopping [inventRightTV — fyUYT-gfzfU @ 00:01:25], then search for the concept rather than the word by grouping OR-joined synonyms per concept [Edel Patents — SDXhhmDKGJo @ 00:00:56].
2. Run the structured Advanced query
   - Build parenthesized concept groups with end-position indexes in Patent Public Search Advanced.
   - Put the index at the end wrapped in periods, drop legacy syntax because it will not retrieve accurate results, and pad PN and DID fields to seven characters with leading zeros [USPTOvideo — np8RKbVMSLg @ 00:15:25].
3. Map ownership and the licensing contact
   - Pull assignment records for every close patent and identify the owners behind them.
   - Use the USPTO patent assignment search, which needs no account and is often your starting point for a license [Patents and IP with Adam Diament — k0S01jxvk6M @ 00:03:43], or open Application events, Show all events and the USPTO assignment link in Google Patents to reach the signed PDF [Patents Demystified — zel57Kej5nY @ 00:04:44].
4. File the provisional and set the deal floor
   - Upload the provisional in Patent Center, pay the fee the same day, then anchor royalty terms before pitching.
   - Pay $130 small or $65 micro on the same day as submission, on Eastern time [Idea2PatentAI — ixENhsfAdEA @ 00:05:22], then pitch a tiered royalty of 7% if granted, 5% if pending and 1% if it never issues [inventRightTV — CS31715RNQk @ 00:11:10].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Search and Ownership Edges Most Inventors Miss**
> 
> Small moves practitioners use to pull hidden ownership data and avoid false negatives during invention research.
> - Abstract searches narrow so fast they can drop important documents, so loosen the abstract filter before concluding nothing exists [Edel Patents — SDXhhmDKGJo @ 00:04:28].
> - Assignees printed on the face of a patent are only current as of the issue date, so pull the assignment record for the present owner [USPTOvideo — np8RKbVMSLg @ 00:10:51].
> - Reel/frame numbers in assignment records expose bulk deals such as 50 patents moving at once, and assignor searches reveal behavior patterns [Patents and IP with Adam Diament — k0S01jxvk6M @ 00:04:40].
> - A PNC Bank entry in a Tesla chain was a security agreement, meaning the application was bank-loan collateral; hover over entries to spot simple name changes like Tesla Inc [Patents Demystified — zel57Kej5nY @ 00:06:15].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to pay for a patent before pitching. inventRightTV goes all-in on no: Stephen reports that 99% of the students Stephen and Andrew taught over 15 years licensed without a patent [CS31715RNQk @ 00:01:31], and after defending six-plus patents against a toy company in federal court concluded that patents are just words. The replacement is perceived ownership through a provisional, with the licensee negotiated into paying for the expensive non-provisional [fyUYT-gfzfU @ 00:03:13]. Even inside that camp the line bends: Andrew hedges with I wouldn't say no when asked whether patents matter, and big ideas still demand a wall of patents. John Ferrell, Esq. frames the question as a business test rather than a creed, opening with the rule that nobody should get a patent without a business reason [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:00:00]. Marilyn Nash of USPTOvideo puts numbers on what both camps are reacting to: provisional fees of $325 large, $130 small and $65 micro, nonprovisional fees of $400 to $2,000, and attorney fees running about $6,000 to $18,000 [binARAl4AK8 @ 00:29:33]. inventRightTV's own dividing line holds: small consumer products that sell for two to three years ride a provisional and shelf space, while big ideas justify the full patent wall.

Search-tool advice splits just as hard. Erinnae Baker walks beginners through Quick Search with Boolean pairs like bicycle AND speedometer and speedometer NOT automobile, and warns that the advanced search is pretty difficult to use [Awpsichbouw @ 00:02:56]. Edel Patents rejects that starting point outright: Quick Search rarely works for patent searching and is not worth your time, so build every query in Advanced Search around concepts made of OR-joined synonyms, wrapped in parentheses and joined by AND [SDXhhmDKGJo @ 00:00:18]. Dilshay Singh of Intellectual Property Ontario splits the difference, keeping basic search for known items, names and dates, as in assignee Apple AND inventor jobs returning 617 records, and reserving the command-line advanced mode for concept work such as disc brakes from five named assignees published 2015 to 2022 [2L5qDLM_EkY @ 00:00:39]. On speed versus reliability for pulling a known application, Edel Patents prefers Public PAIR and flags that Google Patents is very fast but unsuccessful more often [oOln2IDvhqE @ 00:06:09], while Patents Demystified builds its whole ownership-history workflow inside Google Patents through Application events, Show all events and the USPTO assignment link. Edel Patents boils it down: fast is only great if it works.

Ownership transfer is where the deal camps diverge. John Ferrell, Esq. treats a permanent assignment of patents, copyrights, trademarks or trade secrets as a rookie mistake, because if the manufacturer goes bankrupt it owns your rights, so license them, exclusively if needed, and never transfer outright [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:02:24]. Patents Demystified starts from the opposite direction: inventors own by default, there is no work-for-hire rule as with copyright, so companies pull rights in through employment, partnership and collaboration agreements and then record them to perfect title like a county deed [zel57Kej5nY @ 00:01:36]. Idea2PatentAI lists recording an inventor assignment through Assignment Center as an optional sixth step after a provisional filing, and Adam Diament calls recording technically optional but highly recommended because the first to record usually wins disputes [k0S01jxvk6M @ 00:03:00]. On royalty structure, inventRightTV skips the fight over whether a patent will ever issue by tiering the payout: seven if granted, five if pending, one if it never issues, while opening the grant of license so new improvements and fresh provisionals keep the inventor patent pending [CS31715RNQk @ 00:11:10]. Ferrell goes further, keeping every improvement, whether suggested by the manufacturer or a customer, with the inventor and insisting the contract be written by a lawyer in the inventor's state rather than pulled from an internet template.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest mistakes happen at the negotiating table. John Ferrell, Esq. describes the buyer who asks the price and gets silence, which signals you are not ready to talk; walk in without a number and you become part of someone else's agenda [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:01:28]. Ferrell's other traps compound: no annual minimum, leaving a 5-cents-per-widget deal without the clause that ends the contract and sends the rights back to you [Te8GX-Xx4mo @ 00:03:02], and indemnifying the manufacturer for products liability, manufacturing mistakes, late delivery or advertising fraud. Ferrell's rule is blunt: never indemnify the manufacturer. inventRightTV flags industry selection as a silent killer: the tech industry is a terrible industry where those guys don't play fair, and packaging, the speaker's own industry, is just hard, while toy, pet, kitchen and hardware stay open [fyUYT-gfzfU @ 00:02:03]. The litigation path carries its own scar tissue. Stephen defended six-plus patents in federal court against a toy company and walked away convinced patents are just words, the root of the no-patent playbook [CS31715RNQk @ 00:06:32]. Ferrell's final gate is relational: perception is reality, and if a partner does not feel right, back away before anything is signed.

Research failures burn days instead of dollars. Edel Patents warns that the filing receipt carries two eight-digit numbers, and grabbing the wrong one means hunting for an application that doesn't exist, while the USPTO search rejects full application numbers outright [oOln2IDvhqE @ 00:01:47]. Examiner Edward of USPTOvideo stacks more syntax traps: legacy format no longer retrieves accurate results, document and patent number fields need seven characters padded with leading zeros, the PN button will not add those zeros for you, and dropping parentheses turned a 46-hit query into 316 [np8RKbVMSLg @ 00:15:25]. Sessions carry no accounts and the workspace cannot be saved, with a 30-minute idle timeout, a 2-minute warning and a 5-minute grace period. Edel Patents adds that abstract searches narrow so quickly you can lose important documents [SDXhhmDKGJo @ 00:04:28]. Ownership data has its own blind spots. Adam Diament notes that errors recorded in the assignment database last forever, down to a missing comma in Cool Startup, Inc., that many licenses are private and never recorded, and that unrecorded company assignments cause headaches in fundraising and acquisitions [k0S01jxvk6M @ 00:05:38]. Examiner Edward also warns that the assignee printed on a patent is current only as of its issue date.

Filing and practice traps close the loop. Idea2PatentAI stresses that a provisional only protects what it discloses, so leaving out implementation details can impact your rights, and filing fees of $130 small or $65 micro must be paid the same day on Eastern time [ixENhsfAdEA @ 00:05:22]. Marilyn Nash of USPTOvideo resets expectations on timeline and enforcement: office action rejections are normal, first action takes 24 to 36 months unless Track 1 compresses disposition to about 12, and the USPTO is not an enforcement agency [binARAl4AK8 @ 00:35:34]. For anyone running research or filings as a service, Jim Selbach treats unauthorized practice of law as both an ethical violation and a crime, notes that exercising independent judgment counts as advice, and cites a PI lawyer who lost a license over paralegal settlements [0r9cstgrqZM @ 00:06:42]. Patents Demystified passed the patent bar in the first semester of law school and built a solo firm off referrals, then watched a merged downtown firm dissolve over attorney non-attorney profit sharing issues [GOd61uYqq-8 @ 00:01:28]. Berkeley Center for Law & Technology panelists add a retention signal: Adam Cole recommends two-plus years as an agent before law school, and one hire quit after about a year and a half.
