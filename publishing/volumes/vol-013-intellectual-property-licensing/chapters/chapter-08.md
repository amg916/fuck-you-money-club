# Chapter 08: Value and Monetize Patent Portfolios

## Executive Summary

Operators who buy, broker, fund, and assert patents converge on one rule: a patent with no evidence of use and no enforcement threat does not sell and does not license. Funders pass on 19 of every 20 portfolios, brokers accept one or two percent of intake, and money flows to claim-charted, multi-family portfolios backing cases with at least $100M in damages. The street numbers are blunt. US litigation runs $12-20M at full rates. The starting proceeds split is 50/30/20 between owner, funder, and law firm. Broker commissions run 10-30%, and sales take 6-18 months. Qualcomm's 3-5% per-phone royalty and Arm's $2.61B royalty line show the upside. Practitioners split on whether NPEs are essential leverage or a value-draining middleman, and on whether a falling buyer's market still rewards selling over licensing.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who touches money in this market starts from the same premise: a patent is worth what its owner can credibly enforce. Fractus co-founder Carles Puente pivoted from manufacturing millions of antennas to research and licensing. Puente puts it flatly: without enforcing the rights, patents have no value. For Fractus as an SME, that enforcement had to happen in the US, and it was painful and difficult [fAwWxzitmUk @ 00:03:48]. Silicon Valley Business School host David Smith is even blunter: companies usually do not pay patent licenses until they are forced to, so licensing is driven by litigation [hOFtwRua-qc @ 00:05:16]. The Transpacific IP guest on IPWatchdog describes the same reality from the owner side. Their firm runs carrot licensing, but the carrot only works when backed by a heavy stick [qZqv5lEPPQc @ 00:00:25]. Louis Carbonneau of Tangible IP has brokered more than 4,500 patents, and they turn this into a sales law: if there is no evidence that anyone is practicing the patents, they can predict with 100% certainty that the portfolio will not sell [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:34:27]. The operator takeaway is that the asset is evidence of use plus a believable enforcement path. The certificate itself is just paperwork.

The second point of agreement is that the overwhelming majority of granted patents are dead inventory. Berkeley Law & Technology Group cites the figure that roughly 90% of patents never generate revenue, because inventors patent ideas and never commercialize them [oRUwDt8nD5k @ 00:00:00]. Anders Arison of Parallel North IP, a former head of patent acquisitions at Nokia, narrows it further. Over 1 million patents are granted every year, yet only about 5% are monetized, held back by gaps in awareness, funding, and expertise [_bueYwQ3iHE @ 00:10:00]. Silicon Valley Business School frames the same waste as a placement problem: so many patents sit unused because they are essentially in the wrong hands, while the channels to market are held by a few large corporations [vPziFABPW9w @ 00:01:35]. The people who actually write checks confirm how narrow the funnel is. The IPWatchdog funding panel reports that funders pass on 19 of every 20 portfolios. They check damages, venue, marking, prior licenses, grant interests, and market size before they ever look at technical merits [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:23:42]. For an operator, the default outcome for any portfolio is rejection. The job is to prove the asset belongs in the small slice that actually moves.

Practitioners also agree that a single patent rarely carries a campaign. Depth, coverage, and honest internal triage come first. The funding panel moderated by Brad Close warns that one newly issued patent means a portfolio is not ready. The panel urges owners to file multiple families and aim for three to six independent assets [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:32:09]. The host of the Transpacific IP conversation goes further, estimating that a portfolio of 10 good patents requires at least 100. The guest adds that a few patents only work in support of an operating company [qZqv5lEPPQc @ 00:25:01]. Inside an operating company, Dean Keebler of Samtec runs every patent through three tests. Does it cover our product or a competitor's? Will it survive litigation? Will a third party think it survives? If not, Keebler questions its value [1UEOTCZ_qYA @ 00:04:56]. Useless Money adds the commercial filter that separates paper from cash flow: legal protection and commercial demand are two different things, and no universal royalty rate applies to every patent, brand, or software product [xkXjle5Spjw @ 00:05:23]. The baseline prerequisite is a portfolio deep enough to survive invalidity attacks and mapped to real products before anyone spends a dollar on outreach.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The cost of an enforcement campaign is where most owners get sticker shock. On the IPWatchdog economics panel moderated by Brad Close, a US case at full rates runs $12-20M. Litigation finance is non-recourse, so investors lose their capital on a loss. Firms defer 40-50% of their fees, which drops a $12M case to about $6M, while the funder still covers $2-3M in hard costs [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:05:41]. The same panel's starting split is 50% to the patent owner, 30% to the funder, and 20% to the law firm, and funding is reserved for damages of at least $100M. Louis Carbonneau prices a suit against Samsung or Apple at $3-5M and says plaintiffs win roughly one in three or one in four times, so plaintiffs must choose cases very selectively [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:56:03]. Defending the asset costs real money too. The Transpacific IP conversation puts a PTAB defense at $300K to about $1M [qZqv5lEPPQc @ 00:46:07]. David Smith prices an IPR at about $250,000 and expert time at $356-478 per hour, based on more than 35,000 cases [hOFtwRua-qc @ 00:07:16]. The operator math is simple: unless the damages pool dwarfs a multi-million-dollar spend, no funder or contingency firm will carry the case.

Selling or brokering runs on its own price list and calendar. UpCounsel reports that most brokers work on a commission of 10 to 30% of the sale price, and that most transactions take 6 to 18 months. The deal moves from a sales package to redacted outreach to a purchase agreement with independent legal review [xRRc9i7j5A8 @ 00:04:11]. Anders Arison quantifies the glut. Asking prices total about $44B against roughly $12-13B actually sold. Brokers represented about 70% of patents and 87% of packages, and it is now a buyer's market with falling prices [_bueYwQ3iHE @ 00:16:24]. Louis Carbonneau's vetting order mirrors a funder's: assertion potential using litigation-grade claim charts, then validity including priority and section 101, then likely targets, then damages. The blunt rule is that $1 million in damages against $5 million to litigate does not sell [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:35:13]. J.D. Houvener, who claims about 500 patents over 10 years, says hiring a broker is better if you can afford it. A broker takes the emotionally invested inventor out of the talks, and buyers lower their guard with a professional across the table [a7AlJrwA2Jo @ 00:01:15]. Price, timing, and representation are set in the broker agreement before the first buyer call. UpCounsel says that agreement covers exclusivity, compensation, and duration.

The royalty structures that pay at scale are surprisingly simple. The Strategy Breakdown reports that Qualcomm collects 3 to 5% of the wholesale price of nearly every phone. Qualcomm refuses pass-through: chipmakers license the IP just to build the chip, and OEMs pay a separate device-level royalty on the full wholesale price. Its licensing arm, QTL, is about 20% of revenue but 60% of profits [uNc4jO7TeCo @ 00:05:14]. Kingacademic UK describes Arm's two-layer model: an upfront license fee opens access, and a royalty is paid on each shipped product. In FY2026 that produced royalty revenue of $2.61B, up 21%, and license and other revenue of $2.31B, up 25% [aRUsXPICxpY @ 00:01:23]. Useless Money breaks down InterDigital: about $834M of 2025 revenue and $461M of operating income. A July 2025 Samsung arbitration result of $1.05B covers January 2023 to December 2030, which the company equated to roughly $131M in recurring revenue per year [xkXjle5Spjw @ 00:06:53]. For inventors feeding an NPE, David Smith insists on gross revenue-sharing over profit-sharing, because it is difficult to argue about the definition of revenue. Smith cites a case where $1M was billed for photocopies. Under an 80/20 split, a $1M license pays the inventor $200,000 [hOFtwRua-qc @ 00:18:09]. Lock down the royalty base before negotiating anything else.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Audit and Map the Portfolio
   - Build a patent taxonomy tying every asset to products, segment revenue, and growth curves, then flag non-core assets.
   - Carl Marsh maps patents to product, segment revenue, and growth curves, and favors patents in segments the owner has exited, where there is no boomerang effect [IPWatchdog, Inc. — 1UEOTCZ_qYA @ 00:06:23]. Target pivoted or discontinued products, unlicensed technology, and inactive marks, and drop assets whose maintenance cost earns no revenue [Patent pc — Tv_OWgYldPw @ 00:01:01].
2. Vet Like a Funder Before Anyone Else Does
   - Run assertion, validity, target, and damages checks in that order using litigation-grade claim charts.
   - Carbonneau vets assertion potential with litigation-grade claim charts first, then validity including priority and section 101, then likely targets, then damages [Clause 8 — t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:35:13]. Funders gate on damages, venue, marking, prior licenses, grant interests, and market size before technical merits [IPWatchdog, Inc. — pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:23:42].
3. Package and Run Targeted Outreach
   - Turn the vetted portfolio into a tailored value proposition and marketing package, then approach a short list of buyers.
   - Parallel North IP moves from technical review to market review, landscape, environment, network, value proposition, and tailored package before any outreach [International Trade Council — _bueYwQ3iHE @ 00:37:49]. Brokers prepare a sales package, run redacted outreach, and close with a purchase agreement under independent legal review [UpCounsel — xRRc9i7j5A8 @ 00:02:11].
4. Pick the Route, Close, and Record the Transfer
   - Choose a lump-sum sale, a licensing campaign, or a hybrid revenue share. Then transfer the full family and record the assignment.
   - A lump-sum sale is low complexity with a lower return. A licensing campaign needs high investment and litigation for a high return. A hybrid takes a cut of the buyer's licensing revenue. Transfer the whole family to avoid a messy split across jurisdictions [International Trade Council — _bueYwQ3iHE @ 00:30:14]. Record the transfer with the USPTO via forms and fees so both parties get confirmation [Berkeley Law & Technology Group — oRUwDt8nD5k @ 00:03:01].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Sell the asset or keep the stream is the first fork, and the operators split on it. UpCounsel frames an outright sale as 'a one-time payment with no future responsibilities, but also no control over how the technology is used' [xRRc9i7j5A8 @ 00:03:38], while Berkeley Law & Technology Group pushes sellers to check licensing first, arguing a patent 'is likely to have a high value if it has significant licensing potential' [oRUwDt8nD5k @ 00:01:00]. Parallel North IP's Anders Arison lays out three routes with clear trade-offs: a lump-sum sale is low complexity and low investment with a lower return; a licensing campaign demands high investment plus litigation and pays a high return; and a hybrid hands the asset to a buyer in exchange for a cut of that buyer's licensing revenue [_bueYwQ3iHE @ 00:30:14]. Useless Money adds the long-game perspective: royalty, fixed fee, upfront-plus-royalty, and milestone structures keep income flowing, while an outright sale surrenders future income and control, and 'there is no universal royalty rate that applies to every patent, brand, or software product' [xkXjle5Spjw @ 00:03:24]. The operator read: cash today buys certainty and speed; licensing buys upside but drags the owner into funding, time, and enforcement risk.

Whether to put a broker between the patent and the buyer draws mixed verdicts. J.D. Houvener says flatly, 'I would say hiring a broker is better if you can afford it,' and wants one with consumer-goods background in the specific technology who can reach decision makers; the payoff is removing the emotionally invested inventor so buyers 'have their guard down' [a7AlJrwA2Jo @ 00:00:41]. SVBS backs the leverage logic: lone inventors use brokers because of the power imbalance against corporate buyers, and VC-backed startups out of runway do better through a broker than selling direct [vPziFABPW9w @ 00:04:41]. UpCounsel prices that leverage at commissions 'typically ranging from 10 to 30% of the sale price' with deals taking 6 to 18 months [xRRc9i7j5A8 @ 00:04:11]. The counterweight comes from inside the trade. Louis Carbonneau of Tangible IP, who has brokered more than 4,500 patents, gets four or five portfolios a day and says yes 'maybe one or two% of the time,' rejects the 'crazy inventor' profile, and tells would-be brokers to 'run away' because 'you eat what you kill' [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:32:03]. Translation: a broker is a filter as much as a sales arm, and most sellers never clear the filter.

The sharpest split is over handing patents to assertion players. Fractus co-founder Dr. Carles Puente, who pivoted from manufacturing 'millions and millions' of antennas into research and licensing, argues 'without enforcing the rights, patents have no value' and treats patent assertion entities as essential for SMEs that lack the means and know-how to enforce [fAwWxzitmUk @ 00:03:48]. SVBS host David Smith counters with the NPE bill: the NPE takes a 'huge chunk' of proceeds, peace agreements can put target companies off-limits, and public NPEs under quarterly pressure have dumped a license worth more than $20M for $10M, so the advice is to find one 'under the radar and not reporting to Wall Street' [hOFtwRua-qc @ 00:10:54]. Corporate sellers draw the opposite line: SVBS reports they sell in stealth and 'refuse to sell to enforcement-based buyers as they don't want to feed their adversaries' [vPziFABPW9w @ 00:05:41]. LG Energy Solution's Kyle Valisillo takes a middle road, running a patent pool through a separate vehicle so LG avoids looking 'litigious' and keeps cross-licensing with Panasonic open [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:11:30]. Pick the route by who you are: an SME needs a stick, a brand-sensitive operator needs distance from it.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is underwriting a campaign without the war chest. The IPWatchdog funder panel puts a US case at full rates at '12 to $20 million,' and even when firms defer 40 to 50 percent of fees, the funder still eats $2 to 3 million in hard costs [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:03:44]. Funders pass on 19 of every 20 portfolios, screen damages, venue, marking and prior licenses before technical merit, and want at least $100 million in damages before writing a check [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:23:42]. Louis Carbonneau runs the same math at the brokerage level: $1 million in damages against $5 million to litigate does not sell, and suing Samsung or Apple runs $3 to 5 million with plaintiffs winning roughly 1 in 3 or 1 in 4 times [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:35:13]. SVBS stacks more cost on top: an IPR at about $250,000, expert witnesses at $356 to $478 an hour and often several million per case, plus fee-shifting exposure where losing can mean paying more than $10 million [hOFtwRua-qc @ 00:07:52]. The economics check is blunt: if provable damages cannot carry the litigation bill, funders walk and buyers do not bid.

The second cluster of failures is self-inflicted at the market stage. Carbonneau can 'predict with 100% certainty' that a portfolio with no evidence of use will not sell, and Tangible IP declines portfolios that have been shopped so widely they are burned [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:34:27]. Parallel North IP sees the same burn: contacting companies too early means no response when you come back on a second approach, so outreach should be limited to the entities that matter for the case and timed before budgets close [_bueYwQ3iHE @ 00:46:06]. UpCounsel warns owners against handling sensitive outreach themselves, because 'this approach could undermine the patent's value and complicate negotiations,' and the broker should handle those communications [xRRc9i7j5A8 @ 00:02:43]. Thin inventory kills deals too: the IPWatchdog economics panel says one newly issued patent means 'not ready,' pushes multiple families, and warns teardown tests can exceed $13,000 just to prove a single redundant claim limitation, because 'reverse engineering can be a huge cost on a portfolio' [pyi_x9MFGjs @ 00:32:09]. Each of these mistakes is cheap to make and expensive to undo: a burned list, a tipped-off target, or a one-patent pitch rarely gets a second hearing.

The third trap is treating licensing revenue as steady. Useless Money flags that InterDigital's headline numbers included catch-up revenue from deals like the $1.05 billion Samsung arbitration, so the stream is not even and recurring, and warns about customer concentration and the gap between recognized revenue and cash received [xkXjle5Spjw @ 00:07:40]. Kingacademic UK makes the same point for Arm's royalty flywheel: 'Customers must keep choosing ARM over alternatives,' and 'a lost design can reduce royalties for years' [aRUsXPICxpY @ 00:01:56]. The Strategy Breakdown shows how fast a toll road can stall: Apple stopped paying Qualcomm in 2017 and shifted to Intel, while Taiwan, Korea and the EU stacked billions in fines and Huawei withheld royalties before Apple settled on the first day of trial [uNc4jO7TeCo @ 00:07:14]. Inside large companies the enemy is often in-house: Carbonneau found Microsoft business units territorial, blocking sales and licenses mainly to avoid embarrassment if a buyer succeeded where they failed, and pre-litigation licenses hit the unit's P&L while post-suit settlements came from corporate funds [t98CwzofUz0 @ 00:16:20]. Stress-test any royalty model against concentration, catch-up lumps, design losses, and internal vetoes before banking on it.
