# Chapter 05: Negotiate the License and Term Sheet

*Royalties and equity, exclusive versus non-exclusive rights, binding clauses, joint development, board-game rights duration, reversion and sublicensing*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Licensing operators converge on one playbook: lock the commercial skeleton in a short term sheet, then fight over scope, money, and exit triggers in the definitive license. Term sheets are treated as non-binding except for exclusivity/no-shop and confidentiality, with expenses and validity dates sometimes binding too. Grant of rights is negotiated as a matrix of exclusivity, field, territory, and manufacture/use/sell rights, never handing over unvalued rights. Street numbers: royalties typically land at 3 to 7 percent with 5 percent as the anchor, industry bands run 0.1 to 25 percent, university equity runs 2 to 5 percent, and one board game publisher asks for five-year terms. Disagreements surface on exclusive versus non-exclusive economics, distribution reach versus headline rate, and whether rights revert when a licensee company is sold.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

The first consensus point is that the term sheet is a map, not a contract. Ryan Floyd of Storm Ventures calls it a non-binding agreement whose job is to summarize the principal economic and governance terms so nothing major is still unresolved when the legal agreement gets drafted, and he warns that any big issue that matters to you must be written in, because the other side will certainly write in theirs [f7jKIsIKO1c @ 00:00:35]. He counts only two binding provisions: exclusivity, meaning you stop shopping the deal to other parties once you sign, and confidentiality [f7jKIsIKO1c @ 00:01:05]. Campus Fund describes term sheets as morally binding but legally not binding, and lists four clauses that typically do bite at signing: exclusivity or no-shop, confidentiality, expenses, and a validity date after which the sheet automatically lapses unless definitive agreements are signed [rbNcenaCppU @ 00:01:37]. The docstocTV checklist adds that the expenses clause can itself be binding and obligate one party to pay the other side's expenses if the transaction does not proceed, while in other deals it stays non-binding until definitive documentation, so that single provision deserves a careful read before anything is signed [HbM8XU4hPCc @ 00:02:20]. Whatever lands in that sheet sets the frame for the definitive license, so a licensor's money terms belong there from day one.

Every practitioner who drafts licenses treats the grant of rights as the core asset being sold. John Baxter opens every agreement with the scope question: exclusive, sole, or non-exclusive, with a sole license defined as exclusive except that the licensor also keeps commercial rights to exploit the technology itself, and the deciding question being whether other licensees will be needed now or later [yl8L7tuwNuI @ 00:01:11]. He then layers manufacture, use, and sell rights, territory, application field, the exact IP being licensed, and sublicensing or have-made rights into a matrix, and gives the example of hydraulic IP licensed for earth-moving equipment over 10 tons in one territory and for forklift trucks under five tons in another [yl8L7tuwNuI @ 00:03:14]. His rule is to analyze that matrix cell by cell and never grant commercially unnecessary, and therefore unvalued, rights. Bao Tran runs the same play from the licensor side, partitioning field of use, such as heart treatment versus diabetic treatment, separately from territory so the patent owner can extract more value from each slice [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:10:49]. Andrew Ayers adds the enforcement angle: the single most important feature of an exclusive license is the right to sue and stop anyone else from using the licensed product [lVFYoVaNnrs @ 00:02:17].

The third point of agreement is that an exclusive grant without performance teeth is a trap. J.D. Houvener warns that going exclusive puts a lot of eggs in one basket and insists on a performance clause so that if the licensee decides to shelve the product you can get out of the contract, while noting the upside that an exclusive licensee is usually on the hook, sometimes contractually, to help enforce the rights against infringers [-VhDIVEwvzI @ 00:00:44]. UpCounsel names failing to secure performance obligations that force the licensee to hit specific milestones, and setting inadequate royalty rates, as the two common mistakes that cap an inventor's profit [Lpv4Uq-cOLA @ 00:01:19]. Bao Tran pushes further: anticipate that product development will not go as planned, write plan B into the agreement, understand what is and is not in the licensee's control, and negotiate an explicit right to terminate on a missed milestone, which he separates from a softer good-faith duty to renegotiate the milestones [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:13:01]. Baxter closes the loop on the money: never warrant the validity of your own patent, insist on the right to inspect the licensee's financial accounts and sales records, and make the audit clause cover what happens whether it reveals underpayment or overpayment [NU_eh8zb6Uk @ 00:00:46].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On rate, Ryan Stewart of Licensing Basics pins the typical royalty at about 5 percent, with 3 to 7 percent as the average band, and says the final figure moves with the product category, your leverage, and whether the product is already selling or is still a napkin drawing that forces the company to put far more at risk in development and manufacturing [uBnJLlSWMLk @ 00:02:31]. His headline lesson, though, is that distribution beats rate: a 12 percent royalty from a company sitting in roughly 50 stores produces a small check, while 3 percent from a company in every big-box outlet and thousands of stores can pay far more [uBnJLlSWMLk @ 00:01:00]. UpCounsel's benchmark sheet puts software at 8 to 12 percent, pharmaceuticals and biotech at 6 to 10, electronics at 4.8 to 5.5, aerospace at 3.5 to 4.5, and automotive at 3 to 3.8 [cURFfKlt_Ek @ 00:00:34]. The same source anchors valuation on the rule of 25 percent of pre-tax operating profit from the licensed product, alongside profit split, residual income, and risk-adjusted models, and tells negotiators to pull comparable deals from KT Mine and Royalty Source while weighing the licensee's margins, since higher margins can justify a higher rate [cURFfKlt_Ek @ 00:01:16].

Structure matters as much as rate. Baxter's baseline remuneration package is an upfront license or disclosure fee plus either a once-only royalty, which can be stage-gated, or a per-unit royalty expressed as a percentage of sale price or a fixed currency amount per unit, sometimes topped with an annual minimum royalty [yl8L7tuwNuI @ 00:04:02]. Bao Tran splits the money by stage: expense reimbursement of patent costs, development milestone payments per country or per licensed product, and maintenance fees before sales, then annual minimum royalties, running royalties, sublicense fees, tiers that may step down over time, and sales milestones once product ships [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:07:31]. On equity he repeats the VC line that nine out of ten companies fail, so a stake carries roughly 90 percent odds of being worth nothing, which is why universities typically take 2 to 5 percent as a substitute for upfront cash that startups lack, never as a substitute for royalties, giving the licensor another leg for the exit [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:09:28]. Trenner, who negotiated licenses from the inside as senior patent counsel at a national lab, reduces it to the question inventors fumble: upfront payments, royalties per unit, or performance milestones, and how the deal shares risk [dSzZD5CnwK8 @ 00:03:52].

Clock terms are where the board game trade gives the hardest numbers. The Flatlined Games operator behind Boardgame Publisher has seen three to four years pass between first agreement and a finished game, and sets the publish-by window at two to three years, because promising next year voids the contract if it slips, while promising ten years gets refused by any designer who doubts the company will still exist [SOV77fL52Dw @ 00:01:42]. Once published he wants five years of publication rights, followed by automatic renewal one year at a time for as long as the game keeps reprinting and selling, with the designer able to reclaim rights and have the publisher sell out remaining stock if volumes disappoint [SOV77fL52Dw @ 00:02:30]. His sublicensing model is a reserved right written into the designer contract from day one: he publishes French, English, Dutch, and German editions himself and sublicenses the rest by language or territory, such as all of North America to Stronghold Games for Steamrollers and Polish-language rights to Portal Games [SOV77fL52Dw @ 00:08:06]. Getting the final paper checked is cheap by comparison: ContractsCounsel puts the average flat-fee review at $440 across dozens of projects, rising with location, contract length, complexity, and whether redlining and negotiation are added [U51x7R7u71M @ 00:03:45].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the term sheet skeleton
   - Draft a short term sheet naming parties, consideration, and the binding clauses before any definitive license work.
   - Treat exclusivity/no-shop, confidentiality, expenses, and validity expiry as the clauses that bind [Campus Fund — rbNcenaCppU @ 00:01:03], and spell out who gets paid, how much, when, in what form, and who is obligated to pay [docstocTV — HbM8XU4hPCc @ 00:01:20].
2. Build the grant-of-rights matrix
   - Map exclusivity, manufacture/use/sell, territory, field, licensed IP, and sublicensing as separate cells.
   - Analyze the rights using a rigid matrix cell by cell and never grant commercially unnecessary, unvalued rights to a licensee [BaxterIPAttorneys — yl8L7tuwNuI @ 00:03:46].
3. Price against distribution and leverage
   - Set the royalty after sizing the licensee's store count and how central the IP is to the product.
   - A 3 percent rate across thousands of big-box stores can beat 12 percent in 50 stores [Licensing Basics — uBnJLlSWMLk @ 00:01:20]; if the IP covers core product value the licensee negotiates from the weaker position, if only a minor fraction it pushes hard to cut cost [Bao Tran, Esq. | Patent Attorney — SkIAUEkenak @ 00:06:10].
4. Wire performance, audit, and reversion triggers
   - Add milestone termination, audit rights, and automatic reversion events before signing.
   - Negotiate an explicit right to terminate on missed milestones [Bao Tran, Esq. | Patent Attorney — SkIAUEkenak @ 00:13:01], audit accounts covering under- and overpayment [BaxterIPAttorneys — NU_eh8zb6Uk @ 00:00:46], and automatic reversion if the licensee goes bankrupt or ceases to exist [Boardgame Publisher — SOV77fL52Dw @ 00:04:37].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Ryan Stewart of Licensing Basics ranks distribution above the royalty rate: a twelve percent royalty from a company stocked in only 50 stores produces a small check, while a 3% royalty from a licensee in every big-box store and thousands of outlets can pay far more [uBnJLlSWMLk @ 00:01:00]. When pinned down, Stewart puts the typical royalty at about 5%, with 3 to 7 percent as the average band [uBnJLlSWMLk @ 00:02:31]. UpCounsel works from a much wider, formula-driven map: rates running from 0.1% to 25%, software at 8 to 12%, pharmaceuticals and biotech at 6 to 10%, aerospace at 3.5 to 4.5%, and automotive at 3 to 3.8%, plus the rule of 25% that hands the inventor a quarter of the pre-tax operating profit from the licensed product [cURFfKlt_Ek @ 00:00:52]. Bao Tran adds the licensee's counter-play: a company taking the long view pushes for fixed-fee and fully paid agreements so that higher sales never raise its cost [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:08:26]. The result is a split playbook. Stewart's camp negotiates the size of the shelf footprint first and treats percentage points as secondary, while UpCounsel's camp anchors the number on industry benchmarks, comparable deals pulled from KT Mine and Royalty Source, and the licensee's profit margins.

Exclusivity splits the room. Andrew Ayers tells clients who are intent on protecting a product to use the exclusive license, because its most important feature is the ability to sue and stop anyone else using the licensed product, yet concedes a non-exclusive license could make a lot more money because more people can use it [lVFYoVaNnrs @ 00:03:25]. J.D. Houvener frames exclusivity as putting a lot of eggs in one basket that has to be managed harder, pushing for a performance clause so the licensor can exit if the licensee shelves the product, while flagging the upside that an exclusive licensee is usually on the hook for infringement protection because it is fully vested [-VhDIVEwvzI @ 00:00:44]. UpCounsel's own videos do not describe the transfer the same way: one says an exclusive license transfers all ownership rights to the licensee while the licensor keeps title [Lpv4Uq-cOLA @ 00:00:32], the other says it transfers certain ownership rights, including the right to sue for infringement, and can be fenced by field or territory [AaEw4SGbkk0 @ 00:01:50]. The practical fork: Ayers warns the non-exclusive route lets a competitor down the street sign the same deal, while Houvener's non-exclusive model keeps royalties flowing from multiple manufacturers and retailers at once.

On the term sheet itself, the practitioners disagree on how much actually binds. Ryan Floyd of Storm Ventures counts only two binding provisions, exclusivity and confidentiality, and calls everything else an understanding about terms rather than an agreement [f7jKIsIKO1c @ 00:01:22]. Campus Fund lists four binding clauses, adding expenses and a validity date after which the term sheet automatically lapses unless definitive agreements are signed, and describes the remainder as morally binding but legally not binding [rbNcenaCppU @ 00:01:37]. docstocTV splits the difference: the expenses provision may be binding and force one party to pay the other's costs if the deal dies, or may stay non-binding until definitive documentation, and exclusivity is not always included at all [HbM8XU4hPCc @ 00:02:20]. The camps also diverge on effort. Campus Fund says not to overindex on the term sheet or let it slow the transaction, while Floyd warns that whatever terms go in are likely to persist throughout the existence of the business, so big economic and governance issues must be settled there first. On payment form, Bao Tran sets equity apart from royalty: universities take 2 to 5% of a startup's equity in place of upfront cash, knowing nine out of 10 companies fail [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:09:28].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The Boardgame Publisher operator behind Flatlined Games has lived the timeline trap: some titles took more than three or four years from first agreement with the designer to a published game [SOV77fL52Dw @ 00:00:51]. Write 'published next year' and miss it, and the contract is null and void, forcing a renegotiation from a bad position; write ten years and the designer will not agree, because the company may not exist by then. The working ballpark is two to three years to publication, then five years of publication rights with an automatic one-year renewal while reprints continue. The economics check is blunt: if a 3,000-copy print run would take ten years to sell through, reprinting makes no sense, the contract lapses, and the rights revert to the designer [SOV77fL52Dw @ 00:04:07]. Bao Tran tells licensors to assume product development will not go as planned, write plan B into the agreement, and negotiate an explicit right to terminate on a missed milestone, which differs from a good-faith promise to renegotiate milestones [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:13:01]. UpCounsel names the same failure from the inventor side: not securing performance obligations that force the licensee to hit specific milestones, alongside setting inadequate royalty rates that cap the inventor's profits [Lpv4Uq-cOLA @ 00:01:19].

John Baxter of Baxter IP flags currency as the quiet loss: a royalty set in a foreign currency can win on exchange gains, but a licensor can also lose and lose badly if driven to a fixed royalty in a foreign currency, so always provide proper indexation [yl8L7tuwNuI @ 00:05:12]. Per-unit dollar royalties should likewise be indexed for inflation, and remuneration wording must be unambiguous enough to survive interpretation by a court. In a follow-up session Baxter adds two traps: never warrant the validity of the licensor's patent, and write audit provisions that cover the awkward case where the audit turns up either underpayment or overpayment of royalty [NU_eh8zb6Uk @ 00:01:02]. Ryan Floyd shows how headline numbers mislead in a term sheet: at $8 million pre-money with a $2 million raise and a 10% post-money option pool, the effective pre-money drops to $7 million, and converting notes and warrants collapse into that same calculation [f7jKIsIKO1c @ 00:03:18]. Floyd's rule is that the option pool is really just a valuation discussion: a bigger pool means a lower effective pre-money. Trenner Law Firm, which negotiated licensing deals from inside a national lab, says many inventors lose out at exactly this stage because they do not understand the mechanics of the deal [dSzZD5CnwK8 @ 00:04:12].

The surprise Andrew Ayers warns clients about is believing they signed an exclusive license and later finding it was non-exclusive, with no ability to stop the competitor down the street from adopting the same software [lVFYoVaNnrs @ 00:04:49]. Trenner Law Firm warns against targeting the wrong licensees: if an invention requires a company to build an entirely new factory, it probably will not license it, and approaching the wrong targets wastes everyone's time and can prematurely wear out interest in the invention [dSzZD5CnwK8 @ 00:02:53]. UpCounsel's mistake list runs from insufficient research on licensees and weak or vague agreements to sharing technical details without protection and overvaluing the patent, and it warns that royalty income can be inconsistent and licensee compliance must be monitored [AaEw4SGbkk0 @ 00:05:23]. Bao Tran describes the counterparty's hardball play, which Tran calls ethically questionable: the efficient infringement approach, where a company practices the claimed invention and forces the owner to prove validity and infringement, front-loading the owner's expenses while carrying significant risk for the company if infringement is found [SkIAUEkenak @ 00:04:38]. That is the leverage reality: a patent covering the core of a product puts the licensee in the weaker seat, while one touching a minor fraction lets the licensee negotiate the price down hard.
