## Executive Summary

Nick Saraev, Automate AI Consulting, and Liam Ottley connect recurring revenue to successful delivery, existing trust, and work that continues after installation. Their packages range from maintenance and prepaid build time to ongoing automation partnerships. Jeff Sauer sells accountability through a 90-day relationship; Sean Malone sells the client's problem disappearing rather than the underlying AI. Execution varies sharply: Sean Standberry proposes $500 setup plus $1,000 monthly for avatar content and lead conversion, while Agency Stack Guide illustrates a $297 managed newsletter. Wayfront and Brett Malinowski distinguish fixed deliverables from request queues. Practitioners disagree about beginner retainers, pitch timing, communication access, and account ownership. Their economics checks expose developer hours, client revisions, underused capacity, and voice-platform access fees as decisive parts of the monthly offer.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Nick Saraev makes the first project the opening move, not the whole relationship. Introduce the intention to work together monthly during the first sales conversation, choose an initial build that exposes other business bottlenecks, and document what the installation changes. Their monday.com CRM proposal explicitly raised a future retainer while still quoting the one-time job. Automate AI Consulting gives beginners a narrower starting line: close two to four projects with fixed scope, fixed price, and a clear endpoint; save screenshots and testimonials; then offer ongoing help once the system works. Liam Ottley agrees that early delivery experience matters more than assembling an elaborate monthly offer. Their beginner prescription is one niche, one deliverable, and repeated sales until delivery becomes familiar. The shared sequence is proof before a large ongoing commitment: an installed system, a client who has experienced the work, and identifiable reasons to keep working together. Saraev starts talking about that destination early; Automate AI Consulting makes the continuation conversation concrete with updates, fixes, and improvements to what already runs. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Saraev's conversion sequence starts before delivery. Establish measurable indicators such as return, fulfillment cycle time, or software savings; send project updates every day or two; and collect additional opportunities while working inside the business. At handoff, add the configuration, a Google Doc operating procedure, and Loom training rather than sending only the finished build. Their delivery message spends roughly 70% celebrating completed work and 30% discussing further opportunities. One actual Upwork client progressed from low hourly work to fixed-price projects and then a long-term retainer after a delivery email proposed additional margin improvements. Ottley's maintenance-plus-improvement package supplies a specific recurring task: capture chatbot questions answered with 'I don't know' through Zapier into Google Sheets, review them weekly, obtain answers from the client, and add those answers to the knowledge base. ArriSse Corp supplies the intake structure around delivery: a payment confirmation, welcome packet, and kickoff link within two hours, followed by a diagnostic intake with one designated sign-off lead. Its scope fence makes exclusions as visible as inclusions, while out-of-scope requests become either a phase-two backlog item or a separately priced change with a revised schedule. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

There is no shared monthly price across these playbooks. AI Money Math proposes $100 monthly for missed-call text-back and explicitly puts monthly tools at $20 to $50 per client, leaving $50 to $80 before other work. Its first-client strategy sacrifices the $750 setup fee for proof. Ottley takes the opposite beginner position: small retainers can consume sales time, create admin, and leave too little profit after developer work. Their preferred early move is repeated sales of the main product, with ongoing maintenance becoming more practical once an employee can handle the routine. Standberry rejects $3,000 to $5,000 monthly as the entry offer for the local businesses they target and proposes $1,000 for content plus conversion infrastructure. Sauer instead describes a broader enablement role worth $2,000 to $15,000 monthly, depending on scope, with a roster of five to ten clients. These speakers are not quoting the same bundle: one message and an owner handoff, maintenance obligations, ongoing avatar marketing, and continuing business enablement are different jobs. Their actual disagreement is how much recurring responsibility a beginner should accept and what client relationship makes that responsibility worthwhile. Timing and access reveal another split. Saraev plants the monthly idea during the first conversation but asks for the retainer immediately after project delivery, when the client has received the work and the extra documentation. Their offer can begin with one month and no lock-in, supported by a 90-day roadmap. ArriSse Corp argues that introducing a retainer only at the end makes it feel discretionary; its 60-day engine puts a no-charge value snapshot at day 21 and a quarterly business review at day 45. Sauer gives the relationship a longer initial runway, saying 90 days are needed to get the first real project working and learn how the client operates. The service channel also changes the bargain. Saraev includes strategy calls and a defined Slack availability window as valuable extras. Ottley's Morningside model deliberately keeps communication in Trello comments to reduce Slack chatter. Clients prioritize scoped automation requests, developers track work centrally in Airtable, and the next task starts after the current one is completed. Saraev sells accessible partnership; Ottley describes a production queue with more communication friction and less administrative back-and-forth. Ownership and billing create alternatives even when the automation looks similar. Ottley's basic maintenance and improvement packages run in the client's accounts, with the client paying platform costs. Saraev's daily retainer examples favor bundled deliverables or hours, and they say they usually do not bill by usage. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first failure can happen before any retainer exists. Automate AI Consulting describes spending months improving demos and rewriting offers without sending a single outreach message. Their first cold-email campaign sent about 200 emails and received zero replies. Looking back, they identify insufficient outreach, missing follow-up, an unclear offer, and copy that resembled everyone else's. Malone reports a related selling slump after roughly six months of running a mastermind: their pitch drifted toward hiring, onboarding, training, and constructing a lead-generation system. They changed the framing to getting three hours back for family time. Sauer identifies a retention trap after the sale: about half the people they coach are DIYers who ask intensely, get the answer, and leave to implement it themselves. Those engagements deliver value but do not compound into the continuing revenue Sauer wants. Their alternative client wants execution without operating the technology. Across these firsthand accounts, technical work alone does not settle the commercial problem: Automate AI Consulting had to show the offer, Malone had to sell the destination, and Sauer had to distinguish temporary instruction from an ongoing operating relationship. Recurring access can become recurring drag. Ottley introduces their retainer advice through mistakes and pitfalls from running their own agency, then singles out difficult clients, underpricing, long lock-ins, and selling retainers too early. A difficult client paired with subjective chatbot responses creates repeated arguments about whether delivery is acceptable. A $300 or $500 monthly agreement can leave only about $100 profit in their example once an employee contributes a few hours weekly, while the owner still carries messages and mental clutter. ArriSse Corp's scope controls target the same operational leakage: delayed assets, contradictory stakeholder feedback, revision loops, and requests sent to a personal phone outside business hours. Its response is one sign-off lead, visible exclusions, a 48-hour feedback expectation, and day-for-day schedule movement when the client causes delay. Saraev adds a firsthand reminder about delivery timing: the client whose margins improved by more than the proposed 5% took longer than initially anticipated. Their earlier retainer pitch became a real relationship, but the improvement still required more time. Monthly pricing and optimistic delivery expectations remain separate operating questions in these accounts.
