## 1. Days 1–7: Sell One Authorized Workflow Before Building a Platform  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with a single operational buyer and an observable queue: for example, a B2B distributor whose service team manually classifies inbound order-status requests. Sell classification, retrieval from approved order records, and a draft response for staff review; exclude payment changes, credit decisions, and unattended customer messages. The offer is a fixed-scope implementation, not ownership of the client's entire AI roadmap. Assign the first full-time operator to interviews, proposals, collections, and account decisions. Assign the second to architecture, integration, release control, and technical support. The three contractors cover workflow integration, independent evaluation, and security or data-contract review under explicit hour limits. These are specialist roles, not three additional full-time delivery seats. Candidate #29's 23 mapped plays belong in a coverage ledger with an owner, evidence link, estimated hours, and current disposition. Use the 23 local execution slots below to organize that ledger; they are working assignments, not claimed source titles for the mapped plays. Preserve original play identifiers when transferring the ledger into the broader manuscript or operating system. This prevents a large play catalog from becoming an accidental promise to deliver 23 separate services during the launch month.

Days 1 through 3 establish the commercial boundary before production access. P01 is buyer selection, P02 is workflow baseline, and P03 is the fixed-scope offer. Require a buyer to identify weekly ticket volume, current handling time, responsible staff, and the consequence of a wrong answer. Measure a sample of at least 30 historical tickets with timestamps rather than accepting an executive's rough savings estimate. A buyer reporting 500 monthly tickets at eight minutes each has approximately 66.7 monthly handling hours; reducing that workload does not create 66.7 hours of agency revenue. State the deliverable as one queue, two approved connectors, one escalation destination, and a defined evaluation set. Price the initial illustrative pilot at $7,500, with $3,750 due at signing and $3,750 after documented acceptance. Specify what happens when client credentials or representative examples arrive late: the delivery clock pauses rather than silently consuming the pod's capacity. Define change requests by connector count, output type, and exception category. A new CRM, another language, or automatic sending becomes a separately estimated change, not an informal favor absorbed by the technical operator.

Days 4 through 7 complete P04 access authorization, P05 data and IP terms, and P06 qualification. Obtain written permission from the client resource owner identifying systems, environments, permitted operations, and access expiry. Use a dedicated HubSpot sandbox or Microsoft 365 test mailbox where available; never ask a salesperson to share their personal administrator password. Computer access outside authorization can implicate 18 U.S.C. § 1030(a)(2)(C), so record the actual authorization boundary rather than treating possession of a token as permission. Contract language should distinguish client inputs, agency reusable components, third-party dependencies, and commissioned deliverables. An intended copyright ownership transfer requires a signed writing under 17 U.S.C. § 204(a). For covered California personal information, evaluate the applicable service-provider or contractor terms under California Civil Code § 1798.100(d). Build the first 60-account prospect list around one workflow and buying role. Target 12 completed discovery meetings by Day 30, but do not equate outreach activity with validated demand. Each meeting must record urgency, an accountable budget holder, accessible source data, and an agreed next action before it enters the qualified pipeline.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Checks Before Any Client-System Connection**
> 
> The technical owner records evidence for each check. A failed check blocks the affected connection while sales and synthetic-data work continue.
> - Resource-owner authorization names the exact environment, permitted reads or writes, expiry date, and emergency revocation contact.
> - The processing schedule records permitted data fields, retention periods, subprocessors, and deletion responsibility before personal information enters the workflow.
> - The acceptance contract identifies the evaluation set, consequential-decision reviewer, payment milestones, and signed ownership provisions.
> - A tested kill switch disables outbound actions, revokes connector tokens, and returns unresolved work to the client's existing human queue.

## 2. Days 8–14: Build the Smallest Measurable Implementation  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Week two turns buyer evidence into six more local execution slots: P07 process mapping, P08 connector design, P09 reference implementation, P10 evaluation construction, P11 exception routing, and P12 proposal closure. Map the present workflow before inserting a model: intake, identity lookup, record retrieval, draft generation, staff review, and final disposition. Use n8n or a small Python service when those tools fit the client's environment; select one orchestration path instead of supporting both during the pilot. Keep deterministic rules outside the model: allowed account IDs, field permissions, retry counts, and destination addresses belong in ordinary code. The model should return a constrained classification and structured draft, not executable instructions. Treat incoming email text and retrieved documents as untrusted content. A message telling the agent to reveal credentials must remain data, even when it resembles an internal instruction. Store credentials in the client's approved secret manager, separate development and production identities, and record connector permissions in the deployment manifest. On Day 10, demonstrate a synthetic ticket flowing through the entire route, including an intentional failure that lands in the human queue without sending a customer message.

Build a frozen acceptance set of at least 100 representative cases for each workflow before tuning prompts against it. Reserve those cases from development examples, label the expected routing and factual requirements, and include ambiguous requests, missing records, duplicate events, hostile instructions, and cross-account lookup attempts. The launch threshold is at least 95 passing cases out of 100, zero critical data-boundary or unauthorized-action failures, and human review of every consequential decision. A case passes only when all mandatory checks pass; averaging a correct classification with an invented order status hides a real defect. Record the model identifier, prompt version, connector version, source-record timestamp, latency, and measured cost with every evaluation run. Use pytest for deterministic assertions and an independent contractor's rubric for response quality. An automated grader can triage cases but cannot certify its own unsupported answers. Repeat the acceptance suite after any model, prompt, retrieval, or permissions change. These results support a narrow claim about tested conditions, not universal reliability. Under Section 5 of the FTC Act, 15 U.S.C. § 45, performance claims must avoid deceptive representations; maintain the evidence supporting each published number.

The commercial operator continues discovery while the technical operator protects build capacity. Submit four qualified proposals by Day 30, each with the same scope boundary and a buyer-specific baseline attachment. Do not discount away uncertainty: offer paid discovery when connector feasibility remains unresolved, and count that as discovery revenue rather than a completed implementation. For the $7,500 pilot, budget 45 direct delivery hours at an illustrative loaded rate of $80, $300 of runtime and tooling, and $350 of warranty allowance. Total direct cost is $4,250; project contribution is $3,250, or approximately 43.3%. This is contribution before shared overhead, selling costs, and the full owner-profit calculation. Track both operators' delivery time even when they defer cash compensation. A pilot apparently producing 70% contribution because founder hours are missing is a pricing error. On Day 14, require a working demonstration, versioned evaluation results, and either a signed funded pilot or a documented next buying event. The scorecard's one-to-four-month first-deal window means an unsigned proposal at two weeks is evidence to manage, not automatic proof that the opportunity has failed.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Four Ways Experienced Operators Protect Contribution**
> 
> The best launch shortcuts reduce uncertainty without hiding delivery cost or transferring an uncontrolled failure to the client.
> - Capture connector setup as a reusable checklist, but keep credentials and client-specific identifiers outside the reusable package.
> - Include deliberate missing-record cases in the sales demonstration; a clean human handoff often sells reliability better than a polished best-case answer.
> - Make the client nominate one acceptance reviewer and a two-business-day review window, with explicit escalation rather than presumed acceptance.
> - Quote the retainer against included review hours, runtime allowance, and response windows; price additional queues as separate implementations.

## 3. Days 15–21: Deliver Evidence Within a Measured Cash and Capacity Envelope  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Week three covers P13 deployment preparation, P14 staged release, P15 usage monitoring, P16 quality monitoring, P17 acceptance, and P18 support packaging. Begin with read-only access and staff-visible drafts. Enable writes only when the contract expressly includes them, the resource owner authorizes them, and idempotency tests show retries cannot create duplicate actions. Use a unique event key and durable processing record rather than trusting a webhook provider to deliver each message once. Log request IDs, status transitions, costs, and error categories while excluding unnecessary message content. Configure Sentry or the client's existing observability stack to alert on repeated connector failures and dead-letter queue growth. On Day 18, reconcile production samples against the frozen rubric, then investigate discrepancies between test and live traffic. On Day 19, demonstrate credential revocation and restoration of the original human workflow. Acceptance requires the client's named reviewer to confirm scope completion and unresolved exceptions in writing. A successful internal demo is not client acceptance, and an issued invoice is not collected cash. These distinctions keep the sprint board honest when technical progress moves faster than procurement, customer review, or payment processing.

Set the first-month gross cash-spend ceiling at $7,500 before committing contractor work. Allocate $2,500 to overhead, $2,500 to specialist payments, $750 to software and API usage, $750 to acquisition activity, and $1,000 to contingency. These envelopes sum exactly to the ceiling; using the contingency requires removing the amount from its remaining balance. Maintain a separate accrued-cost ledger because deferred operator compensation does not disappear economically. For planning, add a $5,000 founder-labor charge if that labor is unpaid during launch, producing $12,500 of modeled first-month cost before any further accruals. Do not add that charge twice when allocating delivery labor into project contribution. Cap the two full-time operators at 240 combined planned hours for the month: 160 for delivery and 80 for sales, administration, and support. Cap contractor usage at 60 combined hours, with actual rates reconciled to the $2,500 envelope before booking. If specialist rates consume the cash allowance earlier, cash controls the limit. Reserve at least 40 delivery hours for defects and handover. Run one live implementation at a time during launch rather than converting theoretical hours into overlapping customer promises.

Keep sprint economics separate from the canonical annual scenarios. Conservative remains $100,000 revenue less $80,000 total costs, producing $20,000 owner net profit at 20% margin with 2 FT + 3 C. Base remains $550,000 less $385,000, producing $165,000 at 30.0% with the same pod. Scaled remains $1,000,000 less $600,000, producing $400,000 at 40% with 3 FT + 4 C. Illustrative revenue bridges are eight $7,500 implementations plus forty $1,000 support-months; forty $10,000 implementations plus one hundred $1,500 support-months; and sixty $12,500 implementations plus one hundred twenty-five $2,000 support-months, respectively. These equal the three revenue totals but do not establish that launch capacity can deliver them. The Base bridge requires material reuse and shorter implementation cycles before forty annual projects fit. Support-months count active billable months, not distinct clients or unearned annual contract value. The published $2,500–$18,000 monthly opex range is not a substitute for each scenario's total annual costs. Include delivery labor, contractors, acquisition, support, and other operating expenses in the reconciliation. None of the bridges recognizes client sales, order value, or estimated labor savings as agency revenue.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the release candidate
   - Record the model, prompts, connectors, permissions, and evaluation version.
   - The technical operator signs the manifest; the testing contractor verifies that the evaluated version matches the proposed deployment.
2. Run acceptance and recovery tests
   - Execute at least one hundred held-out cases and demonstrate rollback.
   - Require at least 95% case-level passing results, zero critical failures, and a working human destination for every blocked action.
3. Release through the authorized boundary
   - Start with reviewed drafts and observe live exceptions before expanding traffic.
   - Use resource-owner-approved credentials, retain event identifiers, and disable the affected path whenever an access boundary fails.
4. Close acceptance and reconcile economics
   - Collect written acceptance, invoice the milestone, and post actual delivery costs.
   - Separate invoiced revenue, collected cash, deferred obligations, and remaining support commitments before publishing project contribution.

## 4. Days 22–30: Close the Evidence Ledger and Apply Kill, Fix, or Scale  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Week four completes P19 incident rehearsal, P20 economic reconciliation, P21 proof packaging, P22 renewal and referral development, and P23 portfolio decisions. Day 22 runs an incident exercise covering leaked credentials, wrong-account retrieval, and an unavailable connector. Day 23 reconciles recorded hours, runtime charges, warranty usage, invoices, and collections. Day 24 prepares a case study limited to measured results and obtains explicit permission before publishing client identifiers or operational details. Day 25 presents a bounded support retainer and asks for a referral only after acceptance. Day 26 reviews all 23 ledger slots, distinguishing completed evidence from deferred work. Day 27 has the independent tester challenge the latest release with new adversarial cases without replacing the frozen acceptance benchmark. Day 28 refreshes the cash forecast and remaining delivery commitments. Day 29 writes a one-page decision memo with raw denominators, failed criteria, and one recommended action. Day 30 is the decision meeting, not a ceremonial launch announcement. Both full-time operators sign the memo, assign the next experiment or capacity increase, and preserve the underlying evidence. Contractors contribute their findings but do not create open-ended work commitments for the next month.

Apply the decision matrix in order: any applicable Kill condition overrides Scale; Scale requires every listed commercial condition plus the common delivery controls; all remaining cases are Fix. Kill normally means stop selling the tested offer or segment, not delete customer records or abandon contracted support. A critical security failure triggers immediate suspension of the affected workflow regardless of meeting date; resume only after remediation and independent retesting. Common Scale controls are at least 95% passing cases on each workflow's minimum 100-case acceptance set, zero unresolved critical failures, at least 40% aggregate implementation contribution, rework no greater than 10% of direct delivery hours, and at least 90% on-time accepted deliveries. With fewer than ten deliveries, require every delivery on time. Count rework as correcting defective agreed functionality, not separately purchased changes. Require collection of at least 95% of amounts currently due, no overdue critical incident, and no pilot above 45 direct delivery hours. Treat these thresholds as operating choices, not scorecard-derived promises. In particular, Day-30 Scale is an ambitious evidence threshold within a business whose first deal may reasonably take four months.

Keep cumulative gross spending within $7,500 by Day 30, $20,000 by Day 60, and $35,000 by Day 90, while limiting cumulative unrecovered launch cash to $7,500 throughout. Calculate unrecovered cash as cumulative cash outflows minus cumulative client collections; protect tax reserves and any refundable balances separately. Additional spending after Day 30 must fit both the gross ceiling and that net-exposure limit, so optimistic receivables cannot fund hiring. A Fix decision authorizes one fourteen-day experiment with a named owner, a single variable, and at most $1,500 inside the existing ceilings. Test a narrower buyer, simpler connector, revised price, or stronger exception route; do not change everything simultaneously. Scale first means repeating the accepted package, not adding employees. Increase concurrent implementations from one to two only after three consecutive accepted projects meet the common controls and planned utilization stays below 75% of available delivery hours. A $1,500 monthly retainer with $600 direct service cost contributes $900 before overhead; reserve those service hours before selling another sprint. Move toward the canonical 3 FT + 4 C scaled team only when contracted work and collected cash support the added capacity.
