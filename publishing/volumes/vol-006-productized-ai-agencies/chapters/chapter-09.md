# Chapter 09: AI Agents, RAG, and Knowledge Graphs Explained

*An introduction to retrieval-augmented generation, embeddings, vector databases, knowledge graphs, and agentic workflows—covering hallucinations, retrieval strategies, Open WebUI knowledge bases, and enterprise-ready RAG systems.*

## Executive Summary

Jeff Su, IBM Technology, and Mirketa put the same problem first: a capable model still needs the company's actual knowledge. Retrieval supplies that context; agents choose tools and next steps. Professor Patterns demonstrates knowledge-base uploads in Open WebUI, while Adam Lucek builds GraphRAG with 1,200-token chunks and 100-token overlaps. Cole Medin favors reranking, agentic retrieval, and context-aware chunking. The architecture debate stays practical: IBM favors long context for bounded whole-document reasoning; Neo4j emphasizes connected knowledge; Writer preserves graph relationships through a different storage approach. Mirketa rejects vector search for every problem. Across these approaches, the recurring bottlenecks are weak retrieval, broken context, stale information, and added processing costs. Unstructured ties the build to business outcomes, permission-aware retrieval, and continuing evaluation.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The shared starting point is missing company knowledge, not a model that cannot write. Jeff Su illustrates the distinction with a coffee-chat request: an LLM can draft the invitation, but it cannot know the next appointment without access to the calendar. Their explanation separates limited proprietary knowledge from passivity: the model waits for an input rather than independently operating the business [FwOTs4UxQS4 @ 00:01:56]. Kaushal makes the same point through a new developer asking for the company's production deployment policy. The answer already exists across documents and internal systems; the hard part is finding the right knowledge for that question, rather than returning every page containing the word production [cxGMp3DBkMk @ 00:01:49]. Mirketa's Rajiv and Alec bring that distinction into support. An eligibility-check question can require the customer's configuration, a troubleshooting article, three similar resolved cases, and a recent release note. Those pieces give the model something specific to reason with instead of a generic explanation of claim failures [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:04:48]. In each account, the useful change is concrete: retrieve the business evidence before generating the response. The knowledge layer supplies the circumstances that the base model does not already possess.

The baseline mechanics are deliberately ordinary: prepare the documents, retrieve relevant pieces, and include those pieces with the question. KodeKloud explains embeddings as numerical representations that preserve meaning, then applies the same conversion to the user's question. Semantic search compares that question representation with stored document representations; augmentation inserts the retrieved information into the prompt at runtime [_HQ2H_0Ayy0 @ 00:01:50]. This is different from expecting the model to memorize the company's entire document collection. IBM's Marina Danilevsky describes the resulting prompt as three parts: the instruction, the retrieved content, and the user's question. The model then generates an answer that can point back to evidence [T-D1OfcDW1M @ 00:04:00]. Mirketa adds an important division of labor: the vector database does retrieval, not reasoning. It neither decides the next action nor answers the customer; its job is finding candidate information [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:12:28]. That separation also explains the attraction of retrieval for changing business knowledge. Danilevsky's approach updates the content store rather than retraining the model whenever the underlying facts change. The answer is still generated, but the relevant knowledge is supplied at answering time, with an identifiable source rather than only a remembered association.

Retrieval and agency solve different parts of the job. Jeff Su's make.com example moves news links through Google Sheets, Perplexity summaries, and Claude-written social posts on a daily schedule. Because Su sets the path and manually revises disappointing prompts, they call it a workflow. The transition to an agent happens when the LLM becomes the decision maker, reasoning about the approach and using tools to execute it [FwOTs4UxQS4 @ 00:05:25]. Hey sidG makes the business distinction as recalling information versus acting on it: finding a travel policy is different from filing an expense report or escalating a support ticket. Their hybrid architecture gives the agent retrieval as one tool among others, rather than replacing knowledge lookup with orchestration [r4hp_y9_V3w @ 00:04:29]. Unstructured's Daniel Scoffield puts the commercial starting point before either architecture. Look for high-value friction: expensive employees spending time finding important information, support staff moving between sources, or salespeople searching internal repositories for questionnaire answers [ruilmyfVgrI @ 00:10:01]. The common operating picture is therefore grounded answers attached to an actual workflow. Retrieval supplies knowledge, tools provide actions, and the business use case determines which capabilities are worth assembling in the first place.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Professor Patterns demonstrates the shortest visible path to a working knowledge base in Open WebUI. Open Workspace, select Knowledge, create a collection, and upload a directory of documents. Their example uses candidate resumes in PDF and Word formats. In the chat, attach that collection, paste a job description, and ask which candidates fit; they then open the underlying resumes to inspect the recommendations [LmXaPUky_u4 @ 00:00:28]. The document settings expose the next operational layer: the embedding model, chunk settings, top K, and the RAG template. Top K controls how many relevant chunks are passed along, while the template provides instructions and citation examples around the retrieved context [LmXaPUky_u4 @ 00:09:24]. KodeKloud builds the corresponding Python lab with a virtual environment, UV, Chroma DB, Sentence Transformers, OpenAI, and Flask. The lab inspects Markdown handbooks, product specifications, meeting notes, and frequently asked questions before creating persistent vector storage [_HQ2H_0Ayy0 @ 00:05:28]. Both demonstrations make the document collection tangible rather than abstract. One exposes ingestion and retrieval through an interface; the other walks through scripts and file-based checks. The common sequence is to load known documents, make them searchable, ask concrete questions, and inspect the resulting evidence and answer.

Chunking and ranking are the hands-on adjustments, not decorative settings. In KodeKloud's chunking walkthrough, the script uses size 500 and overlap 100, prints chunk statistics, and writes a verification file. The ingestion stage later specifies size 500 and stride 400 while storing embeddings alongside metadata [_HQ2H_0Ayy0 @ 00:06:16]. Cole Medin's preferred retrieval adjustment is reranking: first fetch a larger candidate set, then use a specialized model, often a cross encoder, to select the few chunks the answering model actually receives. They describe this as considering more knowledge without dumping 20, 50, or more chunks directly into the LLM; the second model adds some cost [tLMViADvSNE @ 00:02:48]. Mirketa turns relevance filtering into an empirical task. When tuning Case Resolver, Alec tested retrieval separately, measured the scores of real matches and unrelated matches on known cases, and placed the similarity cutoff between those groups [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:30:10]. These are three different controls: how documents are broken apart, which candidates get promoted, and whether a candidate is relevant enough to use at all. KodeKloud also asks learners to inspect the semantic-search results rather than treating a successfully written output file as the end of the exercise.

Adam Lucek's GraphRAG build starts with a fine-tuning guide converted from PDF to text, with the glossary, abstract, title page, and references removed. Initialize the project, configure the model and embedding credentials in the settings, and index the corrected input directory [6vG_amAshTk @ 00:09:07]. The indexing flow splits text, extracts entities and relationships, merges the per-chunk subgraphs, embeds nodes, detects communities, and generates community reports. Their demonstration uses 1,200-token chunks with 100-token overlaps, producing about 53 chunks [6vG_amAshTk @ 00:12:17]. The Neo4j presenters divide construction into a lexical graph of documents and chunks, an entity-and-relationship layer, and enrichment through graph algorithms. Existing structured entities can be supplied so extraction becomes recognition and relationship finding rather than starting from nothing [XNneh6-eyPg @ 00:10:42]. Cole Medin supplies another concrete storage arrangement for agentic retrieval: Neon with Postgres and PG Vector, one table containing chunks, and another holding document-level information. The agent can choose a semantic search or read a whole document [tLMViADvSNE @ 00:03:56]. Across these builds, the richer retrieval path comes from explicit stored structure: document membership, entity relationships, community summaries, or access to the complete source behind a retrieved chunk.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

IBM Technology gives long context a serious place instead of treating a vector database as mandatory. For a bounded collection requiring global reasoning, such as comparing full requirements with release notes, the speaker favors placing the documents directly in context. A search for omitted requirements can retrieve matching snippets without exposing the gap between the complete documents [UabBYexBD4k @ 00:06:15]. That simplicity has a processing trade-off: IBM's example of a 500-page manual represents roughly 250,000 tokens processed repeatedly, while RAG pays the manual's indexing cost upfront. Prompt caching partly offsets the repeated work for static information, not constantly changing streams [UabBYexBD4k @ 00:07:35]. Cole Medin offers an intermediate playbook through hierarchical retrieval: search small chunks precisely, use their metadata to identify the parent file, and return the entire document when it is small enough to read [tLMViADvSNE @ 00:09:06]. Mirketa draws a different boundary altogether. For account balances, case status, entitlement levels, or order numbers, Alec recommends ordinary structured database queries. Semantic retrieval earns its place with nuanced documents, emails, and case notes [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:26:41]. The contrast is about the question and the data: complete-document comparison, targeted passage retrieval, and exact structured lookup are not the same operational job.

Graph advocates also take different implementation paths. The Neo4j presenters describe a graph retriever beginning with an index search, then following relationships from those entry points to bring back additional relevant context. The starting search can be vector, full text, or another supported approach; the graph adds connected information rather than merely another isolated text fragment [XNneh6-eyPg @ 00:12:44]. Writer's Sam Julien reports a historical move away from a conventional graph-database implementation after the team encountered scaling costs, expertise limits, and difficulties with the matching they needed. Writer instead stored graph-structured data points as JSON in a Lucene-based search engine, retaining relationships while using infrastructure the team understood [XlAIgmi_Vow @ 00:09:42]. Adam Lucek explores another distinction inside Microsoft's GraphRAG: local search gathers specific entity-linked information, global search works with community reports, and DRIFT combines broader community information with follow-up local searches [6vG_amAshTk @ 00:22:49]. Their comparison produces different answer styles, not simply different storage labels. Local retrieval emphasizes particulars; global retrieval emphasizes themes; DRIFT generates a more detailed response through additional exploration. Lucek explicitly describes graph methods and regular embeddings as complementary. Julien likewise argues that the benefits of graph relationships can be obtained through different underlying storage approaches.

The sharpest agentic disagreement concerns how much machinery the problem deserves. Hey sidG promotes an agent as the orchestrator, with RAG in its toolbox and a loop that retrieves, reads, critiques, and searches again when the evidence is insufficient. They also acknowledge the extra latency and compute from repeated thinking and retrieval [r4hp_y9_V3w @ 00:04:46]. Kaushal objects to skipping directly from simple RAG to agentic RAG while ignoring intermediate choices such as better chunking, metadata, hybrid search, and reranking. If classic retrieval already solves the business problem, their position is to keep it rather than move automatically to graphs or agents [cxGMp3DBkMk @ 00:18:36]. Mirketa challenges another shortcut: agentic does not automatically mean autonomous. Alec describes recommendation, preparation, reviewed action, and automatic action as different levels; Case Resolver drafts responses and recommendations with human review for outgoing work [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:30:45]. Cole Medin favors agentic retrieval but calls it less predictable and asks for clear instructions about when each search method should be used [tLMViADvSNE @ 00:04:26]. These positions share retrieval as useful infrastructure while differing on orchestration and autonomy. The additional loop is a capability with operational costs, not a requirement attached to every knowledge-base project.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Writer's journey shows where a working retrieval prototype stopped being enough. Sam Julien reports that ordinary search handled basic keywords but did not deliver the advanced matching the team needed. Vector retrieval then introduced two problems: chunking could blur facts, and dense enterprise collections used so much overlapping language that nearest-neighbor results became difficult to distinguish. Their Apple timeline example confuses the Macintosh date with the nearby Lisa date, while the phone-company example has thousands of documents sharing camera and battery terminology [XlAIgmi_Vow @ 00:05:34]. Graphs improved context, but converting data into graph structure became challenging and costly at scale. The team also encountered expertise limits and matching difficulties, leading to a specialized extraction model and a different storage approach [XlAIgmi_Vow @ 00:07:47]. Adam Lucek's demonstration exposes the other side of richer retrieval: DRIFT gives a longer, more specific answer, but arrives after minutes rather than the seconds seen with earlier searches. Their closing comparison explicitly includes construction, maintenance, computational overhead, and scaling difficulty [6vG_amAshTk @ 00:38:31]. Cole Medin similarly describes LLM-based graph extraction as slower and more expensive [tLMViADvSNE @ 00:05:08]. Preserving relationships improves what can be retrieved, but these builders do not describe that structure as free.

Mirketa's most practical retrieval trap is that nearest does not necessarily mean relevant. Alec explains that vector search can return the closest available match even when the question concerns an undocumented problem. Case Resolver therefore adds similarity thresholds and reranking instead of accepting the first returned candidate [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:11:39]. The same team calls a confident answer built on a stale document especially hard to catch: the wording looks correct even though the evidence has expired or been replaced. Their framework communicates document age and replacement status, while their evaluation separates retrieved information, interpretation, and whether the recommendation helped resolve the case [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:17:28]. IBM's Marina Danilevsky identifies the opposite failure: a model instructed to answer only from grounding information may decline an answerable question if the retriever fails to surface the right source [T-D1OfcDW1M @ 00:05:35]. KodeKloud's own walkthrough also requires careful reading of settings. The initial chunking explanation says overlap 100, but the closing recap says overlap 400 for the test while retaining stride 400 for ingestion [_HQ2H_0Ayy0 @ 00:09:18]. Those are different stated settings, not a single consistent preset. The working checks remain the retrieved passages, their boundaries, and the answers produced from them.

Enterprise rollout introduces failures beyond the answer itself. Unstructured's Daniel Scoffield describes organizations with thousands of pilots and inconsistent ingestion stacks: separate teams write PDF scripts, extract Salesforce information differently, and use incompatible chunking logic. Their team has repeatedly been brought in to untangle that maintenance problem, and Scoffield favors a shared ingestion and RAG ETL layer [ruilmyfVgrI @ 00:19:29]. Their evaluation workflow begins with important question-answer pairs, tests context relevance and answer faithfulness before changes, and routes negative user feedback into retrieval-versus-generation triage. Fixed cases then join the golden set [ruilmyfVgrI @ 00:17:28]. Professor Patterns demonstrates a smaller but immediate maintenance trap: changing the embedding model in Open WebUI requires reimporting the documents even when the collection still displays them [LmXaPUky_u4 @ 00:08:04]. Mirketa brings the economics back to support outcomes: did resolution improve, did agents accept recommendations, did escalations decline, and did the case remain resolved [8N3k2qP_0SY @ 00:30:26]? Scoffield likewise criticizes measuring only technical accuracy or tool engagement while ignoring business outcomes. A successful upload, a generated answer, and a sophisticated retrieval diagram are separate from that outcome measurement. The recurring operational work is keeping ingestion consistent, diagnosing failures at the correct stage, and checking whether the system improves the workflow.
