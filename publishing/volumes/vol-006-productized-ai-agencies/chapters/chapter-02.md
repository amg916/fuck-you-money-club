# Chapter 02: The Designjoy Model: Building a Profitable Solo Design Subscription

*Brett Williams’s one-person design business, the productized-service model behind it, and practitioners’ perspectives on pricing, client acquisition, delivery, profitability, replication, and sustainability—including skepticism about its million-dollar revenue claims.*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Brett Williams sells immediate access to a defined design service, not an unlimited production team: clients queue requests, prioritize one active item, and receive finished work asynchronously. Harry Roper and Demandflow also favor recurring payment and repeatable delivery, while using different onboarding and communication systems. Williams reports a $5,000 monthly package and teaches five clients at that price rather than copying their largest solo workload. Figma, Trello, Stripe, and Webflow support the operation; quality, speed, and strict boundaries support delivery. Acquisition remains separate from packaging: Roper reactivated previous clients, while Williams used launch exposure, communities, referrals, and public business updates. Bal Sieber disputes subscription-first selling to strangers and favors proving results before offering recurring service. Demandflow instead schedules agreed workdays with a delivery team.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Sell a service that can be bought without rebuilding the deal for every customer. Williams describes an immediately accessible, fixed-price subscription: the client buys access, submits work, and avoids hourly estimates or individual project quotes. Their unlimited promise applies to submissions, not simultaneous production; one request moves through the queue at a time. Williams also acknowledges that the word unlimited attracts buyers while creating mismatched expectations [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:03:10]. Demandflow makes the same commercial case from a different delivery setup: predictable monthly costs, rapid access to talent, no long contracts, and recurring income around one repeatable marketing proposition. The buyer avoids discovery and quoting negotiations rather than purchasing a freshly negotiated project every time [w9gOdSLVu14 @ 00:00:38]. Harry Roper's starting point was similarly practical: replace one-time payments with monthly recurring revenue. Roper launched at $1,000 per month and reports more signups and better financial predictability [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:00:00]. These operators agree on repeatable purchasing and recurring payment, but their delivery promises are not identical. Williams uses sequential requests; Demandflow sells agreed working capacity; Roper emphasizes recurring updates. Williams's package also removes mandatory discovery meetings, while Demandflow emphasizes removing unexpected invoices and providing a repeatable offer.

The capability requirement is good work delivered quickly, not merely a subscription checkout. Williams and Chris Do emphasize that a designer who spends three weeks grinding through one assignment creates an unmanageable queue. Williams credits repetition with developing speed and describes moving from $2,000 to $5,000 as a decisive improvement, while declining further increases that could bring unwanted pressure [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:17:54]. Williams tells Malinowski that experienced designers might begin around $2,000–$3,000, while beginners may need two or three years before attempting this model. The stated prerequisites are quality and speed together; clients want fast, good work rather than the ultimate version of perfection [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:34:40]. Bal Sieber acknowledges Williams's talent but argues that reputation, recognition, and referrals explain success better than a copied subscription landing page. Sieber warns that repetitive production can exclude deeper relationships and strategic work [rI1y1OsDxdw @ 00:04:29]. Williams's own practice is narrower than a full-service promise: productize strong, fast specialties instead of accepting everything. The agreement is on actual capability and customer confidence; Sieber does not share Williams's preference for selling the subscription before a strategic engagement has established that confidence.

Distribution still has to put the offer in front of buyers. Williams tells Tom Hunt that design competence and a novel business model are insufficient without self-marketing, distribution, and exposure. They also reject changing a working public strategy simply because other designers want more portfolio posts [-UGc0T1KXkg @ 00:04:42]. Roper started closer to existing demand: email previous clients about inexpensive beta access for overdue website updates. A YouTube post also produced a subscriber, and Roper recommends social posting for natural leads. For someone without customers, Roper suggests serving the first client free to gain experience, testimony, and portfolio material before charging [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:00:38]. Williams's early community route was to participate in Indie Hackers and productized-service Facebook groups, announce launches, share progress, and build publicly. Other productized businesses became early customers; Williams explicitly describes slow growth rather than instant takeoff [-UGc0T1KXkg @ 00:28:19]. Sieber's warning targets the missing relationship: subscription does not make selling to strangers easier, particularly for someone copying Williams without a network [rI1y1OsDxdw @ 00:00:32]. Roper's customer emails, Williams's community participation, and Sieber's trust objection all address finding buyers rather than configuring the subscription itself. Their acquisition approaches therefore remain distinct from the mechanics of charging monthly.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Williams's onboarding starts after payment: copy a prepared Trello board, email the invitation, let instructional cards explain the service, and receive the first request. They estimate approximately fifteen seconds of onboarding labor in the Malinowski interview; the client does not need an elaborate orientation [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:23:50]. On The Futur, Williams describes three working columns: unlimited backlog, exactly one current request, and approved. Clients move their own cards, and a second current request turns the column yellow. Chris Do reinforces using one system and removing nonbillable meetings and management [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:34:31]. Roper handles intake differently, using interactive onboarding with checkboxes, forms, and company information collected upfront [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:02:06]. Williams keeps the request format loose: a Trello title, brief body, links, and comments can be enough; video briefs and Google Docs are accepted, and no universal template is imposed across incompatible design categories [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:39:02]. Communication stays inside the card rather than becoming a separate synchronous conversation. Roper's checkboxes collect setup information before production, whereas Williams relies on instructional cards and flexible briefs. Both describe removing small administrative steps rather than building an elaborate onboarding system around every new customer.

Execute in the tools Williams already uses quickly: Figma for design and browser handoff, Webflow for development, Trello for client requests, Stripe for payment, Shutterstock for imagery, and Airtable for the internal consolidated request list. Williams manually reviews separate client boards and updates one Airtable row per client and current request, rejecting automation because inspection catches changed cards, approvals, and unresolved issues [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:36:20]. For a new client, Williams spends disproportionately on the first project to establish the aesthetic foundation, then reuses that foundation rather than repeatedly exploring fresh directions [o-UR3rkPgJ4 @ 00:14:21]. Large jobs remain possible: when Chris Do initially excludes them, Williams describes accepting fifty- or sixty-plus-page redesigns and breaking them into two-day deliverables. One request can stay active for months, but updates contain finished designs ready for development, not status messages [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:45:41]. Roper's delivery clock is different: individual dashboards carry a guaranteed update every three days, with a tracker recording the last update and time remaining. Color warnings trigger reprioritization as tracking becomes difficult across multiple clients [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:02:59]. Williams's two-day delivery cadence and Roper's three-day update promise are separate service commitments, not interchangeable turnaround claims.

Price and utilization explain more than the headline client count. Williams reports a $5,000 most-popular package, discounted quarterly and annual options, and 99% choosing month-to-month. Existing clients retain their agreed prices; some still pay below $2,500. Doubling new-client pricing was intended to suppress demand and reduce stress, not to promise twice the output [o-UR3rkPgJ4 @ 00:01:36]. Chris Do calculates $100,000 monthly gross from twenty full-price clients, but Williams qualifies that illustration with legacy rates and says they no longer track MRR. Williams estimates that 20–25% underutilize the subscription while paying for availability, but rejects depending on wholly inactive $5,000 subscribers [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:07:09]. Billing flexibility also changes the relationship: pausing midway through a paid cycle preserves unused time, the board, history, and the locked rate. Cancellation finishes the paid month, closes and deletes the board and history, and requires current pricing on return [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:49:06]. Demandflow chooses another capacity mechanism: agree one or two working days weekly, schedule them after signup, and assign a designer, developer, and project lead [w9gOdSLVu14 @ 00:08:05]. That staffed, scheduled model is distinct from Williams's solo queue. Their prices and workload promises therefore describe different operating arrangements.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Bal Sieber rejects the idea that subscription packaging solves acquisition: strangers still need trust, and copying Brett Williams's landing page does not reproduce their reputation. Sieber's alternative starts with a specific client niche, a focused results offer, a repeatable process, and a clear timeline; delivery and case studies precede the subscription or retainer. They personally favor sequential large-client contracts and deeper strategic work [rI1y1OsDxdw @ 00:09:33]. Harry Roper took a different entry route: email previous clients about inexpensive beta access for overdue website updates, then use content to bring in additional subscribers. Roper reports launching at $1,000 monthly and reaching $5,000 quickly, with existing relationships supplying the initial audience [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:00:38]. Williams's early acquisition account is different again: a distinctive Webflow page, a Product Hunt hunter, community participation, and resource-site exposure. On Indie Bites, Williams says reaching $10,000 monthly took about three years, while acknowledging that luck and context limit repeatability [FpPgkqEoNDY @ 00:06:20]. Their later advice on Tom Hunt is explicitly gradual: join chronological communities, announce launches, share progress, and build publicly. Other productized-service owners supplied early customers; an immediate breakout is not the expectation Williams sets for someone starting without followers [-UGc0T1KXkg @ 00:28:19].

The delivery disagreement is sharper than a choice of project-management software. Williams removes client calls, lets customers prioritize one active Trello request, and sends finished design increments rather than filling the calendar with coordination. On The Futur, the host initially treats large projects as unsuitable; Williams counters that a 50–60-plus-page redesign can stay active for months while the customer receives usable designs in roughly two-day installments. The request is large, but the delivered increments remain bounded [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:45:41]. Demandflow instead sells agreed workload, such as one or two scheduled days weekly, with a designer, developer, and project lead. Their lead coordinates priorities in Slack, handles quality checks, and batches sequential tasks across the working day before issuing a consolidated update. ClickUp and shared Google Drive support that team-based arrangement [w9gOdSLVu14 @ 00:08:05]. Roper keeps asynchronous delivery but adds individual dashboards, interactive onboarding, and a tracker warning when the promised three-day update approaches [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:02:28]. Williams deliberately resists the automation assumption: earlier automations complicated delivery, so Stripe payment is followed by manually copying a Trello demo board and inviting the customer. They describe that setup as taking approximately 30 seconds, not as an elaborate automated onboarding sequence [-UGc0T1KXkg @ 00:09:43].

The revenue debate contains both skeptical commentary and conflicting explanations of what makes the workload possible. Zimri Mayfield divides the advertised annual revenue into monthly subscriptions, estimates roughly 22 clients at $5,000, and questions whether one designer can fulfill that load. Mayfield's accusation is their interpretation, not a firsthand account of Designjoy's operations [Z7hn_pkhIxM @ 00:03:42]. Williams's answer on The Creative Brief is utilization: among 30 clients, perhaps seven or eight have new requests, another seven or eight need revisions, and others are waiting or have nothing queued. They report many new tasks taking under 30 minutes and revisions taking five to fifteen, while emphasizing that prior experience makes this possible [Xn_pruXtu0s @ 00:16:20]. Client quality also gets competing treatment. Starter Story's solopreneur analysis divides buyers into demanding $500 customers and more secure $5,000 customers [vedkm3ZmHIE @ 00:05:33]. Williams gives a less hostile account on The Futur: early technical founders could be respectful and enjoyable, yet their limited budgets drove rapid requests and departure after initial work. Higher-paying clients sometimes request only one item monthly and remain longer. Williams's distinction concerns budgets, utilization, trust, and retention, rather than declaring every inexpensive customer difficult or every premium customer inactive [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:23:28].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Williams's capacity failures were not abstract warnings about being busy. On Creative Crew, they describe accepting money they struggled to refuse until the business left no room for breaks, vacations, or sickness. Their response was to guard volume, select clients more carefully, and consider whether the projects were interesting rather than accept everything available [o-UR3rkPgJ4 @ 00:06:09]. On The Futur, Williams recounts constant work during COVID-era growth, commonly twelve or thirteen hours daily, followed by severe burnout after monthly revenue rose from $80,000 to $160,000. They canceled prospect calls, blocked bookings, doubled rates, and removed demanding heavy-use accounts [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:09:32]. The Koerner Office account adds hospitalization after overwork and says doubling prices increased demand instead of suppressing it. Raising the number on the pricing page did not reliably remove the need to turn work away [bAzg8BugEVY @ 00:07:28]. Williams's later teaching target is substantially smaller: five customers paying $5,000 monthly, not reproducing their painful period of maximum volume. On Brett Malinowski, they recommend beginning with an attractive offer and increasing toward a workable value-and-stress balance. They explicitly separate the $25,000 teaching target from the overwork behind their own much larger revenue claims [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:32:19].

Communication exceptions created their own workload trap. Williams tells Tyler Gillespie that Slack support was popular but had to be rolled back because real-time responses overloaded capacity. The same interview records difficulty managing dozens of screens and states in Trello, and Williams's admission that financially irrational accommodation could keep them working at three in the morning [0IHziP_l6cc @ 00:33:07]. On The Futur, the earlier habit of accepting email and client-specific tools caused mistakes; Williams subsequently standardized the process and stopped changing the service to win individual accounts. Scope, communications, and purchasing expectations belong on the landing page before a customer joins, rather than being negotiated into a different workflow afterward [8vwgUwgH2Js @ 00:29:33]. Roper reports a smaller-scale tracking problem: once three to six or more clients are active, remembering who last received an update becomes difficult. Their response is a tracker showing elapsed time and color warnings, tied to a promised update every three days [rO0i7sGwhCU @ 00:02:59]. Williams identifies another trap in no-code development: custom scripts introduce uncertainty and can expose an essential but impossible feature a month into work. Their Webflow offer stays within native capabilities, with expectations established before page-by-page implementation begins [o-UR3rkPgJ4 @ 00:07:40].

The economics accounts separate cheap software from the other costs and fluctuations Williams reports. Starter Story quotes a precise $176 monthly operating bill covering Figma, Webflow hosting, Adobe, and Shutterstock, with free Trello and Airtable [dXKzST0FE-A @ 00:04:54]. On Brett Malinowski, Williams separately qualifies the profit discussion with 2.9% Stripe fees, substantial taxes, and other expenses below $200 monthly. They also describe customers paying for access while using relatively little of the service, rather than every subscription producing an identical workload [IvZxfvECZnA @ 00:29:56]. Revenue itself moves: on The Koerner Office, Williams reports churn and pauses creating $50,000–$60,000 swings, with a low around $40,000 and a peak around $200,000. The same account says prices reached $8,000 before being reduced, so the pricing history is not an uninterrupted staircase upward [bAzg8BugEVY @ 00:08:33]. Retention reporting has another complication. On The Creative Brief, Williams says pauses require Stripe cancellation, inflating apparent churn; some buyers leave because their projects or budgets end, while others have remained five or six years. They expect greater churn than software subscriptions and do not offer a measured churn rate. Their account distinguishes temporary pauses, exhausted project demand, and long-running relationships instead of treating every cancellation as the same customer outcome [Xn_pruXtu0s @ 00:58:13].
