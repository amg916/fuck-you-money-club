LinkedIn acquisition starts with a recognizable offer, relevant prospects, and a profile that makes the next step obvious. ## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with the buyer and the offer, not the sending tool. Patrick Dang reduces the business to who you want to work with, what you sell, and whether that audience actually wants it. They validate through customer conversations or by examining existing offers aimed at the same market; content cannot rescue something people will not pay for. Tommy Clark carries that specificity into the profile: explain why someone should follow you in one clear sentence, state your role and the value of your content, and provide an obvious next step through featured links. Their recommended destinations include a website or demo page alongside a newsletter or lead magnet for visitors who are not ready to buy. Chris Prouty adds the company page, arguing that prospects inspect it when deciding whether a business has expertise and authority. These operators all put understandable positioning ahead of elaborate execution. Dang wants a defined customer with a problem; Clark wants that customer to recognize the reason to follow; Prouty wants the business page to show what working together can accomplish. Their starting work is making the offer and identity legible before trying to manufacture more attention. [lzh20RhrVW4 @ 00:00:40] [MXBaHCO4WI8 @ 00:04:45] [8yepbhYel-Q @ 00:00:50]

Content becomes prospecting material when it gives someone a reason to recognize you and a reason to answer. Lead Gen Jay teaches miniature case studies built from a client problem, a specific intervention, and a numerical result, followed by a question that invites a relevant conversation. Their next move is not a random pitch: look for business owners and founders making substantive comments about response rates, lead generation, or acquisition. Ross Simmons on HubSpot Marketing similarly connects outreach to an observable interaction, including a prospect liking your post or a colleague's post. They tell sellers to explain why they want to connect rather than expecting an unexplained request to carry the conversation. They inspect each person against the offer before starting a conversation, then establish fit before booking a call. Jay uses proof, Simmons uses context, and Dang uses identifiable engagement. None treats a visible audience as the finished sales process: each describes a deliberate move from recognition into a conversation about the prospect's business, needs, or interest. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Adam Holmgren's advertising account has two campaign groups: cold audiences and retargeting. The cold layer uses text ads, image ads, and thought-leader ads; the retargeting layer keeps text and thought-leader ads but substitutes conversation ads for images. Their cold audience centers on marketing roles at technology companies and agencies, with 11 to 200 employees and a preferred audience size of 50,000 to 70,000. They exclude competitors, previous employers, signups, and companies outside the product's fit. Manual bids start around two-thirds of LinkedIn's recommendation, then get checked against daily budget delivery. Before sponsoring an organic post, Holmgren adds a website link and product information so landing-page clicks can be tracked separately from social actions. Their retargeting combines website traffic, company-page visits, and cold-ad engagement across 90-day windows. Prouty's route into paid distribution begins after organic content gains traction, then uses ads to expand reach and recognition. Clark supplies a specific organic publishing baseline: five weekly posts, split into one broad post, three industry-specific thought leadership posts, and one bottom-of-funnel post. Holmgren's cold thought-leader content likewise discusses company growth and the technology stack without making the product the immediate pitch. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

There is no single practitioner agreement on how a profile should look or how often its owner should publish. Tommy Clark recommends a straightforward corporate headshot, explicitly rejects AI for that picture, and says the header matters relatively little. Alex Berman wants a distinctive photo and gives the banner, headline, and custom button central roles in the funnel; they are comfortable using ChatGPT to fill out supporting profile sections. Ross Simmons also treats the banner as a billboard and encourages AI headshot tools when someone lacks a good photograph. The writing advice diverges as well: Clark wants the about section written personally, while Berman says the less important profile material can be generated. Publishing frequency is another explicit split. Clark's starting cadence is five weekly posts, mostly middle-of-funnel thought leadership. Lead Gen Jay says tests of posting three to five times daily and spamming comments failed across more than 100 agencies; their recommendation is three weekly posts centered on client problems and case-study proof. Jay's failed high-volume routine is not Clark's five-post week. The actual alternatives are different photo preferences, different uses of AI, and different content schedules tied to the practitioners' own approaches. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Bad targeting is not an abstract warning in Ben Greening-James's account. They searched for buyers agents, treated the list as ready, and automated outreach about property services. One recipient was a sports-car buyers agent, not a property buyers agent, and responded by offering help buying a sports car. Greening-James attributes the mismatch to inadequate filters and now emphasizes Boolean qualification, company information, and checking the lead list. Will Aitken describes the same title-matching problem from the receiving side: their deliberately inflated title, 'Chief Vice President Director of Managing Things,' attracts senders who assume they are a relevant executive. Lead Gen Jay reports a separate failure at the messaging stage: sending 100 pitches daily produced zero responses. Their replacement sequence waits after connection acceptance, engages with recent posts, and offers a relevant resource before pursuing the conversation. Greening-James also admits that early automated pitching booked some calls, but says it damaged trust by selling before establishing relevance or whether the recipient was in the market. These firsthand stories identify distinct breakpoints: a title can describe the wrong business, a senior-looking headline can attract irrelevant sellers, and connection acceptance does not mean someone wants an immediate pitch.
