## Executive Summary

Across these sixteen practitioner accounts, the recurring offer is paid diagnosis before development: interview the people doing the work, map expensive friction, rank feasible improvements, and present a priced roadmap. Antonio sells a $1,500–$3,000 bronze audit; Andrew Dunn describes $5,000–$15,000 engagements and a fourteen-day delivery process. Liam Ottley reports progressing from a free audit that cost $30,000 to a $60,000 engagement. Agreement ends at packaging: Carly Meyers permits audit-only delivery and referrals, while Dunn emphasizes implementation and recurring support. Human interviews compete with Orcus self-service intake; custom infrastructure competes with ready-made tools and non-AI integrations. Clifford Almeida sells visibility assessments around buyer prompts, whereas DataSnipper demonstrates evidence-based Excel procedures. Scheduling, adoption, weak discovery, inconsistent economics, and unreliable inputs remain recurring operational checks.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Sell the diagnosis before selling the machine. Antonio's starting problem is familiar: friendly calls, interested prospects, and no clear next step. Their paid business audit replaces that drift with a visual roadmap of high-leverage opportunities, expected returns, recommended projects, and the next ninety days. The intentionally small offer is not a hundred-page collection of unused ideas; the client helps build the recommendations and understands what comes next [YaFtlj9qXQc @ 00:02:04]. Andrew Dunn makes the commercial argument more aggressively: clients cannot visualize a workflow and care about results. Their strategic offer investigates operational pain, maps processes, projects ROI, and puts tools and timelines inside a transformation plan. A lead chatbot becomes one component of a broader lead-generation and nurturing project rather than the whole sale [HPGSE4LXaQA @ 00:04:05]. Stefan starts from the same business problem but describes it as a complexity ceiling: buried leads, fragmented data, and specialists acting as digital glue. Their sequence is problem, cost, operating logic, then technology. A generic tool added to a broken workflow can become another tab instead of removing work [NFQnvr9xNf8 @ 00:00:26]. These offers sell an explanation of where effort and money disappear, followed by a concrete path to change—not an isolated automation demo.

The recurring discovery move is to compare management's intended operation with the employee's actual day. Lobo Brother's separates leadership and frontline interviews because those accounts are rarely identical. Leaders identify expensive bottlenecks and failed attempts; employees explain repetitive work, hidden failures, and broken handoffs. Their interviewer frames automation around removing paperwork, re-entry, and chasing rather than replacing jobs, because replacement fears can suppress truthful answers [dqDWqV07r6M @ 00:03:21]. Nick Puru pushes beyond job descriptions: ask what happened yesterday. In their sales example, representatives spent the morning assembling lists, checking LinkedIn and Salesforce, removing duplicates, and copying fields. Map that sequence with its time, tools, handoffs, and blockages before deciding what to automate [7UpuiFHBvec @ 00:04:19]. Tom Hartnell adds the measurement discipline: establish time, cost, volume, error rate, and speed before implementation, while documenting the existing technology the solution must fit. Their CV-screening example starts with three hours per role across eight monthly roles, making the baseline twenty-four hours per month [WFW7vb6wXZM @ 00:04:23]. The shared prerequisite is access to people who perform the work and can describe its actual sequence. The roadmap follows that operational account, rather than assuming that an executive's first requested tool identifies the real constraint.

Paid discovery is also a boundary around presales labor. Milan Tahliani describes process mapping, infrastructure investigation, integration analysis, and recommendations as consulting that should already be paid. Their audit produces an ROI-backed roadmap before development, with the relationship positioned around strategic partnership rather than an open-ended developer role [2goNKtgnIKg @ 00:01:54]. In Liam Ottley's conversation with the Monk founders, Ali initially resisted charging $1,000 for an hour-long strategy call; a prospect accepted immediately. The founders describe paid discovery as a way to avoid long conversations with nonbuyers, educate skeptical owners, and establish paid trust before larger implementation commitments. They report roughly 83% audit-to-development conversion and explain that audit cash supports a growing delivery team while larger projects take time to close [2CdNLDttOSg @ 00:11:36]. Carly Meyers agrees that clarity is a saleable deliverable but does not require the consultant to perform implementation. Their sequence runs from content and DM qualification into paid interviews, a report, ROI, and a recommended project; execution can stay internal, move to a commission-paying partner, or remain with the client [stoZ9wFob2E @ 00:24:45]. The common commercial unit is a bounded diagnostic with a useful decision at the end. The follow-on business can be development, advice, referrals, or software access.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start the sale with qualification, then put the delivery calendar in place before interviews scatter. Andrew Dunn uses budget, authority, need, and timing, followed by signed scope, cleared money, and scheduled onboarding. Their smaller local audits may close in one call, while larger engagements need several conversations and multiple decision-makers. Leadership onboarding identifies the sponsor, organization chart, roles, headcount, tools, revenue model, acquisition process, and perceived pain. Book the final presentation immediately and gather interviewees to schedule discovery together [LSmxwtNHu4k @ 00:06:31]. Carly Meyers makes payment trigger the welcome email, calendar booking, intake questions, and follow-up automation. Their intake asks about revenue, team, and frustrations; interview count influences the fee. The delivery promise is clarity, with internal fulfillment, outsourcing, or advisory chosen according to the consultant's preferred working model [stoZ9wFob2E @ 01:06:44]. Liam Ottley's beginner assessment gives a concrete smaller-business scope: two weeks, $5,000–$10,000, three to five key people, potentially ten to fifteen interviews lasting thirty to forty-five minutes. Week one produces interview summaries, process diagrams, and a pain inventory; week two assesses solutions and validates recommendations [noivN2hIXLY @ 00:19:07]. This is scheduled discovery with named participants and outputs, not a promise to inspect every employee or solve every department before presenting a first decision.

Turn the interviews into task-level maps, then test each proposed intervention. Andrew Dunn maps acquisition, delivery, and support, marking time, quality, and risk sinks. Their sales example distinguishes strategic targeting from repetitive prospecting: people retain the targeting judgment while search, HubSpot checks, data entry, and sequences are candidates for automation. A broad request such as follow-up becomes opening the CRM, filtering leads, checking fit, reading the submission, drafting, sending, and setting the next action [AG_L5ecwXkU @ 00:03:52]. Nick Puru then applies structured inputs, predictable outputs, rule-based decisions, and sufficient repetition as feasibility filters. Missing context, strategic judgment, conflict resolution, and uneconomic rare tasks do not fit the same automation pattern. Their tenant example puts routine questions into twelve categories while leaving exceptions with people [7UpuiFHBvec @ 00:09:11]. Carly Meyers records interviews with Fathom or Fireflies and feeds the transcripts into a ChatGPT custom GPT. They call the first output a starting point, roughly sixty percent of the work, then challenge it and apply personal judgment. Subscription spreadsheets and High Level documentation can inform the analysis, while Gamma turns vetted copy into a branded report [stoZ9wFob2E @ 00:46:37]. The software accelerates synthesis; the practitioner still chooses which recommendations survive.

Package the recommendation as a decision, not a tour of technical components. Mert Yerlikaya's five-slide report opens with scope and objectives, then shows an impact-versus-effort matrix, a phased roadmap, current-and-future workflow deep dives, and an ROI summary. The financial slide names the solution, one-time implementation cost, annualized savings, and payback. Their sales-research example describes qualified prospects ready each morning, checked against Salesforce, rather than leading with API or model terminology [1leregs81ik @ 00:07:31]. Andrew Dunn adds an alignment stage before polishing the final deck. During days eight through eleven, a feasibility call and a simple bullet list test whether the client actually wants the proposed priorities. If sales matters more than marketing, revise the plan before the presentation. The final deck then reflects an already-discussed decision instead of trying to persuade the client of unfamiliar findings [LSmxwtNHu4k @ 00:10:24]. Tom Hartnell sequences implementation around confidence: quick wins during the first one to two months, additional wins and larger projects around months two through five, and transformative projects after confidence exists [WFW7vb6wXZM @ 00:06:08]. Their shared delivery mechanic is a ranked opportunity inventory with an intelligible starting point, a visible later phase, and business measurements attached to the work the client is being asked to buy.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

There is no single audit size or mandatory route into the business. Carly Meyers promises clarity within five days and lets beginners start with roughly five free business audits, then $200 or $500 engagements before progressing toward $5,000–$10,000. Interview count and client size shape the package, and implementation can be outsourced or omitted from the consultant's own workload [stoZ9wFob2E @ 01:06:44]. Milan Tahliani takes the opposite presales position: never give away audits. Their earlier paid engagements were $1,000–$5,000 for one or two weeks maximum, while later pure-strategy work reached upwards of $25,000. They prefer flat fees tied to checkable deliverables but accept hourly work when the client prefers it and profitability remains intact [2goNKtgnIKg @ 00:10:58]. Liam Ottley's enterprise progression is different again: an initial free eight-week audit cost Morningside $30,000, the next engagement charged $30,000 over six weeks, and a subsequent engagement charged $60,000 over four weeks. Their smaller-business beta wanted immediate tools and ROI; larger organizations needed education, alignment, cultural planning, and accommodation of legacy systems [noivN2hIXLY @ 00:12:56]. Ottley does not present the enterprise engagement as effortless beginner work. The differences concern experience, organizational access, deliverables, and whether the initial audit is a learning investment or a paid standalone service.

Automation inside the audit is another dividing line. Andrew Dunn rejects AI-voice substitutes for discovery interviews, arguing that they reduce effectiveness and the fees the consultant can command. Their analysis phase can still use AI: Consultics reportedly compresses production of maps, matrices, ROI models, and an initial deck to thirty to forty-five minutes [LSmxwtNHu4k @ 00:09:03]. The company in progress practitioner deliberately offers both self-service and consultant-led Orcus audits. A thirty-eight-question intake accepts uploads, transcripts, or spoken answers, then generates findings and opportunities. The speaker nevertheless says interviews create a more personalized, detailed plan than automatic generation [fSAazWLuDyQ @ 00:03:44]. Tool selection also separates the playbooks. Stefan emphasizes logic-first operational infrastructure rather than generic tools dropped into a fragmented business [NFQnvr9xNf8 @ 00:01:07]. Lobo Brother's evaluates employee adoption difficulty, not merely developer build difficulty, and uses a different traffic-light meaning: red can designate valuable structural work for later, rather than a low-value project to reject [dqDWqV07r6M @ 00:06:37]. Their green projects must justify investment before expensive builds proceed. These distinctions change the interview format, the implementation architecture, and the meaning of a roadmap label; identical colors or the shared word audit do not make the operating methods interchangeable.

Visibility audits and finance procedures have different objects of inspection from an operational opportunity assessment. Clifford Almeida sells a $299–$999 visibility audit into a prioritized ninety-day plan, productized implementation, and monthly retainer. The work examines buyer-intent prompts across selected AI platforms: mentions, competing recommendations, cited sources, content, and technical foundations. Their generator creates twenty prompts, and the client selects, edits, or replaces ten before paying, preventing later arguments about whether the questions resemble real customer searches [tT1dV4_aC0M @ 00:19:56]. DataSnipper instead demonstrates Excel agents performing revenue testing, accounts-payable approval checks, control-report extraction, and bank reconciliation. The system uses documents, spreadsheet templates, specialized agent roles, and evidence snips; it is not simply a chatbot placed inside Excel. Planning mode helps organize work before execution, and existing document-reading and snipping capabilities remain available [fabs_LzN3oI @ 00:00:00]. Tom Hartnell's opportunity audit has a third deliverable: a prioritized inventory of workflow improvements followed by phased implementation. Their North Star Recruitment example is explicitly fictional, using screening, job-ad generation, scheduling, and candidate matching to demonstrate prioritization [WFW7vb6wXZM @ 00:07:17]. Almeida measures visibility around chosen prompts; DataSnipper inspects evidence against specified procedures; Hartnell maps where a business might improve. Each practitioner names a different decision the client or operator is trying to make.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The expensive failure is often the selling model before it is the software. Milan Tahliani reports probably millions of dollars lost, unpaid work, and scope creep before adopting paid audits. Their corrective sequence limits unpaid presales, sells the diagnostic, presents findings, and then proposes development. A prospect refusing payment can indicate poor qualification, but Tahliani also tells the agency to inspect its own value and framing rather than automatically blaming the prospect [2goNKtgnIKg @ 00:09:22]. Carly Meyers describes an agency created by customer demand that eventually produced burnout and holiday interruptions. The two clients they wanted to retain had both completed audits, prompting the return to diagnostic selling [stoZ9wFob2E @ 00:13:56]. The Monk founders report twelve months of trending chatbots, cold outreach, volatile income, and little progress before repositioning around audits. Copied agency demos and no-code posts generated almost no buyer meetings; their later content emphasized distinctive identity, business problems, and outcomes [2CdNLDttOSg @ 00:28:37]. Andrew Dunn identifies a less glamorous bottleneck: poor scheduling can leave an audit waiting three weeks for one person, while replacement fears undermine cooperation and adoption [LSmxwtNHu4k @ 00:06:31]. These accounts locate friction in unpaid diagnosis, unclear boundaries, weak positioning, interview access, and employee expectations—not simply in the difficulty of building an agent.

Keep sale prices, estimated benefits, pipeline, and delivered results in their original categories. Liam Ottley's free steel-company audit created fifteen opportunities and roughly $250,000 US of development pipeline. Their recommendation subset paired implementation cost with estimated savings and redeployed capacity; pipeline is not the same figure as collected implementation revenue [noivN2hIXLY @ 00:09:37]. Mert Yerlikaya's SDR example states two hours per representative daily, eight representatives, sixteen daily hours, $40 loaded hourly cost, $3,200 weekly cost, and over $166,000 annually. The same explanation also says eight hundred weekly hours, which conflicts with its stated weekly dollar total. Keep that mismatch visible rather than presenting all the figures as one reconciled calculation [1leregs81ik @ 00:05:28]. Nick Puru reports $215,500 impact, 166% first-year ROI, and 9.2-month payback for a property-management implementation, alongside figures whose units and totals are not consistently reconciled [7UpuiFHBvec @ 00:15:05]. Lobo Brother's Coastal Comfort portfolio is explicitly hypothetical: its hours, savings, recurring revenue, and $96,000 build cost illustrate an investment comparison rather than a realized client outcome [dqDWqV07r6M @ 00:10:47]. Each example has a stated role in the sale. Historical audit fees, projected implementation value, and modeled operating improvements are different entries in that business case.

Production shortcuts still leave inspection work. The Orcus presenter questions an automatically generated quoting-engine timeline instead of accepting the estimate untouched. Their commercial description also contains conflicting self-service prices: an early $995 becomes repeated $9.95 references later, alongside $299 for platform access without a stated billing interval [fSAazWLuDyQ @ 00:15:25]. DataSnipper says its demonstrations assume data preparation has already happened: incoming listings may be incomplete or irrelevant, and documents must be reconciled before procedures. Operators can inspect evidence snips, interrupt execution, and review workpapers. The bank-reconciliation timing is described as twenty minutes and later fifteen, against an earlier two-hour task; the presenter emphasizes correctness, traceability, and unchanged human accountability [fabs_LzN3oI @ 00:24:43]. Clifford Almeida treats visibility scores as prompt-dependent baselines, not universal commercial targets. Changing a few words can substantially change results, and AI-assisted discovery may later appear as direct or branded organic traffic. Their reporting tracks visibility, clicks, and leads over time rather than promising a fixed lead uplift [tT1dV4_aC0M @ 00:45:36]. Almeida also reports that weak fresh content can briefly rank and then disappear within seven to fourteen days. Their response is stronger competitive content analysis and ongoing Client Reports, not rerunning the initial audit every month.
