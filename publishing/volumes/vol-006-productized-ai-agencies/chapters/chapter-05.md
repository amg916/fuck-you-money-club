## The Practitioner Consensus

The shared sales move is to make the existing business problem visible before introducing the machinery. Michael Reimer opens discovery as a friendly business checkup: where customers already try to reach the owner, where follow-up breaks down, and which measurable area deserves attention first. Their questions cover inbound calls, missed calls, customer value, response speed, old CRM contacts, reviews, website traffic, and resulting leads. The answers select the service: voice for missed calls, conversational AI for slow replies, reactivation for old contacts, reputation work for weak trust, or website optimization for traffic that produces no leads. Lily Latzo makes the same commercial distinction through an offer one-pager: businesses buy reduced costs, increased output, and saved time rather than AI as an abstract upgrade. Their package specifies a repetitive workflow, a metric, a demonstration, and a next step. AI Automation Clips gives the broader implementation version: examine business processes, identify real problems, and establish the financial case before development. Its audit moves through discovery, process analysis, opportunity identification, and a roadmap. Reimer's smaller starting point is one measurable workflow rather than an all-at-once transformation; Lily similarly packages one automation with its workflow design and documentation. Reimer [IWNNb6VOVz8 @ 00:00:30]; Lily [NjvNF17_97w @ 00:01:53]; AI Automation Clips [RHZMQe6R_5A @ 00:01:06].

A defined buyer makes both prospecting and fulfillment more concrete. Bennett Black recommends a niche because an agency can build a system catalog, price repeatable deployments beforehand, and show buyers other systems already built for their industry. Small modifications become additional integration work instead of forcing every conversation into a completely new scope. Johnny targets businesses with the ability to carry a retainer, demonstrating managed service providers and IT companies with 10 to 100 employees. Their offer eventually shifted away from trying to charge substantial retainers to startups that could not afford them. Ian Cushing's local-business sourcing takes a different route because national databases can miss construction owners without LinkedIn profiles. HousePro scrapes Google Maps, searches state business entities, looks for the owner, and tries email variations to reach a named person instead of a generic sales address. Its cleaning campaigns target apartment complexes, realtors, retirement facilities, and commercial buildings rather than assuming home services means individual homeowners. Cushing describes the demand as existing already: the outreach puts a cleaning service in front of someone who needs cleaning. Black's catalog, Johnny's purchasing-power filter, and Cushing's local ownership research each start with a specific kind of buyer rather than a universal AI pitch. Black [HpBFCMSfM-I @ 00:05:05]; Johnny [MlJ3XSy3k7A @ 00:07:37]; Cushing [NT0JXm1lCyE @ 00:10:14].

Automation earns its place by removing research and administrative work, while several operators keep the commercially sensitive conversation human. Johnny automates verification, lead research, subject lines, and icebreakers but personally handles the offer copy and positive replies. Their preference is explicit: produce interested prospects, then let someone strong at sales book the appointment. Marcus Pizarro, speaking with Carson Reed, similarly automates initial LinkedIn contact but manually answers prospects once they respond. Questions about cost or the catch require contextual replies; Marcus does not want an inaccurate automated answer damaging a client's reputation. Jake at Aletto extends that division across the sales operation. AI can initiate contact, research prospects, follow up, score recorded sales calls, and draft a proposal using the buyer's own words. Humans still close deals and conduct coaching conversations. Aletto's proposal workflow reads the call, fills a locked one-page template, and puts the result in front of a human to check; Jake says proposals go out within the next 20 minutes. The same operating pattern appears after booking: automated reminders support the appointment, but a human confirmation call creates a personal commitment. For these operators, the valuable output is a conversation that progresses, not merely a completed automation run or an inbox full of replies. Johnny [MlJ3XSy3k7A @ 00:17:58]; Marcus [L1LsEN_iuIc @ 00:31:05]; Aletto [IfS3QohXxG8 @ 00:12:14].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Tom Gaddis's local prospecting workflow starts inside HighLevel's agency dashboard: choose Prospecting, add a business category, set a location, and adjust the radius. Their demonstration searches plumbers in Las Vegas with a 3.1-mile radius. The results expose website presence, Google Business Profile status, reviews, and other marketing signals. Premium bulk selection creates reports and contacts; using the connected subaccount makes those contacts available to workflows. Gaddis then uses Smart Directory AI to turn selected contacts into listings by adding the business tag. The opening conversation asks the owner to verify an existing free listing rather than buy an AI service. After a reply, Gaddis raises a specific gap, such as a missing chat widget, and proposes a strategy call. Cushing uses a more direct local approach: the first email usually asks whether an estimate would help or when someone can visit. A cleaning quote can start with square footage and bathroom count. Lily's research sheet instead records company name, decision maker, LinkedIn profile, a why-now signal, and outreach status. Hiring, layoffs, new locations, and backlogs provide the reason for contact. Each workflow arrives at a concrete opener: a listing to verify, an estimate to request, or a specific operational change to discuss. Gaddis [A_LppzjgUi0 @ 00:12:29]; Cushing [NT0JXm1lCyE @ 00:12:08]; Lily [NjvNF17_97w @ 00:06:19].

Johnny's email system combines Zapmail domains and mailboxes, ReachInbox campaigns, Apollo targeting, Google Sheets, and n8n workflows. They recommend three mailboxes per domain and describe at least a couple of weeks of warm-up. The processing workflow double-verifies addresses, then creates a personalized subject line and icebreaker using LinkedIn information. Johnny moved away from scraping whole websites because that was expensive at scale and often returned old information. Once processing finishes, an email notification signals that the enriched sheet is ready for campaign setup. Their approximate entry cost is $100 monthly for roughly 300 daily outreaches, while their own 1,500-daily setup costs around $400 to $500 monthly with white-label expenses. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Gaddis argues that conventional cold outreach is collapsing under repetitive AI pitches, yet their replacement still uses email alongside listings, texts, forms, and calls. The distinction is the opening offer: a business already featured on a community website has something concrete to inspect and verify. Their directory method tries to create a conversation before introducing recurring services. Shepherd takes the opposite position on scale. After infrastructure trouble and a missed sending target, they still call cold email far from dead and defend volume because it permits more campaigns and more chances for a strong segment to carry weaker ones. Their final update reports 98 interested responses and about 20 booked meetings. Cushing's local service model is smaller and more direct: typically 2,000 to 4,000 emails per client monthly for eight to twelve warm qualified billable leads. The first email often asks for an estimate opportunity rather than a relationship-building asset. Cushing also tests a demo link in the signature after domains and inboxes have aged for three to four months with strong deliverability, reporting bookings without replies. These are different operating bets: Gaddis sells through a community relationship, Shepherd through broad campaign testing, and Cushing through an immediate local service need followed by a phone call. Gaddis [A_LppzjgUi0 @ 00:29:39]; Shepherd [RQg5jgraVg8 @ 00:19:02]; Cushing [NT0JXm1lCyE @ 00:15:29].

The sharpest disagreement concerns how the first conversation is manufactured. Pavlo deliberately opens as though the connection broke up, asks whether the business is still open, and says it appeared closed. They explicitly say they do not know whether it is open or closed and use the line across the list; if challenged, they answer that they saw it somewhere on Google. Pavlo also says an AI employee or website has already been built, then explains that nothing is built beforehand. Interested website prospects receive a duplicated HighLevel site with their logo before the presentation. Marcus rejects the same customer-like ambiguity in DMs. They report that vague openings followed by a sudden pitch made prospects ignore or ghost them and their clients. Their replacement includes a short personalized hook, an honest bridge explaining the relevant work, and a question about the prospect's current acquisition approach. Lily's alternative is a demonstration-first message: identify the observed issue, share a five-minute walkthrough, and provide a calendar link. Pavlo's tactic depends on curiosity before explanation; Marcus makes commercial intent visible without dumping features; Lily supplies the demonstration before asking for the call. Their scripts should be read as contrasting approaches, not interchangeable versions of one shared opening. Pavlo [GCChOuvd7Sg @ 00:03:05]; Marcus [L1LsEN_iuIc @ 00:22:37]; Lily [NjvNF17_97w @ 00:07:38].

What gets sold after the meeting is equally unsettled. Black offers three structures: a flat-fee system with optional maintenance, a form-triggered micro-SaaS charging through credits or tokens, and an unlimited-project retainer. Their micro-SaaS example accepts information through a form, triggers an automation, creates a Drive folder, and shares the output without an onboarding call or custom project scope. Johnny distinguishes infrastructure licensing from a managed appointment-generation service. Licensing leaves the client operating tools and paying their costs; the managed service keeps the tooling with Johnny and delivers booked calls, notifications, weekly reports, and a weekly call. Rico Parnaste challenges the premise that companies mainly want someone else to build isolated automations. Their alternative teaches the team to use AI and build its own repetitive workflows through workshops, multiweek training, consulting, and a continuing retainer. The training product uses a repeatable program with each company's examples, then follows up to establish habits. Samuel offers another route: turn a client's desired outbound workflow into a subscription application integrating existing services. Its users bring their own enrichment credentials and operate through one interface. The commercial split is therefore not just setup versus monthly pricing: Black packages systems, Johnny operates acquisition, Rico transfers skills, and Samuel packages access to an integrated application. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Shepherd's experiment exposes the gap between planned volume and delivered campaign performance. The target was 250,000 emails, but the first update reported around 100,000 sends and a 0.36% reply rate. Shepherd traced the problem to widespread trouble affecting the .info domains used for most inboxes. The backup infrastructure used .info too, so the intended fallback did not solve the problem. Replacements required further warm-up, and the final update still missed the original target. Their later overall reply rate approached 0.66%, with better-targeted individual campaigns performing more strongly. The end report separates interested responses from actual bookings; the subsequent deal count is expressed through a close-rate calculation. Cushing reports a different failure of easy replication: pressure-washing campaigns launched the same week with almost identical targeting and scripts produced 15 leads in two weeks in Toronto but one in Fort Worth. Johnny's own cost lesson is to verify addresses even after using Apollo's verified filter. They target a bounce rate below 1% and describe skipped verification leading to damaged inboxes, replacement domains, and another warm-up cycle. The operators' numbers attach to specific infrastructure, markets, and campaign stages rather than a single universal email-to-client conversion figure. Shepherd [RQg5jgraVg8 @ 00:13:29]; Cushing [NT0JXm1lCyE @ 00:17:22]; Johnny [MlJ3XSy3k7A @ 00:09:50].

Gaddis catches an audit error while presenting the very workflow they sell. Claude reports no responses to a dentist's reviews, but Gaddis opens the Google Business Profile and sees that the business is responding. Claude had also explained that some profile information came from aggregated sources rather than a direct pull. Gaddis's instruction is to spend ten or fifteen minutes checking the audit, visiting the profile, and confirming the supposed opportunity before presenting it. Their own directory-software story contains another bottleneck: setup had taken so long that they struggled to reach the money-making work, especially when waiting for traffic. Aletto identifies a related implementation burden after noticing too much time spent checking AI's work. Jake's response is to define what done means, inspect the real result, let the system attempt fixes up to three times, and escalate with proof rather than accept a success message. Rico's training delivery adds a practical counterpart: survey participants before the workshop, verify accounts and equipment, and use real tasks. Their follow-up sends the recording and playbook within 24 hours, checks progress after seven days, holds an office hour after fourteen days, and reviews results after thirty. The deliverable includes checking and adoption, not just generating an impressive-looking output. Gaddis [Z3ZaxxexqYE @ 00:16:04]; Aletto [IfS3QohXxG8 @ 00:21:25]; Parnaste [KVhWoj-Irv4 @ 00:15:23].

Johnny's appointment-setting purchase is a direct reversal of the fully automated sales promise. After spending multiple four figures on an AI appointment-setting license, they concluded that it handled much of the routine work but performed poorly on the smaller portion that mattered most. They prefer answering interested prospects themselves rather than sacrificing bookings to save a few hours. Their licensing model created another sales bottleneck: because clients had to operate the tools, discovery filled with questions about why each tool was needed instead of staying focused on booked calls. Marcus reports a parallel delegation problem: a virtual assistant duplicates the operator's existing process, including its failures. They recommend proving the process personally with at least two or three appointments weekly before delegation. Black adds the recurring-revenue check. An unlimited-project retainer requires constant communication, demonstrated progress, and clear delivery timing, while the client's list of worthwhile systems can eventually run out. They rank that arrangement below individually priced systems or packages. For flat-fee builds, Black has clients create their own automation accounts and API keys so usage belongs to the client's system; maintenance then pays for management rather than quietly absorbing execution costs. The recurring fee needs an ongoing job behind it, whether that job is management, delivery, or a continuing service. Johnny [MlJ3XSy3k7A @ 00:17:06]; Marcus [L1LsEN_iuIc @ 00:38:12]; Black [HpBFCMSfM-I @ 00:03:57].
