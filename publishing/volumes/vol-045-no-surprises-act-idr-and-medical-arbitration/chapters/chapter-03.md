*CMS QPA calculation webinar, AMA out-of-network payment process, good faith estimate and PPDR requirements, provider GFE training, Texas, Michigan, and New Hampshire state dispute regimes, fully insured vs. self-funded ERISA plans, preemption and anti-subrogation, and business associate agreements*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from the AMA, CMS, state regulators, billing firms and law firms converge on one operating reality: No Surprises Act protections are narrow, facility-bound, and governed by a strict precedence of all-payer model, specified state law, then federal IDR, with self-funded ERISA plans staying federal unless they opt in. The street mechanics are hard numbers: 30 days to invoke negotiation, 4 days to file IDR, a 50-minute portal timeout, QPA built on the 2019 median contracted rate with at least 3 rates, and a $400 per-provider PPDR trigger with a $25 fee. The fights are real: the AMA says the QPA presumption tilted the scale toward insurers, NSCHBC Edge speakers see resolutions sliding to the lowest Medicaid rate, and Michigan, New Hampshire, and Texas each layer state-specific routes, fees, and opt-in rules on top.

## The Practitioner Consensus: Narrow Protections, Strict Precedence, and Two Anchor Documents

The protected zone is narrower than most billing offices assume, and every serious practitioner lines up on that point. The American Medical Association panel maps three protected lanes: emergency services at out-of-network hospital EDs or freestanding EDs, non-emergency out-of-network care delivered inside an in-network hospital or ambulatory surgery center, and air ambulance, with payers owing payment within 30 days and an initial payment that must be reasonably complete [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:08:13]. The NSCHBC Edge Podcast discussion with healthcare attorney Amanda Wish lands in the same place: the facility rules hit hospitals, long-term acute care, ambulatory surgery centers, and facilities, not physician clinics, and the federal law only applies in very limited circumstances [uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:21:49]. DAS Health draws the transport line, with air ambulance inside the protections and ground ambulance outside them [e5b1ICVPSZs @ 00:10:37]. The economic engine behind the whole fight is the staffing model rather than the patient: WFAA's reporting shows most Texas ER doctors do not actually work for the hospital, they are contracted out, and they do not take the same plans as the building they stand in [_ofpUW91u8o @ 00:00:37]. Operators therefore start every file with setting triage, confirming the place of service, the facility network status, and the physician arrangement before quoting a single protection to anyone.

Which law governs a claim is the second point of agreement, and no practitioner pretends the answer is easy. The AMA panel lays out a strict order of precedence, an all-payer model first (Maryland only), then a specified state law, then the federal IDR, with ERISA plans generally staying federal unless the plan opts in to the state process [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:12:56]. Zotec's Marc Eisenberg and Thomas Harold, speaking at Becker's Hospital Review, name New Jersey and Texas as states letting self-funded plans opt in and flag the operational trap: remittance files do not identify whether a plan is ERISA [RJps0omOazk @ 00:28:17]. Miller Johnson's Michigan team hits the same wall, warning there is no reliable way to tell self-funded from insured coverage when a card may carry only a small 'sf' [EwlPyAVGU8k @ 00:08:21]. New Hampshire's insurance department confirms the pattern at the state level: its IDR route is mandatory for fully insured plans, optional for self-funded plans, and no self-funded plan had opted in yet, though Consumer Services can confirm plan type on request [C6hIoQuEWeA @ 00:16:15]. Plan-type confirmation is therefore a standing intake step, not an afterthought, because the wrong forum assumption burns the filing clock.

The two documents that move money are the qualifying payment amount on the plan side and the good faith estimate on the provider side, and the speakers agree on what each one is. CMS's Colin Goldfinch and team define the QPA as the plan's median contracted rate as of January 31, 2019, increased for inflation, and IDR entities must consider it [Sp7aK2UsmlE @ 00:07:55]. The AMA panel argues the original rules put a thumb on the scale for insurers by presuming that median correct, which drove the AMA/AHA and Texas Medical Association lawsuits and the February vacatur of the weighting in the Eastern District of Texas [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:02:14]. On the estimate side, CMS presenters treat any cost inquiry as a GFE request [RRI2VT8QiiI @ 00:18:56], and HIPAA TV goes further, saying any cost conversation triggers a GFE, the rule reaches every licensed provider including cash-only, non-HIPAA practices, and the exposure runs to ten thousand dollar penalties [T99RB14fdY4 @ 00:02:44]. The operating consensus is blunt: the QPA sets the plan's anchor price, the GFE sets the yardstick a self-pay patient can dispute against, and front-desk staff who discuss price without issuing paperwork create exposure on the spot.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Clocks, QPA Math, and GFE Thresholds

The federal clock is unforgiving and the AMA panel walks it day by day. The plan pays or denies within 30 days; the provider then has 30 days to invoke a 30-day open negotiation by form notice, then 4 days to initiate IDR on the portal, where the parties pick an IDR entity or the federal government picks one [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:21:22]. Offers are due within 10 days, the decision within 30, and payment within 30, with a 90-day cooling-off period during which claims are batched; the portal form also carries a 50-minute timeout, so the file has to be assembled before login [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:24:00]. The arbiter must select one of the two offers, baseball style, and the providers are really in the driver's seat about initiating. New Hampshire's insurance department pitches a faster state lane: no administrative fee, each party paying half the entity's fee instead of loser-pays, and up to 25 qualified items batched for a single fee when they share provider, carrier, and a 30-day window [C6hIoQuEWeA @ 00:04:45]. The enforcement teeth are real: a party that fails to submit its half of the fee by day 10 eats a default judgment, and the decision lands by day 30 [C6hIoQuEWeA @ 00:12:38].

Inside the QPA math, CMS presenters give operators the exact levers to probe. Each separate contract counts as one rate, a group contract with a uniform rate counts once, and each unique rate inside a multi-provider contract counts separately, while single-case agreements and letters of agreement are excluded and rented or TPA-managed network rates count [Sp7aK2UsmlE @ 00:11:20]. Sufficient information means at least 3 contracted rates as of January 31, 2019, anesthesia runs as indexed conversion factor times base, time, and physical-status modifier units, and every initial payment or denial must carry the QPA, a compliance certification, notice of the 30-day negotiation and 4-day IDR windows, and contact information; on request the plan must also disclose non-fee-for-service rate use, the database used, related codes, and excluded incentive payments [Sp7aK2UsmlE @ 00:36:07]. The pressure point is enforcement, not arbitration: the AMA panel stresses that the IDR entity does not check whether the QPA was calculated correctly, so suspected errors get reported to the government [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:31:34]. CMS then audits up to 25 plans or issuers a year plus complaint-triggered audits, demands a step-by-step methodology narrative and a file of all QPAs, and penalizes up to $100 per day, per entity, per affected individual [Sp7aK2UsmlE @ 00:39:14].

On the estimate side the numbers are hard-coded. CMS presenters set the PPDR trigger at billed charges of $400 or more over the GFE, assessed per provider, with a $300 estimate against an $850 bill qualifying, a 120-day filing window, a $25 administrative fee, no collections during the dispute, and 6-year retention [RRI2VT8QiiI @ 00:40:00]. HIPAA TV hammers the per-provider math: three providers each over by $300, $900 in total, does not trigger arbitration, and the listed defenses are acuity, scope, and complications [T99RB14fdY4 @ 00:18:25]. DAS Health runs the same test with a $1,000 estimate split $500/$500 and billed $800/$700, which is not eligible, and adds that a settlement must cut the bill by at least half the fee with notice within 3 business days [e5b1ICVPSZs @ 00:39:42]. For recurring care, Dr. Marie Fang of Private Practice Skills works from the CMS template, customizes it per client, and issues one recurring GFE covering up to 12 months, roughly 40 to 52 sessions, starting with a TBD diagnosis and re-issuing whenever costs change or annually with rate increases [JT1pxEvMFxw @ 00:08:54]. That single document collapses dozens of per-session estimates into one renewal cycle, the admin relief Fang wants as a clinician who would rather see clients than do paperwork.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Classify the claim before touching a form
   - Confirm the plan, the provider or facility, and the item or service, then rule out a plain billing error.
   - Run the three-variable check on plan, provider/facility, and item/service to decide whether a state law applies [American Medical Association (AMA) — 6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:15:21]; the first analysis is whether the NSA applies at all or whether the file is simply a billing error [NSCHBC Edge Podcast — uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:10:30].
2. Read the remit for the federal signal
   - Check whether the plan sent a QPA and the required disclosures with its initial payment or denial.
   - A plan that believes federal law applies sends the QPA with its remittance advice [American Medical Association (AMA) — 6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:19:50]; that initial payment or denial must also carry a compliance certification, the 30-day negotiation and 4-day IDR notice, and contact information [CMSHHSgov — Sp7aK2UsmlE @ 00:36:07].
3. Run the negotiation and filing clock
   - Invoke open negotiation inside 30 days, then file IDR inside the initiation window on the correct portal.
   - Invoke the 30-day open negotiation within 30 days of the payment or denial, then initiate IDR within 4 days [American Medical Association (AMA) — 6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:22:04]; in New Hampshire the provider can instead file the state route within 4 business days after negotiation ends, uploaded via the portal [New Hampshire Insurance Department — C6hIoQuEWeA @ 00:09:29].
4. Build the offer on allowed factors only
   - Load the offer with permitted factors and strip out every prohibited benchmark.
   - Argue provider expertise, training and quality, market share, patient acuity and complexity, teaching status and case mix, and 4 years of good-faith network efforts [American Medical Association (AMA) — 6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:25:42]; keep billed charges, Medicare/Medicaid rates, and usual-and-customary charges out of the submission [American Medical Association (AMA) — 6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:26:17].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the compliance layer is over whether anyone should trust the qualifying payment amount. The AMA panel argues that the implementing regulations put 'a thumb on the scale' for insurers by presuming the QPA, which is the plan's own median in-network rate, is the correct answer [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:02:14]. Michael Culver adds that the IDR entity never checks whether that QPA was calculated correctly, so suspected errors get reported to the government instead of being argued inside the arbitration [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:31:34]. CMS presents the opposite picture. It describes a rule-bound median of contracted rates as of January 31, 2019, inflated by CPI-U. Single-case agreements and letters of agreement are stripped out, and each separate contract counts as one rate regardless of claim volume [Sp7aK2UsmlE @ 00:07:55]. The Zotec speakers on the Becker's panel reject that comfort and call carrier databases unverified, 'a black box' [RJps0omOazk @ 00:53:01]. Operators should read the gap literally. The regulator describes a precise formula, while billing-side practitioners say nobody outside the plan can see the inputs. The QPA printed on the remittance is therefore a number the plan controls. The only outside pressure point the AMA names is a report to the government, not a challenge inside the IDR file itself.

Practitioners also split on whether a provider ever gets to pick a forum. The AMA panel's line is flat: there should be one process that applies to any given claim, either the state or the federal process, and parties get no choice between them [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:54:45]. The New Hampshire Insurance Department describes its Chapter 143 process as an alternative to federal IDR that the provider chooses. It sells that lane on hard terms: no administrative fee, faster turnaround, each party paying half the entity fee instead of loser-pays, and a department that strictly enforces carrier payment [C6hIoQuEWeA @ 00:02:55]. Amanda Wish on the NSCHBC Edge Podcast frames the question as layering rather than choosing. Her instruction is to first comply with the federal law and then also comply with the more specific state law [uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:27:05]. The ERISA wrinkle cuts across all three views. The Zotec speakers say New Jersey and Texas let self-funded plans opt into state rules, and Washington was added in Q&A [RJps0omOazk @ 00:27:38]. New Hampshire, by contrast, reports that none of its self-funded plans had opted in yet. The playbooks really do differ. A New Hampshire provider holding a fully insured claim gets a cheaper lane, while a Texas operator faces what the AMA calls all Texas or all federal.

On the good faith estimate, the front-desk trainers give operators conflicting instructions. Dr. Marie Fang of Private Practice Skills tells therapists to ask whether the client will use in- or out-of-network benefits and to document the answer. Fang treats those clients as exempt for now but still advises issuing a GFE anyway [JT1pxEvMFxw @ 00:06:15]. HIPAA TV takes a harder line: group-plan members who are not using their benefits count as self-pay, and any cost conversation triggers an estimate [T99RB14fdY4 @ 00:01:45]. The co-provider requirement splits the same way. DAS Health's Lindsay Lanning reports that co-provider estimates were not enforced in 2022 and were pushed to 2023 after industry letters cited the manual burden [e5b1ICVPSZs @ 00:33:29]. Amanda Wish, however, says the co-provider requirement is still there [uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:16:40]. Delivery timing diverges too. Fang counts three business days from the date the appointment is scheduled, while HIPAA TV counts back to three days before the service date for appointments booked ten days out. Record retention adds a third fork, with Miller Johnson's Michigan disclosure rule running seven years against HIPAA TV's six-year NSA minimum. Fang's fallback of issuing the GFE even to exempt clients lines up with HIPAA TV's estimate-everyone stance, and it keeps a practice on the stricter side of both camps.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures are clock failures, and the speakers describe them bluntly. Michael Culver of the AMA panel warns that the federal IDR portal form times out after 50 minutes, so the claim data has to be gathered before logging in. Culver also notes that claims ready before April 15 had to be filed by May 6 [6_ODgJ97UtA @ 00:24:00]. The same panel warns that parties stalling while they work through a state regulator can miss the federal deadlines entirely, because the four-day initiation window keeps running. Miller Johnson's Michigan team describes the state-side version. A claim submitted after 60 days leaves the provider stuck, with no ability to balance bill [EwlPyAVGU8k @ 00:46:02]. Once a dispute reaches arbitration, the team says, you are not supposed to be adding additional information, so you want to do it right the first time when arguing with the plan [EwlPyAVGU8k @ 00:27:15]. New Hampshire adds a cash trap: a party that fails to submit its half of the IDR entity fee by day 10 has a default judgment entered against it [C6hIoQuEWeA @ 00:12:38]. Each of these losses is procedural. The record built during open negotiation is the record that decides the dispute, and a missed fee, a stale claim, or a late portal filing can end the fight before value is ever argued.

The economics checks raised by billing-side speakers are about leverage, not paperwork. Marc Eisenberg and Thomas Harold of Zotec, speaking on the Becker's panel, warn about California's standard, spoken as 25% of Medicare. They say it is pushing carriers to cancel contracts priced above that level, and they report a Georgia contract cancelled after a demand for a 25% rate cut [RJps0omOazk @ 00:06:29]. They also put a price on the dispute machinery itself: IDR costs, slower recoveries, and roughly $20,000 to use Colorado's all-payer claims database [RJps0omOazk @ 00:09:23]. On the NSCHBC Edge Podcast, the hosts describe what happens on the ground. Billing companies file hundreds of IDR requests and get pennies on the dollar without reaching a resolution [uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:04:15]. The podcast also reports an anesthesia group closed out of UHC contracting. It adds that many resolutions end up paying just the very lowest Medicaid rate [uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:13:20]. The Zotec speakers warned of a slide toward Medicare allowable for all. They calculated that the House Pallone threshold would leave fewer than 4 to 5% of hospital-based claims eligible for arbitration. In their account, an out-of-network position that once funded negotiation now triggers contract termination, while arbitration volume rises and per-claim recovery falls.

The quieter failures happen inside the provider's own workflow. On the NSCHBC Edge Podcast, a lab client drew two patient complaints after ASC-sourced specimens turned out to be out of network with one plan. The lab then adjusted the bills to the Medicare allowable. The speakers call that noncompliant, because the remit and EOB never showed in-network patient responsibility [uUBAmK-bcwg @ 00:19:32]. HIPAA TV reports that the EMR-generated estimates evaluated so far fail the GFE requirements and that dental-style estimates are wholly inadequate. A practice relying on software defaults is exposed to penalties the trainer pegs at $10,000 [T99RB14fdY4 @ 00:21:37]. CMS's PPDR walkthrough shows the cost of sloppy scoping on the dispute side. A bill without credible justification is cut to the GFE amount, and an unjustified new item drops to $0 [RRI2VT8QiiI @ 01:05:37]. The subrogation lawyers at Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer describe a parallel recovery leak on the plan side. They see far too many claims abandoned because the plan is non-ERISA or fully insured, even though saved statutes and governed-by clauses can leave room to recover [M-sP3O4XgCs @ 00:42:33]. Across these cases, the damage comes from treating a default as the compliant answer, whether that default is an EMR template, a Medicare adjustment, or a preemption assumption.
