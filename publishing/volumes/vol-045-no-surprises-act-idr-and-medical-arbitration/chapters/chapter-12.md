## Executive Summary

Back-end recovery operators converge on one model: AI agents draft appeals, verify eligibility and route denials, while humans review, sign and handle exceptions. Guidehouse claims 25 to 3500 appeals a week, KandR demos a 394-claim queue with $884,000 open, and OpenBots freed 4 FTE at a 550-patient-a-day behavioral health client, pricing inaction at $5 per manual check and $118 per denial rework. Practitioners insist on clean data, standardized processes and hard financial outcomes before scaling, with Hood arguing partial automation wins and the HealthLeaders panel warning AI cannot fix unstandardized processes. Splits emerge over vendor reliance, autonomy limits and whether IDR economics favor providers or payers.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every serious operator on the back end has landed on the same division of labor: the machine drafts, a human signs. Guidehouse frames its medical necessity appeal agent as a drafting engine that assembles the clinical documentation, meets the medical necessity criteria, and hands the letter to a reviewer who sends it [VQBtuhx0a5U @ 00:00:00]. Wayne Grosky of Pivotal Health calls appeal-writing technology table stakes now, insists on appealing both technical and clinical denials, and runs the same pattern of AI drafting the letter while a human reviews it [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:08:51]. NTT DATA builds the identical checkpoint into its denial agent, which finds the root cause, checks appeal eligibility, and then waits for physician sign-off before anything moves [xJZ_wqqTQhY @ 00:02:02]. The ceiling on autonomy is still real. Michael Haney at MUSC runs Epic's Penny for appeal assistance and reports it still needs significant human effort [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:05:36]. The operator takeaway is blunt: put drafting horsepower on the queue to clear volume, but staff the review seat, because the practitioners who are scaling recovery all keep a person accountable for what goes out the door, and even the vendors pitching heavy automation still route the final send through a human reviewer before submission.

The second point of agreement is that automation amplifies whatever process it lands on. OpenBots' Gabriel Skelson and Ronaldo Banerjee warn that automating bad data makes the problem bigger, and they peg coordination of benefits at about half of all eligibility errors [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:14:02]. The HealthLeaders Media panel says it flatly: AI cannot be the solver of all the problems if processes are not standardized [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:33:32]. AGS Health CIO Thomas Tataputi gives the same order of operations, telling providers to make siloed data AI-ready under governance before moving to denial and prior-auth prediction [x0zeGvoXqsc @ 00:13:39]. David Hood of 42 Robots AI pushes the architecture version of the argument: the model is not the solution, the solution is not the model, and LLMs need to be used as tools by classic code rather than sitting at the center of the system [A7ARXTSHAk0 @ 00:08:14]. Hood also resets the target, saying the objective is not 100% automation and that 50% automation on handwritten faxes still counts as a win. The consensus playbook is to clean and standardize the inputs, wrap the model inside classic code, accept partial automation that pays, and only then turn up volume across payers and work queues.

The third consensus: tie every tool to a number the CFO already tracks. Lindsay Bennett of Modern Healthcare Finance & RCM sets the benchmarks, with total denials under 5%, clean claim rate above 95% and anything under 90% a warning sign, submission lag of 2 to 3 days, and net collections of 95 to 98% [2fda29sEg6M @ 00:14:36]. Russell Hendrickson and Dan Marino of Practical Data Solutions push past the raw denial rate to a matched denial rate, mapping denials and payments back to the billing month; in their example 15% of charges were denied and 7% recovered, producing a net denial rate [4SW5Ihdk8ps @ 00:21:14]. They also report that finance leaders will spend money when there is ROI. Tataputi warns that buzzwords are worthless without financial outcomes such as a denial success rate down 50%, and cautions against overpromising because CFOs are skeptical [x0zeGvoXqsc @ 00:10:46]. OpenBots backs the same discipline with a cost-of-inaction model of $5 per manual eligibility check and $118 per denial rework, plus a claim that ROI turns positive by month three to four [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:36:03]. Benchmarks first, automation second, and the dashboard decides what survives the next budget cycle.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the appeals side, Guidehouse spells out the agent's input stack: it absorbs medical-necessity denials and applies them against payer-website policies, other criteria documentation, the denial letter itself, and previously successful appeal letters to build payer- and clinical-situation-specific intelligence [VQBtuhx0a5U @ 00:00:22]. It then checks the contract for timely filing and other constraints, pulls supporting records, submits, and follows up until a response lands. The claimed throughput is 25 to 3500 appeals in a week, and the agent goes after marginal 50/50 appeals that a human reviewer would pass on [VQBtuhx0a5U @ 00:01:22]. KandR's build shows the queue mechanics: an IAR bot reads the remittance, tags the claim 'no authorization, appeal requested' after all 5 lines denied for missing prior auth, recommends verifying retro-auth, obtaining auth, and resubmitting, then auto-routes and logs it. Its demo work queue held 394 claims, over $3.6 million in charges and about $884,000 open, and a 'generate appeal' function auto-fills fields, drafts the narrative and exports a .docx [-yVkokS2iaE @ 00:04:19]. Innobot's IRIS wires the safety net into configuration, with task types running a fallback chain of API, then bot, then human, so that if a bot fails, it falls back to a human [uMy2M4PPrMU @ 00:09:13].

Front-end eligibility is where the cleanest unit economics live. OpenBots picked eligibility because it is the easiest to automate and the hardest to ignore. They report a Missouri behavioral health client running about 550 patients a day with a 10-person team; agents check guarantor start and end dates, set priority order in Netsmart myAvatar, and freed 4 FTE, with volume climbing to 650 a day [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:22:40]. Before automation that client was billing lapsed insurers and writing the claims off. Each insurance check runs 2 to 3 minutes by hand and triples under coordination of benefits. The revenue math they present: 3% of revenue leaks to eligibility, half of that, 1.5%, to COB, which is $1.5M on $100M, with denial causes split COB 50%, benefit maximums 27% and plan coverage 17% [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:26:16]. When coverage is not found, the agent tries the next 3 portals, then drops the case to an exception queue or a phone call, with turnaround of 1 to 2 minutes at best and 4 minutes at worst [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:49:07]. Bennett's benchmark sets the bar the bots have to clear: re-verify eligibility 24 to 72 hours before service and hit better than 95% [2fda29sEg6M @ 00:02:32].

The deepest back-end numbers come from the HealthLeaders panel. Grosky reports handling 200,000 IDR submissions a month and says AI lets teams avoid backfilling open roles [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:19:30]. On No Surprises Act arbitration, Grosky says providers are paid 2 to 3 times the QPA and win 75% of cases, some closer to 80 to 90%, cites a $750,000 case paid at about 70% of charges, and says IDR can lift net patient revenue by a quarter to half a point [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:55:37]. Haney runs vendor placement off Epic placement and return files, auto-placing accounts worth about $5,000 untouched for 30 to 60 days, with second-level appeal vendors as a backstop [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:24:40]. Terry Meyer of UAMS keeps high-dollar accounts internal, sends low-dollar ones out, and scores vendors on AR days, aging and insurance net collection ratio. For the single-claim grind, MBC RCM's 45-minute call cross-referenced each denied date of service against a paid claim in the same episode, disputed the records demand citing the authorization, single case agreement and paid history, and locked EOBs by fax within 24 to 48 hours plus a 14-calendar-day plan-to-plan review window [VoQc5Oh8ot4 @ 00:04:54].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Baseline the Net Denial Rate
   - Build a matched denial rate and KPI dashboard before buying any automation.
   - Map denials and payments back to the billing month to get the net denial rate (15% denied, 7% recovered in the example) [Practical Data Solutions - Healthcare Analytics — 4SW5Ihdk8ps @ 00:20:29], then benchmark against total denials under 5% and clean claims above 95% [Modern Healthcare Finance & RCM — 2fda29sEg6M @ 00:14:36].
2. Pilot Eligibility on One Aggregator Portal
   - Set success criteria, pilot a single aggregator portal covering 30 to 80% of volume, and recruit champions.
   - OpenBots sizes the leak at 3% of revenue lost to eligibility, 1.5% of it to COB, or $1.5M on $100M, and reports ROI turning positive by month 3 to 4 [OpenBots — ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:26:16].
3. Deploy Drafting Agents With a Human Fallback
   - Route denials to an appeal agent that checks contract constraints, with a fallback chain of API, then bot, then human.
   - The Guidehouse agent checks the contract for timely filing, pulls records, submits and follows up [Guidehouse — VQBtuhx0a5U @ 00:00:56]; in IRIS, if a bot fails, it falls back to a human [Innobot Health — uMy2M4PPrMU @ 00:09:13].
4. Place, Score, and Escalate Back-End Inventory
   - Auto-place aged low-dollar accounts, keep high-dollar accounts internal, and contract for real-time vendor reporting.
   - Haney auto-places about $5,000 accounts untouched for 30 to 60 days, Meyer scores vendors on AR days, aging and insurance net collection ratio, and Grosky demands real-time reporting written into the contract [HealthLeaders Media — BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:31:22].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how much judgment to hand the machine. Guidehouse pitches its medical necessity appeals agent as a drafting engine. The agent pulls payer-website policies, the denial letter and prior winning appeal letters, assembles the clinical documentation, and a human reviews and sends. Claimed capacity is '25 to 3500 appeals in a week,' with a mandate to chase the marginal 50/50 appeals a human reviewer would pass on [VQBtuhx0a5U @ 00:01:22]. On the HealthLeaders panel, MUSC's Michael Haney reports that Epic's Penny assists with appeals but still demands significant human effort. Pivotal Health's Wayne Grosky calls appeal-writing technology table stakes, with AI drafting the letter and a human reviewing it for both technical and clinical denials [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:05:36]. KandR pushes further. Its AI quality audit agent grades worked accounts as 'good' and lists gaps such as an unconfirmed work-related injury, and the vendor says no separate manual QA team is needed [-yVkokS2iaE @ 00:05:36]. AGS Health CIO Thomas Tataputi goes furthest, arguing that agents can decide exceptions themselves, subject to audit. That follows roughly 15 years of RPA point solutions that made money but were 'not repeatable and scalable' [x0zeGvoXqsc @ 00:06:15]. Tataputi still expects the full shift to take two to four years.

Practitioners also disagree on what 'done' looks like. David Hood of 42 Robots AI rejects the 100% target outright: 'the objective here is not 100% automation.' On a handwritten-fax project, Hood counts 50% automation as a win and prefers ten 80–90% solutions over one 99% solution [A7ARXTSHAk0 @ 00:10:22]. Hood also warns that Copilot or ChatGPT Enterprise alone leaves 99+% of the value unused, and that 'the llms need to be used as tools by classic code.' OpenBots' Gabriel Skelson and Ronaldo Banerjee land in the same band on eligibility. Their agents cover 80–90% of the work, then try the next three portals before handing off to an exception queue or a phone call [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:17:27]. LandingAI's Quinn and Andrea Crop report prior-auth extraction accuracy rising from under 60% to 90% on 100K pages a day with confidence routing [SE4fu2hvD94 @ 00:12:14]. On sequencing, OpenBots starts with eligibility because 'it's the easiest to automate and the hardest to ignore.' Tataputi insists siloed data must be made AI-ready under governance before predicting denials or prior-auth outcomes [x0zeGvoXqsc @ 00:13:39]. Hood's own advice splits the difference: start with low-hanging fruit and put a chief AI officer, fractional if necessary, over the roadmap.

Back-end ownership and denial math split the room. UAMS's Terry Meyer keeps high-dollar accounts internal and sends low-dollar work to vendors under KPI-loaded contracts. MUSC's Haney uses Epic placement and return files to auto-place accounts worth about $5,000 that sit untouched for 30–60 days, and keeps second-level appeal vendors as a backstop [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:24:40]. Haney also prioritizes denial fixes by frequency as well as dollar amount. On No Surprises Act IDR, the same panel diverges hard. Haney is using an external partner and has not filed a case. Grosky reports 200,000 IDR submissions a month, providers paid 2–3x the QPA, a 75% win rate with some closer to 80–90%, and IDR lifting net patient revenue by a quarter to half a point [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:55:37]. On scorekeeping, Lindsay Bennett benchmarks total denials below 5% and notes that denial rates can be counted by claims or by dollars [2fda29sEg6M @ 00:14:36]. Russell Hendrickson and Dan Marino of Practical Data Solutions instead map denials and payments back to the billing month for a matched 'net denial rate.' In their example, 15% of charges were denied and 7% were recovered [4SW5Ihdk8ps @ 00:21:14]. Their dashboards add a loss-versus-at-risk toggle and a recovery rate by procedure, which show that Blue Shield surgical and E/M denials were rarely recovered.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start upstream of any appeal. OpenBots' Missouri behavioral health client runs about 550 patients a day with a 10-person team. Before agents started checking guarantor start and end dates across 1,650+ records, that client was billing lapsed insurers and writing those claims off [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:08:53]. The same team warns that automating bad data makes the problem bigger. COB drives about half of eligibility errors, and every denial rework costs $118 [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:36:03]. Practical Data Solutions has watched organizations sabotage themselves with pre-claim edits that cause timely-filing write-offs, and reports clearinghouses truncating diagnosis codes [4SW5Ihdk8ps @ 00:16:40]. RCM Pulse Academy's CARC 197 walkthrough shows how small the fatal error can be. 70553 was approved, 70551 was billed, and the crosswalk supported it. The denial traced back to a case number keyed into the authorization field [Ut5VjfFul68 @ 00:12:34]. The other traps are just as mechanical: under CARC 302, an authorization valid for only 45 days; under CARC 296, a mismatched site; under CARC 284, a crosswalk that does not run in reverse. The baseline rule from that walkthrough is 'An authorization number does not guarantee or authorize payment,' and the payer's clock starts at receipt, not submission.

Back-end recoveries can evaporate after posting. Meyer warns of PLB takebacks in which DRG downgrades from 2023 were clawed back two years later. Haney flags denials masked as underpayments, plus 'AI' that is really repackaged RPA [BKBNIfZ081U @ 00:12:45]. The panel's diagnosis is blunt: lack of bandwidth means 'we get the behavior we tolerate.' Tataputi warns that buzzwords are worthless without financial outcomes such as a denial success rate down 50%, and that CFOs are skeptical of overpromising. Tataputi's rule is to drop failed ideas fast: 'If a idea doesn't work, you have the humility to say it doesn't work' [x0zeGvoXqsc @ 00:17:05]. OpenBots cites an HBR study that 90% of AI projects fail. Its countermove is explicit success criteria, a single aggregator-portal pilot covering 30–80% of volume, and internal champions [ZpELLvKSrF8 @ 00:26:16]. OpenBots also prices the cost of inaction at $5 per manual eligibility check. Physician-side leakage compounds the damage. Practical Data Solutions shows that at a 10–20% denial rate under RVU-based comp, 'you're actually paying the physicians more than you are collecting' [4SW5Ihdk8ps @ 00:18:40]. Their fix is to filter non-recoverable denied claims out of comp calculations. Grosky calls physician re-education whack-a-mole.

Even the demos expose verification gaps. NTT DATA's walkthrough opens on patient Robert Shaw, yet its Nera conversational summary reports 'Robert A. Chen,' 66, with $42,101.97 billed [xJZ_wqqTQhY @ 00:03:05]. Innobot Health builds for failure instead. Task types carry fallback chains from API to bot to human: 'If a bot fails, it falls back to a human.' Payer-failure rules require manager approval. An AI trend alert caught an 18% spike in Aetna auth-not-obtained denials in July against the prior three-month average [uMy2M4PPrMU @ 00:09:13]. Manual disputes carry their own trap. MBC RCM spent 45 minutes on the phone fighting a BCBS 'pending medical records' denial and found only one of three dates of service disputable; two were correctly denied. Sending psychotherapy notes in the records packet is a violation [VoQc5Oh8ot4 @ 00:05:25]. The call still secured commitments: EOBs faxed within 24–48 hours and a firm 14-calendar-day window for the plan-to-plan review. Lindsay Bennett's hospital-metrics warning applies the same discipline to KPIs. Shaving average length of stay even by a fraction of a day lifts margin under fixed per-discharge DRG pay. But an ALOS that runs too short can signal quality problems that show up in avoidable readmissions [nQA0SW59EWQ @ 00:04:08].
