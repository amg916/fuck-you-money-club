# Chapter 08: Out-of-Network Facility Revenue: ASC, Behavioral Health, and Complex Claim Recovery

*Collect Rx's six keys to OON reimbursement, top ten best practices for complex OON claims, ASC OON revenue tactics, single-case agreements and recoupment defense, underpayment detection and recovery, cash-flow realities before going OON, A/R audit triage, OBL payer contracts, and getting OON providers paid at in-network rates*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from Patriot Group, MediGain, Collect Rx, AR Specialist and The Auctus Group agree out-of-network facility revenue is shrinking under SIU audits yet remains highly profitable for operators who run a dedicated OON workflow instead of in-network billers. The street mechanics start at verification: benefits checked within 24 hours, allowance methodology and pricer identified, UCR plans prioritized, and SPDs pulled from employers. Post-payment, every allowance is recomputed against Medicare and plan language; Force values two UCR claims at 60-70% of charges at roughly 30 in-network claims, while Collect Rx reports 90-100% of charges on pre-payment negotiation and additional payment on over 60% of qualified appeals. Silent PPO layering, 180-day OON appeal windows, and retroactively voided single case agreements are the recurring traps. Practitioners split on whether to promise recovery rates up front and on how hard to threaten network exit.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Out-of-network is not dead; it is harder. Thomas J. Force of Patriot Group says OON revenue has dropped significantly over three years and SIU audits are climbing, yet the business is 'still very very profitable' [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:01:21]. His first diagnosis of a bleeding practice is blunt: it hands OON claims to in-network billers who do not know the game [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:02:14]. Their payoff case is that reimbursement can run 'upwards of 10 to 30 x' in-network rates because the provider is not bound by contracts it never agreed to [Hd2UT1OUlA0 @ 00:01:40]. The shared prerequisite is a dedicated OON workflow with staff who verify, negotiate, and appeal as separate disciplines, not a network billing desk running OON on the side.

Every practitioner who touches facility claims agrees the money is won or lost at verification. Liz Taylor, an ASC operations manager presenting for MediGain, wants verification of benefits finished within 24 hours of scheduling and scripted to capture in-network and OON benefits, daily maximums, ASC limitations, allowance methodology, the third-party pricer such as First Health, the UCR percentile, and multiple-procedure reduction rules [w387XkK0lkk @ 00:03:21]. Force sorts plans into four allowance methods: internal fee schedule, percent of Medicare (110% or 200%, which he calls 'not good'), UCR or prevailing rate, and discretionary 'lesser of / greater of' language, and he tells operators UCR plans are the ones to hunt [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:02:31]. He pulls the Summary Plan Description from the employer, not the insurer, using an SPD request form and the ID card [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:04:38]. Rita Singh of Collect Rx adds that employers set OON benefit levels, which is why the same treatment pays differently, and that limited-benefit policies such as UHC MNRP and Cigna MRC should be flagged during verification because they can still be negotiated above the Medicare-based rate [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:12:23] [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:16:15]. Verification is the gate that decides whether a case is worth scheduling at all.

The second point of agreement is that the first number a payer offers is a test of nerve. Taylor's rule is 'Never accept the first negotiation offer' [w387XkK0lkk @ 00:09:47], and Dr. Siddi, who spent 23 months winning commercial contracts for an OBL, says 'The first offer is never the final offer' [5aFK91a1KPY @ 01:04:03]. Ryan Ellison of Collect Rx explains why: repricing vendors receive over 10M claims a year each, run algorithms to pick the lowest-paying tactic, and keep staff dossiers listing which billers sign off at 'Mary 30%, Bill 50%,' so a single cheap sign-off becomes precedent for every future claim [Pqx2hpp_cLU @ 00:05:42] [Pqx2hpp_cLU @ 00:07:18]. Singh tells teams to answer every counteroffer and refuse 'check back in 10/15/30 days' stalls, noting the vendors work on commission [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:08:33]. She leads with the phone even though it is the most time-consuming channel, because it is the best way to optimize the result, and she reports an average lift of 33% from negotiating [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:10:35]. The consensus operator stance is that every lowball is a counteroffer to reject, document, and escalate rather than a final answer, and that the biller who caves becomes the payer's preferred phone contact.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The front end is scripted and fast. Taylor's VOB call asks for the pricer and the multiple-procedure reduction, because Viant reprices at '12510' while UHC uses '15050,' and she checks that patient cost covers the case before approving it, then calls the patient early with payment plans or financing to cut cancellations [w387XkK0lkk @ 00:07:03] [w387XkK0lkk @ 00:05:25]. The Auctus Group puts the patient-balance problem in numbers: OON patient benefits average $13,000 or more against a $3,500 in-network out-of-pocket maximum, so the practice must decide whether and how much to pre-collect, how to estimate, and how to explain it with a front-desk script that never promises refunds or 'no bill' [Hd2UT1OUlA0 @ 00:00:17] [Hd2UT1OUlA0 @ 00:00:45]. Post-payment, the first move is arithmetic. Singh shows how silent PPO contracts erode payment: many are based on allowed rather than billed charges, so $10K billed, $6K allowed, times 70% yields $4,200 [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:33:05]. When negotiation stalls, the appeal is short and exhibit-driven. **SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Verify and Classify the Plan
   - Run the scripted VOB within 24 hours of scheduling and pull the SPD from the employer.
   - Capture OON benefits, daily maximums, ASC limitations, pricer, UCR percentile, and MPR on the call [Plano Texas — w387XkK0lkk @ 00:03:21], then classify the plan as fee schedule, percent of Medicare, UCR, or discretionary using the SPD obtained with a request form and ID card [Patriot Group — dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:04:38].
2. Lock the Intake Packet
   - Collect every signed form before service and send cost-share letters on any reduced patient balance.
   - Secure the AOB, authorized-representative designation, carrier-specific forms, No Surprises Act waiver, and OON financial agreement [Patriot Group — dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:10:25], with the AOB covering appeal rights, ERISA, and full and fair review [Beckers ASC — KkjI85NtmNE @ 00:21:17].
3. Audit the Remit Against the Methodology
   - Recompute the true allowance on every EOB and follow up at 10 days, not 20.
   - Subtract group-RC amounts from billed charges to find the real allowance [Plano Texas — w387XkK0lkk @ 00:13:57] and route anything at or below Medicare, or around 2x Medicare, into negotiation or reprocessing [Patriot Group — dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:08:09].
4. Negotiate, Then Appeal Inside the Window
   - Reject the first offer, then file a two-page exhibit appeal well inside the 180-day OON window.
   - Track day zero as the EOB date because OON appeals close at 180 days versus 365 in-network [AR Specialist — OLFRzRe0L2A @ 00:06:34], and keep the appeal to two pages with forms and EOB as exhibits sent by fax or certified mail [Patriot Group — dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:13:15].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is the channel you fight on. Rita Singh of Collect Rx tells operators to lead with the phone, admitting it is the most time-consuming route but calling it the best way to optimize the result, and warns that settlement portals only let you accept or decline, which kills any counteroffer [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:09:58]. Vicky Davis of Streamline, working OBL denials, runs the opposite playbook: use payer portals, avoid mail, and highlight the payer's own policy inside the appeal [5aFK91a1KPY @ 00:51:05]. Thomas J. Force of Patriot Group sits in between. Force keeps written appeals to two pages because carriers have short attention spans, ships them by fax or certified mail, and expects a response within 60 days [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:13:15]. The AR Specialist breakdown shows why no single channel wins. Quantum Health takes fax or mail at Dublin, Ohio, while UMR accepts no faxes at all, only its portal or mail to Salt Lake City [OLFRzRe0L2A @ 00:07:21]. The operator read is that negotiation happens live on the phone, where a rep can be pushed past a first number. Formal appeals ride whatever channel the specific payer actually honors, with proof of receipt and a tracked response window.

Practitioners also split on whether to quote recovery numbers at all. Collect Rx's Ryan Ellison puts the prepayment negotiation average at 90 to 100 percent of billed charges. Ellison backs it with a client who moved from an 80 percent average to 94.1 percent on $1.5 million, netting more than $135,000 after fees [Pqx2hpp_cLU @ 00:12:07]. Talisman Solutions refuses that pitch outright, telling auditors to avoid promising a recovery rate before reviewing the portfolio. Talisman measures value first by clear root causes and speed to action, and only then by eventual recovery [Zf9f4f3lmAw @ 00:01:11]. The pricing anchor is contested too. Force treats Medicare as the floor, not the ceiling, and flags any allowed amount at or below Medicare, or around two times Medicare, for negotiation or reprocessing [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:06:10]. Singh goes further, insisting usual and customary is not tied to a percentage of Medicare rates and that UCR is not 150 percent of Medicare [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:14:27]. Operators wanting a fast screen can run Force's Medicare comparison on every EOB. Ellison's camp anchors to the full bill instead, with the blunt rule that charges mean charges, and judges every negotiated outcome as a percentage of billed charges rather than any Medicare multiple.

The biggest strategic fork is whether to leave networks at all. The Auctus Group pitches out-of-network reimbursement at upwards of 10 to 30 times in-network rates, because you are not bound by contracts you never agreed to [Hd2UT1OUlA0 @ 00:01:40]. Collect Rx's John Bardis calls out-of-network the last great opportunity for many providers to improve reimbursement overall [KkjI85NtmNE @ 00:17:03]. Force tempers that, conceding OON revenue has declined significantly over three years and SIU audits are up, while still calling it very, very profitable [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:01:21]. The OEIS panel runs the in-network counter-playbook. Lori tells OBLs to renegotiate rather than let agreements auto-renew, and not to threaten to go out of network unless they are serious. Dr. Siddi rejected Anthem for a full year and used single case agreements at 300 percent while holding out [5aFK91a1KPY @ 01:09:24]. That panel also logged the downside of having no contracts: a Florida client got Medicare approval in nine days but heard network closed from every commercial payer. The practical split is clear. Auctus and Collect Rx build the revenue model around OON leverage, while the OEIS operators use the OON posture and single case agreements as pressure to extract escalators and carve-outs inside a signed contract.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest failures arrive after the money lands. The AR Specialist case shows Quantum Health and UMR retroactively voiding single case agreements they had already approved, citing in-network facilities within 50 miles. A $1,048.25 payment made on October 7 was reprocessed on November 4 and pulled back into recoupment [OLFRzRe0L2A @ 00:02:52]. The fix is to prove you are the only provider in the radius by calling competing facilities yourself, then request the gap exception up front. The clock is brutal: exactly 180 days from the day-zero EOB to file an out-of-network appeal, against 365 days in-network. Force reports a Cigna client facing a clawback in the millions for failing to send cost-share letters, the fee-forgiveness audit exposure Patriot Group flags directly [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:11:30]. Force also caught insurers calling a plan 110 percent of Medicare when the plan was actually UCR, which is why the Patriot team pulls the SPD from the employer rather than trusting the carrier [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:07:33]. Singh adds that signed settlement offers are not binding on the payer, so a closed deal can still be reopened, and tells operators to fight recoupments rather than absorb them [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:22:53].

The second trap is reading an EOB at face value. Liz Taylor of MediGain walked through a UHC EOB where the allowed column showed $12,677 against $23,750 billed. The true allowance is billed minus the group-RC amount, and anything not allowed at 100 percent without a stated methodology gets appealed [w387XkK0lkk @ 00:13:57]. Ellison flags a newer payer move, double silent-PPO layering that tops allowables out closer to 30 and 40 percent, caught only by auditing EOBs [Pqx2hpp_cLU @ 00:18:50]. Singh's silent-PPO math shows how contracts keyed to allowed rather than billed charges bleed value: $10,000 billed, $6,000 allowed, times 70 percent yields $4,200, and the payer picks whichever of contract or policy rate is lower [7h4H8g1S01E @ 00:33:05]. Behavioral Billing's dramatized case shows the small-practice version. A downloaded superbill template coded telehealth sessions as POS 11 in-person and used a generic anxiety code the clinical notes did not support. On a $150 session with 60 percent expected reimbursement, the $90 the client counted on vanished, risking session reduction, dropout, and practice cash flow [yYlBBM1kBMU @ 00:02:38]. Corrected superbills, client-assisted appeals, and a smarter template were the fix.

Economics checks separate real operators from hopefuls. The Auctus Group warns that out-of-network cash flow runs three to six months to payment, not 30 days. OON patient benefits average $13,000 or more against a $3,500 in-network out-of-pocket maximum, so pre-collection rules, estimates, and front-desk scripts must be settled before switching [Hd2UT1OUlA0 @ 00:00:17]. Cedar's PFX benchmark team adds the patient-side cost: four out of 10 patients have changed providers because of bad financial experiences [U7ayI8K_Ggw @ 00:02:08]. Collect Rx sizes the leakage at 5 to 10 percent lost to poor A/R follow-up and 30 to 40 percent lost on OON claims that are never negotiated. A practice collecting $200,000 a month loses roughly $20,000 [jW7NdP3R2Lc @ 00:05:13]. Force's math shows why the fight still pays: two UCR claims out of 10 paid at 60 to 70 percent of charges equal roughly 30 in-network claims, provided follow-up hits at 10 days, not 20 [dUdxVBDLf5w @ 00:08:09]. Those numbers only hold when follow-up discipline holds. A slow biller turns the 10-day chase into a 20-day chase, and the aging claims stop compounding into the multiples Force describes and start sliding toward write-off territory instead.
