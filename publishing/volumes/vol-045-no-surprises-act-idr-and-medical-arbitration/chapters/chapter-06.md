Practitioners across employer, payer-analytics, policy and provider-filer camps read the same IDR scoreboard. HaloMD counters that only 6.1% of eligible claims reach IDR, that insurers defaulted on 24% of their losses, and that QPA itself is set too low. ## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus — Volume Exploded, Providers Win, Employers Pay

Every camp reading the scoreboard agrees on the first line: volume exploded. Regulators planned for roughly 17,000 to 22,000 disputes a year. Chris Keyser of the Texas Business Group on Health counts more than 7 million cases opened since 2022, adding about $22B to the system [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:05:50]. Zelis counts 1.4M disputes in 2024, up 115%. More than 600,000 were still pending going into 2025, and 69% of open disputes were older than 30 days. Its blunt verdict is that the system is broken [lhoUoQSpOCg @ 00:00:23]. The Atlantic reporting narrated by Long Reads, Explained puts 2025 at 2.6 million provider-filed disputes. Providers trigger more than 99% of them, and first-half 2026 volume is running another 50% higher [cSaC2n-3r9o @ 00:06:58]. Dr. Eric Bricker tracks the quarterly climb from 200,000 to 250,000 to over 300,000 disputes. He frames the original problem plainly: out-of-network radiology, ER, anesthesiology, pathology and surgical-assistant groups were billing patients who had walked into in-network hospitals [6E_HRECi-t8 @ 00:02:04]. The volume figures disagree at the edges, and every speaker reports the curve still climbing. Operators should treat dispute count as a rising baseline rather than a one-time spike that will fade on its own.

The second line of consensus is the win rate. IDR is baseball-style arbitration. Each side submits one best offer, and the arbitrator picks one without splitting the difference. Under that format, Bricker reports physician groups winning 80 to 85% of the time. Carriers offer the QPA, the median in-network rate, while providers average about 4.5x QPA. Radiology lands near 6x QPA and neurosurgery at 11x QPA, roughly 16x Medicare [6E_HRECi-t8 @ 00:02:19]. Dr. Tia Sawhney is an FSA and NYU adjunct who previously worked prepayment recovery. She puts the provider win rate above 85% and calls the system statistically biased toward providers. Offers are not disclosed, and there is no rebuttal and no appeal [lqE3aZxq024 @ 00:11:42]. The anesthesia-side operator at About The Rest says sophisticated groups running baseball-style arbitration win 85 to 88% [x35n4T9CppE @ 00:01:03]. HaloMD's Becker's panel cites the latest CMS report at 86%, up from 84% [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:07:21]. Bricker's sharpest single example is a surgical assistant awarded $200,000 for one operation, which left that employer running over budget. The read for any plan operator is that once the counterparty files, the base case is a loss at a multiple of the plan's own benchmark rather than a negotiated middle.

The third consensus point is that a small number of filers run the scoreboard and employers absorb the bill. Sawhney counts 2.2M disputes in 2025, with about half coming from five vendors. HaloMD alone filed more than 400,000 and is paid a percentage of the uplift [lqE3aZxq024 @ 00:09:31]. The Atlantic reporting assigns half of filings to Radiology Partners and TeamHealth combined and 27% to HaloMD. It names IDR the largest single driver of next year's premium increase, at 25 to 40%, ahead of GLP-1s and AI coding. National IDR cost ran $1.6B a year from 2022 through 2024 and hit $16.6B in 2025 [cSaC2n-3r9o @ 00:15:45]. Texas employers put the damage in budget terms. The Texas Health Benefits Pool spent more than $20M in added claims and fees, and the panel states that self-funded employers bear about 90% of IDR costs [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:51:59]. Loren Adler of Brookings confirms the pass-through. These awards do not just come out of insurer margins; they flow into premiums and cost-sharing, with the hit showing in 2026 and more in 2027 [c7whA-tTpq4 @ 00:04:30]. For an employer or plan operator, the consensus is that IDR is now a recurring premium line item rather than a rounding error buried in claims.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers — Ticket Cost, Filing Engines, Payer Countermoves

The street math starts with the cost of a ticket. Eric Christensen of the Neiman Health Policy Institute lays out the fee ladder. The administrative fee was $50 in 2022 and $350 in 2023, until a court vacated it back to $50, and then $115 in 2024. On top of that sits a loser-pays entity fee that climbed from $200-$500 to $200-$1,173, which puts the expected cost to enter IDR at $173 to $455 [rYqWvbw_kGo @ 00:02:41]. Under the 2023 batching rules, claims could ride together if they shared billing code and modifiers, health plan and a 30-business-day window. Even so, the average batch held only 3.1 claims, or 2.2 for radiology. His viability math is brutal for small tickets. Recovering a quarter of the charge-minus-QPA gap makes IDR worth it on only 35 to 52% of claims overall and 11 to 22% of radiology claims [rYqWvbw_kGo @ 00:04:54]. The ticket price has since collapsed. HaloMD confirms the $15 admin fee is already in effect, alongside a 50-line batch cap from November 1 and a cooling-off period cut to 30 business days [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:49:59]. The About The Rest operator reads the drop from $115 to $15 bluntly: now you are going to have a tidal wave of new filings [x35n4T9CppE @ 00:01:40].

The filer side runs IDR as a production line, and HaloMD's leadership walks through the engine. Alla Laro ranks win ratio only about fifth when choosing an IDR entity. Refund speed matters most: entities have 30 business days to refund, and the good ones do it in 5. Next come eligibility accuracy, flexible batching and award timeliness, and 4 entities routinely beat the 30-business-day award deadline [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:28:04]. Megan Roush's back-office rule is equally mechanical: enroll in ACH refunds with all 11 IDR entities and expect some of them to demand verbal bank authentication before money moves [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:21:09]. Nutex Health shows the payoff curve. Revenue across its 24 hospitals in 11 states fell 35% when the NSA started. Nutex began IDR in Q2/Q3 2024 with 60 to 70% of visits eligible and an 80 to 90% win rate. Results took 4 to 5 months to arrive, and revenue then rose 270% year over year [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:38:19]. HaloMD's data session adds the payer-side weakness the engine leans on. 24% of insurer losses were outright defaults, where the insurer submitted no offer at all and could not have been named the prevailing party, and 8.2% involved insurer offers of $1 or less [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:30:43].

On the payer and employer side, the mechanics start with finding the disputes at all. Sawhney's detection tell is a claim payment reversed two to six months later and then repaid at a much higher amount. Reason codes exist, but they are often not shared with the employer [lqE3aZxq024 @ 00:39:40]. Bricker tells employers to demand their claims with IDR claims flagged and to expect a we-can't-or-we-won't answer. In his view, not knowing which claims went through IDR is almost de facto not being an adequate fiduciary [6E_HRECi-t8 @ 00:07:36]. The Texas Health Benefits Pool's Jennifer Hoff measured awards against the in-network average: 5x for ER facility, 10x for ER physician, 16x for anesthesia, 25x for assistant surgeon, 30x for neuromonitoring and 65x for path and lab. The pool countered with Green Imaging, Tap telehealth putting an ER physician on screen within 2 minutes, DPC, and opting into the state IDR [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:19:34]. San Antonio's Renee Freda tracked freestanding ER visits costing 138% more, rising from $732 to $1,742. She found informal settlements landing at 200 to 240% of Medicare versus roughly 400% through IDR, a spread that makes direct settlement the cheaper exit whenever a filer will take the call [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:32:15].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split is over whether the dispute flood is abuse or arithmetic. The narration of Roge Karma's Atlantic piece sets the critic baseline: regulators budgeted for 17,000 disputes a year, providers filed 2.6 million in 2025, providers trigger more than 99% of them, and first-half 2026 volume is running up 50% [cSaC2n-3r9o @ 00:06:58]. The anesthesia-side operator at About The Rest goes further, calling the real run rate 5 million against the 17,000 prediction, and reads the fee drop from $115 to $15 as the trigger for a tidal wave of new filings [x35n4T9CppE @ 00:01:40]. HaloMD's Patrick attacks the premise itself: the 17,000 estimate was built on New York data that excluded emergency medicine, and re-running the projection on Texas data produces 1.54 million disputes in year one and 2.69 million by year four, against roughly 2.5 million actually filed [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:09:18]. The same HaloMD deck argues the system is under-used rather than abused, because only 6.1% of 19.7 million eligible claims ever reach arbitration [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:12:15]. Volume figures quoted across these speakers range from 1.4 million to 5 million disputes, so the number that lands on a board slide depends entirely on whose count is used, which year is measured, and whether the speaker is filing disputes or paying the resulting awards.

The second fault line is who is gaming whom. Provider-side figures quoted in the Atlantic narration say 8% of insurer offers were $1 or less and 25% of disputes drew no counteroffer at all, while Hoadley counters that 90% of insurer offers sit at or above the QPA and that HCSC alone staffs more than 400 people on IDR [cSaC2n-3r9o @ 00:11:39]. HaloMD presses the provider case with public-use-file math: 24% of insurer losses were defaults where the payer submitted no offer at all and could not have been named the prevailing party, and 8.2% involved offers of $1 or less [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:30:43]. Chris Keyser, speaking to Texas employers, estimates that about 40% of disputes should never have been filed, yet refuses to pick a side, blaming plans for low offers and providers for flooding the system with roughly 15 entities paid only when a case is filed [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:10:12]. Eligibility is the sharpest numeric clash: non-initiating parties claimed 42% of disputes were ineligible, but the arbitration entities themselves found only 19% ineligible [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:17:20]. The street read for payers is blunt: skipping the offer step is a free win handed to the filer, and for providers, junk filings feed the 40% narrative that employers and their lobbyists now carry into every hearing.

The third disagreement is whether the benchmark or the award is the broken number. Dr. Tia Sawhney says pricing fails at both ends: the QPA is too low because it is a 2019 median trended by CPI-U, padded with ghost rates and weighting providers equally, yet awards land at 10 to 100 times market value [lqE3aZxq024 @ 00:08:45]. HaloMD argues only the first half, citing the TMA3 ruling that found the QPA methodology unlawful, claiming that stripping ghost rates can lift the in-network figure up to 600%, and quoting NDP work showing 60% of QPAs mismatched with the true median about 290% higher [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:33:10]. The same HaloMD team claims roughly $3 billion in emergency-medicine savings versus pre-NSA, a direct collision with the Texas Health Benefits Pool, which reports more than $20 million in added claims and fees and says half of a 16.5% three-year rate rise traces to the NSA [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:14:40]. On fixes, Loren Adler of Brookings prefers a hard benchmark such as 250% of Medicare or an in-network guarantee that matches networks, while HaloMD urges its audience to back the Murphy bill and critics point to a House proposal that would scrap IDR outright [c7whA-tTpq4 @ 00:12:00].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is filing claims that cannot pay for themselves. Eric Christensen of the Neiman Health Policy Institute puts the expected cost to enter IDR at $173 to $455 once the administrative fee and the loser-pays entity fee are blended, with entity fees climbing as high as $1,173 in 2024 [rYqWvbw_kGo @ 00:02:41]. At a realistic recovery of one quarter of the charge-minus-QPA gap, IDR pencils out for only 11% to 22% of radiology claims, leaving three-quarters to nine-tenths of radiology claims underwater, and the average radiology batch held just 2.2 claims under the 2023 batching rules [rYqWvbw_kGo @ 00:04:54]. HaloMD's Alla Laro confirms the pain from the filer side, flagging the fee burden that radiology's small claims carry into every dispute [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:17:45]. Then the economics flipped: the About The Rest operator reports the fee falling from $115 to $15 and predicts that groups running an IDR engine will thrive while everyone else gets screwed [x35n4T9CppE @ 00:01:40]. The pre-filing check is plain arithmetic on fee exposure against a quarter-share of the delta, and Christensen adds a structural warning on top: because the QPA sits below in-network rates, clinicians lose bargaining leverage, so a lost dispute costs more than the entity fee alone.

The second trap is winning on paper and never getting paid. Laro says about 70% of disputes are paid late even though payment is due within 30 calendar days, and that payers raise objections after awards despite a 3-day statutory objection window and award finality [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:16:53]. Megan Roush traces 95% of payment failures to missing data such as the EOB and award letter, tells filers to send that packet proactively, net out recoup-and-repay remittances, and stop trusting CARC and RARC codes [g2udecJ21bI @ 00:23:45]. HaloMD's employer-facing webinar claims one in four or more awards are withheld, pushes employers to grill their TPAs with uncomfortable questions, and flags anecdotal shared-savings fees charged on the award delta [oEWBNDsvXOQ @ 00:44:51]. Enforcement is thin on every side of the table. Loren Adler notes there is no clear private right of action, courts are split, and the government can fine insurers but has not done so [c7whA-tTpq4 @ 00:08:12]. The Crowell litigator at the Texas webinar adds that courts have dismissed nearly all IDR process cases, which matches the About The Rest report that payers suing everyone have lost or been dismissed. An award is a receivable, and the collection workflow decides realized yield.

The third set of failures lands on self-funded plans. The Texas Health Benefits Pool pushed 9,000-plus claims through review, found 370 ineligible worth about $200,000, and could not appeal any of them [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:17:00]. The City of San Antonio watched IDR claims climb from $18 million in FY25 to a projected $43 million in FY26, with fees jumping from $1.4 million to $10.5 million, until 24% of medical claims spend went to IDR [8zjJbZyS2j8 @ 00:28:17]. Sawhney's elective-surgery warning is the nastiest leak: an unsigned consent form lets a physician file, assistant surgeons, anesthesiologists, and neuromonitoring providers are interlinked, and a $20,000 to $40,000 surgery can turn into $500,000 to $800,000 [lqE3aZxq024 @ 00:20:47]. Detection is the bottleneck because reason codes exist but are often not shared; the tell Sawhney teaches is a payment reversed two to six months later and repaid much higher [lqE3aZxq024 @ 00:39:40]. Jennifer Hoff adds that plans with lower stop-loss attachment points are the exposed ones, and Sawhney notes there is no appeal, with the only recourse a CMS web gripe. Plans that cannot see these claims, in Dr. Eric Bricker's words, are almost de facto not adequate fiduciaries.
