# Chapter 01: No Surprises Act Foundations: Scope, Protected Services, and the Balance-Billing Ban

*Emergency services, non-emergency care at in-network facilities, and air ambulance coverage; patient cost-sharing limits; notice-and-consent exceptions; provider and facility balance-billing prohibitions; and how the law routes payment disputes into IDR*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Across sixteen practitioner sessions, the consensus is tight. The No Surprises Act bans balance billing in three lanes: emergency care, air ambulance, and non-emergency care at in-network facilities. It holds the patient to in-network cost sharing and reaches self-funded plans. Hospital-based ancillary specialties such as anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, emergency medicine, and neonatology can never waive protections. Payment flows through a 30-business-day open negotiation and baseball-style IDR anchored to the QPA, the 2019 median contracted rate indexed for inflation. Street numbers include a $42,000 anesthesia bill cut to just over $21,000 against an $18,000 QPA, and an MRI billed at $4,800 against a $1,200 QPA. Disagreements center on QPA weight after the Texas Medical Association litigation, whether Medicare rates belong anywhere in the math, and how slow and risky IDR really is.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who walked through scope lands on the same three protected lanes: emergency care, air ambulance, and non-emergency care delivered by an out-of-network clinician inside an in-network facility [zyTkKRTBBEg @ 00:07:28]. CMS regulators Ryisha Conway and Judah Katz draw the identical triangle and date the protections to January 1, 2022, or plan years starting on or after that date [aQ87InetyaA @ 00:05:27]. The patient side of the deal is blunt: the plan must apply in-network cost sharing, and the member owes nothing beyond it. The Patient Blueprint frames the rule as 'You owe only your in-network cost sharing,' while Jentry Harris contrasts the pre-law world, where a $30,000 surprise bill was possible, with today, where 'the member is held harmless' [LNSPlo2_U3c @ 00:00:43]. Harris also insists the law 'absolutely applies' to self-funded plans, and the AMA team confirms the federal rule generally reaches self-insured ERISA plans. The carve-outs are just as consistent: Conway and Katz list Medicare, Medicaid, IHS, VA, Tricare, short-term limited duration insurance, excepted benefits, retiree-only plans, and account-based plans as outside the protections, and Holland & Hart's Kim Stenger separately flags Medicare and Medicaid as excluded [aQ87InetyaA @ 00:04:21]. Ground ambulance sits outside the ban entirely, which CMS states flatly.

The consensus target list is the hospital-based ancillary bench. Dr. Eric Bricker names radiologists, anesthesiologists, and pathologists as the typical offenders and says air ambulances charged 'tens of thousands of dollars' [NFJaCM_6M_Y @ 00:01:15], and Dr. Neil Baum adds ER physicians and hospitalists to the most-affected roster. The mechanic that made those bills possible is geographic, not clinical: in Bricker's words, 'you just walk down the hall and all of a sudden you're out of network,' and the surprise bill then lands on a separate out-of-network deductible, so 'you're starting from scratch' [NFJaCM_6M_Y @ 00:07:10]. The law shuts that door hardest for exactly these specialties. CMS lists emergency medicine, anesthesiology, pathology, radiology, neonatology, hospitalists, and diagnostics as ancillary services that can never be waived through notice and consent [aQ87InetyaA @ 00:27:28]. Kim Stenger at Holland & Hart runs the same no-consent list and adds that air ambulance has no consent exception either [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:44:09]. PYA's provider-side panel reaches the same conclusion for ER, anesthesiology, neonatology, and pathology. Rachel Rose puts the stakes at anesthesia and pathology balance bills above $20,000 and air ambulance bills in six figures. For an out-of-network group in these specialties, every practitioner points the recovery fight toward the plan payment, open negotiation, and IDR.

On the money side, the shared vocabulary is the QPA and baseball arbitration. Kim Stenger defines the QPA as the 2019 median contracted rate for the same geography, CPI-adjusted [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:10:37]. CMS pins it to the median contracted rate as of January 31, 2019, indexed for inflation, and bases patient cost sharing on the lesser of billed charges or the QPA. Bricker calls the QPA 'the key' to the entire law. The workflow is equally uniform. Jentry Harris lays out a 30-day open negotiation, after which either side initiates IDR. The arbitrator weighs the QPA, market data, complexity, contracted rates, and training and experience, then picks one offer rather than splitting the difference: 'winner takes all' [LNSPlo2_U3c @ 00:01:29]. Stenger calls it baseball arbitration in which 'the IDR entity picks one or the other' [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:19:11], and Dr. Neil Baum adds that the losing party pays the arbitrator. Practitioners also agree that the patient exits the fight: Remodel Health says IDR keeps the patient out of the middle, and HHS sums it up as 'no surprise bill.' The AMA's Emily Carroll and George Cox warn that the QPA has already become a 'de facto payment rate' in the market [YJ1Bgt_KIyw @ 00:03:32].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The federal clock is where money is won or lost. Rachel V. Rose of First Healthcare Compliance walks through the sequence. A 30-business-day open negotiation comes first, then 4 business days to initiate, landing roughly on day 34, with filing through the CMS federal IDR portal. Within 10 business days each side submits its offer as both a dollar amount and a percentage of the QPA, with the Medicare fee schedule excluded, and the entity decides within 30 business days [uQSw1Ucy6L4 @ 01:02:22]. The PYA panel treats the calendar as a kill switch: missing a deadline 'by a day or two' can invalidate the IDR, so they push trackers on every claim [j1DGgPw1__E @ 00:26:37]. They add a 90-day cooling-off period, which stretches the full process past 140 days [j1DGgPw1__E @ 00:27:53], and tell providers to expect 90 to 120 days before a first decision. The AMA webinar team notes IDR could open no sooner than 90 days after service, putting the earliest filings in April [zyTkKRTBBEg @ 00:14:29]. Plans face their own clock: Kim Stenger says payers must pay or deny within 30 days of a clean claim. PYA also labels IDR 'a very high risk strategy,' and Radiology Partners' Rich Heller says insurers delay out-of-network payment to earn interest.

For scheduled non-emergency work outside the ancillary list, the only lawful way to balance bill is a signed waiver, and the mechanics are rigid. Stenger's timing rule: deliver notice 72 hours before the service, at scheduling if the visit is booked later, or 3 hours before if same-day [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:34:08]. CMS's Conway and Katz specify CMS Form 10780, kept separate from other intake forms [aQ87InetyaA @ 00:35:01]. It must be offered in the 15 most common languages in the state or facility's service region, or through a qualified interpreter. The patient-protection notice runs one page, double-sided, in 12-point font, backed by signage and website posting. Retention is 7 years: 'you have to maintain a copy of that notice and consent for 7 years' [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:39:06]. Remodel Health pairs the 72-hour cost notice and consent requirement with the reminder that out-of-network care is 'not always negative.' CMS says providers may refuse treatment but may not charge a cancellation fee, and plans must be notified. The Patient Blueprint adds that the waiver can never touch emergency, anesthesia, radiology, or pathology. Stenger's bottom line for operators planning around waivers: 'it's going to be likely very difficult to obtain the patient consent' [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:46:53].

The worked numbers show what the ban and the QPA do to a claim. Jentry Harris's Arizona self-funded plan faced a $42,000 anesthesiology bill. The plan's QPA was roughly $18,000, IDR sided with the plan, and the final allowed amount landed just over $21,000, saving about $20,000 with 'no balance bill to the member' [LNSPlo2_U3c @ 00:02:13]. Bricker's DFW example is an in-hospital out-of-network MRI, CPT 72148, billed at $4,800 against a QPA of about $1,200 [NFJaCM_6M_Y @ 00:07:28]. Bricker's wife received a 2021 radiologist bill of $1,800 for a six-minute read. The prior year's in-network allowed amount was $220, and after a phone call from Bricker the practice conceded $220 [NFJaCM_6M_Y @ 00:07:49]. The Patient Blueprint's cost-share math: an out-of-network anesthesiologist charges $3,000 against a $1,000 allowed amount at 20% coinsurance, so the patient owes $200 and 'the $2,800 difference is banned' [tNSTH4Y29D8 @ 00:01:00]. Penalty figures diverge. Dr. Neil Baum cites 'up to thousand dollars' per occurrence, while Stenger puts fines at $10,000 with the enforcement rule still pending. For uninsured and self-pay patients, the AMA team says the good faith estimate cannot be opted out of, and a bill $400 or more above it opens patient-provider dispute resolution.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how much weight the QPA carries in arbitration, and the answer depends on which rulemaking cycle a speaker was working from. Thomas J. Force of Audio Webinars, an attorney who owns a billing company serving out-of-network providers, read the interim final rule as making the QPA 'presumed correct' at the median in-network rate, calling that 'not good for out of network providers' [T6j0qhLPruk @ 00:03:06]. Rachel V. Rose of First Healthcare Compliance walked the same interim mechanics and stated the IDR entity must select one of the submitted offers and is 'required to choose the offer closest to the qpa' within 30 business days [uQSw1Ucy6L4 @ 01:04:14]. The PYA panel framed it as a rebuttable presumption with the burden sitting on the provider [j1DGgPw1__E @ 00:19:52]. Emily Carroll and George Cox of the AMA then reported the reversal: after the TMA win, the rebuttable presumption was removed, and the arbiter now must select the offer that best represents the value of the service after considering the QPA and information submitted by the parties [YJ1Bgt_KIyw @ 00:07:24]. Even so, Carroll and Cox warned that the QPA has become a 'de facto payment rate,' including in the market, so the number still shadows every contract talk.

Benchmarking splits operators into camps that cannot all be right at once. Remodel Health told employers the law sets 'no benchmark,' explicitly not 'medicare plus 125,' and pitched IDR as the mechanism that keeps the patient out of the middle [tNA4dmumP8o @ 00:03:39]. Remodel Health also stressed that out-of-network care is 'not always negative' and that a 72-hour cost notice and consent is required for non-emergency out-of-network care. Rich Heller of Radiology Partners said Medicare was rejected as a benchmark because it is not market-based, and that arbiters may not consider billed charges or public payer rates [q1qFNKqrEcs @ 00:09:31]. Holly at PracticeForces runs the opposite street playbook on the patient side: the practice bills patients using the Medicare fee schedule as its proxy for the in-network rate, and refuses to bill patients 'the full charge that you have in your fee schedule' [s5b-Kz13ecw @ 00:01:02]. Holly also draws the scope line tight, applying the act to facility and emergency out-of-network care only, and saying a patient seen in the office who fights the bill under the act will find it 'does not apply.' Rachel V. Rose cut the other way on facility scope, relaying the departments' explanation that a visit to an in-network facility 'does not require the patient to set foot in the facility' [uQSw1Ucy6L4 @ 00:50:16].

The third split is whether the law is a patient win or an insurer weapon. IndeMed, the independent-physician group chaired by Adam Brigham, says the act is working: 10 million Americans spared surprise bills and '$567 in a recent study' saved for every patient every year, while interests try to 'undercut or remove or undermine' it [YSVbYfiNgWE @ 00:01:00]. Rich Heller of Radiology Partners calls surprise-billing law a 'Trojan horse' that disrupts good-faith network negotiations to benefit insurers, and says 'They're going after the 98%,' meaning the in-network majority whose rates get recalibrated downward once the median becomes the benchmark [q1qFNKqrEcs @ 00:05:15]. Carroll and Cox of the AMA back that with field reports: practices with 'multi-year, decades-long contracts' now seeing rate cuts or removals from networks, which raises patient access risk [YJ1Bgt_KIyw @ 00:14:32]. Dr. Eric Bricker of AHealthcareZ lays out both sides plainly: physicians call insurers 'bullies' paying 'unfairly low' rates, while the law itself passed bipartisan under Trump and Biden kept implementing it [NFJaCM_6M_Y @ 00:04:46]. Heller still conceded 'First and most importantly, the law protects patients,' and singled out the QPA criterion as the one unbalanced piece of the compromise.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The fastest way to lose an IDR claim is the calendar. The PYA panel warned that missing a deadline 'by a day or two' can invalidate the entire IDR, and told providers to run trackers [j1DGgPw1__E @ 00:26:37]. Rachel V. Rose mapped the clock: 30 business days of open negotiation, then 4 business days to initiate, roughly day 34, through the CMS federal IDR portal; each side then has 10 business days to submit an offer as a dollar amount and as a percentage of QPA, and the decision lands within 30 business days [uQSw1Ucy6L4 @ 01:02:22]. The cash-flow drag compounds. PYA flagged a 90-day cooling-off period that stretches the process to 140+ days, told operators to expect 90 to 120 days to a first decision, and reported 46,000 disputes filed with 3 of 11 IDR entities near closure. Kim Stenger of Holland & Hart noted plans must pay or deny within 30 days of a clean claim [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:06:29], but Rich Heller of Radiology Partners reported insurers delay out-of-network payment to earn interest [q1qFNKqrEcs @ 00:06:09]. Rose's checklist closes the loop: update policies, procedures and IDR timelines, and coordinate with EHR vendors and clearinghouses before the first dispute.

Baseball arbitration punishes greedy offers. Jentry Harris laid out the 'winner takes all' mechanic: the arbitrator weighs QPA, market data, complexity, contracted rates, and training and experience, then picks one offer, not the average [LNSPlo2_U3c @ 00:01:29]. The Arizona self-funded case Harris walked through shows the provider downside: an anesthesiology group billed $42,000 against a QPA of roughly $18,000, IDR sided with the plan at just over $21,000, and the plan saved about $20,000. Dr. Neil Baum of Grand Rounds in Urology added the second sting: 'the losing party has to pay the cost of the independent dispute uh resolution arbitrator' [_42GJfeJNw4 @ 00:04:48]. The PYA panel called IDR 'a very high risk strategy' and listed the evidence traps: do not double count training that the code already implies, and use prior in-network rates from the past 4 years as a defense [j1DGgPw1__E @ 00:29:26]. PYA also told providers they may reject an IDR entity they have used before and should challenge down-coding. Carroll and Cox of the AMA reported the August 19 final rules require down-coding to be explained, keep the standard federal initiation notice always permitted, and address ghost rates through separate per-specialty medians [YJ1Bgt_KIyw @ 00:04:56].

Notice-and-consent is the trap that looks like a workaround. Kim Stenger of Holland & Hart warned 'it's going to be likely very difficult to obtain the patient consent,' given notices due 72 hours ahead, offered in the 15 most common local languages, and retained for 7 years [aaDjROGKKC0 @ 00:46:53]. CMS regulators Ryisha Conway and Judah Katz said ancillary services, including emergency medicine, anesthesiology, pathology, radiology, neonatology, hospitalists and diagnostics, can never be waived, and that providers may refuse treatment but may not charge a cancellation fee, 'even if the provider or facility gets the individual's signed consent' [aQ87InetyaA @ 00:39:57]. The CMS team also told providers to bill the plan directly to find out whether protections apply. Dr. Eric Bricker reported providers using the patient's signed payment-responsibility form as leverage, and then showed how fast a balance bill collapses: a 2021 radiologist bill to Bricker's wife ran $1,800 for a six-minute read, and the practice conceded the $220 prior-year in-network allowed amount after Bricker called [NFJaCM_6M_Y @ 00:07:49]. Penalty numbers disagree: Dr. Neil Baum cited 'up to thousand dollars for each occurrence' [_42GJfeJNw4 @ 00:02:59], while Stenger cited $10,000 fines with the enforcement rule still pending.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Checks Before Filing IDR or Sending a Patient Bill**
> 
> Each check maps to a loss, reversal, or bottleneck the practitioners reported firsthand.
> - Load every open-negotiation, initiation, and offer deadline into a tracker before the first dispute, because missing one by a day or two can invalidate the IDR [PYA, PC — j1DGgPw1__E @ 00:26:37].
> - Never rely on notice-and-consent for emergency medicine, anesthesiology, pathology, radiology, neonatology, hospitalists, diagnostics, or air ambulance, because those can never be waived [CMSHHSgov — aQ87InetyaA @ 00:27:28].
