# Chapter 05: The Shifting Rulebook: IDR Operations Final Rule, TMA Litigation, and Congressional Fixes

*New federal IDR portal, batching limits, administrative and entity fees, November 1, 2026 deadline changes for radiology, TMA III ruling impacts, Pallone and Cassidy proposals, payer and TPA reality checks, and competing reform agendas from Georgetown CHIR, Paragon, and hospital coalitions*

## Executive Summary

Payer, provider, and policy practitioners agree that the No Surprises Act's patient protections work. IDR is where the system broke: 17,000 expected disputes became more than 2.6 million, a handful of repeat filers including HaloMD drive most of the volume, and providers win roughly 85 to 88 percent of determinations. The May 28, 2026 operations final rule changes the plumbing, not the substance. The CMS administrative fee drops from $115 to $15 per party. Batches are capped at 50 line items for open negotiations starting on or after November 1, 2026. Non-initiating parties must respond by business day 15. A 30-business-day cooling-off period now feeds a four-business-day filing window with no grace period, and unregistered plans default at the offer stage. Practitioners split on whether the batch cap rose from 25 or from unlimited, and on whether the rule fixes anything that matters.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus: Protections Work, IDR Broke, and the Rulebook Keeps Moving

Across payer, provider, and policy camps, the starting point is the same: the patient-protection half of the No Surprises Act works, and the arbitration half is where the money leaks. Brian O'Hara of The Phia Group says the law 'really did accomplish its goal of preventing surprise balance bills' and argues that reform should not weaken those protections [8QIP4F1EGTA @ 00:01:47]. The volume numbers explain why IDR became the battlefield for every stakeholder. Kenna Watts at the Center on Health Insurance Reforms puts 2025 IDR costs above $16 billion, roughly 3.5 times the prior year. The expectation was 17,000 disputes; the actual count was more than 2.6 million, or upward of 7,000 a day [wJkWOgxZj6k @ 00:10:12]. Paragon's panel lands in the same zone with different counting: a 22,000-per-year estimate versus 258,000 merit line items in 2023 and 4.2 million in 2025, about 5.7 million counting every dispute [PpfXacIXrAc @ 00:11:31]. O'Hara adds the business-model read: a few provider groups have turned IDR into routine revenue-cycle management rather than an exception process [8QIP4F1EGTA @ 00:05:07]. The numbers disagree on scale, but nobody on either side argues that the original forecast survived contact with the market. That volume is the baseline every workflow change now has to absorb.

The second point of consensus is that a small set of repeat filers drives the machine and wins most of the time. Watts reports that five provider groups file roughly three-quarters of all disputes: four private-equity staffing groups plus HaloMD. The staffing groups win 90 to 96 percent, awards run 6 to 10 times the median in-network rate, and up to $1.4 billion has gone to contingency fees [wJkWOgxZj6k @ 00:12:03]. HaloMD does not dodge its footprint. Its own leadership says the firm initiates about 22 percent of IDR disputes and operates in all 50 states [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:00:32]. Brady Bazar of The Phia Group, working from CMS data for January to June 2025, puts provider wins at about 88 percent of determinations and plan wins at 12 percent. The prevailing offer landed above the QPA in about 88 percent of cases [ksxW878eWWk @ 00:07:44]. Paragon tracks the trend line: provider win rates of 82.1 percent in 2023 and 85.2 percent in 2025. In Major League Baseball arbitration, the format members believed would force reasonable offers, players win 45 percent and owners 55 percent [PpfXacIXrAc @ 00:12:43]. Whichever dataset an operator trusts, the payer side loses the large majority of determinations, and the filers on the other side are concentrated and still growing every year.

The third point of consensus is that the rulebook keeps shifting and the newest federal rule changes the plumbing, not the substance. KLGates describes the final rule issued May 28, 2026 by HHS, Labor, Treasury, and OPM as operational rather than substantive. It was driven by the hundreds of thousands of claims filed since 2022, far more than the agencies anticipated [NwapzBNojY0 @ 00:00:39]. Katie Johnson of the American Benefits Council calls the rules an 'initial step' that took years to finalize. Johnson says substantial further improvement and oversight are needed and that the rules miss 'the most egregious areas of abuse' [30BDiubQ0cg @ 00:00:59]. Underneath the rule, the courts keep rewriting the math. The Zelis speaker puts it bluntly: 'litigation continues to kind of steer the policy.' TMA3 on QPA calculation is unresolved, and CMS typically follows major rulings with guidance 6 to 12 months later [D63N2KsthY8 @ 00:05:52]. Ron Howrigan of Fulcrum Strategies reports that Judge Kernodle sided with TMA on every QPA calculation point and vacated the guidance. TMA lost on disclosure because the statute is not prescriptive there, which leaves CMS deference, and Howrigan scores the outcome a 'triple' [f9Q5ImwNeS0 @ 00:09:18]. Any team running eligibility, QPA, or payment workflows is working against a target that moves with each ruling and each guidance cycle.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Fees, Batches, Clocks, and the Registry

The headline mechanic is the cost of entry, and every camp quotes the same number. HaloMD's team states that the CMS non-refundable administrative fee 'is going from $115 to $15' per party, $30 total for both sides, effective June 11, 2026 [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:02:33]. Anders Gilberg of MGMA, whose group advocated for the cut, calls it 'a significant reduction' and says that 'for smaller practices that is a huge improvement' [K4eVTdf3xa0 @ 00:04:28]. Xpert Billing Talk supplies the street math behind that statement: a $150 anesthesia claim was not worth fighting at $115, because 'you'd basically lose money trying to get your money' [CwSXrQBZiOg @ 00:02:01]. Batching is the second lever, and its trigger is a date rather than a dollar figure. According to Xpert's radiology briefing, open negotiations starting on or after November 1, 2026 are 'strictly capped at exactly 50 line items, period.' Those batched disputes then sit through a new 30-business-day cooling-off period meant to force negotiation and clear the backlog, while single, bundled, and older batch disputes keep the 90-calendar-day rules [g3C-DoM_9CI @ 00:00:45]. The operating consequence Xpert spells out is two parallel tracking systems running at once: one for legacy disputes and one keyed to the open-negotiation start date of every new batched dispute in the queue.

The clocks are where cases now die. KLGates reports that the final rule requires the non-initiating party, 'which is often the payer, to issue a response by the 15th business day of the open negotiation period,' inside the 30-business-day window [NwapzBNojY0 @ 00:04:19]. HaloMD adds the fine print: that response requires no counteroffer, and the non-initiating party must answer an IDR initiation within 3 business days [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:10:39]. The batch side is less forgiving. Once the cooling-off period ends, Xpert describes a four-business-day filing window that the portal enforces with no grace period. Late claims 'just get dumped right into your denial management queues as permanent losses,' and Xpert says spreadsheets are inadequate for tracking the window [g3C-DoM_9CI @ 00:02:21]. On the back end, The Phia Group reminds plans that 'the IDR awards have to be paid within 30 calendar days of the date that a determination is rendered' and tells them to build a dedicated award-payment tracker [ksxW878eWWk @ 00:14:13]. Each of these deadlines is counted differently: business days on the negotiation and filing side, calendar days on the payment side. That is why Xpert pushes a calendar-based tool and Phia pushes a standalone tracker for every determination that lands against a plan.

The third mechanic is identity and coding, and it is the one most likely to produce outright defaults. HaloMD reports that plans must register in the new IDR registry within 90 days, a window the same speakers later restate as 90 business days [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:15:34]. The penalty for skipping registration is severe. An unregistered plan cannot respond and defaults at the offer stage, and a plan that registers late is covered only for steps going forward [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:19:47]. KLGates adds that payers must use CARCs and RARCs to flag IDR eligibility and must include contact information with denials. That requirement is effective August 3, 2026 but still awaits further guidance from the agencies [NwapzBNojY0 @ 00:02:32]. The intake channel is changing at the same time. Xpert says the 30-day open-negotiation clock now runs from portal date stamps and that fax and email proof is 'completely dead.' Missing a stamp means permanently losing the dispute window, so the portal has to be monitored every day [CwSXrQBZiOg @ 00:03:36]. For payers, the registry is the sharpest edge in the rule, because missing registration converts a defensible dispute into a default at the offer stage. For providers, the remittance codes become the eligibility flag KLGates describes, telling billing teams which claims belong in the federal process.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest fight is over what Congress should bolt onto IDR. Tom Leefrieded of the AFL-CIO calls ranking member Pallone's bill "really the gold standard" because it trends 2019 rates forward, forces payment at 30 days, strips out ghost rates, mandates CMS audits of QPAs, and eliminates arbitration outright [9SCzxZp8uAE @ 00:34:14]. James Greenspan reads the same bill as a nonstarter. Pallone floated the identical idea in 2019, was overruled, and then signed the version that kept arbitration. The new bill has no co-sponsors and "not a lot of appetite on the Hill," so Greenspan treats it as an anchoring tactic ahead of next-Congress negotiations [HTDpgKwIfRg @ 00:00:33]. Brian O'Hara of The Phia Group sees a payer-side danger in a benchmark as well, warning that payers could end up setting rates unilaterally. O'Hara also points to New Jersey bills running $100K to $500K and assistant-surgeon claims topping $1M on a usual-and-customary basis that the NSA does not permit [8QIP4F1EGTA @ 00:17:27]. Greenspan flags a third lane that sidesteps the rate fight entirely: a possible Cassidy legacy bill. It would not narrow which cases qualify. Instead it would move the eligibility determination earlier in the process and make it binding, ending the repeated eligibility challenges that both sides now fight over on case after case [HTDpgKwIfRg @ 00:01:36].

Practitioners split just as hard on whether the system is broken or merely being abused. At the Coalition for Affordable Hospitals panel, Maya Holmes of the Culinary Health Fund rated the NSA net negative. Tom Leefrieded of the AFL-CIO rated it net positive, on the grounds that the patient protections are still needed [9SCzxZp8uAE @ 00:38:26]. Provider-side operators reject the runaway-cost story. Pilgrim of SCP Health says the group is now in network more often and at lower rates, and adds: "We are actually making less dollars after the No Surprises Act has been implemented than we did before" [PpfXacIXrAc @ 00:53:49]. Anders Gilberg of MGMA concedes that abuses exist but calls them "not the majority." Gilberg argues that payers are "hiding behind" egregious cases, such as a Florida physician's six-figure claim, when they should be offering amounts nearer to in-network rates [K4eVTdf3xa0 @ 00:08:37]. The payer camp answers with concentration data. Kenna Watts reports that five provider groups, four private-equity staffing groups plus HaloMD, file roughly three-quarters of all disputes. Watts adds that up to $1.4B has gone to contingency fees, and that staffing groups win 90% to 96% of the time with awards running 6 to 10 times median in-network rates [wJkWOgxZj6k @ 00:12:03].

The operations final rule itself gets graded on a curve. Gilberg, whose MGMA pushed for the cut in the initiation fee from $115 to $15, sums up the provider reaction: "we'll take it as a win and move on from there" [K4eVTdf3xa0 @ 00:16:02]. Katie Johnson of the American Benefits Council calls the same rules only an "initial step." They were proposed years ago, took a long time to finalize, and in the Council's view miss "the most egregious areas of abuse." The Council is pressing Congress and the Trump administration for more rigorous oversight [30BDiubQ0cg @ 00:00:59]. The litigation track divides operators too. Judge Kernodle sided with TMA on every QPA calculation point. Ron Howrigan of Fulcrum Strategies predicts that CMS still will not audit QPAs, so the next move is a "TMA-5" suit against CMS or a class action against carriers. Howrigan expects the class action to land harder [f9Q5ImwNeS0 @ 00:11:50]. Brady Bazar of The Phia Group runs the defensive play for TPAs. Plans should use a good-faith interpretation of the 2023 methodology until the Fifth Circuit rules en banc, document that approach without writing a "40page memo," and keep QPA disclosures carrying certification language [ksxW878eWWk @ 00:08:47].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest firsthand trap is the clock. Xpert Billing Talk warns that batched disputes now sit through a new 30-business-day cooling-off period and then get a four-business-day filing window. The portal blocks late filings with no grace period, and those claims "just get dumped right into your denial management queues as permanent losses" [g3C-DoM_9CI @ 00:02:36]. The HaloMD team hammers the same point on the batch resubmission window, which has been cut to 4 business days: "Do not miss this particular change because you will expire cases" [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:30:03]. The proof standard is tightening at the same time. The second Xpert Billing Talk breakdown says the 30-day open-negotiation clock now runs off portal date stamps and that fax and email proof is "completely dead." In that breakdown's words, "Missing a deadline now has zero margin for error," so the portal has to be monitored daily [CwSXrQBZiOg @ 00:03:54]. Xpert Billing Talk also says spreadsheets are not up to the job and teams need a calendar-based tracking tool. Radiology coders face an extra trap: they must manually cross-reference Category I CPT codes, because mixing eligible and ineligible codes in a batch triggers automatic rejection [g3C-DoM_9CI @ 00:03:53].

Plans and TPAs report their own bleeding points. Brian O'Hara describes one claim split into 15 disputes, one per CPT code, spread across seven or eight IDREs with mixed outcomes: "Some IDREs have dismissed disputes, some are letting them go through, some we haven't heard back from" [8QIP4F1EGTA @ 00:07:06]. O'Hara also reports plans learning of determinations months after the fact. IDREs refuse to correct errors the provider caused, such as filing with the wrong email address, and eligibility problems surface in roughly half of all cases. O'Hara's verdict: "This is all the time" [8QIP4F1EGTA @ 00:16:00]. HaloMD flags a new default trap in the plan registry. A plan that has not registered cannot respond and defaults at the offer stage. Registering late helps only with steps going forward, because "It is forward-looking only" [of6CD7esHY0 @ 00:20:52]. Brady Bazar puts numbers on the eligibility war: eligibility was challenged in 40% of disputes, about 476,000 in the first half of 2025, making it the main bottleneck in the entire process. For TPAs, that turns eligibility proof into the core daily workload. The provider side faces the mirror image of that grind, since every one of those challenged filings is a claim stuck outside the payment pipeline [ksxW878eWWk @ 00:05:31].

Winning is not the same as collecting. Anders Gilberg says providers win more than 80% of the time but often go unpaid, which is why MGMA wants accountability for timely payment and why enforcement legislation is pending [K4eVTdf3xa0 @ 00:07:01]. Brady Bazar explains the hole. In Guardian Flight, the Fifth Circuit held there is no private right of action to enforce IDR awards, and the Supreme Court declined review, so enforcement runs through HHS civil penalties. Awards are still due within 30 calendar days, and nonpayment carries reputational and regulatory risk. TPAs are told to build an award-payment tracker [ksxW878eWWk @ 00:12:11]. On the payer ledger, Bartlett of ERIC describes one employer whose IDR costs went from $3.5M in 2025 to a $12M pace, about $4,000 in excess per claim, with settlements stretching from one month to three. Another employer is absorbing awards at 4.5 times billed charges [wJkWOgxZj6k @ 00:33:59]. The network fallout cuts both ways. Ron Howrigan cites an ASA survey in which more than 40% of respondents reported a UnitedHealthcare termination or threat of one, alongside Blue Cross NC threat letters. Both carriers stopped once the lawsuits began [f9Q5ImwNeS0 @ 00:19:50].

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Ways Disputes Die Before Anyone Argues the Merits**
