## Executive Summary

Practitioners from Priceline, Danske Bank, Roku, Under Armour and Avala treat SaaS and licensing as a second cloud bill carrying the same waste rate. Gartner's up-to-30% figure is quoted repeatedly, and SaaS now sits inside 90% of FinOps scopes. The consensus mechanics are to build one central vendor list, rank it by spend, focus on the top 20 to 25 vendors, and use identity and SSO data to reclaim inactive seats on 30- or 90-day thresholds. Street numbers include Danske's 3,000 discovered apps against a 600-800 target, a concurrent-license cut from 50 seats to 20 after peak use of 15, Mason's $100K shortfall example on a $1M commit, and soft caps giving 20% free overage. Disagreements center on headline waste stats (Danske rejects the 50% figure), on central versus business-unit ownership, and on whether a tool or Excel comes first.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on this circuit treats SaaS and licensing as a second cloud bill hiding in plain sight. Chris Reed runs IT finance inside the Priceline CTO org and owns the full tech budget. Reed ranks software as the number-two spend after cloud and repeats the Gartner line that up to 30% of SaaS spend is waste, the same ratio quoted for cloud [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:04:01]. Eddy Says Hi goes further, arguing that software spend can be 'as big, if not bigger' than infrastructure and using a mortgage analogy to land the point [sIJo4dVD-jc @ 00:01:02]. At the Applying the FinOps Framework session, Amy Ashby of Under Armour and Lindberg, director of cost optimization at Avala, stacked the waste figures. More than 30% of desktop, software and SaaS spend is wasted, 50% of installed software sits unused, and SaaS overspend is projected at 25% [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:01:29]. The State of FinOps 2026 numbers presented by Steve Trasch show the mandate has already landed. SaaS now sits inside 90% of FinOps scopes, and nearly half of practices also manage data center spend [O3FqsxJScI4 @ 00:10:51]. The operating conclusion is blunt: a FinOps practice that stops at the cloud invoice is ignoring a cost pool of comparable size carrying the same waste rate.

The second point of agreement is that visibility comes before any negotiation, and that it starts crude. Peter Greenshaw, interviewed by FinOps Weekly, lays out the order: build one central vendor list, rank it by spend, deep-dive the contracts at the top, and run the cycle year-round instead of only at budget season [sqpO85TKjEI @ 00:05:26]. Scott Beasley at Danske Bank, roughly 22,000 employees, shows what that list looks like once discovery is switched on. Over two years, 3,000 applications surfaced, the target estate is 600 to 800, and about 100 new apps appear every month through near-real-time discovery [F-5GIklN_Ps @ 00:04:28]. Reed starts from the P&L and pulls the highest software categories. Each one is classified as usage-based or license-based, and then vendors are asked for monthly utilization reports, a request vendors answered with 'no one has ever asked us' [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:13:11]. The 2026 trends podcast on Eddy Says Hi names the control point those lists hang from: 'Identity is rapidly becoming the primary control plane for SAS cost management' [byD2FJ-NF0M @ 00:12:17]. That is why SSO and identity data feed nearly every reclaim workflow in the mechanics below. Nobody on the circuit waits for a perfect tool before starting this inventory; the first pass is a list, not a platform.

Third, the practitioners agree on ownership and on where to aim limited hours. Derer Mason runs a three-person Cloud CoE at Roku and insists on being the single commercial point of contact for every vendor. At the same time, Mason acts as 'a facilitator, not the owner' of each contract and keeps the whole function centralized [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:20:47]. On accountability, Reed pulled costs out of a single 'core Tech' bucket and pushed them into product cost centers covering hotels, rental cars, flights and packages. Reed then published showback with named owners, explicitly framed as not a name-and-shame exercise [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:14:54]. Reed also applies the 80/20 cut hard: the top 20 vendors carry roughly 80% of spend, so the long tail is simply ignored during audits [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:33:09]. Lindberg lands in the same place with about 25 focus vendors, the top 20% of the base. Lindberg's reasoning is that the same hour of optimization work returns far more on a $1M vendor than on a $10K one [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:15:37]. The combined rule is simple: one central commercial desk, named budget owners who can see their own numbers, and scarce analyst hours concentrated on the handful of vendors that actually move the total.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

License harvesting is where the hard money gets clawed back, and the mechanics are already scripted. Michelle Campos of the ServiceNow Community ranks integrations by strength: direct vendor integration first, SSO through Azure or Okta second, and custom open API last. The Salesforce playbook then auto-builds a software model and a reclaim rule with a 30-day inactivity threshold. Campos recommends reviewing that threshold and possibly stretching it to 90 days [8mJc7j3DW28 @ 00:03:48]. In the live demo that rule surfaced 572 reclaim candidates, which can be reclaimed one at a time, all at once, or on full automation [8mJc7j3DW28 @ 00:05:14]. Scogo AI ran the same play against Microsoft 365 with a natural-language prompt in Cira CLI, stripping Business Premium licenses from disabled and never-signed-in users. Out of 25 licenses, the available count jumped from 21 to 24. That is three seats reclaimed, including one the presenter had missed, confirmed in the admin portal [hkNjUpPIx6M @ 00:02:27]. Procure IT's module hooks the same identity providers (Azure Entra, Okta or G Suite) to split power users from light users. The renewal conversation therefore starts with a list of who does not need a seat [_PyR6G51QW4 @ 00:01:11]. Every one of these workflows runs on the same two inputs: an identity signal and an inactivity threshold.

The contract math is where these practitioners get brutally specific. Mason walks through the commitment shortfall at Roku. Commit $1M over 12 months, spend only $900K, and you owe the vendor $100K. The escape hatch is a mini business case committing to $1.2M, which may get the shortfall forgiven [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:14:06]. The opposite failure bites too. Burn through prepaid funds at month nine and the discount ends, forcing an early renewal, which matters because some of those Roku discounts run over 90% [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:15:42]. At the negotiating table, Mason asks for discounted overages, free training and support, and an at-will termination clause carrying 30, 60 or 90 days' notice [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:25:11]. Reed bakes soft caps into a standard tech-contract framework originally built at UBS, complete with reporting clauses. Usage up to 20% over the cap is free in year one, and in year two the customer pays 50% of the delta [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:29:33]. That clause structure converts a surprise true-up into a negotiated, predictable ramp. Together these levers turn every renewal from a vendor-driven event into a buyer-scheduled one, with the shortfall, overage and exit terms written in well before anyone signs.

Rate versus usage is the sharpest piece of street math on the board, and it is where most negotiated wins quietly evaporate. Lindberg's worked example is to buy 1,000 licenses up front and watch the rollout take a full year. Every gap between contracted and active licenses is pure waste. The effective rate, contract cost divided by actual usage, can equal or exceed on-demand pricing, so procurement needs to hear that its recorded savings are not real [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:11:03]. The fix is to buy in steps rather than full-year upfront; it annoys accounts payable but saves 'thousands…hundreds of thousands' [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:16:36]. Greenshaw attacks the tier mix instead: 'I bet 90% of your organization is just updating PDFs and signing them,' so most Adobe users move to cheaper PDF tools while creatives keep full licenses [sqpO85TKjEI @ 00:09:41]. On concurrent licensing, Greenshaw found peak use of 15 seats against 50 purchased and cut to 20. The heat map behind that cut had to be built by hand [sqpO85TKjEI @ 00:11:09]. The pattern across all three plays is the same: measure actual peak usage first, then buy to that line plus a little headroom rather than to whatever volume the vendor proposes.

1. Build the central list and rank by spend

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is who owns the SaaS book. Amy Ashby runs FinOps at Under Armour with no central team at all: business units own their SaaS, and there is no central tool or process beyond purchasing [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:04:23]. Lindberg's shop at Avala went the opposite direction, recently folding IT software into the cost-optimization program so roughly 60% of software spend is now managed by one organization [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:05:27]. Derer Mason at Roku backs centralization from the commercial side and is blunt about it: 'make sure you're the single point of contact,' with every vendor conversation routed through one desk instead of scattered across requesters [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:20:47]. Yet Mason draws a line that pure centralizers skip, telling operators to be 'a facilitator, not the owner' of the contract itself [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:37:12]. That distinction is the real fork in the road. Ashby's model keeps decisions close to the business units that consume the tools but leaves purchasing as the only shared checkpoint. The Avala and Roku models concentrate leverage and visibility in one function, and Mason's facilitator rule keeps the business owner carrying the decision. The operator move: if the org chart will not centralize, centralize the vendor contact point and the spend data first, then let owners keep the call.

The second fight is rate versus usage. Derer Mason plays commitment as the weapon and reports bluntly that 'some of our discounts are over 90%' [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:15:42]. Lindberg pushes back on rate obsession. Typical vendor discounts land at 10, 20 or 30%, and 'if you're getting 5%' you are 'not working hard enough' [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:10:20]. But Lindberg's core warning is that the headline number can lie: 'that best rate you negotiated actually may cost you more because of underutilization,' so the ladder is to optimize usage rather than rate [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:13:59]. Chris Reed at Priceline takes a third path and writes elasticity into the paper itself, with a soft cap where usage 20% over the cap is free in year one, then 50% of the delta is paid in year two [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:29:33]. That gives operators three distinct playbooks: deep commit for discount, usage first and rate second, or cap the overage inside the contract. They are not mutually exclusive, but they create opposite instincts at renewal. Mason's approach leans toward a bigger committed number to unlock price, Lindberg's approach leans toward proving utilization before any number reaches the vendor, and Reed's approach buys room to be wrong on the forecast.

The third split is how big the problem really is and what it takes to see it. Chris Reed cites Gartner that up to 30% of SaaS spend is waste, the same figure Gartner gives for cloud [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:04:01]. Scott Beasley, running ITAM at a 22,000-employee bank in the middle of an AWS migration, refuses to accept the popular '50% not being utilized' figure for Danske [F-5GIklN_Ps @ 00:06:02]. On tooling, Peter Greenshaw says flatly that no tool 'scrapes all of that SaaS data natively' into a single pane, and the field's single source of truth is still mostly spreadsheets [sqpO85TKjEI @ 00:01:28]. Michelle Campos at ServiceNow shows the opposite end of the spectrum, letting the platform surface reclaim candidates, 572 of them in one demo, to be reclaimed one at a time, all at once, or fully automated [8mJc7j3DW28 @ 00:05:14]. The spreadsheet camp and the platform camp are not arguing about whether reclaim works; both assume it does. They are arguing about when the tooling pays for itself. The operator read: get the first utilization report out of whatever data you already have, then automate once the reclaim queue runs into the hundreds.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive surprises arrive at contract end. The Eddy Says Hi true-up example is the cleanest: licensed for 100 CPUs, spike to 120, and 'the vendor can true up your bill to that 120 rate for the entire year,' with no true down when usage falls back [sIJo4dVD-jc @ 00:02:38]. Chris Reed warns the same mechanic scales up badly, with a three-year over-license recoup landing at contract end [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:23:09]. Derer Mason runs the opposite failure, the shortfall: commit $1M over 12 months, spend only $900K, and you owe $100K anyway. Mason's escape hatch is a mini business case committing $1.2M, which may get the shortfall forgiven [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:14:06]. Amy Ashby maps how differently vendors punish overage: some bill overages at a higher rate, some cut features off, and some force a true-up or a higher contract at the end [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:19:57]. Each trap is set at signing and sprung months later. Under-forecast and you pay the true-up at the spike rate; over-forecast and you pay for the gap you never consumed. Either way, the contract terms drive the bill as much as usage does, so read the overage, shortfall and true-up clauses before the price line.

The second failure family is spend nobody signed off on. Chris Reed found recurring corporate-card charges still billing after employees left: '$5,000 a month, that's 60k a year,' multiplied by 20, plus a $300 charge multiplied across 800 other engineers [hQDjTANIaTo @ 00:09:23]. Lindberg reports a vendor that allowed overage and then threatened a cutoff at renewal, with 'millions of dollars' on the table [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:34:27]. Derer Mason's version is pure usage velocity: an intern's logging change cost $30,000 a day, which is the argument for near-real-time monitoring rather than waiting on the invoice [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:27:49]. Amy Ashby flags load tests as the recurring spike trigger: 'people kicking off load tests and then I'm seeing all these SAS products spiked because they're all tightly integrated,' and questions whether dev environments even need log shipping and advanced analytics [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:37:31]. None of these losses came from a bad negotiation. They came from usage and purchasing that happened outside the renewal conversation, where nobody was watching the meter. The economics check is simple: a monthly review catches the $5,000 card charge, but only a threshold alert catches a $30,000-a-day logging change before it compounds across a billing cycle.

The third trap is data you cannot get and paper you did not check. Peter Greenshaw asked every vendor to adopt FOCUS and roughly one agreed [sqpO85TKjEI @ 00:02:00]. Amy Ashby confirms the gap from the consumption side: consumption SaaS often lacks APIs, overage data is rarer still, and SAM and ITAM tools show only contract-level data [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:23:37]. The payoff for pushing anyway is real. Ashby hounded one vendor for months to get an extract on an unmetered enterprise SKU, found a calculation error, and received a credit [iIwpcQbBkmM @ 00:41:34]. Derer Mason's paper trap is the marketplace: the EULA must match the signed contract, because 'you don't want the vendor to have the option to choose' which terms apply [LF1AK1WypX0 @ 00:33:07]. Each of these failures is quiet. No alert fires when a vendor declines a data format, when a SKU calculation is wrong, or when a marketplace click-through carries different terms than the negotiated agreement. The economics check: put data delivery into the contract ask, reconcile vendor extracts against your own usage at least once per term, and match every marketplace EULA to the signed agreement before the first invoice lands.
