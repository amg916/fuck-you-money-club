# Chapter 06: Operating as a Microsoft CSP

*Direct vs. indirect CSP models, CSP pricing, margins and incentives, new direct-bill requirements, getting started through an indirect provider, CSP agreements, procurement ROI, the commercial opportunity for MSPs, and moving customers between CSPs*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Operators at Pax8, Stratos Cloud Alliance, Manage Protect, Leader and the m365 Show agree on the core CSP play: the partner owns billing, provisioning and first-line support, the license throws off thin margin, and the money sits in services stacked on top. The street split is direct versus indirect. Direct bill now demands $1 million in trailing CSP revenue, up from $300,000, plus a solutions partner designation, a paid support plan and a billing marketplace Stratos prices upwards of $100,000, so Pax8 expects upwards of two-thirds of direct partners to move indirect. Indirect resellers need only $1,000 USD a year to stay authorized and $25,000 to chase rebates, and Stratos insists they earn identical back-end incentives. Friction remains over whether the security score binds indirect resellers, and where enterprise agreements undercut CSP at scale.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on record frames CSP the same way: the partner, not Microsoft, owns the customer. AppXite describes both sales motions as partner-centric, with the partner running pre-sale, post-sale, cross-sell and upsell plus billing and support [rSX-pZstKp8 @ 00:00:17]. Robert Kofi of the Demystifying Microsoft series says partners explicitly demanded to own the Office 365 billing relationship, and CSP was built so they could bundle services, set pricing and own billing terms [lGN8Dnmwb08 @ 00:00:34]. The m365 Show puts it bluntly for the customer side: buy through a CSP and Microsoft is no longer your billing contact; a 50-person shop gets one monthly invoice and calls the partner when someone cannot log in [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:00:54]. Crimson Line adds that CSP drops the enterprise agreement baggage entirely: no 500-user minimum, no software assurance requirement, and aggressive net pricing where the end price is driven by the partner's value-added services rather than deployment volume [pRWl0RalG28 @ 00:02:05]. The structural takeaway is consistent across these four voices. The license is a door-opener and a billing hook; the account, the support queue and the invoice are what the operator actually controls, and that control is what makes the rest of the economics work.

The second point of agreement is that direct bill is being squeezed into a tiny elite. Chris Sharp of Pax8 says the bar used to be roughly $300,000 a year transacted plus a solution designation, and the jump to $1 million could push upwards of two-thirds of direct partners to find a new home with an indirect provider [Xywyds1IfvM @ 00:05:09]. Jeff Edwards at Stratos Cloud Alliance confirms the $300,000-to-$1 million move and lists the rest of the stack: a designation specific to each solution area, a heavier security score, and a mandatory premier or advanced support plan [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:03:12]. The m365 Show adds a requirement the others skip, 12 months of prior experience as an indirect reseller, and puts the number of US direct bill partners at only about 30 [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:02:00]. Belen Tamayo of Microsoft's channel marketing frames the trade the same way: direct providers must fund billing, provisioning and first-level support, while indirect resellers lean on the provider's platform, credit and training, and break even four times faster [ybzVv-GZvMc @ 00:03:41]. The practical read across all four: if trailing CSP revenue sits anywhere below seven figures, indirect is now the default lane.

Third, the money is in services, not the SKU. Eva Osborne of the Demystifying Microsoft series, while laying out an 8% managed-partner rebate, warned that profitability has to be driven by services and lower cost of sales through scale and automation, with incentives treated as an extra rather than the core driver [wzXR-g6_lxo @ 00:01:53]. AppXite says partners can theoretically earn up to 25% per sale since they control markups, but it is unlikely to reach that, and transacting out-of-the-box SKUs is no longer a scalable model; MSPs layering their own IP on top are the ones with a profitable future [rSX-pZstKp8 @ 00:02:41]. The m365 Show sizes license margin at 8 to 15% depending on incentives and volume and says the real profit lives in managed services wrapped around it [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:03:47]. Jeff Edwards closes the loop on rebates: the misconception that back-end incentives get split between distributor and reseller is wrong, because the indirect reseller earns exactly what a direct seller earns [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:16:13]. So the consensus operator model is simple: sell the license at or near list, collect whatever back-end incentive the tier allows, and build the margin on the service bundle.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Getting live as an indirect reseller is a Partner Center exercise. CloudStrategist walks the sequence: enroll free in the partner network to get your MPN ID, sign the Microsoft Partner Agreement, and have each end customer sign the Microsoft Customer Agreement; the distributor then defines how you order, either a self-service portal or orders placed on your behalf [-_TJlxiXPJc @ 00:03:50]. Alex, Leader's national trainer, says the first move after registration is verification under the cogwheel, account settings, legal info, where you prove ownership of the business and the domain; partners routinely stall here by missing Microsoft's emails [GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:08:01]. David Bujaider of Manage Protect adds the October vetting rules: the legal entity name must match your registration, the address must be your primary place of business, never a PO box or your accountant's office, and the contact must be a current employee rather than a former staffer behind an email redirect [Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:08:21]. Leader's operational tip is to create customers inside Leader Cloud rather than Partner Center, since the distributor-side record typically auto-creates in Partner Center but not the reverse, and to key in your partner ID correctly because it ties reseller, distributor and customer together [GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:13:09].

The cost sheet for staying direct is brutal. Scott May at Stratos says direct bill requires your own marketplace for transacting and billing, an initial build priced upwards of $100,000, before the ongoing people, resources and money needed to maintain it [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:08:45]. On top of that sits the support mandate: the advanced plan at a minimum of 15k, often more, or premier at about $50,000 annually, both of which vanish when you move indirect because the distributor carries support [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:12:32]. The m365 Show independently pegs advanced support for partners at about $15,000 a year as the floor, says it ends in January 2027 with Unified for Partners replacing it, and flags up to 50% off that replacement for partners who earn the support services designation [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:11:32]. Contrast the indirect entry ticket from David Bujaider at Manage Protect: stay authorized by generating at least $1,000 USD, roughly $1,500 AUD, of Microsoft licensing revenue over a rolling 12 months, the equivalent of selling four annual Business Premium licenses [Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:09:56]. That is the spread pushing sub-scale operators indirect: six figures of fixed infrastructure and support against a four-figure annual revenue floor.

Once inside, the pricing levers are concrete. For partners chasing rebates, Bujaider sets the incentive tier at $25,000 USD, about $38,600 AUD, over 12 months, and says a partner sitting just short should bundle more Microsoft services, upsell to E3 or E5, or push longer-term agreements to close the gap [Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:16:48]. The m365 Show lays out the NCE price ladder: the monthly term runs a 20% premium over annual list, so $100 becomes $120; annual paid monthly carries a 5% premium; annual paid upfront sits at list; and triennial locks price for three years at roughly 12 to 18% savings versus renewing annually [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:05:11]. The same show models the bundle that actually pays: Business Premium sold at $22 per user per month, identical to buying from Microsoft, plus identity setup, device management and a help desk for another $8, landing the customer at $30 on one invoice [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:04:01]. Alex at Leader flags a quieter margin lever: the distributor charges no credit card fee, which depending on who you buy from today can mean 2 to 4% more margin [GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:27:02]. Stack those and the service layer, not the SKU, carries the account.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Register and Clear Verification
   - Enroll in the partner network, obtain the MPN or partner ID, and push account verification through legal info.
   - Enrollment is a no-cost process once your business entity details are handy [CloudStrategist — -_TJlxiXPJc @ 00:03:31]; then prove business and domain ownership under account settings, legal info, and watch for Microsoft emails so vetting does not stall [Leader Academy — GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:08:01].
2. Pass Business Vetting and Sign the MPA
   - Update legal name, primary physical address and a current employee contact, then accept the new Microsoft Partner Agreement.
   - Confirm CSP enrollment status under legal info; once it shows qualified, accept the new MPA in the agreements tab, and note only a global admin on your tenant can complete this step [Manage Protect — Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:10:39].
3. Lock the Security Baseline
   - Enforce MFA across partner tenants, adopt the secure application model, set a security contact and staff alert response.
   - All CSP partners must enable multi-factor authentication for all users in all partner tenants [Microsoft Americas Partner Community — ybzVv-GZvMc @ 00:09:59]; set a security contact and respond to any alerts within 24 hours ahead of the new security score dashboard [Manage Protect — Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:12:30].
4. Link Customers and Cut Over Licenses
   - Send the reseller relationship link, collect Microsoft Customer Agreement acceptance, and schedule transfers around renewal dates.
   - Once the customer accepts the reseller relationship link you get global admin privileges into their tenant [Leader Academy — GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:11:41]; for inbound transfers, schedule the license cutover carefully around renewal dates after the customer approves you in Partner Center [Plow Networks — KkM553ycNrA @ 00:01:41].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether losing direct-bill status is a demotion or an upgrade. Eva Osborne of the Microsoft SMB team frames the trade plainly: indirect CSP margins are typically lower than direct, but there is no upfront investment to get started, and distributors layer on bill-on-behalf and customer support services that cut your cost of transacting [wzXR-g6_lxo @ 00:03:29]. Jeff Edwards at Stratos Cloud Alliance attacks the margin half of that claim head-on, calling it a misconception that back-end incentives get split with the distributor; he says the indirect reseller makes the exact same back-end incentives as a direct seller [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:16:29]. His colleague Scott May adds the cost the direct camp tends to ignore: running your own marketplace, billing and transaction infrastructure can run upwards of $100,000 up front, then takes people, resources and money to maintain [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:08:45]. Microsoft's own Americas partner deck lands on the indirect side, stating that indirect resellers break even four times faster and that direct providers must fund billing, provisioning and first-level support themselves [ybzVv-GZvMc @ 00:03:41]. Stratos still keeps the door open both ways, pitching indirect as a scaling runway that could carry a partner back to direct-bill status later rather than a permanent ceiling on growth.

Where the money actually lives is the second fault line. Osborne quotes an 8 percent rebate on CSP sales for managed partners, identical in direct and indirect, yet warns that services margins run significantly higher and incentives should never be the key profitability driver, especially since rebates change fiscal year by fiscal year [wzXR-g6_lxo @ 00:01:53]. AppXite goes further: incentives have been trending down, the latest move swapped them for partner earned credits under the new commerce experience, and while partners can theoretically mark up to 25 percent because they control their own markups, it is unlikely to get there; transacting out-of-the-box SKUs is no longer a scalable business model [rSX-pZstKp8 @ 00:02:41]. The m365 Show puts the license margin at 8 to 15 percent and says the same Business Premium seat costs $22 whether bought from Microsoft or the partner, so the partner stacks identity setup, device management and help desk for $8 more and bills $30 [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:04:01]. Softspend pulls the other way, pitching fiscal year 27 growth incentives as a reason to attach assessments and new services because Microsoft is incentivizing that heavily [ziNVpGkDNI4 @ 00:02:07]. That puts the Softspend playbook on a collision course with Osborne's warning to treat incentives as extra, not core.

The EA-versus-CSP line moves depending on who draws it. Carl O'Doherty at Version 1 sets the break point around 2,500 users: above that, EA costs are typically lower unless competitive CSP pricing is put in place, while organizations from 500 to 2,500 users should see near parity on online services [PLfTJs7m4ww @ 00:22:05]. The m365 Show draws the line much lower, saying EA pricing typically comes in 20 to 35 percent lower per seat at 500 or more users, CSP is the call under 500, and under 300 seats CSP is almost certainly the best bet [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:07:59]. Softspend argues the historic EA price edge has already evaporated: Microsoft 365 E5 lists at $57 per user per month in both channels, and Windows 365 is priced better in CSP than in the EA [zwXrzaNyZB0 @ 00:00:18]. The m365 Show also reports new EA enrollment stopped in October 2024 and MPSA closed to new business in mid-2025, making CSP under new commerce the default for most buyers [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:09:23]. O'Doherty still keeps the EA alive for renewals, custom contractual terms, three-year fixed pricing and software assurance on on-premises servers, telling customers facing renewal to price the alternatives before rolling over.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The direct-bill cliff is the biggest firsthand shock. Jeff Edwards at Stratos says the bar jumped from $300,000 to $1 million in annual CSP revenue, layered on solution designations now required in each solution area you sell into, a heavier security requirement you cannot tick overnight, and continued premier or advanced support plans [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:03:12]. Scott May prices those plans at a basic tier of at least $15,000, or premier support at about $50,000 annually, and stresses that enforcement hits at each partner's annual renewal rather than a single October cut-off [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:12:14]. Chris Sharp of Pax8 estimates upwards of two-thirds of direct partners could be forced to find a new home with an indirect provider [Xywyds1IfvM @ 00:05:09]. The m365 Show adds that direct-bill now demands 12 months of prior experience as an indirect reseller and that only about 30 partners in the entire United States are direct bill [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:01:47]. Edwards singles out legacy partners still sitting on large GP and SL on-premises customer bases as most exposed, because the gold and silver competency route to CSP incentives has largely ended. His fix is to step on the accelerator: lock the solution designation and drive cloud volume well before renewal.

The paperwork traps are mundane but lethal. David Bujaider at Manage Protect has watched partners stall in business vetting because they listed a PO box or their accountant's address instead of their primary place of business, or left a contact who left five years ago sitting behind an email redirect [Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:08:21]. He warns that only a global admin can accept the new Microsoft Partner Agreement, that Microsoft may begin de-authorizing partners who have not accepted it by their program anniversary following October 1, 2025, and that partnerships are already being suspended [Je2jbg9tm0Q @ 00:11:52]. Leader Cloud's Alex sees the same choke point from the onboarding side: partners miss Microsoft's verification emails and the process gets stuck, and the distributor cannot force it through because Microsoft does the vetting itself [GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:08:16]. Scott May at Stratos flags the security score as the one requirement nobody escapes, because after October 1 neither direct nor indirect partners can transact CSP without meeting the Partner Center minimum [rV-Q2IVur6I @ 00:15:12]. Bujaider muddies that picture: while listing MFA for admins, a security contact and 24-hour alert response, he says it is not applicable to indirect resellers, with enforcement and the dashboard still pending.

Even compliant partners bleed margin through commitment and consumption mechanics. The m365 Show lays out the new commerce trap: a 7-day cancellation window, then the term is binding with no mid-term seat reductions, so a customer who planned 100 seats in January and needs 80 by March keeps paying for 20 empty seats; month-to-month costs a 20 percent premium, and an expired subscription slides into an extended term at the monthly rate plus a 3 percent uplift [LGgw20GtaDQ @ 00:05:42]. Leader Cloud warns that some Azure resources are quite expensive to provision and that reserved instances only prepay compute, leaving disk, data flow and transfer charges still billed [GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:18:28]. Version 1's Carl O'Doherty sees organizations lift and shift all their cloud spend into CSP without ever right-sizing actual usage, and on the EA side, customers stuffing agreements with incentivized products that become rolled-over shelfware [PLfTJs7m4ww @ 00:09:52]. Leader also points to a quieter leak: credit card fees of two to four percent that a distributor charging no card fee hands back as margin [GAif9Dapd50 @ 00:27:02]. The m365 Show's survival rule is blunt: plan headcount carefully, and if unsure, overbuy slightly, because seats can be added but never removed.
