## Executive Summary

AWS Marketplace practitioners agree the private offer is the enterprise closing tool: a custom contract pinned to a buyer's AWS account ID, with AWS invoicing, collecting, and disbursing the money. Setup is free and requires an eligible jurisdiction for paid listings, a paid listing, management portal access, and ideally a dedicated seller of record account. Mechanics: a five-step offer wizard, up to 23 buyers per SaaS contract offer, durations up to five years, installment schedules, and a worked example where an $80 discounted price nets the ISV $72 after a 10 percent listing fee. Renewals run through agreement-based offers that replace the live subscription. Speakers diverge on pace and packaging: AWS staff push standard contracts, CRM API integration, and future dated agreements, while PartnerCloud argues against bucketing everything under one company-name listing.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every speaker who touches the mechanic lands on the same definition: a private offer is a custom contract created from a listing and pinned to a specific buyer AWS account. Prunal, CEO and co-founder of Clazar, frames it as customized contracts carrying special pricing or terms for specific customers, all inside the AWS Marketplace ecosystem, with AWS still handling billing and payments so no separate invoicing is required [h7liwzzA_-I @ 00:00:20]. The AWS ISV operations walkthrough states it the same way: the customer receives pricing that is not publicly available, negotiates terms with the seller, and the partner creates the offer for the AWS account the customer designates, with each offer carrying its own pricing, licensing terms, and a set expiration date [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:01:04]. David, presenting at re:Invent 2024, boils it down further: partners can write custom quotes to their customers through private offers, covering volume discounts, custom contractual terms, and specific payment schedules [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:02:50]. Tyler from the AWS Marketplace team positions the same feature as the white glove lane, distinct from self-service click-to-subscribe, where sellers control contract terms and payment schedules as they see fit [HU_SJTFHcGU @ 00:02:20]. The shared takeaway for an operator is simple: negotiate the deal directly with the buyer, then let the offer and AWS billing carry the paperwork and collections.

The baseline prerequisites are short and repeated across sources. The ISV operations walkthrough lists three: the partner must have a paid listing in AWS Marketplace, must have access to the AWS Marketplace Management Portal, and the buyer must be an AWS customer [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:05:26]. Tyler's registration walkthrough states there is no charge or fee to register as a seller and recommends a fresh AWS account as the seller of record, because every Marketplace interaction ties to that account; sellers told AWS that separating production accounts from the seller of record account simplifies management, separates disbursements, and adds a security layer [w6XECzZOfXY @ 00:01:44]. David's re:Invent sequence matches the onboarding order: sign in with an AWS account, agree to the terms and conditions, set up the seller profile and banking info, create the first product listing, integrate with the Billing API for contract or usage consumption, then start selling [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:04:52]. On the profile side, the PartnerCloud host walked the horizontal tabs live: a public profile needing only a 300 by 150 logo file, display name, URL, and a 600 character max description, then tax information, bank account, KYC with extra requirements in EMEA and South Korea, service link roles for resale authorizations, and notifications [qXei9F9hLNU @ 00:02:35].

The commercial case is consistent across the speakers: buyers want to consolidate third-party spend onto the AWS bill, and sellers ride that procurement path. Tyler reports the most common customer feedback is love for consolidating third-party software expenses into the AWS bill, plus pay-as-you-go consumption and flexible payment terms, and on the seller side some sellers shared they accelerated sales cycles upwards of 50 percent because the heavy lifting of working with procurement teams and approving software purchases gets eliminated [HU_SJTFHcGU @ 00:05:22]. David layers on vendor outcomes of 80 percent bigger deals, 81 percent return on investment, 27 percent higher win rates, and 40 percent higher deal sizes, and notes customers often buy through Marketplace to meet AWS commitments [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:02:05]. Prunal pitches private offers as one of the best ways to accelerate enterprise transactions, specifically for enterprise customers needing tailored pricing and contract terms, multi-year or volume deals, and channel motions through CPPO [h7liwzzA_-I @ 00:01:15]. The PartnerCloud host adds a field-level angle: a professional services purchase through Marketplace burns down the buyer's Marketplace commit, which becomes a selling point written straight into the listing copy [qXei9F9hLNU @ 00:14:47].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The build path is identical across demos. Log into the AWS Marketplace Management Portal, open the Offers tab, and hit the orange Create Private Offer button; David describes a five-step wizard covering pricing details, duration, terms, legal documents, and the customer's AWS ID [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:05:38]. The SaaS contract demo runs the same order in practice: select product type, pick the product, wait up to 30 seconds for the offer ID to generate, set offer name, description, renewal flag and expiration date, then configure pricing and duration, add the buyer account ID, and attach legal terms such as the Standard Contract for AWS Marketplace [2qDBJ9lxIdQ @ 00:01:42]. That same demo caps targeting at up to 23 buyers on a single private offer and treats an Active status as the signal the offer is published and available for the buyer to accept [2qDBJ9lxIdQ @ 00:03:53]. The earlier generic demo shows the payload: dimensions are the features, services, and usage amounts of the software; the seller can let customers pick quantity, designate price, select zero dollar pricing, and add service agreement details, then copy the offer URL and email it straight to the buyer [PVL4069oqC0 @ 00:01:11].

Pricing structure is where the deal gets shaped. David splits private offer pricing into four basic structures: contract pricing with an upfront fee for a set term, usage pay-as-you-go, installment payment schedules where AWS invoices on dates the two sides agree, and hybrids where a contract converts into overage once the customer hits the max; installment dates themselves often become a negotiation lever [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:03:34]. The ISV walkthrough lists standard SaaS contract durations of one month, one year, two years, or three years, with custom contracts for any number of months up to five years [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:02:17]. Its worked unit economics: a $100 list product discounted 20 percent becomes an $80 private offer price, AWS charges the customer $80, and after a 10 percent listing fee the ISV nets $72, with VAT or other taxes added on top of the customer price at invoicing [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:10:25]. In the installment demo, the seller starts the contract on acceptance with a 12 month duration, picks dimensions, applies a bulk unit price to usage dimensions, enters total contract price and number of installments, sets the first invoice date, generates the plan, and can manually edit each invoice amount or date [2qDBJ9lxIdQ @ 00:02:43].

After signature, the money and renewal mechanics take over. Customers receive AWS invoices on the third of the month, Marketplace purchases ride on the monthly AWS invoice under AWS net payment terms, and buyer funds can take one to two days to post to the sub ledger before they count toward a disbursement [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:11:43]. For upgrades and renewals on SaaS contracts, Chloe Weaver walks the agreement-based offer: open the Agreements tab, search the active subscription by buyer AWS account ID, click create agreement-based offer, set end date, dimensions, quantity, usage pricing, payment schedule, and EULA, mark it as a renewal, then allow up to 45 minutes for the new offer ID and URL to appear [KzcE0ZWyjzk @ 00:01:58]. The companion demo adds that the seller can co-term on the existing end date, keep the current EULA or swap to standard, enterprise, or custom terms, bill upfront by scheduling the invoice for today, and set expiration at least two weeks out because AWS systems run on UTC [MlboB3rLbvE @ 00:02:38]. Future dated offers let the seller pick New Offer starting at a future date with service start and end dates; once accepted it becomes a future dated agreement [xLqQjXa2edo @ 00:01:16].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Register the seller of record account
   - Sign into a dedicated AWS account, open the Sell in AWS Marketplace tab, and click Register now.
   - On the registration page review the legal business name, accept and download the seller terms and conditions, and answer the questions on product types, pricing models, category, and industry segment before signing into the Management Portal [Amazon Web Services — w6XECzZOfXY @ 00:02:44]
2. Build the seller profile and first listing
   - Complete the profile tabs, then create the first product with descriptions, highlights, support, categories, and keywords.
   - Upload a 300 by 150 logo with a 600 character max description, add tax, bank, and KYC details, then build the product with an S3 URL thumbnail, short and long descriptions, three highlights, support details, up to three categories, and keywords [PartnerCloud — qXei9F9hLNU @ 00:07:43]
3. Create and send the private offer
   - From the Offers tab, run the Create Private Offer wizard and share the URL once the offer is Active.
   - Fill pricing details, duration, terms, legal documents, and the buyer AWS ID in the five-step wizard [AWS Events — K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:05:38], attach the Standard Contract for AWS Marketplace, review, create, and monitor until status reads Active [Amazon Web Services — 2qDBJ9lxIdQ @ 00:04:15]
4. Collect, then renew through agreement-based offers
   - Track invoicing and disbursement, then build renewals off the active agreement in the Agreements tab.
   - Customers are invoiced on the third of the month and funds take one to two days to post before disbursement [Amazon Web Services — qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:11:43]; renewals are created as agreement-based offers from the buyer's active subscription [Amazon Web Services — KzcE0ZWyjzk @ 00:01:15]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fork is how the cash moves. The AWS SaaS-contract walkthrough lays out two pricing tracks: contract pricing with upfront payment, where the buyer picks quantities and AWS invoices the full amount the moment the private offer is accepted, versus contract pricing with an installment plan, where the seller enters a total contract price, a number of installments and a first invoice date, then can hand-edit each invoice amount or date [2qDBJ9lxIdQ @ 00:02:24]. David at re:Invent treats installments as a bargaining chip rather than an admin setting: AWS sends the invoice on the specific date seller and customer agree on, and the installment date and distribution becomes a factor in the negotiation [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:03:56]. The catch surfaces on renewals. Chloe Weaver points out that every agreement-based offer is created with scheduled payments, which requires the buyer to be on invoicing terms with AWS [KzcE0ZWyjzk @ 00:01:37]. The buyer-side walkthrough spells out the fix: switch the account to invoicing terms, also called ACH direct debit, by opening a customer service case under Account and billing support in the Marketplace category with company name, contact name, phone, email and mailing address attached [XvBBSBimMn4 @ 00:05:47]. Upfront billing dodges that friction; installments demand the buyer's billing setup be sorted first.

The second split is where offers get built. The ISV operations overview describes the old way, manually entering deal details into a form for customization, and the new way: the seller creates tool inside the AWS Marketplace Management Portal, which lets the partner build the offer independently without being in touch with the AWS team, with a direct ask to reach out if the tool is not working on the account [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:06:07]. David pushes past the portal entirely once volume climbs: integrate with the Marketplace API so private offers go out from the seller's own CRM instead of logging onto the portal every single time, and create products and read agreements there too, which David calls how you go fast and go big [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:14:28]. Clazar CEO Prunal pitches a third lane, working with an implementation expert to launch on AWS in a couple of weeks or less [h7liwzzA_-I @ 00:01:50]. Inside the AWS Ops Squad, the favorite low-tech fix is simply drafts: one team member calls saving as drafts a game changer because folks no longer have to complete private offer creation in one step and can walk away until they have all of the information [gNv2wvhYkzA @ 00:04:12]. Low-volume sellers can live in the wizard; high-volume sellers are told to wire the API.

Packaging is the third fault line. The PartnerCloud host, listing professional services, skipped tiered dimensions like platinum, gold and silver, kept a single monthly dimension and chose to shoehorn everyone into that one option, pushing differentiation into private pricing; by default the whole listing routes through private pricing, and the host is still curious what showing a public price for the assessment would look and feel like [qXei9F9hLNU @ 00:17:19]. David argues for a different emphasis: tap product-led growth with self-service click-to-buy, free trials, request for demo and request for private offer so customers start on Marketplace, become leads and get access to larger private-offer deals [K-GonrtXvKA @ 00:12:16]. Tyler from the AWS Marketplace team frames the same split as two purchase paths, self-service subscription with little to no intervention versus private offers as a white glove experience where sellers control contract terms and payment schedules as they see fit [HU_SJTFHcGU @ 00:02:20]. The PartnerCloud host also takes a swing at big partners who bucket every transaction under one company-named listing, arguing that separate SKUs per offering and per industry, healthcare versus education versus automotive, make the seller findable by buyers and AWS field sellers alike [qXei9F9hLNU @ 00:20:51]. The trade is reach through a public price versus control through a custom quote.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common dead deal is a link that stops working. The ISV operations overview is blunt: every private offer carries a set expiration date, and if the customer does not accept by then the offer is no longer available, leaving the partner to either extend the expiration date or create a new offer [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:01:43]. The agreement-based offer walkthrough adds the clock detail that trips sellers: AWS systems run in UTC time, and best practice is to set the expiration date for at least two weeks [MlboB3rLbvE @ 00:02:38]. When the buyer clicks and hits a 404, the AWS troubleshooting list runs in order: confirm the buyer is logged in to the correct account ID, confirm with the ISV or channel partner that the offer was created for that account ID, and check whether it expired, which the seller fixes by modifying the expiration date in the Offers tab [XvBBSBimMn4 @ 00:06:43]. Two quieter blockers follow: a buyer running a private marketplace needs the product on its allow list, and ISVs using limited listings, products not available on the public Marketplace, must allow the customer's account to view it; only after that does the advice drop to clearing cache and cookies, incognito windows or a different browser, never Internet Explorer [XvBBSBimMn4 @ 00:07:16].

Renewals and SaaS fulfillment carry their own landmines. Chloe Weaver warns that when a buyer subscribes to an agreement-based offer it completely replaces the current subscription: invoices already generated stay untouched, but invoices from the original deal that have not been generated yet are replaced by the new payment schedule, and no cancellation of the previous subscription is necessary [KzcE0ZWyjzk @ 00:02:16]. Timing is not instant either; the agreement-based offer walkthrough says the offer is created within 45 minutes and only then becomes available to send from the Offers tab, so the link is not ready the instant the offer is submitted [MlboB3rLbvE @ 00:03:17]. On SaaS, the create-offer demo stresses that AWS does not provision SaaS products: after subscribing, the buyer must register on the seller's website, and if the product includes consumption, additional usage is billed on top of the upfront amount [PVL4069oqC0 @ 00:02:32]. The buyer-side walkthrough ties that overage to the seller's plumbing, since additional usage is billed on the third of the following month provided the ISV sends AWS metering records [XvBBSBimMn4 @ 00:04:25]. Skip the registration page or the metering feed and the deal closes on paper while the product access or the overage revenue never lands.

Run the economics check before granting the discount. The ISV overview walks the math: a $100 list price with a 20 percent negotiated discount lands the customer at $80, and when the customer pays the AWS invoice, AWS disburses that $80 minus applicable listing fees, which at the 10 percent fee used in the example nets the ISV $72 [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:10:25]. The cash lag is structural: customers receive invoices on the third of the month, AWS invoice net payment terms apply, and once a payment is received it can take one to two days to post to the sub-ledger, which must happen at time of disbursement for the funds to be included [qWl-wamuA-Q @ 00:12:05]. The PartnerCloud host hit a different wall during setup: the listing review got hung up because the website had no support section, a professional-services shop with no support component to point to, so the host added one and went back under review with the company profile still limited to private visibility [qXei9F9hLNU @ 00:07:08]. The Ops Squad team describes the year-end failure mode future dated agreements were built to kill: running around last minute at Christmas asking whether a deal is done with everyone's hair on fire, with some partners implementing future dated agreements at scale and seeing renewal rates go up [gNv2wvhYkzA @ 00:03:43].
