# Chapter 01: The VAR Business Model

*VAR vs. MSP roles, choosing an ERP or vendor partner, ISV engagement asks from VARs, carrier and TMS reseller programs, customer success hand-offs, customer case studies, and what high-performing resellers do before marketing*

## Executive Summary

VAR operators agree the license is a thin-margin entry ticket and the money sits in implementation, training, customization and support layered on top. Positive Results says most software providers push over 80 percent of sales through VARs and pay the VAR directly, while Phil Morettini warns VARs will not create demand for a vendor. Steve Preda's XAPT case shows the upside of verticalizing a commodity Microsoft ERP: $24 million in revenue and $14 million pre-tax profit, near 60 percent margin. TC2's Ben Fox details how markups hide in shaved OEM discounts and buried fees, and Stuart Sutton lays out a full license stack of setup fees, monthly minimums, usage pricing and per-hour customization. The friction points: Scott Alan Miller says a VAR's sales incentive overrides any advisory role, and Sheldon Needle flags small, high-turnover VARs.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The operators converge on one blunt definition: a VAR resells someone else's platform bundled with the labor that makes it work. Jim Ward, CEO of BrainSell, says his firm sells software platforms and adds value by supplying training, implementation, development and business-application know-how, because vendors build good software but are not on the ground day to day with its hard parts [Y3mGiwmy2l0 @ 00:01:19]. His line: software is stupid and needs to be told what to do, so the VAR maps it onto people and process. Jillian Craig of Eye on Tech lists the add-on forms as customization, additional offerings, installation, and consulting or design, and warns that small VARs often get pushed into buying through a two-tier distribution model instead of direct [WEqXH4PuF8c @ 00:00:20]. Sheldon Needle of CTS Guides frames VARs as independent companies reselling a vendor's software under license, becoming the party that sells, installs and trains the user [2tRDmrgGASk @ 00:00:00]. Steve Preda traces the model to the early 90s, when SAP, Microsoft and Oracle dominated, smaller developers struggled to sell, and system integrators began earning money by customizing, training and consulting around those platforms, taking a small margin on the license and bundling all services on top [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:02:09].

Everyone who touches the money agrees the license itself is thin and the services are the profit engine. Preda says the original VARs made a small margin on the software and sold their bundled services alongside it [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:02:09]. Ben Fox of TC2 calls resale good for revenue but a low-margin business, and says systems integrators and telecom carriers wax and wane on VAR services depending on whether they are chasing revenue growth or healthy margin that year [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:03:05]. Phil Morettini of PTM Consulting tells vendors that VARs will not be motivated solely by high product margins; what they market and sell very actively is their own high-margin services, so a product that enables those services puts the vendor in great shape with the channel [L1YsH5QkIus @ 00:01:22]. The flip side is leverage over vendors: Positive Results claims most software providers do over 80 percent of their sales through VARs, that the VAR is compensated by the software company, and that it never charges the client more on monthly user fees [z6rVTIy-FOg @ 00:02:59]. Fox adds that OEMs such as Cisco run channel strategies and prefer to transact through partners rather than directly, which makes VARs practically inevitable for enterprise buyers sourcing from many manufacturers at once [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:01:16].

The third point of agreement is that winners pick a narrow lane and vet the vendor hard before selling it. Ridge Consulting says low performers grab a technology, sprint into multiple segments without checking long-term fit and run short-term marketing, then scramble to survive when business and technology cycles turn; high performers run a full strategic review with vendor support, identify one segment with high strategic fit and build tactics to dominate it [NbukIA2E8Nw @ 00:00:50]. Preda's XAPT story proves the payoff: a Hungarian Microsoft ERP implementer customized Navision for one Caterpillar dealer, got referred to another, and turned heavy-equipment dealers into a global niche that Microsoft embraced, putting the firm on stage with Steve Ballmer [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:03:26]. Positive Results screens every partner on six factors: true cloud access from any device, functionality tested against real customer use cases rather than developer claims, easy integrations, simple user experience, a mobile-first build and world-class security [z6rVTIy-FOg @ 00:00:42]. Morettini insists the vendor, not the VAR, must position the product and create demand, and must have sold it successfully direct first so it has best practices to hand partners, because VARs will not create demand or spearhead a new marketplace for anyone [L1YsH5QkIus @ 00:02:38].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Ben Fox of TC2 breaks down exactly how the VAR's cut gets taken, and it is rarely one clean number. The markup is the charge for VAR services, applied either as a percentage over net cost of goods or by shaving the OEM discount: if the OEM gives 60 percent off, the VAR passes through 58 [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:04:10]. Fox stresses that a one percent markup on cost and a one percent cut to the discount sound identical but land very differently on the final price, and that the least transparent model simply quotes a net discount per OEM so nobody sees the margin [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:04:42]. Markups also float by country, by OEM, and by whether a distributor sits between VAR and manufacturer, which lines up with Eye on Tech's point that small VARs get routed through two-tier distribution and only mark up the distributor's product a small amount [WEqXH4PuF8c @ 00:01:33]. Fox's checklist of fees that get buried includes import duties, customs clearance, import broker fees, exchange-rate hedging, extra logistics layers and management fees; only shipping and sales tax should ride on top [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:05:51]. On recurring cloud licenses, Positive Results says the vendor pays the VAR, leaving client per-user fees untouched [z6rVTIy-FOg @ 00:02:59].

Stuart Sutton's Full Circle TMS walkthrough lays out the most granular VAR license structure of any operator. Applicants must be non-transportation entities so subscribers trust their data stays away from competitors, they get a geographic area or vertical, their office must sit inside that territory, and the product is rebranded to look like theirs; the license is kept only by generating sufficient business within the first two years [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:03:43]. The vendor bills the VAR a one-time setup and training cost, a monthly VAR fee, a wholesale price on subscriber usage, on-demand support charges above a threshold, and cost-plus-management-fee pay for any dedicated developer, who stays a Full Circle employee that the VAR cannot poach [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:07:26]. The VAR earns back through a setup and training fee, a minimum monthly charge, module-based usage fees, paid extra training and per-hour customization work [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:08:10]. Preda shows what customization does to price: generic Salesforce ran 25 per seat, while a firm that tailored it for investment banks charged 250 a month for the same basic package and he judged it clearly better [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:05:29]. XAPT later spun off its customization arm into a US-headquartered company of 450 employees, three times the original business [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:07:04].

The day-to-day motion runs on fast qualification, joint deals and adoption follow-through. VAR panelists at Channel Marketing Academy want vendors to hand them ballpark tiers, such as entry, medium and hard implementations at roughly a month and a half, six months and two years, because a customer who will spend $5,000 may balk at $50,000 and the VAR will not risk the embarrassment of presenting an unpriced product [vLO0gA3TgxE @ 00:02:14]. They want a 15-minute pitch and a one-page cheat sheet, not an hour-long demo. Brian Tanney of GetWireless, grown from two people in a basement to over 90 team members in 25 years, describes a VAR-led program being architected with AT&T where VARs bring deals in and either bill the hardware on their own paper or run it on AT&T paper with the buy-down showing on the customer's bill [-gG3jIROESw @ 00:03:27], alongside six state and local contracts and a GSA federal contract [-gG3jIROESw @ 00:02:42]. Atlassian's partner customer-success program checks deployment after purchase and again four months before license expiry; one quarter it flagged 20 at-risk high-volume customers, remediated eight, saved over 250,000 in revenue and lifted renewals 5 percent, and it names failure to go live in production as the top reason customers do not renew [4qYKEjVIAjc @ 00:00:57].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock a niche and vet the platform
   - Choose one segment with high strategic fit and test the vendor against real customer use cases before signing.
   - High performers complete a full strategic review and pick a segment they can dominate [Ridge Consulting — NbukIA2E8Nw @ 00:00:50]; Positive Results builds use cases from its customers and tests function and completeness instead of trusting developer claims [Positive Results — z6rVTIy-FOg @ 00:00:42].
2. Extract the vendor cheat sheet and ballparks
   - Get a 15-minute pitch, a one-page cheat sheet and entry, medium and hard implementation ballparks before presenting.
   - The sheet should list products the vendor works and does not work with, industry sweet spots, minimum requirements and customer examples [Channel Marketing Academy — vLO0gA3TgxE @ 00:03:22], with tiered timelines of roughly a month and a half, six months and two years [Channel Marketing Academy — vLO0gA3TgxE @ 00:01:38].
3. Build the fee stack against vendor costs
   - Price setup and training, a monthly minimum, module usage and hourly customization so they cover every vendor charge.
   - Sutton tells VARs to understand their own costs and set fee structures to hit their profitability goals [Stuart Sutton — yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:05:17], billing per-hour customization to offset the dedicated programming resource [Stuart Sutton — yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:08:25].
4. Drive adoption through to renewal
   - Set up and train new subscribers, follow up hard in the first months, and re-check deployment four months before license expiry.
   - Sutton calls initial setup, training and the first few months critical to long-term subscriber success [Stuart Sutton — yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:04:31]; Atlassian checks deployment after purchase and again four months before the license expires [Summer Zaldivar — 4qYKEjVIAjc @ 00:00:36].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The money question splits the field. Positive Results tells buyers flatly that value-added resellers never charge more for monthly user fees because the VAR is compensated by the software company, and claims most software providers do over 80 percent of their sales through VARs [z6rVTIy-FOg @ 00:02:59]. Eye on Tech frames it the other way: VARs mark up the distributor's product a small amount and cannot control the cost of what they sell [WEqXH4PuF8c @ 00:01:33]. Ben Fox of TC2, speaking on Staying Connected, treats the markup as the actual charge for VAR services and splits it into two mechanics: a straight percentage on the net cost of goods, or a quiet haircut to the OEM discount, where a 60 percent OEM discount becomes 58 percent by the time it reaches the buyer [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:04:10]. Fox stresses that the two sound identical yet hit the invoice differently, and that the least transparent VARs simply quote a net discount per OEM with no visible markup. Full Circle TMS, pitched by Stuart Sutton, runs a fourth model: the vendor bills the VAR a wholesale price and expects the VAR to mark up what it sells [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:07:26].

On margins, the practitioners report opposite worlds. Steve Preda pulled the last public financials of XAPT, the Hungarian Microsoft ERP reseller founded by Zoltan Schwartz: 24 million dollars in revenue and 14 million dollars profit before tax, almost a 60 percent net margin, earned by customizing a commodity Microsoft ERP for Caterpillar and heavy equipment dealers [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:04:18]. Preda's read is that the margin came from inventing the customization, not the license. A second data point comes from Preda's investment banking days: a plain Salesforce seat at 25 a month versus a niche firm charging 250 a month for the same base package customized for investment banks, a 10x price point purely from vertical tailoring [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:05:29]. Ben Fox describes resale as the opposite: good for revenue but a low margin business, which is why systems integrators and telecom carriers wax and wane on pushing it depending on whether they are chasing growth or margin [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:03:05]. Eye on Tech goes further, reporting that VARs are finding it harder to stay in the market as margins, revenue and competitive positions shift, and are migrating toward an MSP role [WEqXH4PuF8c @ 00:02:13].

Identity and territory are the third fault line. Scott Alan Miller of SAM IT holds a hard line: once a VAR, always a VAR, any MSP work is just part of the value add, and a reseller presenting itself purely as an MSP is misleading to the point of being questionably ethical or legal, because the sales side is paid to represent the vendor, not the customer [bek-1YEtW80 @ 00:03:15]. Eye on Tech treats the MSP shift as a survival move rather than a disclosure problem. On customization ownership, the playbooks diverge sharply. XAPT built its own NAXT vertical layer and eventually spun the customization business into a United States company with 450 employees, roughly three times the size of the original business, per Steve Preda [5n6g3xVFV88 @ 00:07:04]. Full Circle TMS closes that path off: any dedicated developer must be a Full Circle employee or contractor billed to the VAR at cost plus a management fee, the vendor owns all IP, and the VAR cannot recruit vendor staff, though it keeps prioritization control [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:05:49]. Ridge Consulting adds the segment question: high performers pick one segment with high strategic fit and, with vendor support, plan tactics to dominate it [NbukIA2E8Nw @ 00:01:06].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Phil Morettini says many software management teams get off on the wrong foot with the VAR channel, and in the worst cases it ends in failure of the company. The core trap: VARs will not create demand, will not spearhead a new marketplace, and will not be motivated solely by high product margins; they push their own high-margin services, so the product has to be the enabler of those services. Morettini's rule is that no one can sell your product if you cannot, so sell directly and successfully first, never take a sale direct to grab a few extra points, and never over-distribute by assigning too many VARs in one local area, because brutal competition strips out the profit margin and the incentive to sell [L1YsH5QkIus @ 00:03:16]. Ridge Consulting documents the reseller-side version: low performers pick a technology, grab multiple market segments without checking long-term fit, and launch short-term marketing, which works until later business and technology cycles leave them in a painful scramble to survive, if not outright disaster [NbukIA2E8Nw @ 00:00:15]. On the Channel Marketing Academy panel, a VAR states they will not even present a vendor that refuses budgetary numbers, because a 50,000 dollar surprise to a 5,000 dollar client is embarrassing [vLO0gA3TgxE @ 00:02:14].

Operational traps hit both buyers and VARs. Sheldon Needle of CTS Guides warns that VARs are often small organizations with heavy turnover, may lack experience in your industry or in applications like EDI, CRM or HR, and some vendors will not back you up by assigning another VAR when one underperforms [2tRDmrgGASk @ 00:00:38]. The worst case is a staff change mid-project that leaves the customer high and dry. Ben Fox lists the fee leakage: import duties, customs clearance fees, import broker fees, exchange rate hedging, extra logistics layers and extra management fees, when the only costs above product plus markup should be hardware shipping and sales taxes [BJPNVL1CIiM @ 00:05:51]. Fox has seen the variation in logistics, shipping and even taxes between VARs exceed the variation in the markup itself, and has caught importation and customs fees disguised as taxes in quotes. Geographic coverage is the other trap: without an in-country entity licensed to sell the product, the customer ends up as importer of record, which defeats the purpose of hiring a VAR. Stuart Sutton's Full Circle model adds on-demand support charges once a VAR exceeds a support threshold, and pulls the license from VARs that fail to generate sufficient business within two years [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:03:59].

Adoption is where renewals die. Atlassian's customer success team, presented by Summer Zaldivar, reports that the primary reason customers do not renew is failing to go live into production; last quarter they flagged 20 high-volume customers at risk, remediated 8 by connecting them with premier support, saved over 250,000 in revenue and lifted the renewal rate by 5 percent [4qYKEjVIAjc @ 00:00:36]. Their ask of partners is to collect feedback at purchase: deployed into production, active users, more to add, business requirements met, blockers, documentation needs; Atlassian then checks back four months before the license expires. Stuart Sutton reaches the same conclusion from the VAR seat: initial setup and training are critical to long-term subscriber success, the first few months are critical, and the VAR must keep following up until the customer runs their entire business on the platform [yasPH-UlTQo @ 00:04:31]. Sutton also tells VARs to charge a minimum monthly amount in case usage fees fall below the cost of cutting an invoice. Positive Results claims the payoff of process-first implementation: never more than a quarter percent annual attrition in over 25 years [z6rVTIy-FOg @ 00:03:18]. AT&T's Shiraz Asan reports the same pattern: when the partners work well together, customers stay much longer and do not turn away [-gG3jIROESw @ 00:02:10].
