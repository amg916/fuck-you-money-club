## The Practitioner Consensus: Banks Are Shut, the Bank Account Is the Credit File, and Every Dollar Needs a Job

Every funding-side operator here starts from the same point: the bank door is mostly shut. Today Capital Group says it plainly. Traditional bank approval rates for small business loans are hovering below 14%, so most founders have to look elsewhere [6tGMlJx13g8 @ 00:00:44]. Alex Shvarts of Unbankable lists what banks actually require now: a 700-plus personal score, an 80-plus business score, two years of consistent profitability, 20-25% down or collateral, debt-to-income under 40%, and a detailed plan with projections. Even then, approvals take 30 to 90 days with no guarantee [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:01:39]. He has watched profitable businesses with strong cash flow get rejected because their credit took a single hit. Revenue-based financing steps into that gap. Joe Camberato, who started funding deals in 2007, says it is the same product as a merchant cash advance. It is written on a purchase and sale agreement and based purely on revenue and cash flow [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:00:00]. It is not a loan in the classic sense. The funder buys a slice of future sales at a discount today and collects it back over time. Camberato credits the product with opening up small-business access to capital that barely existed before alternative lending took off, and with completely changing how business lending works.

Practitioners also agree that underwriting runs on bank statements, not on tax returns or a perfect FICO score. Camberato says that up to $250,000, all you need is the last three to six months of statements, and lenders want one to two years in business. Above $250,000 fewer lenders play, and they start asking for financials [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:00:52]. Shvarts puts the typical revenue-based minimum at about a 550 score, six months in business and $20,000 a month in revenue. The owner fills out one application and sends four months of bank statements. He calls the gap in accessibility versus banks dramatic [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:07:16]. Brian M. Hazel pitches an even lower bar: six months in business, $5,000 or more a month through the business account, and three months of statements. He says his programs are not credit-score driven [e3NbWehFdJs @ 00:00:27]. Marco Carbajo of Business Credit Builders walks through a direct lender with these requirements: one year in business, $200,000 in annual gross deposits, no FICO minimum, and a $3,000 average daily balance. The account can have no more than four negative-balance days a month, and five or more means an automatic decline [L3n2zgxag4E @ 00:05:55]. The common thread is that the bank account works as the credit file. Daily balances and the deposit pattern decide the deal before anyone looks at a credit score.

Operators also agree that capital has to be matched to a specific job before the owner takes it. Enterprise Industry Network says the mistake is treating all funding as the same. A stable company with predictable cash flow fits debt. A high-growth business may consider equity. A company with revenue but uneven cash flow is the natural candidate for revenue-based funding [mXnUmcIc8Ek @ 00:02:28]. Hazel puts numbers on that discipline and gives every borrowed dollar a measurable mission. His rule: $50,000 put into inventory must turn in 45 to 90 days at a margin of at least 35%. If his performance indicators miss five days in a row, he pauses spending and redoes the forecast [e3NbWehFdJs @ 00:02:25]. Shvarts treats revenue-based money as a bridge, not a destination. Many successful businesses use it for immediate needs, then refinance into traditional loans once they have grown and improved their credit profile [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:08:21]. Fractional CFO Robert Cinapri starts earlier, with the cash cycle itself. He tells owners to track which customers are stringing out receivables, which vendors will match payment terms, and how long inventory sits. Then they should work with the bank to build buffers for seasonality, cost spikes and long overseas shipping delays [Ja8jXRIkOK4 @ 00:25:04].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Sizing, Factor Math, Holdbacks and the Lender Box

The sizing math is simple and repeatable. Camberato says lenders pull the last three to six months of deposits and average them. They then advance 100% to 150% of monthly gross revenue, adjusted for average daily balance, time in business, industry, and business and personal credit. Terms run from as short as six months to about 36 months [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:01:22]. Pricing uses a factor rate, not an interest rate. Approve $100,000 at a 1.10 factor and total repayment is $110,000. A 1% to 3% origination fee may also come out of the funded amount and add to the total cost [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:02:24]. The funder then collects through card-sale splits or daily, weekly, biweekly or monthly debits until it has the full purchased amount. Shvarts runs the side-by-side comparison owners actually need. A $100,000 bank loan over five years at 12% means $2,224.44 a month and $33,466.69 in total interest, and the payments are due even in a month with zero sales [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:03:06]. The revenue-based version sells $130,000 of receivables for $100,000 today. At a 10% holdback on $30,000 in monthly sales, the payment is $3,000. Approval and funding both land within 24 to 48 hours, and a month with no sales means nothing is owed for that period [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:03:44].

The holdback percentage is the number that matters day to day, and the quoted ranges differ by shop. Today Capital Group describes repayment as a slice of monthly revenue, usually 4% to 8%, paid until the pre-agreed cap is reached. Payments then stop, and the owner never gives up a single share [6tGMlJx13g8 @ 00:02:03]. Zach Siegel accepts holdbacks from 5% up to 16% and says anything above that puts the deal in the high-risk category [Yq5OojmQIac @ 00:03:17]. Bootstrapp's calculator shows how a flat fee hides an APR that moves. A startup at $250,000 in monthly revenue, growing 3% a month, takes $100,000 with a 6% fee and repays 6% of revenue each month, for $106,000 in total [U0fB6ZY7Cpo @ 00:01:51]. If revenue drops to $120,000, the APR goes down because the same fee is spread over more time. If revenue spikes to $600,000, the advance is paid off in under three months at roughly a 44% APR. At that point, a 9% option would have saved serious money [U0fB6ZY7Cpo @ 00:04:16]. Bootstrapp calls this the core trade-off: time versus the cost of capital. At $25,000 a month, the same advance takes 38 months to repay, which works out far worse for the lender than for the founder.

The details inside real lender requirements get granular. Carbajo's direct lender funds $3,000 to $100,000 on three-to-six-month terms. It runs a soft Experian pull with no FICO minimum and requires a read-only Plaid bank connection instead of uploaded statements. It will take first through third position behind existing advances and gives a 50% discount on outstanding fees for early payoff. It automatically declines cannabis, firearms and nonprofits, and sends agriculture, car and boat dealers, finance and insurance, online stores, real estate and transportation through stricter review [L3n2zgxag4E @ 00:04:25]. Camberato says his platform has over 75 lenders, and he separates three working capital products. Term loans run 12, 24 or 36 months, or up to 10 years through the SBA. Revenue-based advances typically run 12 to 18 months for businesses only six to 12 months old. Revolving lines need a 620 score, two years in business, and a few thousand dollars kept in the account [GwPGzetluZM @ 00:02:14]. Hazel advertises funding from $500 up to $2 million within 48 hours on limited documentation [e3NbWehFdJs @ 00:00:47]. On the receivables side, Matt Dubois says Visa's Product 3 enhanced-data tier can cut interchange by roughly 0.5% to 1.5%. That is $50 to $150 kept on every $10,000 business-card invoice [vPF9bO_izWU @ 00:00:49].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Map the Cash Before Sizing the Ask
   - Build a rolling 13-week forecast of cash in and cash out before requesting any amount.
   - Split inflows into guaranteed and maybe, list what can be cut without hurting sales, and set the amount and repayment date from the forecast. Skip the deal if it will not bridge to higher revenue, seasonal relief, a new contract or an operational fix [Brian M. Hazel Quotes — e3NbWehFdJs @ 00:01:17].
2. Screen the Bank File Like an Underwriter
   - Pull the last three to six months of business bank statements and audit the balances and negative days.
   - Confirm the average daily balance clears $3,000 and there are no more than four negative days a month. Five, six or seven negative-balance days a month means an automatic decline with Carbajo's direct lender, and its Plaid read-only connection must be live [Business Credit — L3n2zgxag4E @ 00:06:10].
3. Route to the Right Working Capital Product
   - Match credit, time in business and amount to a term loan, a revenue-based advance or a revolving line.
   - Use term loans at 12, 24 or 36 months, or SBA out to 10 years, for longer repayment. Use revenue-based advances at 12 to 18 months for businesses six to 12 months old with any credit profile. Use revolving lines only for files with a 620 score and two years in business. Gather financials for any request above $250,000 [Joe Camberato — GwPGzetluZM @ 00:01:57].
4. Price, Stress-Test and Plan the Refinance
   - Convert the factor rate and holdback into repayment scenarios across slow and hot revenue months.
   - Run the flat fee against fast and slow revenue to see how far the effective APR moves. Bootstrapp's 6% fee hits roughly a 44% APR at $600,000 a month and stretches to 38 months at $25,000. If a fast payoff is likely, weigh time against the cost of capital before signing [Bootstrapp — U0fB6ZY7Cpo @ 00:05:12].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Squeezing Cost Out of a Revenue-Based Deal**
> 
> These are the levers practitioners pull to make fast money cheaper and safer to carry once the file is approved.
> - Demand prepayment incentives. Some funders forgive as much as 100% of interest, others take 5-20% off the remaining fees, and an offer with no incentive at all is an instant red flag [Zach Siegel — Yq5OojmQIac @ 00:02:26].
> - Use a revenue-based revolving line as a receivables bridge. Simple-interest, unsecured lines can cost as little as 1% a month: draw to start a job, then pay off the draw when receivables arrive [Zach Siegel — Yq5OojmQIac @ 00:03:37].
> - Tell owners that purchase and sale agreements usually carry no personal guarantee. They typically carry only a performance guarantee that the owner will not divert sales away from the funder [Joe Camberato — U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:03:41].
> - Set up a small line of credit while times are good so it is ready when they get hard. Keep daily balances high and keep a one-page brief covering the need, why now, how you will repay, and the downside plan [Brian M. Hazel Quotes — e3NbWehFdJs @ 00:03:01].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest street-level split is whether revenue-based financing and the merchant cash advance are one product under two labels. Joe Camberato says flatly that revenue-based financing is also known as merchant cash advance, written on a purchase and sale agreement, with the funder collecting through card sales or a daily, weekly, bi-weekly or monthly payment until the purchased amount is back [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:03:10]. Today Capital Group draws the line the other way: the key difference is payment structure, because RBF payments flex with revenue while an MCA typically pulls a fixed daily amount out of the account no matter how much was sold, and a slow week on an MCA can be absolutely brutal [6tGMlJx13g8 @ 00:04:07]. Alex Shvarts of Unbankable sells the flexing version, saying there are no minimum payments and a day with no sales means no payment [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:03:06]. Zach Siegel makes the fixed version his first red flag: if cash gets pulled every weekday, walk away or make sure the money is working toward something better, because one bad day or a few missed payments can trigger overdrafts, defaults and a tanked credit score [Yq5OojmQIac @ 00:00:50]. Read the remittance mechanic before trusting the product name.

Practitioners also split on how to judge price. Camberato frames cost as a factor rate, using $100,000 at a 1.10 factor for $110,000 repaid, plus a possible 1 to 3% origination fee deducted from the advance [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:02:24]. Shvarts concedes revenue-based money is often more expensive up front, models $130,000 of receivables sold for $100,000 today, and argues that paying more for speed and flexibility sometimes costs less once the opportunity cost of a three-month bank wait is counted [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:06:56]. Bootstrapp attacks the same deal from the APR side: on a 6% fee, a company that rockets to $600K a month pays the $100K back in under three months and its APR jumps to roughly 44%, so a founder confident of that result might borrow at 9% elsewhere and save the fee [U0fB6ZY7Cpo @ 00:04:39]. The same model shows APR falling when revenue is slower, because the fee is fixed and simply stretched over more months. Siegel sets the ceiling: a 1.499 factor, meaning $149,900 repaid on $100K, only belongs on a file that already has multiple lenders debiting, and it should never be taken on a first funding [Yq5OojmQIac @ 00:01:47]. Cheap and fast pull in opposite directions.

The widest disagreement is whether to raise outside money at all. Kevin O'Leary and Mark Cuban answer a founder without a wealthy network with the line that sweat equity is the best equity, claiming at least 90 to 95% of small business startups can start without raising any capital, and they tell the founder to keep the day job, drive Uber nights and weekends, and get revenue in before quitting [HKB_cwWcRaE @ 00:00:20]. Taber Small of Prominence Strategic Capital pushes the opposite end: anything under $20 million is barely a private equity deal to family offices, and a $10 million deal takes the same time as a $100 million one, so Small chases bigger structures and bigger investors [2_gq6XRm47I @ 00:23:25]. Today Capital Group frames venture money as selling a permanent 25% slice that shrinks with every round, while RBF leaves the pie 100% yours and adds no new voice to the board [6tGMlJx13g8 @ 00:03:33]. Enterprise Industry Network refuses to crown a winner: debt protects ownership but demands repayment discipline, equity is a control decision as much as a capital decision, and revenue-based funding adds flexibility but can squeeze cash flow if structured carelessly [mXnUmcIc8Ek @ 00:01:33]. Capital has to match the stage.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap sits on the processing statement, not the funding contract. Sam Johnson, presenting on Insightful Accountant, reports that payment companies stuffed Level 3 fields with garbage data to win cheaper interchange, so Visa launched CEDP and began using AI to scrub the data and downgrade false submissions to the worst possible rate [TEwsEYj6XTI @ 00:21:20]. Merchants who believed an automated tool had them qualified watched costs rise and nobody told them, which is why Johnson tells owners to compare current statements against ones from a year ago [TEwsEYj6XTI @ 00:22:57]. Matt Dubois confirms the squeeze from the other side: Level 2 is gone, those transactions have been downgraded to standard rates since April, and placeholder or junk data no longer qualifies for Product 3 [vPF9bO_izWU @ 00:00:49]. The manual fix carries its own bill: one early client saved close to $100,000 a year on Level 3 but kept a full-time administrative employee keying data into every transaction [TEwsEYj6XTI @ 00:46:32]. Johnson also warns that a percentage convenience fee breaks card-brand rules, which require a flat amount tied to real buyer convenience, and that flat-rate pricing inside QuickBooks is usually inflated above an interchange plus model.

On the funding side, money leaks out through fees, guarantees and bank-account behavior rather than headline rates. Siegel reports that some offers turn a $50K loan into $43K after fees, with origination above 10%, and calls any offer without prepayment incentives an instant red flag, since most funders give 5 to 20% off remaining fees or even 100% interest forgiveness for early payoff [Yq5OojmQIac @ 00:02:08]. Marco Carbajo of Business Credit Builders shows how the account itself gets a file killed: the lender Loot requires a Plaid connection with no paper statements accepted, a $3,000 average daily balance, and declines automatically at more than four negative days a month [L3n2zgxag4E @ 00:06:10]. Camberato warns that purchase-and-sale deals usually skip the personal guarantee but can carry a performance guarantee that you will not do anything during repayment to divert sales or money away from the funder [U1zLsMRu1ig @ 00:03:27]. The errors on Shvarts's list are choosing on cost alone, assuming you cannot qualify for a bank loan without actually trying, and waiting too long to explore alternatives after a bank says no [MNdDLiTLoNY @ 00:08:37]. Each of these traps shows up on paper before the money lands.

The hardest losses come from borrowing into a business that cannot convert the money into margin. Brian M. Hazel maps 13 weeks of cash in and out before borrowing and refuses any loan that will not bridge to higher revenue, ease seasonality, fulfill a new contract or fund an operational fix; the payback test is concrete, with $50K into inventory required to turn in 45 to 90 days at 35% margin or better, and if performance indicators miss five days in a row, spending pauses and the forecast is redone [e3NbWehFdJs @ 00:02:25]. Robert Cinapri of CFO Collective names the three symptoms of a business that has outgrown itself: top line growing while profit stays flat, constant cash flow crisis without the working capital to support growth, and an owner burning out [Ja8jXRIkOK4 @ 00:15:58]. Bootstrapp's model shows the downside of RBF when the spend fails: revenue sliding to $25K a month stretches the same $100K payoff to 38 months and leaves the funder waiting three years instead of three months [U0fB6ZY7Cpo @ 00:05:30]. Taber Small adds the broker-side penalty: bring family offices and investment banks deals that do not make sense and they stop answering your phone calls [2_gq6XRm47I @ 00:27:15].
