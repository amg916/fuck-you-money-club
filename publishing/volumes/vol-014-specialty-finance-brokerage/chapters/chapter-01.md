## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one model: the commercial loan broker never lends a dollar of their own money, matches borrowers to the lender whose credit box fits, packages the file, and gets paid when the deal funds. Shane Walton of the Commercial Loan Broker Institute frames the math as funded amount times points, with most products paying 1% to 6% and outliers reaching 15%, so a $2 million loan at 1.5 points pays $30,000. Pay stacks beyond points: upfront packaging deposits, back-end yield spread, correspondent premiums, factoring residuals, and repeat financing events per client. John Neufer prices points on deal size, complexity, borrower hair, and urgency, and targets $5,000 to $10,000 per deal. Sua Truong pushes relationship lending with bank commercial managers, while Bill Rapp sells a 700-lender marketplace as the fix for thin lender benches.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: The Broker Is the Bridge, Paid on Funding

The broker is not the bank. Shane Walton of the Commercial Loan Broker Institute describes the job as a capital consultant who never funds clients out of their own accounts, starts by diagnosing goals, cash flow, and recent financial history, then builds an enticing package and walks it to the one, two, or three lenders whose credit box actually fits that borrower [-HZRdtDpSRo @ 00:02:41]. Walton's analogy is the real estate or insurance broker: they do not own the house or pay the claim, they match the right buyer to the right seller, structure the deal so it works for everybody, and see it through to funding [v3GnrXphwvM @ 00:01:41]. In a separate breakdown Walton sharpens it further: the job is not selling money, it is reducing friction between a real business with a real need and a real capital source, acting as translator, guide, deal designer, and placement professional [FnBYMtrQW9M @ 00:02:06]. Melissa Mueller, a commercial and residential loan officer at Keystone Alliance Mortgage and Capital, adds the street reason the role exists: not all banks finance everything, so finding the lender who lends in the borrower's space and property type is what makes the purchase happen, from a rental single-family to a hundred-unit complex, land, warehouses, restaurants, or auto dealerships [SulpxqBYams @ 00:05:45].

Every speaker ties the paycheck to funding. Walton states that compensation might come from the lender, the borrower, or both on the same transaction, but regardless of who pays it is connected to a successful close [3V8HTzwHS90 @ 00:03:23]. In the Institute's longer business walkthrough the logic is spelled out: the broker helps the borrower find capital and helps the lender find a good client, both sides get value, so often both pay, and the fee is earned at the moment money changes hands [FnBYMtrQW9M @ 00:04:24]. The capital requirement is zero. Walton tells prospects who say they are not bankers and lack $3 million to lend that the broker never puts up a dollar, and that the model rests on four things: knowing loan types well enough to know what fits, packaging cleanly so a lender can read it, holding a lender network to place it with, and guiding the borrower from first call to funding [408PWyXwwrI @ 00:07:24]. The Institute's channel opener compresses the pitch: no inventory, no storefront, deals in the millions, and the person who knows where the money actually is gets paid when the deal funds, and Walton claims the Institute has launched and supported more than 500 independent brokerages whose brokers placed well over $2 billion [68tfdYJmEpw @ 00:00:16].

The third point of agreement is that the fee scales with loan size, not hours. Walton contrasts a $2 million commercial loan at 1.5 points paying $30,000 with a $400,000 home loan at around 1% paying about $4,000, and concludes this industry pays per deal, not per hour and not salaried [v3GnrXphwvM @ 00:04:50]. Walton drives it home with a strip mall: a $3 million retail center at 2% pays $60,000, which equals 120 custom-website sales at $5,000 each and a 10% B2B commission, and argues, drawing on 15 years running their own marketing agency, that the loan takes a bit more effort but not drastically more, because both run the same consultative cycle of discovery, proposal, objections, and close [408PWyXwwrI @ 00:02:10]. John Neufer runs the same arithmetic from the other direction: one point on a $100,000 deal is only $1,000, while one point on a $1 million deal is $10,000, so small deals force more points and big deals leave room to charge fewer [uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:01:39]. Moonshine Capital TV pitches the entry-level version of this consensus, claiming that a few successful deals a month makes $10,000 a month achievable and that commission rates can rise with experience [-uMKPEnEzXI @ 00:00:43].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Points, Fees, Spreads and Stacks

Walton's product rate card, delivered as the Institute's commission ranges by financing type, is the street baseline. Traditional commercial mortgages sit around 1% to 2%, with many traditional CRE plays closer to 1 to 1.5; bridge, private credit, and hard money on the same real estate commonly run 2% to 3%; SBA 7A and 504 loans pay 1% to 2%; and equipment finance commonly lands at 3% to 5% [3V8HTzwHS90 @ 00:05:24]. In the Institute's points-and-splits breakdown the bands widen: conventional bank CRE pays one to two points with one most common, SBA one to three, bridge and hard money two to four, equipment two to six, and short working capital and business term loans anywhere from one to ten points [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:02:49]. Walton defends using 2% in their strip-mall math because it sits at the low end for bridge and the high end for conventional real estate, while equipment can earn 3% and MCAs 10% or more [408PWyXwwrI @ 00:01:13]. Factoring breaks the one-time pattern: the brokerage fee is typically 10% to 15% of the monthly factor fees, paid every time the client pulls on the line, so one placement can pay month after month for the next five years [3V8HTzwHS90 @ 00:07:38].

Lender selection moves the check more than the borrower does. Walton's example is a $1 million revolving line: a bank pays 1%, or $10,000, while private lenders in the Institute's network serving non-bankable clients pay 10% on the same facility, or $100,000 [3V8HTzwHS90 @ 00:07:03]. Yet the Institute warns against chasing headline rates: a clean, bankable $5 million deal at one point pays $50,000, more than three times a riskier $500,000 bridge loan at three points paying $15,000, so the target is total commission dollars [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:04:11]. Front-end money comes before the file is worked. The same breakdown describes a packaging or processing deposit, something like $2,500 on a million-dollar deal, credited against fees at closing, and used as a filter on price-shopping tire kickers; some brokers drop it to $250 or attach a refund clause if they cannot deliver a comparable LOI within 45 days [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:06:29]. A second Institute explainer lays out client-facing commission set by agreement before or at the start of engagement, exclusive or non-exclusive, or an engagement fee such as $10,000 to work the loan plus two points on the financed amount when it funds; in practice it is paid out of escrow at the closing desk [qkeq6lhotvM @ 00:02:02].

The back end is where seasoned brokers double up. The Institute describes lender-facing commission, or yield spread, as negotiated directly between broker and lender, often without the client knowing, and typically wired or sent by ACH a few days after closing as a thank-you for another loan on the books [qkeq6lhotvM @ 00:03:55]. Once a track record proves clean files and performing borrowers, a broker can become a correspondent, get near-wholesale pricing, place borrowers a quarter to a half percent above the lender's par rate, and collect a premium of another one to two points, roughly $20,000 on a million-dollar deal, without charging the borrower more out of pocket [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:09:08]. Splits only leave the independent owner's pocket when someone hands over a deal: on a CPA-referred $600,000 equipment loan at three points, the $18,000 commission pays the CPA a 10% fee of $1,800 and the broker keeps $16,200, which Walton calls the cheapest, highest-quality lead source available [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:07:39]. Sua Truong of CFM adds that on the commercial side, especially unsecured financing, brokers are not regulated like residential mortgage brokers, collect retainer fees, and can get paid on unsecured deals before most people get a townhouse funded [QmriETtG_oE @ 00:14:00].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Run diagnostic intake before quoting anything
   - Build a simple intake checklist and lead with questions instead of products
   - Collect business basics, use of funds, timeline, revenue, cash flow snapshots, collateral, and credit issues, refining the checklist week after week [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — FnBYMtrQW9M @ 00:16:39]. On the call, ask the questions Walton models for a medical practice acquisition: current DSCR, relationship with the existing bank, the numbers on the new location, and what keeps the owner up at night [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — WUTiN5zYjnU @ 00:05:48].
2. Price points on four variables
   - Score deal size, deal type, borrower constraints, and time constraints before quoting points
   - Neufer charges more points on small deals, complex deals with permitting or rehab, difficult borrowers with recent bankruptcies or scattered sources of funds, and time-sensitive files such as maturing notes, and gets more competitive on large, simple, unhurried deals where the borrower has time to shop other brokers [John Neufer — uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:08:33].
3. Lock front-end terms and the packaging deposit
   - Agree commission structure and amount at engagement start and take a credited deposit
   - Set exclusive or non-exclusive terms and the points or engagement fee before or at the start of the engagement [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — qkeq6lhotvM @ 00:00:58]. Take a packaging deposit, roughly $2,500 on a million-dollar file or $250 with a 45-day LOI refund clause, credited at closing so serious borrowers pay nothing extra [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — 7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:05:50].
4. Package, place on fit, and collect both ends
   - Submit to a curated lender bench chosen by actual deal fit, then track front-end and back-end pay
   - Use a curated bench across banks, non-banks, fintechs, and private credit chosen on fit rather than blasting a contact list, with packaging and narrative discipline so lenders trust the data fast [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — MsdnoNJpPrg @ 00:09:37]. Front-end fees pay out of escrow at closing; the lender's yield spread arrives a few days later by wire or ACH [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — qkeq6lhotvM @ 00:04:16].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Pricing is the first fault line. John Neufer refuses any flat fee schedule and prices every file on four variables: deal size, deal type, borrower constraints and time pressure. Neufer tells brokers to charge 'enough points to make it worth your time,' and to load more points onto small loans, because one point on $100,000 is only $1,000 [uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:00:32]. Shane Walton of the Commercial Loan Broker Institute comes at it from the other end. Stop staring at the point number and chase total commission dollars, because a clean $5 million deal at one point pays $50,000, while a riskier $500,000 bridge at three points pays only $15,000 [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:04:11]. Walton's way to grow is to move upstream once capacity is maxed out: larger loans and larger checks instead of more conversations [FnBYMtrQW9M @ 00:14:12]. Neufer's answer is a floor. Close two to five deals a month and clear at least $5,000 to $10,000 per deal, or ask what the point is [uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:10:38]. Both playbooks share one discipline: every file has to justify the hours. They split on where the leverage lives. Neufer squeezes margin out of small, messy, rushed files, while Walton argues the cleaner, bigger, bankable file wins even at a fraction of the rate.

Upfront money splits the camp. Walton's default line is that the broker typically gets paid only when the deal actually funds: 'not just for trying, only for closing the deal' [v3GnrXphwvM @ 00:01:57]. Yet the same Institute teaches charging a packaging or processing deposit, around $2,500 on a million-dollar file, as a filter for tire kickers. The deposit is credited back against fees at closing. Softer operators drop it to $250 or add a refund clause that kicks in if no comparable LOI arrives within 45 days [7OHPVFeEmD4 @ 00:05:11]. A harder version from the Institute's payout breakdown combines both: a $10,000 engagement fee to work the loan, then two points on the financed amount at closing. It can run under an exclusive agreement that locks the client to your lenders for a set period, or as a non-exclusive, success-only deal [qkeq6lhotvM @ 00:02:02]. Sua Truong of CFM goes furthest. On unsecured commercial financing the CFM side collects retainer fees, because commercial brokers are 'not regulated the same way' residential mortgage brokers are, and unsecured deals can pay out before a townhouse even funds [QmriETtG_oE @ 00:14:14]. The practical split is that success-only pay builds trust on clean files, while deposits and retainers protect hours on files that may never close.

Lender access is the third fight. Bill Rapp of CommLoan argues that brokers with only three to ten lender relationships have nowhere to turn after a decline, which means lost commissions and frustrated clients. The CommLoan answer is one submission into a marketplace of more than 700 sources, matched by property type, loan size, DSCR, LTV and geography [WqF62XF9lk4 @ 00:00:54]. Sua Truong runs the opposite playbook, built on people. Truong makes Monday or Tuesday check-in calls to commercial managers to ask where the money is going this week, and books three to five lunches with commercial managers every week. Truong credits that routine with at least one deal a month from a single TD contact [cqOh0arlXGU @ 00:05:27]. Scope draws a second line. Walton once called breadth the broker's real power, citing one broker who placed a real estate purchase, a manufacturer's equipment loan and a restaurant's line of credit in a single month [v3GnrXphwvM @ 00:07:11]. Elsewhere, Walton argues that generalists only survive in stable markets. Selective markets reward depth in a niche: a vertical like healthcare or franchises, a product like SBA or equipment, or a part of the capital stack like bridge, mezz and private credit [MsdnoNJpPrg @ 00:08:32]. Operators have to decide whether breadth or depth is their edge.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

John Neufer's sharpest warning is the goose chase. A borrower on a small file promises more business if the broker cuts points. Neufer's response is to run the first deal as quoted and revisit fees only after new business actually shows up: 'I can't tell you how many times I've been told oh I promise you more business and you never get more business' [uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:02:44]. The second trap is scope creep. A file priced as a simple refinance turns messy with permitting or rehab, and Neufer tells brokers to announce more points mid-deal or walk away, and to set that expectation at the very start [uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:04:38]. Delays caused by the borrower get the same treatment. A client who demands a rush and then sits on documents is not holding up their end of the bargain, so charge more points or walk [uqs40-CCDSc @ 00:09:04]. Walton reaches the same economic truth from the pipeline side. A pipeline full of weak files 'is not an asset, it's a time trash,' and a smaller pipeline of qualified, fundable, well-matched opportunities is worth far more [3V8HTzwHS90 @ 00:11:38]. Both treat unfundable or underpriced files as the silent margin killer, because commission arrives only at funding while hours burn from day one.

Lender capacity kills deals that look fundable on paper. Sua Truong placed seven Subway franchises for one client at TD, then watched the bank refuse the eighth. The portfolio was full of Subways, and the concentration had become high risk. Truong is blunt that the underwriters understood the business fine; the bank simply could not hold any more of it in the portfolio [cqOh0arlXGU @ 00:03:46]. Truong also points out that declines work differently in the two worlds. On the residential side, once an underwriter declines a file it cannot be resubmitted. A commercial deal can go back in after restructuring, so a commercial decline is a prompt to fix the structure rather than a dead file [cqOh0arlXGU @ 00:10:06]. Bill Rapp names the cost of having no alternative when one lender says no: lost commissions, frustrated clients, lower closing ratios and missed referral opportunities [WqF62XF9lk4 @ 00:00:54]. Walton adds that specialized, tech-equipped lenders now say no faster. Brokers who send a stream of marginal or poorly fitting submissions get deprioritized, while curated files actually close [MsdnoNJpPrg @ 00:07:26]. The economics check is simple: relying on one lender puts every commission at risk.

The income math has its own trap. Walton's formula is qualified conversations times close rate times average commission. The classic beginner mistake is scaling conversations before the broker can reliably close: chasing leads, traffic and volume when raising the close rate is the faster path to money. This is how brokers end up busy but broke [FnBYMtrQW9M @ 00:12:02]. The daily reality is firefighting: environmental assessment issues, credit problems and appraisal shortfalls. Income swings from $30,000 one month to $50,000 the next and $5,000 after that, and Walton puts it as a gut check: if that stresses you out, this might not be a fit [WUTiN5zYjnU @ 00:12:24]. Walton is equally direct that commercial deals take weeks, often months, and not every file funds. A pipeline has to be built and tended; it is not a button that spits out money [408PWyXwwrI @ 00:06:29]. Truong flags a structural blocker for residential brokers crossing over. Brokerage owners spread fear because most errors and omissions (E&O) policies do not cover unsecured financing or commercial property financing. The fix is topping up E&O coverage, which Truong calls a little more money that opens the floodgates to mezzanine, bridge, private and working capital files [QmriETtG_oE @ 00:13:12].
