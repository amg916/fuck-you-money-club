## Executive Summary

Practitioners from CLBI, CCTG, CommLoan, Loan Officer Training, and AD Mortgage agree a commercial loan brokerage launches without a license, degree, or bank tenure (California excepted), feeding on bank declines that Derek Brown pegs at 90% of businesses. Deal flow comes from centers of influence (CRE brokers, CPAs, attorneys, hard-money lenders) and relentless follow-up. Street mechanics: 1-10 points, so $2M at 1% pays $20k; USDA B&I lenders charge 2 points and pass 1 to the broker; files are sized on rent roll, P&L, PFS, DSCR, and debt yield; Zevi Shafran collects milestone fees at term sheet and commitment. Ian Tucker banked $49,500 on a 147-rail-car refinance. Disagreements center on cost and focus: launch budgets run $500 to $100k, Kevin Smith says go generalist before niching, while Shafran preaches niches and Bill Rapp warns gross fees are not take-home.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who addressed the entry question lands in the same place: the barrier is skill and relationships, not credentials. Shane Walton of the Commercial Loan Broker Institute says a license, degree, or bank tenure is not required ('But you don't need those things') [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:00:16], while flagging that commercial brokering is generally not state-licensed, with California the noted exception, so the rules vary by state [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:04:07]. Walton's finance-company walkthrough pushes further: 'people have no experience in finance start finance companies day in and day out,' naming truck drivers and salespeople as viable founders and ranking drive, niche experience from CPA, realtor, or attorney careers, and an existing network as the real edge [SrGiDqgbdf8 @ 00:02:56]. Zevi Shafran, who runs a Los Angeles mortgage shop, frames the licensing gap as the reason to pick commercial over residential: residential origination ties the originator to NMLS, since 'you need to get licensed throughout the country because it's attached to your nmls,' whereas commercial deals can be worked nationwide with few state licenses [Asr3dNmXtus @ 00:07:16]. Shafran also points out that at the same roughly 1% rate, commercial pays more simply because the loans are bigger, and commercial refinances recur every 3, 5, or 7 years [Asr3dNmXtus @ 00:06:57].

The second point of agreement is why the brokerage exists at all: traditional lenders say no far more often than borrowers expect, and that decline is the broker's raw material. Walton describes banks as 'narrower and pickier' [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:00:32] and lists the rejection triggers (deals too complex, too small, too risky, or too young) before insisting that 'A bank decline does not necessarily mean that a deal is impossible' and 'Your job is to solve capital problems, not to sell money' [svRIdayYOLo @ 00:04:31]. Derek Brown, a former Wells Fargo SVP with 27 years in banking and a brokerage running since 2007, puts the decline rate bluntly: in the traditional market '90% of the businesses are turned down' [QPWj0c48x1o @ 00:05:15]. Kevin Smith of BMF Advisors in Chattanooga, a former banker, supplies the field proof. A buyer acquiring a glass and painting firm had been declined by two banks and a CDFI over more than six months; Smith placed the file with an SBA preferred lender and closed in roughly 45 days at full price plus working capital [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:12:52]. Smith's explanation for why SBA rescued the deal: 'it's probably one of the only ones you can lend on on projections and and have a collateral shortfalls' [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:12:00].

Relationships are the consensus engine of deal flow, and the speakers converge on the same referral map. CLBI's five-trait screen puts relationships first, because 'relationships are what bring deals to your doorsteps and trust is the currency for getting hard deals done,' followed by an analytical mind, creative problem solving that finds three new routes to yes after a lender no, grit for deals that take months, and a real desire to help, since clients see through commission-chasers [ctWaQk2YDgE @ 00:00:31]. Derek Brown says the old methods of cold calls, bought leads, and mail do not work, and steers launches toward centers of influence: 'my personal favorite is commercial real estate brokers CPAs,' plus bankers, title companies, and attorneys [QPWj0c48x1o @ 00:07:00]. Zevi Shafran adds hard-money lenders who need to reposition their debt, CPAs reviewing client books, and attorneys sitting on bridge-to-takeout deals [SPUcp-lnZ4w @ 00:02:04], and insists 'the money is always in the followup not in the initial contact' [SPUcp-lnZ4w @ 00:09:07]. Kevin Smith is blunter: 'if you don't like talking to people you probably shouldn't be in this business' [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:02:26], and the channel that actually worked for Smith was marketing in person 'with a lot of follow-up' [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:21:27].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The fee math is stated in plain numbers. Walton puts broker compensation at 1 to 10 points [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:10:16] and runs three examples: $2M at 1% pays $20k, a $500k equipment deal at 4% pays $20k, and a $300k working-capital line at 5% pays $15k [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:10:48]. Scaled up, 2 points on $5M is $100k [svRIdayYOLo @ 00:12:34], which is why Walton argues two good deals can produce ten times the revenue of a dozen weak files: 'Busy does not mean productive in our industry' [svRIdayYOLo @ 00:08:50]. Bill Rapp, CCIM, of CommLoan cuts the gross figure down to size: 1% on $3M is $30K, 'But that's also not the same thing as your personal take home income' [TlDScaCI3fE @ 00:05:28], and 'there are no guaranteed commissions and not every loan request closes' [TlDScaCI3fE @ 00:05:45]. USDA Business and Industry deals show a lender-side split: Eric Johnson of North Avenue Capital says the lender charges 2 points and usually hands 1 to the broker, with a 3% USDA fee on the 80% guaranteed portion financed into the loan [UBxQzlMJT7k @ 00:19:27]. One point on $4-5M pays '70 80 000' [UBxQzlMJT7k @ 00:20:02], and a $25M Alabama hospital deal paid a CCTG graduate $250,000 [UBxQzlMJT7k @ 00:03:49].

Packaging is where the practitioners get most specific. Walton's lender checklist is the question set every file must answer: repayment, collateral, cash flow, use of funds, LTV, DSCR, and exit [svRIdayYOLo @ 00:07:05], summed up as 'The story creates understanding in the lender, and the documents create confidence in that story' [svRIdayYOLo @ 00:11:57]. Zevi Shafran sizes up a real-estate deal with three documents before requesting anything else: 'the first one would be the rent roll of the property,' then the P&L 'because that would show you the expenses of the property,' then 'the PFS which is the personal financial statement' [SPUcp-lnZ4w @ 00:06:14]. Bill Rapp adds debt yield (NOI divided by loan) to LTV and DSCR and walks a live gap: a $5M building, a 70% ask of $3.5M, and $350K of NOI 'creates a 10 percent debt yield'; if the lender requires 11%, the supportable loan drops to about $3.18M, a shortfall of more than $300K that pushes the file toward a bridge loan or other lenders [TlDScaCI3fE @ 00:06:00]. On USDA files, Johnson's rule of thumb is that borrowers have 'got to be hitting at 125 or projecting to at least hit a 125,' alongside tangible balance-sheet equity of 10% for existing businesses and 20% for new ones, which need not be cash [UBxQzlMJT7k @ 00:16:40].

Lender access and fee collection round out the launch mechanics. Walton warns that spraying a deal across lenders burns credibility because lenders are wary of unknown brokers [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:06:19], and lists the paperwork that has to exist first: entity, EIN, and broker, fee, engagement, and disclosure agreements [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:03:52]. Beau Eckstein, with over 20 years in lending, says placement has gone digital: 'we put the deal on the platform and it's shared to hundreds of banks,' replacing manual searches for a lender such as a Knoxville credit union, because 'with the technology as a broker we have to evolve' [M48iXqAIzwE @ 00:01:06]. Zevi Shafran's commercial fee workflow is the cash-flow mechanic new brokers miss: once the borrower signs the term sheet, 'you could actually take a milestone payment for your processing and your time,' a second fee lands at the commitment letter, and the lender's third-party report deposit keeps the borrower committed [Asr3dNmXtus @ 00:03:14]. Startup spend is elastic. Walton says 'you can launch with as little as five hundred dollars or you can launch with as much as a hundred thousand dollars,' with roughly $20k the common package, and recommends starting as a broker before evolving into lending because 'this decreases your startup cost as well as your risk' [SrGiDqgbdf8 @ 00:07:24].

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first real split is over what a launch should cost and who should sell it. Commercial Loan Broker Institute prices its full launch platform at $18,750 against a $41,910 a la carte stack, with $9,750 down and $1,500 a month at zero interest [QPWj0c48x1o @ 00:40:43]. Ian Tucker, a Commercial Capital Training Group Gold graduate, says the spend came back within nine months after a $49,500 rail-car refinance and credits lenders that are not really found elsewhere [vakBYx8RoD0 @ 00:05:40]. The Loan Broker Academy pushes hard the other way: much of the training on the market is sold by people who are not working brokers, and the pitch call is built to extract a decision or a deposit before the buyer hangs up [4AAOQcVDMIo @ 00:00:35]. That same Academy still closes with its own offer, leaning on active brokers, a private community, one-to-one field training, and a loan broker playbook [4AAOQcVDMIo @ 00:09:31]. Every voice in this argument sells something, including the critic. The working filter the Academy itself offers is to copy people who are actually doing the work, which means asking any seller for recent funded files, named lenders, and real commission checks before wiring a deposit for a course.

Lender access is the second fault line, and the numbers do not agree. Shane Walton says the Institute network runs to thousands of lenders [SrGiDqgbdf8 @ 00:10:55], while Chris Roglari tells Commercial Capital Training Group enrollees they get set up with 60 lenders, a figure that later drops to 50 in the same session [UBxQzlMJT7k @ 00:02:20]. Beau Eckstein skips the roster debate: deals go onto platforms that share each file with hundreds of banks, replacing the manual hunt for a single credit union in Knoxville, and as a broker you have to evolve with that technology [M48iXqAIzwE @ 00:01:06]. The Loan Broker Academy cuts against all three pitches, arguing the best lenders will not work with inexperienced brokers, because lenders exist to approve loans and make money, not to train anyone [4AAOQcVDMIo @ 00:04:03]. So the operator chooses between three playbooks: a curated lender list bundled with a training company, a marketplace blast that pushes one file to hundreds of banks at once, or slow, earned relationships with specialist lenders who see clean files over time. The headline lender count settles nothing on its own.

The third split is sequencing: niche first or volume first, brand first or market first. Zevi Shafran says the riches are in the niches and builds an officer brand around 50-to-100-unit Los Angeles multifamily, speaking fluently in NOI and debt coverage [SPUcp-lnZ4w @ 00:11:41]. Kevin Smith of BMF Advisors flips the order: start as a generalist, chase the quick-win products, then narrow into one to three niches once deals reveal where the edge actually sits [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:18:14]. On branding, Shane Walton insists a new shop should look like a corporate identity rather than a mom and pop, with logo, site, cards, decks, and social built early [SrGiDqgbdf8 @ 00:09:17]. Smith calls an early fixation on the CRM or the website busyness out of fear, and host Chris watches graduates burn weeks arguing over logo color instead of calling prospects. Even Smith draws a hard floor, though: asking owners to send financials to a broker operating out of a Gmail or Yahoo address is beyond him [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:29:27]. The workable middle is a professional domain and clean basics on day one, then every remaining hour spent in front of referral sources rather than polishing the logo, the color palette, and the deck.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive early mistake is sending the right deal to the wrong lender. Derek Brown, who spent 27 years in banking before opening a brokerage in 2007, says the first three years were a landmine: lost money, getting taken advantage of by other brokers, not knowing how to get paid, and taking deals to the wrong bank [QPWj0c48x1o @ 00:13:54]. Shane Walton names the mechanism: spraying one file across every lender burns credibility fast, because lenders are already wary of brokers they do not know [dIwbKzTxcQk @ 00:06:19]. Bill Rapp explains why the spray fails even on good deals, since lenders specialize by property type and a lender that loves one transaction may likely decline the next one [TlDScaCI3fE @ 00:03:17]. The Loan Broker Academy ties the same pattern to the industry's ridiculously high failure rate, calling the first stretch a minefield for brokers who arrive without lender trust or a track record [4AAOQcVDMIo @ 00:04:22]. The operating fix running through all four accounts is to learn each lender's credit box first, place every file deliberately with a lender whose box it fits, and treat every lender submission as either a reputation deposit or a withdrawal.

Headline commissions hide the real math. Bill Rapp warns that a 1% fee on a $3 million loan is $30,000 gross, which is not the same thing as personal take-home income, and that there are no guaranteed commissions because not every loan request closes [TlDScaCI3fE @ 00:05:28]. Rapp's debt-yield case shows how a deal shrinks under a broker: a $5 million building, a 70% ask of $3.5 million, and $350,000 of NOI produce a 10% debt yield, so a lender demanding 11% supports roughly $3.18 million and leaves a gap above $300,000 to fill with a bridge loan or another lender [TlDScaCI3fE @ 00:06:00]. Shane Walton adds that busy does not mean productive, since two strong deals can generate ten times the revenue of a dozen weak files [svRIdayYOLo @ 00:08:50]. Even showcase numbers need a calculator: the Institute's graduate example lists a $725,000 architectural-firm loan at 2.5% paying two thousand one hundred and twenty dollars, yet 2.5% of that balance is roughly $18,125, and the presenters concede three closings in six months does not happen for everybody [QPWj0c48x1o @ 00:09:51]. Price every pipeline on net, closed dollars, not on gross points quoted on a slide.

Time and clients kill deals as surely as credit does. Host Chris of Commercial Capital Training Group lost a $250 million deal eight months in, and says brokers who try to hold clients to their feet with upfront fees still watch them walk [nxxklr3VSSo @ 00:33:10]. Eric Johnson of North Avenue Capital runs USDA files from screen to pre-flight committee to term sheet to underwriting, with a best case near 90 days and a range of two to three weeks to five months, and the rule in that segment is to respond to anybody within 24 to 48 hours with no excuse [UBxQzlMJT7k @ 00:22:52]. On the DSCR side, the AD Mortgage underwriting team flags the avoidable kickbacks: credit reports older than 60 days at submission, inactive LLCs, missing pages, and unsecured loans used for funds to close [-j6-Ev4L24c @ 00:49:12]. The Loan Broker Academy closes the loop on the human side, warning that success does not care about your emotions and that emotions are what usually make new brokers quit before the first check clears [4AAOQcVDMIo @ 00:08:36]. A dead file, a stale credit report, a lapsed LLC, or a slow reply each costs months of unpaid work.
