## Executive Summary

Equipment finance brokers across ION Capital, Equipment Finance Academy, Credit Suite, Leads For Locals, and Neil.Finance agree on the core seat: a matchmaker who lends no capital, packages an asset-backed deal, and gets paid at close. The shared mechanics are one niche, a short lender list, a complete package on day one, and collateral a lender can resell at liquidation or auction value with no second-position liens. The numbers diverge sharply. Jermane Cheathem builds up to 20 points into the payment and reports 4-5 of 10 weekly dealer applications funding. Josh Feinberg cites 7.5-8% average points and a 3-5% close rate on $50 exclusive leads. Equipment Finance Academy quotes 1-5% and $2,000-$5,000 per deal. The sharpest split is lead source: dealer partnerships and referrals versus bought leads worked with 12-month follow-up.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every camp agrees the broker is a matchmaker, not a lender. Equipment Finance Academy frames the role as a 'guide and matchmaker' who usually never funds a deal and needs no prior experience [nN3wD1zIjqM @ 00:01:07], then repeats it as the 'middleman between Main Street and Wall Street' who lends no capital and risks no cash, telling beginners to expect an awkward first deal and 'do it anyway' [lg-SI4-0oEg @ 00:02:03]. Neil.Finance pitches the same seat from the demand side: banks 'are built to say no,' brokers neither own the asset nor lend, and they get paid when the deal closes because they 'orchestrate the transaction' [YLIvGloANJM @ 00:00:41]. The demand case is consistent across channels. Equipment Finance Academy cites 87% of companies financing equipment and $1.51 trillion financed in 2025, and says owners finance to protect operating cash for payroll and materials while handing the process to brokers because they lack the time to call lenders and chase documents [3PLddU0NhiQ @ 00:00:34]. Neil.Finance adds that equipment is 'one of the most stable forms of lending because it's asset-backed,' with approval criteria that are often more flexible than unsecured bank credit. The operator read is simple: you channel existing demand toward capital that already exists, and your income is a cut of the transaction you orchestrate, paid when it closes.

The second point of agreement is focus. Equipment Finance Academy lists trying to finance everything as mistake number one and tells beginners to pick transport, construction, medical, manufacturing, or first-asset startups [nN3wD1zIjqM @ 00:01:07]. Its shorter beginner cut narrows that to semi-truck, heavy equipment, or medical gear, backed by 2-3 lenders who fund that niche as 'your supply' [lg-SI4-0oEg @ 00:02:23]. Jermane Cheathem, on Credit Suite, niched into medical after 17 years in medical equipment finance and partners with equipment sellers who send him deals, targeting small vendors with 5-10 reps instead of $50M sellers whose bank partners are already in place [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:03:52]. On his own channel he calls this the right way to build: relationships with sellers of trucks, medical equipment, and technology whose buyers already trust them and often carry great credit, versus brokers who 'build backwards' chasing bad-credit owners already shopping multiple lenders [6laX1_643JA @ 00:00:16]. Equipment Finance Academy's referral list overlaps: dealers, manufacturers, contractors, truck dealers, accountants, insurance agents, consultants, commercial real estate professionals, and associations. The consensus is that a referral partner who controls the buyer at the point of sale beats any volume of raw prospecting, and that one niche done well comes before expansion.

Underwriting fundamentals are also shared. ION Capital's coaching call treats equipment as ordinary business funding with no separate application: an intake form, an asset list, three months of business bank statements, and invoices [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:00:20]. The collateral test is resale. Vehicles must be recent models with VINs, equipment must be recent, and the question is whether the lender can resell it after a default. An Alaska client with oil rigs, pages of tractors, gold nuggets, and decent financials was still 'extremely difficult' to place [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:02:54]. Lenders 'do not want to take a second position on an asset,' so an existing UCC filing on the equipment kills the deal, while real estate with significant equity can play differently [jq-MYGn7i3I @ 00:04:16]. Christopher on the same call says lenders price used gear at liquidation or 'fire sale' value and are 'not going to give you fair market value,' with Terry adding auction value [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:09:19]. Equipment Finance Academy's discovery checklist mirrors this: loan or lease, equipment cost, business history, revenue and cash flow, credit, down payment, useful life, new or used, and whether the payment fits [nN3wD1zIjqM @ 00:02:20].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Execution starts with the qualifying question. ION Capital's Ryan says to ask outright whether the client wants financing to purchase equipment or wants to borrow against existing equipment, or the deal goes down a 'rabbit hole' [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:01:45]. After the intake form, asset list, and purpose question, the call runs a funding survey covering business type, seasonality, ownership, and cash flow, and used-equipment asset lists go to 'our equipment guys' before anything is shopped [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:13:17]. Equipment Finance Academy's lead-system video spells out the lender-ready package: legal business name, owner info, time in business, the equipment, the vendor, exact cost, and a signed credit authorization. A complete file can get a decision in 4 hours; anything less lands in the 'more information needed' pile, because 'lenders do not fund conversations' [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:03:08]. Josh Feinberg on Leads For Locals pushes the application onto the live call: taking it by phone converts about 50% of prospects versus about 20% by email, and on MCA deals he stays on the line until the bank statements arrive [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:44:42]. Follow-up runs in a CRM on a fixed cadence: day 1 thank-you and checklist, day 3 vendor invoice, day 7 lock in pre-approval, because 'this business rewards persistence' [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:04:35].

Commission sizing is where speakers publish hard numbers. Jermane Cheathem builds up to 20 points into the payment, so a $100 payment becomes $120, and says a $100k deal pays $20k for under 30 minutes of work. His rule is 'I can always take money out of a deal I can't add money to a deal' [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:19:49]. He claims Section 179 leaves clients with little rate sensitivity, gets cut a check as soon as the client signs, and reports roughly 1,500 deals with only two clawbacks, which trigger on a first-payment default or inside 90 days [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:24:08]. Josh Feinberg reports average points of 7.5-8%, consistent across 10 years within a 3-15 range, and his Equipment Broker School drills buy-rate math of 0.05672 plus 15 points alongside operating versus capital leases [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:16:47]. Equipment Finance Academy quotes 'typically, 1 to 5%' and models $500k at 3% for a $15k check [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:07:20]. Its loan-officer pitch cites Michael Chen earning $4,200 on a $180K excavator in 60 days, which it also gives as the average time to a first deal, and Sam's own deals landing between $2,000 and $5,000 each [zolXG9LvowA @ 00:03:41].

Lead economics are where the math gets explicit. Equipment Finance Academy calls bought shared leads 'a treadmill': $40-80 each, worked by 5 brokers at once, so $800 for 20 leads might close one deal. Its Path A nets about $1,700 after 20 hours, while Path B uses one page, 3 inbound leads, 2 packages, and 1 funding for $2,500 with zero lead cost [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:05:37]. The same video quotes $50,000 at 5% as '$2,550' and then works the math with $2,500. Josh Feinberg's program sells 100% exclusive, round-robin leads with no aged leads and no buckets, live within 24 hours, at $50 each with a 30-lead monthly minimum, $1,500 a month on a 3-month commitment, capped at 500 per brokerage [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:21:25]. He reports an 80% contact rate and a 3-5% close rate, sometimes 6%, meaning 1-2 deals per 30 leads [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:26:35], climbing from 1.5-2% in the first two months to 6-7% by month 12 through follow-up [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:09:14]. Jermane Cheathem's dealer channel runs 10 applications a week with four or five funding, and a 73% approval ratio for scores over 610 with an operating business [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:16:04].

   - Partner with a small equipment dealer whose buyers already trust them, then use that relationship to reach others.
   - Your real work is meeting one equipment seller, then using that dealer relationship to find the next [Jermane Cheathem — 6laX1_643JA @ 00:02:55]. Target vendors with 5-10 reps and open with whether they use financing to sell more equipment [Credit Suite — L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:12:09].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Commission sizing is the widest split on the board. Jermaine, appearing on Credit Suite, prices up to 20 points into the client's payment, so a $100 payment becomes $120, and says a $100,000 deal pays $20,000 for under 30 minutes of work, adding that Section 179 leaves buyers with little rate sensitivity and that 'I can always take money out of a deal I can't add money to a deal' [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:19:49]. Equipment Finance Academy pitches a far tighter band, 'typically, 1 to 5%,' with $500,000 at 3% producing $15,000 [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:07:20]. Josh Feinberg of Everlasting Capital lands in the middle, reporting average points of 7.5 to 8% that have held steady across ten years, inside a working range of 3 to 15 [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:16:47]. The Academy's own named examples sit lower still: Sam's deals ran $2,000 to $5,000 each, and Michael Chen booked $4,200 on a $180,000 excavator [zolXG9LvowA @ 00:03:41]. The operator read is that the point load is a structuring decision, not a fixed fee. Jermaine starts high and can always pull points back out; the Academy frames conservative percentages for beginners. Build your pricing sheet around the lender's buy rate and decide your markup per deal.

Lead sourcing splits three ways. Jermaine Cheathem says most brokers build backwards by chasing bad-credit owners already shopping several lenders, and instead works equipment sellers whose buyers often carry great credit, with the seller introducing the broker to 'a couple select lenders' and no cold calling [6laX1_643JA @ 00:00:16]. When Ty on Credit Suite floated paid ads, Jermaine countered that a partner's ads pulled 100 applications a day with 'not even .01' funding, while Jermaine's own 10 applications a week fund four or five [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:16:04]. Josh Feinberg runs the opposite play: buy 100% exclusive leads at $50 each, minimum 30 a month, and expect a 3 to 5% close rate, roughly one or two deals per 30 leads, on an 80% contact rate [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:26:35]. Equipment Finance Academy rejects shared bought leads, calling $40 to $80 leads worked by five brokers at once 'a treadmill' where $800 for 20 leads might close one deal, and pushes a single niche search page that captures intent like 'excavator financing' [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:01:23]. Pick the channel that matches your temperament and cash: dealer relationships cost time, exclusive leads cost $1,500 a month up front.

Lender access and collateral produce the sharpest contradictions. Josh Feinberg describes a gated market of fewer than 1,000 equipment brokers against 40,000-plus MCA brokers, with lenders demanding two years of financials, credit checks and $500,000 a month in minimum volume [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:14:05]. ION Capital's coaching team claims the opposite experience, adding new lenders through ISO agreements whenever needed: 'no lenders will say no to us they we've never had one turn us down' [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:14:18]. Panel size divides operators too; Jermaine works about three underwriters covering A through D credit plus 20 to 30 vendor relationships, while noting some brokers run 50 lenders [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:06:30]. On the same ION call, Terry argued used semi trailers 'bring pretty good money,' and Ryan and Christopher pushed back that age decides resale and that Terry was 'mixing things up,' with Christopher adding that lenders price at liquidation or 'fire sale' value rather than fair market value [wt92Utt7o6I @ 00:08:23]. The practical split: volume shops face hard lender gates, while ISO-heavy networks report open doors. Run a short, deep panel and send used-equipment asset lists to 'our equipment guys' before quoting anything.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The ION Capital team's most cautionary collateral story came out of Alaska: a client with oil rigs, pages of tractors, photos of gold nuggets and decent financials still proved 'extremely difficult' to place, because lenders could not see a clean resale path on default [4TvufjZUMp8 @ 00:02:54]. The same call flags the lien trap: if a UCC filing already sits on the equipment, 'lenders definitely do not want to take a second position on an asset,' and only real estate with significant equity behaves differently [WfAvAf4wk0k @ 00:04:07]. The structural trap is MCA money for equipment. Christopher pitched a $25,000 to $30,000 revenue company buying at auction with a cash advance, and Ryan shut it down: repayment hits daily or weekly, the client is 'bleeding money,' can default and lose the equipment, and no lender waits six months for a farmer's harvest; 'that's not how a cash advance works' [jq-MYGn7i3I @ 00:11:56]. The cheapest failure to avoid is the first one: ask outright whether the client is buying equipment or borrowing against existing equipment, because skipping that question sends the file down a 'rabbit hole' [DSEdy5KFvBs @ 00:01:45]. Recent model years, VINs on vehicles and a resale test on every asset line screen out dead files early.

Equipment Finance Academy lists six beginner mistakes that kill pipelines: serving every industry, sending incomplete applications, promising approval, skipping follow-up, chasing leads over referral relationships, and leaning on a single course or lender [nN3wD1zIjqM @ 00:08:33]. The incomplete-package problem is mechanical: a lender-ready file with legal business name, owner info, time in business, equipment, vendor, exact cost and signed credit authorization can be decided in four hours, while anything short lands in the 'more information needed' pile, because 'lenders do not fund conversations' [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:03:50]. Josh Feinberg's numbers show what quitting early costs: bought leads close at 1.5 to 2% in the first two months but climb to 6 to 7% by month 12 through follow-up, and Matty adds that a one-to-three-month mindset buys one to three months of longevity, while an ex-roommate still closes deals a year out by calling every 60 days [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:09:14]. The application itself leaks too: Josh and Matty put live phone applications at about 50% completion versus roughly 20% by email, and on MCA files they stay on the call until bank statements arrive [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:44:42]. Log every stalled file with source, equipment, missing documents, lender and stall point.

Run the unit math before buying anything. Equipment Finance Academy's own example states '$50,000 at 5%' as '$2,550' and then works it as $2,500; its Path A of bought leads nets about $1,700 after 20 hours, while Path B, one page producing three inbound leads, two packages and one funding, keeps $2,500 at zero lead cost [dc0_7D431kI @ 00:05:37]. Josh Feinberg's exclusive program runs $50 a lead with a 30-lead monthly floor and a three-month commitment, so a broker is in for $4,500 before going month-to-month, against a reported one to two deals per 30 leads [Oq4y-D6sylo @ 00:21:25]. Commission timing carries its own reversal risk: Jermaine is cut a check the moment paperwork is signed, but clawbacks trigger on a first-payment default or inside 90 days, a hit Jermaine has taken twice across roughly 1,500 deals [L2-ax8GoKVw @ 00:24:08]. Cheryl Tibbs says new brokers quit because nobody handed them lender contacts, approvable deal structuring, or an answer to the vendor question 'Why should I send you my deals?', and that the learning curve cost Tibbs years, money and sleep, which most people never survive [CKRM0VWsZBY @ 00:00:30]. Price every coaching offer and lead package against those failure points.
