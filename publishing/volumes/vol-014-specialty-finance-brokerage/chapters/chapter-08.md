*Eligibility and the Application Process, 504 Owner-Occupancy Rules and Uses, 7(a) vs. 504 for Real Estate and Business Acquisitions, Acquisition Underwriting by a Top Lender, Combined 7(a)/504 Financing up to $10M, Rates and Fees, and Brokering SBA Deals*

## Executive Summary

SBA lending practitioners agree the agency guarantees, banks lend: a 7(a) lender makes a 90% loan with a 75% federal guarantee, while the 504 stacks a 50% bank first, a 40% CDC debenture second, and 10% down, both tied to 51% owner occupancy. Street mechanics are numeric: 7(a) floats at prime plus up to 2.75%, the 25-year fixed debenture sits near 6.2% to 6.3%, owner-user payments run about $6,000 per million, and Trillant Bank wants 1.25 times coverage off filed tax returns with 10% injection on total project. Only about 150 SBA lenders actively close million-dollar deals. Practitioners split on fit: Bill Ebersole says the 504 almost always wins for real estate, while Beau Eckstein currently steers clients to fixed 7(a) options with three-year prepays, and Ryan Smith claims 7(a) carries no prepayment penalty.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same premise: the SBA does not write the check. ClearValue Tax walks through a Chase Bank example in which a $100,000 small business loan carries an $85,000 SBA guarantee, cutting the bank's loss exposure and making it more willing to lend to small businesses [jaczYsctbgs @ 00:00:58]. Ryan Smith of ThinkSBA calls the term SBA loan a misnomer, describing a federal guarantee program that pushes banks to fund businesses that would otherwise fail on time in business, liquidity, repayment ability, or credit history, with the agency covering up to 75% so the bank's default risk is only 25% of outstanding principal [WAbZuTa6KEg @ 00:00:46]. Jeff Hildebrandt, who has run SBA lending since 2009, says the most common misunderstanding in his trainings is that the money comes from the government; the bank lends all of it, the government simply guarantees it, and nobody at the local SBA office answers the phone when a borrower calls [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:33:26]. The operator takeaway is identical across all three: start with a lender, because the lender holds the money, screens the deal, and makes every credit call, while the agency only sits behind the loss [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:34:13].

The practitioners also agree there are two workhorse programs built on different chassis. Ken Rosenthal says most borrowers think there is one SBA loan, when the 7(a) works with a bank or non-bank lender making a 90% loan, the borrower putting 10% down, and the government guaranteeing 75% of that loan [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:15:06]. The 504 splits the stack instead. CDC Small Business Finance describes the standard 50-40-10: a 50% conventional first from what the SBA calls the third-party lender, a 40% SBA second that can never exceed 40%, and 10% borrower injection [5_3hvZIlwhQ @ 00:00:25]. Ryan Smith confirms the anatomy, with a conventional bank holding the first trust deed up to 50% of appraised value and a local Community Development Corporation holding the second on the remaining 40%, each amortizable up to 25 years at a fixed rate [WAbZuTa6KEg @ 00:02:03]. Jeff Hildebrandt draws the use-of-proceeds line hard: 7(a) money can fund any type of business expense, while he estimates 99% of 504 loans are real estate purchases or real estate refinances, even though equipment is technically allowed [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:24:02]. That split drives every structuring decision that follows, from rate type to prepayment exposure to how many underwriters touch the file.

Eligibility on real estate hinges on occupancy, and nobody disputes the line. CDC Small Business Finance says the 504 is built for private, owner-user businesses that will occupy at least 51% of the property [C-omBBP27hg @ 00:00:06]. SBA University adds that on mixed-use buildings the 51% counts hallways, basements, and sometimes customer parking, that borderline properties should be checked with the lender early, and that a 7(a) cannot be used to buy investment property at all [py_IPiHezcw @ 00:00:18]. The second consensus point is that lenders inside one program behave very differently. Ryan Collins of Trillant Bank says the SOP is only the floor, citing a 1.15 times minimum debt service that most lenders sit above, and tells buyers to ask whether a no is institutional policy or a real SBA rule, admitting he used the agency as a scapegoat early in his career because nobody likes saying no [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:02:56] [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:03:44]. Joe Camberato makes the same point from the marketplace side: one bank or non-bank lender may not love restaurants while another does, so a decline from one SBA lender does not mean a decline from the next, which is why shopping the file across lenders is standard practice rather than a last resort [lNnoO9VriRM @ 00:01:16].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Pricing is where the two programs split hardest. Joe Camberato says bank and non-bank lenders build 7(a) rates as prime plus anywhere from zero to a maximum of 2.75%, so an approval at prime plus 2% with prime at 7.5% lands at an effective 9.5%, floating up or down with every Fed move [lNnoO9VriRM @ 00:02:14]. Ken Rosenthal puts the 7(a) norm at prime plus 2.75%, with room up to 3.5%, priced on risk so the stronger the business and credit the lower the spread, and he reminds borrowers that prime ran near 3.5% during COVID before peaking at 8.5% [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:15:26]. The 504 second runs on a different engine: Rosenthal says the debenture is priced off 10-year treasuries, sold as a government-backed bond to Wall Street investors, and is one-size-fits-all at about 6.25% fixed for 25 years whether a borrower barely qualifies or is golden [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:20:09]. Only the 50% first carries risk-based pricing, set by the lender on credit, collateral, and character. Beau Eckstein runs the live blend, pairing a 6.2% debenture with senior debt at prime plus 1% to land somewhere in the sevens [aBEJBn_bUA0 @ 00:01:28], which he says is typically still better than 7(a) pricing [aBEJBn_bUA0 @ 00:00:38].

Underwriting runs on tax returns, not stories. Ryan Collins says an SBA lender must use the IRS-filed returns, so cash pocketed in restaurants, bars, nail salons, or tattoo shops is invisible: if the seller under-reports, the price has to be based on what was reported [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:10:59]. His add-back test is two-part: the expense had to be booked, and it had to hit the return, and his bank wants 1.25 times coverage on the most recent tax return and year-to-date, with the P&L on the same cash or accrual basis as the return [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:24:44]. The 10% injection is on total project, including the guarantee fee, closing costs of 15,000 to 20,000 dollars, and working capital, not purchase price; a HELOC can fund it only if repaid from cash flow outside the business, such as a spouse salary or rental income [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:33:48]. Jeff Hildebrandt structures that 10% as 5% buyer cash plus a 5% seller note on full standby, so on a $1 million purchase the buyer keeps $50,000 for operations [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:13:44]. Ken Rosenthal's lender ladder for owner-user real estate shows how far coverage tolerance stretches: New York Life approves at 1.0, Morgan Stanley wants 1.2, and big banks sit at 1.2 to 1.25 with a rate constant [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:27:32].

Timelines and funding mechanics decide whether a deal closes on the contract date. On the 504, CDC Small Business Finance explains that the SBA second funds only once a month when the SBA sells a bond on Wall Street, so the first-mortgage lender closes a permanent 50% first plus a temporary 40% second to bridge the debenture [5_3hvZIlwhQ @ 00:02:09]. Beau Eckstein says that debenture typically sells 30 to 45 days after closing, or after the certificate of occupancy on a construction deal [aBEJBn_bUA0 @ 00:00:20], while Ken Rosenthal clocks it at about 60 days on a straight purchase, carried in the meantime by an interest-only interim loan, with soft costs such as appraisal, title, and escrow rolled into a total project cost financed at 90% [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:35:04]. Rosenthal warns that once improvements run past roughly 25% of the purchase it becomes a construction loan and the debenture will not fund until the build is done, possibly 18 to 24 months out at an unknown rate [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:36:43]. On the 7(a) side, Ryan Collins quotes 45 to 60 days for acquisitions and closed a CPA buying a CPA firm in 35 days across Christmas because every request came back within three or four hours [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:42:19].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Three-Minute Intake Triage
   - Collect use of proceeds, website, and property address before requesting any documents.
   - Ken Rosenthal screens fit by reviewing the business website and pulling the property on Google Maps or LoopNet, then asks only for the last business and personal return, a personal financial statement, and a business statement with a debt schedule to size the deal in about ten minutes [My SBA Loan Pro Podcast — XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:31:23].
2. Spread the Tax Returns First
   - Underwrite off IRS-filed returns and verify every add-back before quoting a structure.
   - Ryan Collins only credits add-backs that were expensed and hit the return, requires 1.25 times on the latest return and year-to-date, and reconciles returns to IRS transcripts because unfiled seller returns can kill or delay a deal [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — 8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:22:17].
3. Assemble the Complete Package
   - Send business and buyer documents to the lender in one shot.
   - Collins's full package is three years of business returns, recent statements on the same accounting basis, website, LOI, AR/AP aging where relevant, a description of non-ordinary adjustments, plus three years of buyer returns, PFS, resume, and credit score [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — 8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:53:00].
4. Route to a Fitting Lender and Get Paid
   - Match the file to an active lender whose appetite fits, then collect the lender-paid referral fee at funding.
   - Jeff Hildebrandt says the bank pays brokers and agents an SBA-allowed referral fee when the deal funds, not charged to the customer [Commercial Loan Broker Institute — CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:18:58], and Beau Eckstein says most of the time his brokerage is paid by the bank so the service is free to borrowers [Beau Eckstein, Business Ownership Coach — 9w4kgjirJ7g @ 00:00:34].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The biggest split is which SBA program should carry owner-occupied real estate. Bill Ebersole of Community CDC says that for real estate financing the answer almost always is the 504, because it offers fixed rates for twenty years while most 7a loans from a bank float with rising rates [pAuU5b_a7AY @ 00:00:47]. He also warns that some lenders push the 7a on real estate projects even when it is not in the borrower's best interest, because they see it as a more lucrative product for their institution [pAuU5b_a7AY @ 00:02:32]. Beau Eckstein takes the opposite position right now. He quotes a debenture rate of 6.2 plus senior debt at prime plus one, which gives a blended rate somewhere in the sevens. He then argues that the borrower is stuck with a 10-year declining prepay if rates fall into the fives, so he uses lenders that will fix a 7a with only a three-year declining prepayment penalty [aBEJBn_bUA0 @ 00:01:28]. SBA University pushes the 7a even harder for tenants who want to stop renting. It pitches 100% financing with buildout money rolled in and a 25-year fully amortizing term with no balloon [py_IPiHezcw @ 00:00:49]. The choice comes down to hold period versus flexibility: long-term holders lean 504, while value-add and expansion deals lean 7a.

Practitioners split just as hard on rates. Joe Camberato says bank and non-bank SBA lenders price at prime plus zero to a maximum of 2.75%. With prime at seven and a half percent, he tells borrowers to consider the variable, adjusting option to hedge that rates will most likely come down [lNnoO9VriRM @ 00:03:27]. Ken Rosenthal rejects waiting for lower rates. He scores rates like a surf contest: throw out the COVID lows, throw out the 1980s highs, and 10-year treasuries in the fours look fairly normal. On that basis he calls mid-sixes fixed money a great time to buy, because COVID-era rates are not coming back [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:22:11]. The prepayment testimony conflicts outright. Ryan Smith of ThinkSBA tells owners the 7a amortizes up to 10 years with a fixed or floating rate and no prepayment penalty, so they can repay as fast as they want [WAbZuTa6KEg @ 00:01:25]. SBA University and Eckstein both describe a three-year declining prepay on 7a real estate deals. Smith describes the 504 as a bank first of up to 50% of appraised value plus a CDC second on 40%, each amortized up to 25 years at a fixed rate. Eckstein's answer to the conflicting claims is to run a side-by-side comparison on every deal and only then let the client decide [aBEJBn_bUA0 @ 00:00:56].

Equity injection and speed numbers also vary by lender. ClearValue Tax says some SBA loans require 10% to 30% down [jaczYsctbgs @ 00:10:14]. Jeff Hildebrandt at Bank of California typically runs a 90% structure where the 10% can be split into 5% buyer cash and a 5% seller note on full standby. On a $1 million purchase, the buyer hands the seller only $50,000 of the $100,000 injection and keeps the rest for operations [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:13:44]. Ryan Collins of Trillant Bank insists the 10% applies to the total project, not the purchase price. That total includes the guarantee fee, bank closing costs of 15 to 20,000 and working capital. His bank's debt-service floor of 1.25 times also sits above the SOP minimum of 1.15 [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:33:27]. Timelines vary just as widely. ClearValue quotes 2 to 4 weeks for a 7a, while Camberato quotes roughly 90 days at a big bank versus 45 to 60 days through non-bank SBA lenders. Rosenthal reports the widest gap in lender appetite: New York Life will close a deal at 1.0 debt coverage with a 650 FICO, Morgan Stanley wants a 1.2 cushion, and the big banks want 1.2 to 1.25 and add a rate constant [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:27:32]. The SBA treats self-storage as multi-purpose, but his capital sources treat it as single-purpose, so it does not get pricing in the sixes.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Ryan Collins says the deal killer he usually does not see coming is a seller who never filed. Lenders must reconcile the tax returns they receive against IRS transcripts, and more often than people think those returns were never filed or never recognized by the IRS. That either kills the deal or causes a delay while the seller files [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:38:11]. Cash-heavy businesses such as restaurants, bars, nail salons, barbers and tattoo shops are valued only on what appears on the tax return, regardless of the sales report or P&L [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:10:37]. Add-backs fail when the expense was never expensed or never hit the return. Pairing a cash-basis return with an accrual-basis P&L produces large swings in margins, and Collins will not accept that mismatch. He puts a normal timeline at 45 to 60 days, and every gap like this pushes it out. Jeff Hildebrandt adds a deadline trap: under SBA guidelines the seller's returns must be received by June 30. A seller on an October extension either produces drafts or waits, because they cannot take the extension and the SBA loan money at the same time [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:14:49]. On the buyer side, ClearValue Tax treats credit as a requirement, with a minimum generally around 650. The ClearValue speaker reports correcting an error on their own credit report and seeing their score jump 100 points within days [jaczYsctbgs @ 00:11:12].

Choosing the wrong lender is where months disappear. Hildebrandt counts roughly 1,700 SBA-approved banks, about 150 actively lending more than 50 million a year, and about 50 that will finance a business acquisition when the buyer owns no real estate. The local bank that made one $150,000 loan last year will still take the application, work the paperwork for two months and then decline, leaving the buyer convinced they cannot get an SBA loan [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:07:35]. He also blames business brokers who win listings by quoting the highest price, setting a value of a million on a business that brings in $200,000, when only the cash flow on the seller's tax returns supports the loan. His fix is to have a qualified SBA lender run three years of returns through its spreadsheet and solve for the maximum loan amount before pricing [CvS7BttXuu0 @ 00:20:52]. Collins describes the broker trap from the credit desk. An incomplete package combined with pressure for an answer gets an automatic no. Files are sorted into A, B and C buckets, and C means he'll get to it, which never happens [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:52:22]. Community CDC warns that many banks lack 504 expertise. Camberato adds that big-name banks often give little help, and a 30-, 60- or 90-day process can end in a decline [lNnoO9VriRM @ 00:05:21].

Some traps only hit after closing. Rosenthal watched borrowers who bought real estate on quarterly-adjustable 7a loans at COVID-era prime near three and a half percent ride prime up to eight and a half. Their effective rates reached about 10% to 11%, and they called him to refinance into the 504 [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:13:57]. He warns that with ground-up construction, the debenture does not fund until construction is finished, often 18 to 24 months later, so the rate is unknown and probably higher. He steers buyers toward existing buildings that need only moderate improvements [XKIBsDDkBUI @ 00:37:36]. SBA University flags environmental risk. A phase one tests soil samples, and contamination triggers a phase two to remediate it. Buyer and seller then negotiate who pays, and the loan typically cannot close until the fix is done [py_IPiHezcw @ 00:03:38]. Collins describes how a deal unravels when terms keep moving. The purchase type, price and seller note keep changing, the financials expire, and performance lags until the business no longer cash flows the proposed price. If a stable business drops sharply in Q4, the buyer is paying the same price for the business at its worst [8-4I4VOIIqg @ 00:45:32]. Hildebrandt adds that the new $10 million combined cap affects maybe half of 1% of borrowers, since the average SBA loan is about $600,000.
