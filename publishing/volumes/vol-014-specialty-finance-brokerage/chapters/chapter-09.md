# Chapter 09: Merchant Cash Advances: ISO Operations and Debt Restructuring

*MCA Warning Signs, ISO Training and Funder Updates, Stacked Positions, UCC Liens, Default Consequences, Consolidation, Settlement and Restructuring, SBA Refinancing and New SBA Rules, Bankruptcy, and Court Reclassification of MCAs as Loans*

## Executive Summary

MCA brokers, restructuring attorneys, a CRO and ISO trainers all describe the same trap. The UCC-1 filed at funding blocks better credit, so owners stack second, third and up to seventh positions. Combined pulls then reach thirty to forty percent of daily receipts. The shared playbook starts with two moves: stop stacking, and build a debt schedule from the actual funder contracts. From there the owner climbs a takeout ladder. Conventional or SBA refinance comes first if credit and DSCR allow. Asset-backed takeouts come next. Everyone else goes to attorney-led restructures that promise 50 to 80 percent monthly payment relief. Factor rates run 1.14 to 1.45, and settlement shops charge 15 to 30 percent. The practitioners split on SBA. The Approved Guy reports that new SBA rules shut 7(a) MCA refinances. ProjectionHub says SBA permits them but lenders walk away from payoffs that move every day. Nearly everyone rejects reverse consolidation.

## The Practitioner Consensus: The UCC Trap, the Stacking Spiral and the Exit Ladder

Every practitioner who works MCA files describes the same trap door. Once the advance funds, the funder files a UCC filing. Sebastian Boore of The Approved Guy says that filing makes it very difficult to get a better product, so the only offer left is another advance. He has seen up to seven positions stacked against one business [8x-cfQhQpPU @ 00:01:03]. Meredith at Berkshire Financial Services calls it a blanket lien over equipment, inventory, bank accounts and especially future receivables. That lien blocks new financing, lets funders freeze accounts, and gives them a pretext to contact customers and reroute payments [V8S0sywozwU @ 00:00:15]. Mick Wadley of Gap Funding maps the spiral step by step. The first advance covers payroll, a repair or an equipment purchase. The daily pull eats more cash than expected, so a second advance covers the gap the first one created, and then a third follows. Once combined withdrawals hit thirty to forty percent of daily card receipts, most businesses cannot cover rent, payroll and inventory [OWlnOeMGBkg @ 00:01:18]. The Parkins & Rubio attorneys define stacking as using a new MCA to pay off an old one. They read it as a sign of a repeated cash flow problem, not a short-term hiccup. They add that stacking is a refinancing fact that pushes a court toward treating the deal as a loan [A8iNT8Ctrhk @ 00:07:10].

The speakers also agree on how the product gets sold. Boore says boiler-room brokers and direct lenders market all day to businesses that have good credit and no business taking an MCA. The pitch is that there is no interest rate, just a factor rate of 1.35 or 1.45, sometimes 1.25. The advance is 80 to 90 percent of the average monthly statement, repaid by daily or weekly debits over three to twelve months [8x-cfQhQpPU @ 00:01:25]. Zach Siegel of Small Biz Heroes adds that most brokers blowing up the phone sell only that one product, and many hold no license. Their standard hook is: make payments for a month or two and you will qualify for a line of credit. That line rarely shows up. The broker ghosts or offers one more short-term position [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:01:36]. David Rutz, founder of David Allen Capital, told his ISO force about a construction client whose prior broker placed four advances on the same day for the commission. None of the four lenders was told about the others. The client was left sending 40 percent of sales to payments, about 80,000 a month out of 200,000 in revenue [i0yXJe_x34I @ 00:26:34]. Zoryn Capital's operator states one rule that separates winners from casualties. The advance has to be profit-based: take it only if it makes you money, and never to pay bills or survive [ORF9Ue9ge8A @ 00:06:23].

Before any exit, the agreed first move is to stop adding positions. Siegel's rule: if you carry more than three MCAs, take no new ones while the fix is underway, because every new position makes the business harder to save. From that point the goal is fewer, cheaper and slower payments, not shorter, faster and more expensive ones [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:03:16]. The practitioners rank the exits in the same order. Boore lists an SBA 7(a), a business term loan or a line of credit for owners who have good credit and time. Everyone else gets an attorney-led MCA restructure [dDwHOnFlzaA @ 00:01:08]. Michael Granados calls refinance or consolidation into a new loan the fastest route. It takes 10 to 15 days, and up to 30 days of underwriting when a bank is involved. It only works if the owner clears the credit score and debt service coverage hurdles; otherwise he pushes restructuring [lBvWGt7bnCw @ 00:00:14]. Wadley's matrix lands in the same place. If revenue is solid and the problem is payment structure, roll the balances into a term loan, HELOC, line of credit or 0% cards. If a UCC enforcement action, confession of judgment or lawsuit is already live, talk to a business attorney first [OWlnOeMGBkg @ 00:07:44]. Granados, Siegel and Wadley all treat reverse consolidation as a non-exit that stacks more expensive debt on top.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools and Numbers: Sizing, Takeouts, Restructures and Legal Levers

The speakers give the sizing and pricing math in hard numbers. Zoryn Capital's formula is that average monthly sales roughly equals the average approval, so 50k a month in revenue means about a 50k advance. Factor rates run 1.14 to 1.45, with the average around 1.27 to 1.3. At 1.2, borrowing 100 dollars costs a fixed 120, whether you pay early or not [ORF9Ue9ge8A @ 00:03:39]. Zoryn puts typical payback at 6 to 13 months, averaging 6. Across the market terms span 3 to 24 months, and risky files sometimes get 1 month [ORF9Ue9ge8A @ 00:07:30]. Compressing the term drives the effective cost up fast. Charles Rubio has seen APRs of 100 percent or more, and 200 to 250 percent on some facilities [A8iNT8Ctrhk @ 00:14:42]. Neil Goldstein, the CRO who appears on Corporate Bankruptcy A to Z, says most advances sit between 60 and 90 percent interest. Loans, by contrast, have to stay within usury limits [50wAPhzWGmY @ 00:04:42]. Zoryn's profit test runs on the same numbers. Borrow 100 at 1.2, earn 300 in margin after cost of goods, and keep 180, which he calls effectively free money. He has seen both outcomes in his own high-end fashion company. Holiday inventory advances made a killing. Advances taken years later just to pay bills helped sink the business.

Each refinance route comes with specific qualification numbers. Granados lists credit floors of 660-plus FICO, 680-plus or 640-plus across all three consumer bureaus. He also wants a debt service coverage ratio of preferably 1.25x, with 1.0 workable when other factors carry the file: average daily balances, NSFs, negative days and deposit frequency [lBvWGt7bnCw @ 00:00:52]. Siegel's bar for conventional financing is higher: 700-plus credit, net profitability, time in business and securable assets. Below that bar he uses asset-backed takeouts: business-use HELOCs on terms up to 30 years, receivables facilities or invoice factoring, sale-leasebacks on equipment owned free and clear, and stacked unsecured five-year term loans with a co-signer if needed [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:07:10]. Boore's showcase file had five MCAs totaling over 1.5 million dollars, with payments above 300,000 a month. He refinanced it into a 10-year SBA 7(a) at prime plus 2.75 to 3 percent [8x-cfQhQpPU @ 00:03:30]. Adam Hookima of ProjectionHub explains why that is so hard. The SBA requires a 10 percent improvement in monthly cash flow, which MCA files usually clear. The problem is that the SBA lender needs first UCC position and a fixed payoff, and the MCA balance moves every day. So most SBA lenders simply refuse the file [_V39MGbzjw0 @ 00:01:21].

Restructuring and settlement come with their own price sheet. Granados says a restructure cuts total monthly outflow by an average of 50 to 75 percent and reduces face value by more than 25 percent on average. It runs as a six-plus month program, not an overnight exit [lBvWGt7bnCw @ 00:02:16]. Settlement shops price differently. Wadley reports fees of 15 to 30 percent of enrolled or settled debt, with typical outcomes landing at 40 to 65 percent of the balance and no funder obligated to settle. He adds that the FTC rule generally bars a debt relief company from collecting a fee until the debt is settled and a payment has been made [OWlnOeMGBkg @ 00:05:33]. Siegel puts most consolidator fees at 15 to 25 percent of the book, or 15,000 to 25,000 dollars to consolidate 100,000 in advances. Before an owner signs, he wants to see zero balance letters and proof that past clients' funders were actually paid [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:13:49]. The legal lever underneath all of this is getting the advance recharacterized as a loan. Goldstein walks through five factors the Southern District of New York bankruptcy court used: the provider bore no real risk; it knew the end date; it relied on personal guarantees and acceleration clauses; it drafted daily regardless of sales; and it liened all payments. He says each added factor strengthens the owner's ability to fight the usurious rate [50wAPhzWGmY @ 00:08:25].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the Stack and Build the Real Debt Schedule
   - Stop all new positions and put every live MCA on paper from the funder contracts.
   - List how many positions there are, the balance left on each, the daily or weekly payment, and the true remaining payback. Use the funder's agreements, not the broker's paperwork, because you cannot build an exit around numbers the owner is hiding from [Zach Siegel — w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:04:14].
2. Assemble the Restructure Intake Package
   - Collect the minimum file a restructure desk needs to start negotiating.
   - Gather a one-page application, the current debt schedule, month-to-date and last-month business bank statements, the owner's driver's license front and back, and the original MCA agreements, all without alerting the funders [Zeus Commercial Capital | Business & RE Loans — OccRWwWbI2M @ 00:01:55].
3. Run the Takeout Ladder from Cheapest to Most Available
   - Test conventional and SBA first, then asset-backed and specialist refinance products.
   - If SBA lenders balk at the moving payoff, stairstep instead. Go through specialist lenders whose lawyers pay off the MCA funders and take first UCC position, move the client into a term loan, and build toward SBA later [ProjectionHub — _V39MGbzjw0 @ 00:02:30].
4. Route Non-Qualifiers to an Attorney-Led Restructure
   - When refinance is off the table, place the file with attorneys who negotiate payment reductions.
   - The attorneys negotiate the monthly payments down 50 to 80 percent, the owner pays the debt back in full, and the file ends with a zero balance letter that clears the UCC filing [The Approved Guy — spCn3Rbs4XY @ 00:00:59].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Moves the Restructure Desks and Honest ISOs Actually Run**
> 
> Four field-level moves that change the outcome of an MCA exit file before a single payoff letter is requested.
> - Never ask the MCA funder for a copy of the agreement, because that tips them off. Pull the originals from the client's own records and work around the funder if needed [Zeus Commercial Capital | Business & RE Loans — OccRWwWbI2M @ 00:02:10].
> - Audit the UCC-1 itself. One typo in the business name, or a DBA used instead of the legal entity, can invalidate the lien. If the deal was framed as a sale, the funder may have had no right to file a security interest at all [Berkshire Financial Services LLC — V8S0sywozwU @ 00:00:47].
> - Use the withholding percentage. If revenue drops while the fixed payment stays the same, the funder is only entitled to a smaller payment. The trade-off is that good funders may agree but refuse to fund that merchant again [Zach Siegel — w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:10:22].
> - When one lender will not cover the full ask, build a disclosed stack. Rutz's team got two lenders on the phone to split a 350,000 dollar request into 100,000 and 250,000, with payments sized so the owner could afford them [MM BUSINESS SOLUTIONS — i0yXJe_x34I @ 00:30:41].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The SBA 7(a) takeout is the sharpest split on the street. The Approved Guy reports one of the biggest wins on record: a client carrying five advances worth over $1.5 million, with weekly payments of over $300,000 a month, refinanced into a 10-year SBA 7(a) at prime plus 2.75 to 3 percent [8x-cfQhQpPU @ 00:03:30]. Later, the same operator delivered what he called bad news. New SBA rules disqualify businesses with merchant cash advances from using a 7(a) to refinance or consolidate that debt. That leaves the owner two choices: bite the bullet and pay, or bring in an attorney-led restructure [spCn3Rbs4XY @ 00:00:00]. Adam Hookima of ProjectionHub sees the problem as mechanical rather than regulatory. SBA refinancing needs a 10 percent monthly cash-flow improvement, which MCA debt clears easily. The trouble is that the SBA lender demands first-position UCC and a fixed payoff figure, while the MCA balance moves every day, so most SBA lenders simply refuse the hassle [_V39MGbzjw0 @ 00:01:37]. His alternative is a stairstep: a specialist lender pays off the funders, takes first position, and moves the owner toward SBA later. Zach Siegel sets the conventional bar higher still. Before a real term loan or line of credit can buy out the positions, he looks for 700-plus credit, net profitability, long time in business, and securable assets [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:05:08].

When no takeout loan exists, the operators split three ways. Michael Granados ranks the options bluntly. A reverse debt consolidation is just adding more fuel to the flame. A debt restructure is the option he recommends most, dropping 40 to 75 percent plus on average of what goes back to the lenders. Settlement comes last [WX8DT-NQK5w @ 00:01:02]. In his longer breakdown, Granados puts the restructure at a 50 to 75 percent cut to monthly outflow and more than 25 percent off face value. He also warns that it runs as a 6-plus month program, so the owner is not out right away [lBvWGt7bnCw @ 00:01:55]. The Approved Guy defends the opposite trade. His non-SBA refinance products roll two to seven stacked advances into one monthly payment over one to two years, freeing what he describes as 50 or more in new cash flow immediately. He admits it will not save money in the long run, because the owner ends up paying the same amount [8x-cfQhQpPU @ 00:04:32]. Zach Siegel draws a hard line on reverse consolidations. He calls many of them an expensive advance wearing a rescue costume: they pump money into the account to cover daily and weekly pulls, then take it right back during the week [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:08:35].

The third fault line is who negotiates, and from what leverage. Zach Siegel admits his own funders will be angry with him, then tells owners to go straight back to the original funder anyway. An empty bank account collects them nothing. And the withholding percentage in the contract means a revenue drop entitles the funder to less. The catch is that cutting payments can kill future MCA eligibility with that shop [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:10:22]. Thomas at Zeus Commercial Capital runs the opposite play. His attorney-led team charges nothing upfront and wants the original agreements in hand. He tells owners never to call the funder for a copy, because that tips the funder off [OccRWwWbI2M @ 00:02:10]. Rise Alliance dismisses negotiation-only shops, arguing that payment reductions work only if every MCA agrees. Instead, it leans on the contractual right to reconciliation and an Article 9 restructuring through its parent company, Second Wind [BcdnIkuJ7Fc @ 00:01:31]. Parkins & Rubio push the heaviest lever. A Chapter 11 plan can take a balance owed in 12 weekly installments over three months and stretch it into monthly payments over four years. If the claim is treated as secured debt, the rate drops to roughly prime plus 1 to 3 percent [A8iNT8Ctrhk @ 00:14:23].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Stacking is the failure every operator has watched firsthand. The Approved Guy reports seeing up to seven advances stacked against one business, each with its own UCC filing. The first UCC blocks better products, so the owner is fed another advance as the only exit [8x-cfQhQpPU @ 00:01:03]. Mick Wadley of Gap Funding puts the breaking point at combined withdrawals of 30 to 40 percent of daily card receipts. Past that line, most businesses cannot cover rent, payroll, and inventory. He also cites a single Chapter 11 filing carrying 21 stacked MCAs totaling $3.6 million [OWlnOeMGBkg @ 00:01:35]. David Rutz of David Allen Capital, training his own ISO force, describes a local construction owner hurt by another broker. Chasing a big commission, that broker put the owner into four loans on the same day without disclosing them to the lenders. The business now sends 40 percent of sales, about $80,000 of $200,000 monthly, to repayments. It still cannot qualify for new money because it also carries an SBA loan [i0yXJe_x34I @ 00:26:34]. Zach Siegel names the hook that starts the spiral. A broker promises that a month or two of payments will qualify the owner for a real line or term loan, and that second part usually never shows up [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:01:53].

Default and the wrong relief shop are where owners get hurt fastest. Rise Alliance warns that default is a trigger, not a slow burn. One missed payment can bring UCC 9-406 notices telling customers to pay the MCA directly, sweeps of operating accounts, and lawsuits within weeks. It only takes one aggressive funder to collapse the whole revenue system while the others are still negotiating [BcdnIkuJ7Fc @ 00:00:44]. Zach Siegel lays out the predatory settlement playbook in detail. It starts with big upfront or monthly fees and an instruction to stop paying every funder at once. The resulting default triggers lawsuits, frozen accounts, and liens on receivables or card processing. Fees run 15 to 25 percent of the consolidated amount, which means $15,000 to $25,000 on $100,000 [w-ETPb-Trek @ 00:12:39]. Gap Funding quotes industry fees of 15 to 30 percent of enrolled or settled debt, with payoff outcomes landing at 40 to 65 percent of the balance. It also flags confession-of-judgment clauses that let a funder obtain a court judgment before the business gets to argue [OWlnOeMGBkg @ 00:05:18]. Thomas at Zeus Commercial Capital sees the operational end state. Some of his clients hold 5 to 15 advances and have closed accounts and opened new ones, because they had to choose between making payroll and paying the MCA lender [OccRWwWbI2M @ 00:00:33].

The economics check comes down to two questions: does the money earn more than it costs, and is the paper really a loan? Zoryn Capital's founder ran a high-end fashion company in the early 2010s. It made a killing borrowing this product against holiday inventory. A few years later, the company took the same product just to survive and pay bills, and he names that as one reason it went under [ORF9Ue9ge8A @ 00:08:57]. His rule follows from that: factor rates run 1.14 to 1.45, so if the advance will not clearly make money, do not take it. Parkins & Rubio report APRs on these facilities easily reaching 100 percent, with 200 to 250 percent seen. That is why stacking itself becomes evidence of a disguised refinancing loan in usury fights [A8iNT8Ctrhk @ 00:14:42]. Neil Goldstein of Corporate Bankruptcy A to Z walks through the five factors the Southern District of New York court weighed, from no real funder risk to daily drafts regardless of sales. Drawing on more than 30 MCA cases, he still cautions that no two were alike and that funders fight like the devil to get paid [50wAPhzWGmY @ 00:13:57]. Berkshire Financial adds a cheap audit: one typo in the business name, or a DBA used instead of the legal entity, can invalidate the lien [V8S0sywozwU @ 00:00:31].

> **OPERATOR GATE — Gate Before Any MCA Exit Move Goes Live**
> 
> Four checks the practitioners run before a restructure, refinance, settlement, or default strategy touches a single funder.
