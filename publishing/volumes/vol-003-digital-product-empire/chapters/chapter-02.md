# Chapter 02: Build Products with AI: PDFs, eBooks, and Apps

*Claude and Genspark tutorials, rapid product creation, AI-assisted PDF guides and eBooks, no-code app building, and from-scratch business experiments*

## Executive Summary

The practitioners repeatedly start with a specific buyer and painful problem, then use AI to accelerate drafting, design, or software construction. Nathan Nazareth puts the offer before the product; Brian Moran narrows ebooks to one problem and person. Claude, ChatGPT, Canva, Typeset, Genspark, Base44, and Eden support different builds, while human editing remains part of the demonstrated work. Pricing diverges: Baddie In Business suggests $9–$27 beginner downloads, Richard Yu argues for $27–$47 PDFs, and app demonstrations introduce recurring billing. Distribution also splits between marketplace catalogs, owned storefronts, organic content, and creator partnerships. The sharpest disagreements concern generic information versus reusable workflows, manual design versus generated layouts, and cheap downloads versus implementation products. Moran's failed broad ebook and Leon's difficult creator outreach expose bottlenecks beyond creation.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with the buyer's problem, not a blank document and a demand for an ebook. Nathan Nazareth puts the offer before the product and contrasts a generic time-management course with one aimed at outbound B2B sales representatives. Their audience research reaches into an ordinary Tuesday, private aspirations, previous failed attempts, and the buyer's doubts about personal capability. The offer must make the destination, safety, feasibility, and speed clear before creation begins [6cm2o8zzuNw @ 00:01:39]. Brian Moran arrived at similar specificity after a broad baseball ebook failed. Their rule is one problem, one person, one sales page, and one channel, with a clear title. 'How to Hit a Curveball' for eight-to-twelve-year-olds is more precise than a ten-step hitting system aimed across childhood; the paying reader is a parent or coach [3iU4a1yWGn0 @ 00:03:14]. Richard Yu turns that research into an AI listening pass: collect fifty to one hundred comments, give them to Claude, and extract recurring pain and exact customer language. Their demand check looks for small accounts whose posts exceed twice their follower count, then compares five to ten posts. Yu explicitly moves this work ahead of writing, describing thirty to sixty minutes of validation as protection against months spent building the wrong thing [rYUS7ibZhYo @ 00:08:06].

AI supplies a draft; these creators still describe a human shaping the teaching. AMBER SHARNIECE starts with personal knowledge, experience, topics, and a specified voice rather than a bare request for a book. Their YouTube-growth example asks for a fun, sassy, best-friend-but-professional style, then moves from outline to chapter content, title, and synopsis. They explicitly call the quick example insufficiently developed for a real ebook and prescribe fact-checking, editing, and added information [UuQ3BacwIMU @ 00:06:33]. Monica Lee likewise requests an outline first, checks the chapters and bullets, and drafts chapter by chapter. They paste the writing into Google Docs or phone Notes before design, revise unwanted wording and emojis, and retain that text as a reference for later marketing [VoaJoVQaSig @ 00:05:16]. Baddie In Business calls Claude's output a foundation: read it, improve it, add examples, and request rewrites before selling. Their financial-organization product combines emotional-spending triggers, budgeting systems, savings, habits, checklists, and action steps rather than stopping at an attractive title [YS1HBQAWWVk @ 00:03:54]. Eden explains the same human role through expertise: people need experience to recognize good output, ask useful questions, and judge competing systems. They describe learning through creators over two or three months and practicing, rather than outsourcing that judgment to a model [VJ-JR7uMY34 @ 00:00:00].

The shared product mechanic is a usable solution, although the deliverable changes sharply between operators. Money Talk With Leon sells PDF guides around problems stressful enough that customers will pay for guided relief. Their research tool produces guide ideas, while Facebook groups and Reddit provide manual alternatives; a selected title and subtitle become a Claude request for an exportable PDF [B8Z2fA5X0J4 @ 00:01:32]. Emmanuel Adanu instead packages an AI task they already know how to execute. For a thumbnail workflow, they ask the existing ChatGPT conversation to summarize the steps, prompts, and supplied assets, save the summary as a PDF, and attach it to a new product-building conversation. They review the resulting concept, contents, packaging, and sales copy before separating the assets [_YY-8C1VR9o @ 00:03:20]. Alek's downloadable-product course focuses on evergreen, enjoyable, impulse-buyable products priced at five to twenty-five dollars, with enough scope for hundreds of variations. Research starts with buyer searches and three to five successful shops [T-gfoXzz8k8 @ 00:06:36]. Eden adds another delivery shape: lessons with duplicable resources, notes, and an accompanying AI chat. Their life-reset example separates a teaching framework from a guided protocol, making the AI implementation tool central rather than treating the product as only a static document [VJ-JR7uMY34 @ 00:09:56].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

For a straightforward guide, Richard Yu demonstrates a Claude request for a remote-worker focus ebook containing a title, introduction, seven actionable chapters, a daily checklist, and a finished downloadable PDF. They describe the generated result as ninety percent complete in two minutes or less, then reserve the final ten percent for reading, quality control, voice, examples, frameworks, and tips. Free Canva supplies a cover and colors [kxg_sn2vJm8 @ 00:05:43]. AMBER SHARNIECE takes the template-first route: choose a complete nine-page Canva ebook template, apply all pages, and change images, fonts, and colors rather than constructing a layout from scratch [UuQ3BacwIMU @ 00:04:58]. Brian Moran's alternative starts with text in Google Docs, downloads it as Microsoft Word, and imports it through Typeset's Create and Document/ebook controls. Typeset handles pagination; Moran adds photos, charts, graphs, page numbers, brand colors, and theme changes, then exports a portrait PDF. Their slash-menu upgrade button links readers onward, while the same material can also become images, social posts, or a landscape presentation [3iU4a1yWGn0 @ 00:11:54]. These are distinct assembly sequences: Yu begins with a requested finished file, Amber with designed pages, and Moran with document import. Each demonstration still includes creator-controlled changes to the material or presentation rather than treating the initial generated result as untouchable.

For a resource app, Nathan Nazareth chooses Base44 Apps rather than Agents and leaves model selection on Auto. Their proposed format combines a paid guide, member area, and small personalized tool, positioned between an inexpensive PDF and a full-time software startup [6cm2o8zzuNw @ 00:08:21]. Richard Yu's recurring alternative is Fit Mom Reset: their Base44 prompt generates homepage, pricing, login, membership, dashboard, and onboarding. The shown plan is forty-seven dollars monthly with a seven-day trial and cancellation; the generated experience includes fitness and meal-planning features [gjffmgucDSw @ 00:09:02]. Eden builds a different kind of implementation product through existing knowledge. For Human 3.0, they connect through Eden MCP, locate their framework and prompt, discuss the product shape, reject weak suggestions, refine the name, add curriculum, and review the first lesson. They then request an overall plan to manage a large context and continue in small pieces before publishing the store product. The teaching assets and AI instructions are developed together, rather than beginning with a generic app screen [VJ-JR7uMY34 @ 00:19:23].

Stacy La's fast-design route starts in Genspark's Design Agent with a planner prompt naming the problem, required pages, content, style, and colors. References are optional, but they explicitly reject exact copying and ask for a differentiated angle or feature set. Their recommendation is one niche, topic, and problem before expansion [gGW2IMNOz68 @ 00:05:18]. Emmanuel Adanu turns one thumbnail workflow into a bundle: the proposed package contains a core system, prompt-library vault, workbook, scorecard, and video training, but they choose the first three assets. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

A beginner PDF does not have one agreed price or one agreed reason to exist. Baddie In Business recommends nine to twenty-seven dollars for a simple beginner product, placing The Broke Girl Reset around emotional financial relief rather than a generic budgeting label. Their Shopify listing describes the audience, problem, inclusions, and transformation [YS1HBQAWWVk @ 00:06:55]. Richard Yu explicitly rejects nine dollars in their PDF playbook, saying cheap pricing signals low quality, and recommends twenty-seven to forty-seven dollars instead. Their product is a short, edited guide answering an acute problem, with original examples and a Canva cover [rYUS7ibZhYo @ 00:04:27]. Brian Moran permits seven to twenty-seven dollars but prefers twenty-seven, arguing that expertise and specificity support pricing above ordinary bookstore comparisons. Their illustration assumes ten percent conversion: one hundred visitors daily produces ten purchases and two hundred seventy dollars in daily sales. That is Moran's stated expectation and arithmetic, not a sales result from the freshly built demonstration [3iU4a1yWGn0 @ 00:22:14]. Emmanuel Adanu challenges the generic ebook premise more directly: answers that someone can obtain from free ChatGPT in under two minutes are weak products in their view. They favor mastered execution, including reusable Instagram prompts, advertising templates, and demonstrated AI workflows, rather than random information assembled into a book [_YY-8C1VR9o @ 00:00:00].

Design speed is another genuine split, not a single settled tool recommendation. Monica Lee teaches Canva as a practical assembly surface: search for complete page templates, retain the layout when design is difficult, replace text and images, resize elements, and use 'change all' to update matching colors. Pages can be deleted or duplicated before download, so the template does not dictate the final length [VoaJoVQaSig @ 00:07:49]. Stacy La rejects Canva for this particular task, describing it as manual and time-consuming with weak AI design. They also reject Claude Code for this workflow because they regard its setup, training, and coding as unnecessarily complicated. Genspark is their preferred route because they choose tools by completion speed and want to avoid a sprawling twenty-tool system [gGW2IMNOz68 @ 00:03:37]. Brian Moran similarly finds Canva cumbersome, but their replacement is Typeset rather than Genspark. They prefer ChatGPT for writing, then use document import, automatic pagination, theme changes, and visual inserts to package the ebook. Moran also describes video embedding and linked upgrade buttons, making their formatting demonstration more than a cover generator [3iU4a1yWGn0 @ 00:11:54]. Monica's page editing, Stacy's agent-led generation, and Moran's imported-document formatting are three explicitly different production routines, each shown with creator-controlled changes before the file is sold.

Distribution changes the beginner recommendation as much as the creation software. Alek tells beginners without traffic to start on Etsy; an existing audience or advertising skill can justify Shopify or another independent store. Their marketplace argument is that competition also exists in Facebook advertising and Pinterest, while owned-store expansion can follow revenue [T-gfoXzz8k8 @ 00:03:26]. Money Talk With Leon prefers Shopify for customization and persuasion, then seeks YouTubers whose longer viewing relationships may produce stronger loyalty than short clips. Their Creator Closer workflow filters affiliate-only or paid creators, supplies available emails, and generates product-specific outreach and negotiation responses [B8Z2fA5X0J4 @ 00:09:48]. Nathan Nazareth's beginner guide puts organic validation ahead of Meta, TikTok, or Snapchat advertising; paid acquisition is a scaling move after there is something that sells [dr37DaI8g3U @ 00:13:34]. Alek's separate print-on-demand playbook moves beyond pure downloads altogether. They favor physical products fulfilled from digital designs, arguing that low-ticket downloads have limited advertising margin while print on demand offers marketplace traffic, bundles, upsells, and potential paid-ad expansion [_4DF_tkyYfQ @ 00:14:40]. Those are different operating choices: marketplace discovery, creator-supplied traffic, organic-first validation, and a physical-product extension. Alek's later preference does not turn the earlier downloadable catalog into a subscription app, and Leon's creator filter does not make commission-only partners automatic.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Brian Moran's first bottleneck was not the ebook tool. Their first dollar took almost eighteen months, and the broad trainbaseball.com book failed while they worried about redesign and wasted a couple hundred dollars on advertising. Moran identifies the real problem as failing to choose one problem for one person; their later curveball and bat-speed examples are responses to that experience [3iU4a1yWGn0 @ 00:00:00]. Money Talk With Leon describes another bottleneck after product creation: finding suitable creators, locating emails, writing outreach, and negotiating agreements. They acknowledge months of learning and weeks, sometimes up to a month, to secure one or several creators despite roughly ten years in marketing and YouTube. They also warn that beginners often cannot direct ChatGPT well enough to produce effective outreach [B8Z2fA5X0J4 @ 00:06:01]. Emmanuel Adanu's website demonstration exposes concrete delivery cleanup. After assembling generated HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and images, they replace the portrait the model reused with the image they actually wanted and preview the page locally [_YY-8C1VR9o @ 00:19:42]. Later, the deployed site shows a 'not secure' problem; they use the hosting control panel's Force HTTPS option, mention included SSL, and allow time or support assistance for the change. That correction occurs after the product and checkout have already been assembled [_YY-8C1VR9o @ 00:23:27].

A fast generation demonstration still leaves unfinished work visible. Nathan Nazareth describes AI app creation as roughly eighty percent complete, with the remaining twenty percent handled through small prompts. Their sequence prioritizes functioning controls over appearance, then publishing, connecting payments, and purchasing with a real credit card. They explicitly recommend one change per prompt instead of trying to fix everything at once [6cm2o8zzuNw @ 00:18:02]. Stacy La first praises generated spreadsheet formulas and charts, then says AI is imperfect and calls for inspecting every line, page, tab, appearance, and function before selling. Their export step comes after that review, whether the deliverable is a PDF or spreadsheet [gGW2IMNOz68 @ 00:10:00]. AMBER SHARNIECE's ebook walkthrough deliberately does not populate every page; they demonstrate copying, resizing, alignment, color replacement, imagery, and PDF export without presenting the sample as a fully developed publication [UuQ3BacwIMU @ 00:11:37]. Their launch sequence publishes the demonstration, checks the storefront and Download Now path, and says the sample must be deleted quickly. They also tie same-day income to marketing rather than to the upload alone [UuQ3BacwIMU @ 00:21:11]. These operators show specific remaining tasks: functional payment testing, checking generated content and formulas, finishing pages, and inspecting the customer-facing listing after publication.

Keep each money statement attached to what its speaker actually describes. Monica Lee reports more than one hundred thousand dollars in Stan sales and calls its zero-percent transaction-fee proposition '100% profit'; that wording accompanies their platform recommendation, not an itemized operating-cost account [VoaJoVQaSig @ 00:11:26]. Nathan Nazareth's beginner guide claims ninety-to-one-hundred-percent net margins and labels one hundred purchases at four hundred ninety-seven dollars 'pure profit,' while presenting reusable digital delivery as the model [dr37DaI8g3U @ 00:00:00]. Alek's print-on-demand example introduces a different cost picture: the spoken production figure is 'around $94,' shipping is $3.99, and estimated profit is $5.3 per sale. Those figures remain separate from the near-costless-download language; Alek's physical extension includes production and shipping [_4DF_tkyYfQ @ 00:04:46]. Money Talk With Leon's earlier PDF playbook also includes creator compensation: they report using both flat-fee and affiliate arrangements, including fifty-percent commissions. Their access to larger creators draws on longstanding relationships and proof, while even smaller commission-only partners can be difficult to secure. They suggest twenty-thousand-to-fifty-thousand-subscriber creators for cash-poor beginners and prefer paying upfront when possible [LRACQ1AzUH0 @ 00:12:42]. The operators are describing different fulfillment and acquisition arrangements, not one interchangeable profit figure for every PDF, app, or AI-generated design.
