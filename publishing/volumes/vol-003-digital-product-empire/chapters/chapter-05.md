## Executive Summary

The practitioners agree on the central trade: standard Stripe buys control, while a merchant of record takes over tax work for a higher fee. HelperMan values the administrative relief after using both Lemon Squeezy and Paddle; SoloBuild compares their 5% plus 50-cent base pricing with Stripe's layered charges. ## The Practitioner Consensus

Choose who carries the tax work before choosing the checkout skin. Tutorly separates standard Stripe Checkout from Paddle and Lemon Squeezy: Stripe processes the payment, while the other two become the legal seller and manage the associated tax responsibilities. The distinction matters because Tutorly's list of payment operations includes subscriptions, refunds, invoices, and obligations across countries, not merely a successful card charge. Their pitch for Stripe is customization and control; their pitch for a merchant of record is removing the administrative work that grows with international customers [IfaCBBIoVK0 @ 00:00:32]. SoloBuild makes the same distinction but adds a specific boundary around Stripe Tax: calculation and collection do not automatically include registration and filing. They place those services in Tax Complete, starting at $90 a month, and describe Managed Payments as Stripe's handled-for-you alternative [i05UCC3UvNY @ 00:01:53]. Hayes Tutorials lands on the same operational split: Stripe fits developers with tax handling already in place, Paddle fits B2B companies, and Lemon Squeezy fits independent creators. Their recommendation is not that every seller needs the same platform. It is that existing tax capability, customer type, and the desire to delegate determine which platform removes useful work. Across these comparisons, the cheapest processing headline and the smallest administrative workload are different purchasing decisions [mEXMrWuZFRQ @ 00:01:11].

The appeal of delegation is concrete in HelperMan's accounts of using both platforms for around six months. With Lemon Squeezy, they describe quick setup for digital products, subscriptions, and software without a complicated back end. The interface makes products, customers, subscriptions, and payouts straightforward to manage, while the merchant-of-record arrangement removes tax and payment administration. Their enthusiasm is specifically for solo founders and small creators who want to start selling rather than build payment infrastructure [IA4eosnOzIE @ 00:00:17]. With Paddle, HelperMan highlights recurring billing, upgrades, downgrades, and customer payment handling. They also describe the checkout as designed for online software rather than general retail. These are working conveniences, not a claim that outsourcing produces unlimited control: the same review says the additional services cost more than traditional processing and leave the seller with less direct control over payment and customer processes [o3KRrFnoAgM @ 00:00:48]. Calderon Bridger frames the choice as time versus money and, more sharply, one frustration versus another. Running the tax side yourself creates one kind of stress; accepting another platform's limits creates a different kind. Their conclusion is to match the tool to technical patience and the freedom the business actually wants. That matches HelperMan's experience: simplicity is valuable precisely because someone else controls part of the operation [k-wa2TZefr4 @ 00:02:30].

The bundled features explain why equal base fees do not make two platforms interchangeable. How2Genius describes Paddle as a combined checkout, payment, subscription, and tax system: it calculates sales tax, collects payment, and remits taxes for sales in the US, Canada, and other countries. Their practical attraction is a single platform instead of several tools, with one-time purchases, subscriptions, trials, and coupon codes supporting different product models [Y5VXhUB7aNM @ 00:00:18]. houdztech emphasizes the software-business side of that bundle: revenue recovery, subscription analytics through ProfitWell, dunning, and hybrid models combining self-served subscriptions with sales-led invoicing. In the same comparison, Lemon Squeezy is positioned around a simpler interface, email marketing, affiliates, and a no-code storefront rather than the same enterprise-oriented depth [Qq-EyvVj7IM @ 00:02:30]. Hayes Tutorials identifies license keys, affiliate networks, and email marketing as Lemon Squeezy's creator-oriented tools [mEXMrWuZFRQ @ 00:00:52]. Tutorly explains the operational benefit: an independent developer selling apps, plugins, or templates can manage sales, licensing, and basic marketing together instead of connecting multiple services. Their recommendation distinguishes established B2B software from smaller creator businesses. The shared baseline is therefore not merely outsourced tax. It is a payment arrangement whose billing, delivery, and marketing features fit the product being sold and the customers buying it [IfaCBBIoVK0 @ 00:03:01].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Managed Payments receives very different verdicts because the speakers describe different availability and coverage claims. TechWealth Hub presents it as available in public preview, with full merchant-of-record support and selective use by transaction. Their recommendation is enthusiastic: an existing Stripe user can add the role without moving to another platform, while a new software business can start with Stripe and enable it where needed [KRdWGDk-GkY @ 00:04:13]. Captain Vova describes a narrower offering: Europe, the US, Canada, and the UK are covered in the documentation they discuss, while Australia, India, and other locations remain outside that tax coverage. They explicitly reject treating that version as full global sales-tax coverage [5zGuxRrg_O0 @ 00:00:45]. heyymilian discusses another boundary: the seller must be based in a supported location, and the supported product categories are digital products rather than physical goods, professional services, or live in-person events. That is a business-eligibility question, not the same question as where buyers are taxed [0R3CB1EcgKA @ 00:02:54]. Tutorly's alternative framework stays centered on business fit: standard Stripe for control and existing tax handling, Paddle for B2B, and Lemon Squeezy for creators. These accounts do not support treating a broad launch pitch as a universal answer about every seller, product, and market [IfaCBBIoVK0 @ 00:03:32].

The fee accounts also disagree, and the disagreement changes what a comparison means. Yumi Kato Reviews first quotes Lemon Squeezy at 5% plus 50 cents, then says adding Stripe processing puts a referenced table near 7.9% plus a flat charge. They additionally report that Lemon Squeezy refunds its platform fee when a buyer receives a refund [jwFYqAi_nQY @ 00:00:47]. SoloBuild describes a different structure: the 5% plus 50-cent transaction rate, with named extras of 1.5% for international transactions, 1.5% for PayPal, and 0.5% for subscription payments. They also distinguish payout costs, describing free US bank payouts and 1% for bank accounts outside the US [i05UCC3UvNY @ 00:03:53]. heyymilian's November 23, 2026 price capture likewise starts Lemon Squeezy at 5% plus 50 cents with small additional fees in edge cases, while explicitly describing Managed Payments as a surcharge in addition to processing. Their migration promotion is another separate price, not the permanent standard rate [0R3CB1EcgKA @ 00:04:18]. HelperMan offers the useful business test without settling those conflicting totals: decide whether simpler administration and tax handling are worth the extra fees for the actual business model. Their positive six-month verdict still leaves growing businesses wanting more advanced control. Alternative playbooks turn on the feature that cannot disappear during the move. How2Genius prefers Paddle for quick setup and easy subscriptions, but describes FastSpring as the stronger customization route: branded storefronts and checkout, upsells, bundles, localized pricing, flexible billing, and international payment support. Their comparison does not reduce international selling to a tax toggle; it includes the selling experience and the tools around it [Y5VXhUB7aNM @ 00:02:11]. Gaurav Tiwari starts elsewhere when existing customers already hold license keys. Gaurav then attaches the missing pieces: Creem has no PayPal and does not move active subscriptions; Polar's free plan matches Lemon Squeezy's rate, while its $20 plan drops to 3.8% plus 40 cents; Paddle supports PayPal but has no license keys. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The strongest firsthand constraints concern access and operating control, not a reported windfall. HelperMan says Paddle account approvals and reviews can take time, particularly for new businesses or unusual models. Their advice after using it is to understand how the merchant-of-record model changes payments, taxes, and customer billing before choosing it. They still value the service, but their experience does not describe immediate entry or unrestricted control [o3KRrFnoAgM @ 00:01:38]. Gaurav Tiwari identifies a direct migration blocker: they sell plugins from India, and say the Managed Payments replacement is not available to their business there. Their $99-sale comparison also reverses the initial price advantage: they quote $5.45 for Lemon Squeezy, $4.66 on Stripe's migration rate, and $6.64 after that rate expires on December 1, 2027 [cNc29NtuCYU @ 00:01:33]. Gaurav's own checkout experience is different: they sell plugins through FluentCart and also name Easy Digital Downloads and their own Core Forms. None of those, they say, imports from Lemon Squeezy, leaving a manual move [cNc29NtuCYU @ 00:03:31]. Yumi's suspension example is not their own operating loss: they recount another seller's sudden high-risk suspension after a month and slow support. That distinction keeps a reported customer complaint separate from HelperMan's use and Gaurav's business situation [jwFYqAi_nQY @ 00:01:39].

The shutdown reports agree on the hard endpoint but disagree on one tooling date. Folioquest reports March 31, 2027 as the shutdown date, with new signups closed and no deadline extensions. Their account makes active subscriptions part of the cutoff rather than an exception that can continue indefinitely [M61DsgB51BI @ 00:00:18]. heyymilian reports November 16, 2026 for the end of new users and stores, December 6 for eligible Managed Payments migration tooling, and mid-January 2027 as the recommended latest start because the process can take up to eight weeks [0R3CB1EcgKA @ 00:01:28]. Gaurav gives December 2 for that tooling opening and the same mid-January start recommendation. Their operational argument is that a February start leaves no time to fix a failed import; this is a planning warning, not a claim that they personally suffered that failed import [cNc29NtuCYU @ 00:00:19]. Folioquest also gives December 2 and describes moving catalogs, webhooks, and live subscriptions as a four-to-eight-week process. The useful agreement is the size of the migration job and the need to begin well before the final processing date. The disagreement remains December 2 versus December 6; neither date changes these speakers' shared mid-January recommendation or their reported March endpoint [M61DsgB51BI @ 00:01:36].

The handoff fails as a like-for-like replacement wherever payment migration is mistaken for product delivery. Gaurav says the official tooling moves products, customers, and card subscriptions, with customers receiving at least fourteen days' notice. They also say Managed Payments does not provide license keys, file delivery, or affiliates; PayPal subscriptions cannot move, affected subscribers must buy again, and checkout links, buttons, and webhooks require manual replacement rather than automatic redirects [cNc29NtuCYU @ 00:00:55]. heyymilian independently identifies missing storefronts, digital fulfillment, license keys, download links, and affiliates. Their conclusion is that sellers of files or license keys still need a fulfillment tool even when choosing Managed Payments rather than taking tax responsibility themselves [0R3CB1EcgKA @ 00:02:37]. Gaurav's execution order responds directly: export customers, orders, subscriptions, and keys; turn off PayPal for new checkout subscriptions; configure the new provider in test mode; replace every link; then tell customers the new name that will appear on card statements [cNc29NtuCYU @ 00:03:50]. Stephen's demonstration supplies a smaller version of the same unfinished-work problem: opening checkout did not mean backend webhooks were connected. They explicitly left those connections out and described linking a user object to later subscription records. A working payment page was the demonstrated result, not a completed subscription backend [F-WZgXuQIuQ @ 00:03:53].
