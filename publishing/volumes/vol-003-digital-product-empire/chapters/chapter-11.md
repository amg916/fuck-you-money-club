## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with the reader's problem, not the software shopping list. Nicolas Cole argues that positioning, title, subtitle, and the desired outcome have a greater effect on a newsletter than its delivery platform. Their test is whether the publication is valuable enough for a specific person to read even if it arrived as physical mail. Freedom Capital makes the same narrowing move operational: choose a specific niche rather than going general, then bring people from social content or a free lead magnet onto the email list. The publisher building Dope Act on beehiiv adds the missing production discipline: choose a format readers will want to return to. Their issue contains an introduction, recognition of people who posted on the community wall, a deeper piece of writing, and actions readers can take. They describe three reasons to return: usefulness that saves time or money, belonging, and a feeling such as inspiration or amusement. These approaches meet at specificity and repeatability: Cole names the outcome, Freedom Capital names the niche, and Dope Act gives the issue a recurring shape. The Dope Act publisher explicitly recommends picking a format first and iterating from there [RaVsgiJeovE @ 00:04:28] [B4AqprpbfFo @ 00:01:13] [a-8NoqWX86M @ 00:02:08].

The shared business model is broader than charging readers to open emails. Mr. Money describes three integrated beehiiv routes: advertising, digital products, and subscriptions. Their sequence is to publish useful free content, learn what readers value, and introduce paid access when demand becomes clear. Digital products serve people who want a guide, template, report, or other standalone resource without committing to recurring payments. Write • Build • Scale arrives at the same diversification argument from a Substack business with more than 900 paid subscribers: subscriptions can coexist with mini courses, coaching, live cohorts, ebooks, and templates. Their earlier Peak Productivity newsletter was built largely around mini courses rather than an enormous comprehensive course. Freedom Capital adds affiliate marketing and direct sponsorship outreach, recommending tools the publisher already uses and approaching small brands. The agreement is not that every newsletter should sell every offer immediately. Mr. Money emphasizes introducing deeper paid access after learning what readers want, while Freedom Capital recommends affiliate links from the beginning. Both connect monetization to useful publishing and reader trust. Write • Build • Scale's practical distinction is between a free reader and someone who has bought an entry-level offer, then may buy further help [3yBrjrexD08 @ 00:04:53] [HDQk5g412yI @ 00:00:00] [B4AqprpbfFo @ 00:01:42].

The launch mechanics begin with a publication and a readable first issue. Shistata Acharya signs up with Google or email, completes phone verification, and starts from a blank draft or template. Their editorial instruction is concrete: keep the title clear and use the subtitle to explain what opening the email delivers. Set colors, typography, spacing, and background, then choose email, web, or both. Digital Tutorials 101 demonstrates the same progression through composition, audience, email, web, and final review, including the subject line, preview text, thumbnail, and web metadata. Their design instruction is a clean, readable style rather than a particular color scheme. Stack Receipts extends that setup into the sending details: configure sender name and reply-to address, write the welcome email, and actually enable it. With double opt-in enabled, their described order is signup, confirmation, and then the welcome message; Smart Nudge sends one reminder after 48 hours without confirmation. Acharya recommends previewing before publishing or scheduling. Digital Tutorials 101 likewise reviews the finished post before selecting a publication time. These are specific setup actions the speakers teach alongside the editorial job, not replacements for choosing a clear topic and publishing something readers want to open [c3ZVbyfXE1Q @ 00:00:38] [WEE2x3i8_pw @ 00:05:18] [fd2DFt_F31U @ 00:01:48].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

beehiiv demonstrates two advertising workflows with different screens and timing. In the weekly-inbox workflow, paying users receive Sunday opportunities showing advertisers, estimated payouts, and availability. Open Monetize, Ads, Inbox; review an offer; choose a creative version; and select an available date. Reject unwanted offers with a reason so the system can improve matching. Insert the accepted advertisement into the draft and send on its scheduled day; the tutorial says a detailed performance report arrives 96 hours later. In the offers-based workflow, open Monetize, Ads, Offers, inspect the payment per click or per thousand opens, and claim an available offer. This demonstration allows up to five simultaneous claims and five days to publish each claimed advertisement. Choose the creative and add it to a new or existing post. The presenter suggests moving a CPC advertisement higher in the email, or placing it in the middle for closer editorial integration. AshuRex supplies a small-send example: one click on a March 3 advertisement earned $3.15. Their preferences specify business, marketing, and writing, a minimum $1 CPC, and 14 weekly sends because they write two emails daily. Those are their settings and results, not the tutorials' universal publishing schedule [BtzD0OT77Mo @ 00:00:24] [ELxfeUy81Vk @ 00:00:43] [Pv4BDrAVY-o @ 00:06:31].

For audience growth, the Dope Act publisher starts with distribution rather than paid ads. After editing and publishing an issue, they share its link on LinkedIn and other social platforms to bring people back to the email list. Inside beehiiv's recommendation engine, they filter for climate, creativity, and culture, inspect publications with overlapping audiences, and invite reciprocal recommendations. Their lead-magnet build uses beehiiv MCP connected to Claude: brainstorm 15 ideas, select a seven-day dopamine swap, and create a signup form, acquisition tag, and seven-email automation. Each email encourages posting on the community wall; the publisher then checks and rewrites the emails. Digital Tutorials 101 describes the paid recommendation route separately: new subscribers see other newsletters, and the publisher earns when those readers also subscribe. Solo Narrator's navigation is Monetize, Boosts, choose a relevant market, and Apply, with some features requiring a paid subscription. Alex Hitt adds the marketplace economics: buyers set a maximum acquisition cost and fund escrow; approved recommending publishers earn after a two-to-17-day verification period, and beehiiv takes a 20% transaction share. Keep the routes distinct: the Dope Act demonstration explicitly starts with free recommendations, while the other speakers describe paid subscriber referrals and their conditions [a-8NoqWX86M @ 00:06:15] [WEE2x3i8_pw @ 00:08:08] [CLhun0LXdmg @ 00:01:11] [g-jix0jhcmU @ 00:06:01].

For expertise sales, Write • Build • Scale starts by finding repeated questions in comments, direct conversations, and surveys about goals and obstacles. Their mini courses contain roughly one to two hours of lessons and usually sell for $27 to $150. They recommend transformation-led coaching packages at $1,000 to $3,000 rather than selling an abstract hour to pick the creator's brain. Their ebook example repackages 30 to 40 existing productivity articles; templates can become an order bump, upsell, or entry offer. Matt McGarry's live-cohort mechanics begin with one achievable outcome, a short program outline, homework, and completion criteria. Build anticipation and a waitlist before taking payment; sell before creating the full program. Their 30-day marketing schedule assigns days one through 14 to waitlist building, days 15 through 19 to selling exclusively to that list, days 20 through 25 to webinars, and days 21 through 30 to public enrollment. On deadline day, they may send a morning email to the whole list and later follow-ups only to interested clickers. Mr. Money provides the beehiiv product-delivery counterpart: promote products within issues, launch to specific segments, or keep them available as evergreen offers, with payments, delivery, and access handled inside the platform [HDQk5g412yI @ 00:03:20] [1JVlUJAc6to @ 00:32:47] [3yBrjrexD08 @ 00:03:56].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Substack's 10% is either an expensive revenue share or an acquisition expense worth paying, depending on the speaker. CreatorOpsMatrix emphasizes the growing cash cost and argues that beehiiv's list-based software pricing better suits a structured media business. Nicolas Cole accepts the fee for Write With AI, which they say generates $300,000 annually, because Substack brings substantial reader discovery. They show a free list of 114,000 readers and say over half of subscribers came from inside the ecosystem. Cole also rejects lengthy platform research: their advice is to decide within 30 minutes, because email lists can be exported and moved. CreatorOpsMatrix takes the opposite operational tone, calling later migration a potential logistical nightmare. Hayes Tutorials positions Buttondown as the lightweight alternative: list-size-based fees, simple Markdown-focused writing, and no discovery network or advertising tools. Cole identifies Kit as the automation engine their wider business uses, even while they operate a Substack publication. The alternatives therefore differ in more than the fee line. Substack's community is central to Cole's recommendation for beginners without traffic; Hayes recommends Buttondown for simplicity and beehiiv for business tools. Neither Cole's acquisition history nor CreatorOpsMatrix's cost argument requires treating every newsletter as the same kind of business [gmaXb858vdU @ 00:00:21] [RaVsgiJeovE @ 00:11:27] [uSqFZ-Rz98U @ 00:00:34].

Free-plan advertising access is a direct conflict, not a shared prerequisite. Shistata Acharya says the ad network is available on the free plan, while also saying paid subscriptions require an upgrade. Stack Receipts says ads, paid subscriptions, custom domains, and automations sit on paid plans. Their pricing walkthrough, described as checked October 7, 2026, quotes Light at $59 monthly for 1,000 subscribers and $79 for 2,500, with the free plan capped at 2,500. AshuRex instead names Scale as the plan they use to run advertising and quotes a regular $49 monthly price. These speakers are not presenting one consistent plan label or price. Stack Receipts separates free product selling from paid advertising: files and links are available across plans, a free PDF needs no Stripe connection, and paid products need Stripe. Acharya's own instruction is to check account terms and availability before counting on Boosts earnings. Their advice to start with one useful issue remains separate from the disputed monetization entitlement. Stack Receipts further distinguishes a trial from a paid account: automations switch off when the trial ends, and custom domains, paid subscriptions, and test emails to outside addresses stay locked during the unpaid trial [c3ZVbyfXE1Q @ 00:02:28] [fd2DFt_F31U @ 00:00:54] [Pv4BDrAVY-o @ 00:06:52].

Recorded products and live cohorts produce the sharpest disagreement about what expertise buyers want. Matt McGarry argues that traditional information products are losing ground because customers need execution, feedback, and accountability rather than more content. They report 26% to 46% completion in their own cohorts and say reducing their program from six weeks to four made it much better. Write • Build • Scale reports a contrasting commercial history: narrow recorded mini courses produced multiple six figures over roughly three years, often selling on autopilot. Their objection is to oversized courses, not recorded delivery itself. They recommend mini courses precisely because a specific problem, short runtime, and lower price make the product easier to create and sell. The two approaches also disagree on live duration. McGarry calls two to four weeks the sweet spot and five weeks the maximum; Write • Build • Scale includes four-, six-, eight-, and even 12-week cohorts among the available formats. They also identify the calendar drawback: people who are traveling or overloaded with work may not join something they cannot attend live. Mr. Money keeps the recorded-product route open on beehiiv, describing guides, templates, and educational resources as products created once and sold repeatedly alongside the core newsletter [1JVlUJAc6to @ 00:13:35] [HDQk5g412yI @ 00:00:33] [HDQk5g412yI @ 00:16:10] [3yBrjrexD08 @ 00:04:17].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

AshuRex's payment bottleneck occurred after the newsletter opportunity was already attractive. They say their invite-only Stripe application was rejected because the business was not supported. Their reported response was to create a US LLC and US bank account, obtain a Stripe account, and connect it to beehiiv. They describe withdrawals passing through Stripe to the US bank, then through Wise to their Indian bank account. That is their payment route, not a step-by-step company-formation procedure. AshuRex also reports watching other operators click their own ads and create fake accounts to generate more clicks. Those operators, they say, were banned after paying for subscriptions and received nothing back. Their replacement method is content-led audience building, naturally positioned ads, and persuasive copywriting. beehiiv's offers tutorial adds a separate operational failure point: claimed inventory expires after five days, and available offers can disappear when other publishers fill them. The presenter shows how to move an ad between drafts or cancel it to free a claim slot. Stack Receipts adds the settlement timing they report: earnings for advertisements run in the prior month arrive in the beehiiv wallet on the 20th. Claiming an offer, publishing it, and receiving its earnings are distinct stages [Pv4BDrAVY-o @ 00:09:34] [Pv4BDrAVY-o @ 00:11:55] [ELxfeUy81Vk @ 00:00:43] [fd2DFt_F31U @ 00:04:47].

The production trap is overload disguised as value. McGarry says their own move from six-week cohorts to four-week cohorts worked far better, and warns that more than two sessions weekly becomes too much to attend and absorb. Their test for homework is whether completing it can realistically produce the promised outcome; otherwise improve the program or promise less. Write • Build • Scale identifies the offer-design version of the same problem: creators build what excites them without checking audience demand. Their response is to inspect repeated questions, discuss reader goals, and ask about obstacles rather than simply asking what people say they would buy. The Dope Act publisher observes a different bottleneck in ongoing newsletters: stagnation when creators do not know what to write, rather than merely slow subscriber growth. A reusable format turns production into filling in useful parts. These speakers also retain human work after the shortcut. McGarry makes execution homework central rather than adding busywork. Write • Build • Scale says polished videos cannot rescue an unwanted topic. The Dope Act publisher uses Claude to build a challenge sequence but then checks and rewrites its emails. Their saved technical time goes back into creativity, connection, and making the publication feel more human [1JVlUJAc6to @ 00:13:35] [HDQk5g412yI @ 00:04:34] [a-8NoqWX86M @ 00:02:26] [a-8NoqWX86M @ 00:09:22].

The economics check is to keep each speaker's revenue figure attached to its actual cost context. Stack Receipts reports beehiiv's displayed average advertising earnings of $39 monthly at 1,000 subscribers against Light pricing of $49 on annual billing or $59 on monthly billing. At 10,000 subscribers, their displayed $128 advertising figure exceeds the $119 annual-billing monthly price but falls below the $139 monthly-billing price. They also show list growth increasing the software bill, and warn that automatic tier upgrades increase charges without another manual upgrade. CreatorOpsMatrix, by contrast, presents an advertising RPM range of $20 to $100 per thousand opens. Their subscription comparison also combines references to the 10% platform share and payment processing before quoting $325 to $375 of costs at $2,500 monthly subscription revenue. Alex Hitt emphasizes beehiiv's 0% subscription take rate but separately describes a 20% fee on Boosts transactions. None of those speakers describes every revenue channel as cost-free. Stack Receipts' small-product example makes that distinction explicit: a $10 digital product paid with a US card incurs 59 cents of Stripe fees while beehiiv takes nothing.
