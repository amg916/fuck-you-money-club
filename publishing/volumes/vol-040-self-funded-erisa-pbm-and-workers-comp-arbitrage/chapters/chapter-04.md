## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree on the basic design of a medical stop-loss captive. A self-funded employer keeps claims below a specific deductible, a member-owned pool absorbs the next layer, and an A-rated excess carrier sits on top. The goal is to stop year-two lasers, 50 percent renewal spikes, and opaque carrier pricing. The street mechanics are concrete. Captive Resources shows a $100,000 specific deductible with a $600,000 aggregate attachment. CRI reports that 60 to 65% of premium is ceded to the loss fund and $35M has been returned over 10 years. Self-Funded prices a $5 to $6 retail vendor at about $1 inside the captive. BeneSmart reports $215,000 in year-one Rx savings for one 150-life manufacturer. Operators split on three points: size floors and ceilings (16 lives to 1,000+), block versus individual underwriting, and whether retention runs at 98% or 95%.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus - Ownership, Pooling and Control

Every operator in this lane frames the medical stop-loss captive the same way. A self-funded employer keeps its own claims below a specific deductible, a member-owned pool absorbs the next layer, and an excess reinsurer sits on top. Captive Resources defines the group captive as an insurer owned and controlled by its insureds. The firm stresses the word CONTROL and places the captive overlay between the specific deductible and the excess layer [c_F-WNrCoqk @ 00:01:28]. Joe Perelli of Captive Resources adds that members still buy policies from A or A+ carriers. The difference is ownership, not paper quality [BQVeLgnIx8A @ 00:03:35]. On the panel hosted by Chris Martens, the captive is called an 'upside down reinsurance mechanism' that takes a layer from a carrier such as Sun Life or HCC/Berkley [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:06:42]. Pete Thorne and Spencer Smith of Self-Funded put it more simply: a captive is 'a small insurance company' that aggregates employers who share risk. Pooling spreads that risk and reduces the 'blow on stop loss' that any single member would otherwise take [8nD9sJwgMdE @ 00:00:47]. The shared logic is that small and mid-sized firms are slow to self-fund on their own. The captive pools their buying power and gives them large-group leverage without large-group headcount.

Operators also agree on why employers move: the traditional market punishes small and mid-sized groups with opaque, volatile renewals. Captive Resources says the two traditional levers are scaling back coverage or making the company and employees pay more, and 'Neither option is great.' That matters because employees rank health insurance as their most important benefit [c_F-WNrCoqk @ 00:00:18]. Conner Strong & Buckelew names the weaknesses of stand-alone stop loss bluntly: lasers in year two and increases 'upwards of 50 percent.' Fully insured plans, by contrast, hand over no data and build in taxes and carrier profits [f50x6TivjJ4 @ 00:01:50]. Ross & Yerger answer that captive membership brings no new lasers in perpetuity, plus renewal rate caps [zzL4UNZNaFo @ 00:03:22]. Perelli explains why the stop-loss decision carries so much leverage. Stop loss is only 15 to 20% of total spend, below-deductible claims run 75 to 80%, and double-digit increases are now 'normal' [BQVeLgnIx8A @ 00:09:40]. Shopping stop loss every year therefore attacks only a fifth of the budget. The captive instead changes how the whole claims picture is managed and priced, and it rewards the employers whose claims come in better than projected.

The baseline prerequisites also line up across the field, even where headcount floors differ. Ross & Yerger target employers with 50 to 300 employees [zzL4UNZNaFo @ 00:00:03]. TheSpringGroup sets its threshold at 100+ lives because carrier data becomes accessible at that size [L4bWq8jE_JE @ 00:02:07]. Rich of BeneSmart places captives at 50+ lives but warns they do not suit groups that want hands-off arrangements [WXjUaMhMjcQ @ 00:02:09]. The most consistent filter is mindset. Thorne and Smith say employers who treat stop loss as a commodity and buy it over 12 months are 'not a fit' [8nD9sJwgMdE @ 00:05:41]. Taken together, these operators describe a specific buyer. It has enough lives to produce usable claims data and enough cash to carry a retention layer. It also has the patience to stay in a pool through the bad years rather than chase the cheapest quote at every renewal. That buyer must also be willing to engage. Data, vendor choices and renewal results all flow back to the member, so an HR team that wants to sign a form and disappear forfeits most of the structure's advantage. For every one of these operators, the captive is an operating commitment that runs across several renewal cycles, not a one-time product swap.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The Omega walk-through from Captive Resources is the cleanest layer math in the field. Omega picks a $100,000 specific deductible and retains aggregate claims up to 120% of $500,000 in expected losses, which sets a $600,000 Aggregate Attachment Point. The captive then takes the next $200,000 specific, covering claims to $300,000, and the next $200,000 aggregate, from $600K to $800K. Excess reinsurance supplies unlimited specific protection plus $800,000 of aggregate protection above that [c_F-WNrCoqk @ 00:03:37]. Phil, the MedTrans captive manager, runs a comparable stack built around a $50k specific deductible. That figure drops to roughly $25k for 30 lives and rises to $200k for large groups, with 70 to 80% budgeted to claims and 10 to 15% to the captive layer [I33Yc7nNN_c @ 00:12:19]. The Self-Funded team slices the middle layer one of two ways: quota share, splitting risk 60/40 or 50/50, or their own floating layer, which takes 100% of the risk above the spec up to a cap [6st-3ujjXM4 @ 00:00:56]. Their three-layer model adds aggregate protection because executives panic at '50,000 x 200.' Every one of these operators treats the specific deductible as the dial that decides how much volatility the employer keeps and how much it pays someone else to carry.

The money flow is where the captive earns its keep. Captive Resources runs three funding mechanisms. The Member Loss Fund equals stop-loss premium minus operating costs. The Member Assessment is capped at a known percentage of that loss fund. Captive Risk Sharing spreads remaining losses across the pool [c_F-WNrCoqk @ 00:04:13]. Conner Strong & Buckelew describes the same engine as a claim stabilization pool. The carrier refunds unused premium or draws on the pool, contributions are capped, and the carrier pays if the pool runs empty [f50x6TivjJ4 @ 00:02:24]. Perelli tests fit before anyone joins with a look-back analysis that compares a member's historical large claims against its bucket. Exceeding the bucket means an assessment year, while a surplus builds an equity position [BQVeLgnIx8A @ 00:15:20]. On the Martens panel, the CRI numbers get specific. Between 60 and 65% of premium is ceded to the loss fund, and over 10 years $35M has been returned to members on a captive loss ratio of 93 to 101% [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:55:23]. Perelli also rejects the 'overpriced' label outright. The captive layer is priced at break-even, and outperformance flows back to members as dividends and distributions instead of staying with a carrier as margin.

Pricing mechanics decide who subsidizes whom inside the pool, and by how much. Phil Helloka of Complete Captive puts the subsidy in numbers. Block-blending four groups lands the rate at $102 PEPM, so the good group pays roughly $13 PEPM over its own risk. Meanwhile, an adverse 20-life group still prices at $115 even after a 10% discount [PERxwpJ2Rnk @ 00:07:30]. Helloka's conclusion is that ultimately you want individual underwriting, because blending drags down the clean group. The increase math on the Martens panel shows the stakes. On $1M of spend, a 20% increase costs $200K fully insured, compared with $40K when the increase lands on stop loss alone [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:57:40]. Vendor scale is the second lever. Thorne and Smith price a vendor at $5 to $6 retail for a 200-life employer and at about $1 inside the captive [8nD9sJwgMdE @ 00:01:29]. Rich of BeneSmart supplies the pharmacy proof. A 150-life manufacturer paying $35,000 a month for three drugs saved $215,000 on Rx in year one after moving to a captive [WXjUaMhMjcQ @ 00:04:53]. Rich still cautions that switching the funding model won't magically save money, but it does set the table. Once the bundled carrier layers come out, the savings compound.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is headcount. CRI's Joe Perilli sets the floor at 50 employees with a working range of 50 to 5,000, and notes TPAs rarely go below 40 lives [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:23:05]. On the same panel, Jeff Christian pushes back that the market is moving down to 25 and that their own book already carries a 16-life member [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:24:40]. The Paro health team on Self-Funded reframes the fight entirely: headcount is the wrong gauge, because a 1,000-life group sitting on a $500k deductible leaves too little risk premium to pool, while the same group at a $150k deductible may fit [8nD9sJwgMdE @ 00:08:07]. At the top end, Heads Up Adviser argues that groups past 250, maybe 300 lives, historically tend to outgrow the need for the captive altogether [7cqcZyKB5aw @ 00:00:21]. That puts the practical ceiling anywhere from 300 lives to 5,000 depending on who is pitching, and the floor anywhere from a live 16-life member up to the historical 100-life line the Paro team says used to govern the market. The operator read is blunt: stop arguing employee count in isolation. Size the decision on deductible level, on how much risk premium actually sits inside the captive layer, and on how much year-to-year volatility the owner can absorb.

Practitioners also split on how the middle layer is built. Captive Resources walks through the Omega example: the employer keeps the first $100,000 specific, the captive takes the next $200,000 specific up to $300,000 and the next $200,000 aggregate from $600K to $800K, funded through a member loss fund, a capped member assessment, and captive risk sharing [c_F-WNrCoqk @ 00:03:55]. Self-Funded's risk-layer breakdown describes a different engine: quota share programs split risk 60/40 or 50/50, while their own program runs a floating layer that takes 100% of the risk above the spec up to a cap [6st-3ujjXM4 @ 00:00:56]. Conner Strong & Buckelew pitches a simpler claim stabilization pool where the carrier refunds unused premium or draws on the pool, contributions are capped, and the carrier pays if the pool runs dry [f50x6TivjJ4 @ 00:02:24]. Underwriting splits too. Phil Helloka of Complete Captive shows that blending four groups into a $102 block rate charges the good group roughly $13 PEPM over its own risk [PERxwpJ2Rnk @ 00:07:30], which is why Helloka argues you ultimately want individual underwriting. Operators comparing captive offers need to ask which of these engines sits under the label, because the word captive covers a member-owned layer, a quota share, a floating layer, and a carrier-run pool.

Even the retention number is contested between partners on one stage. CRI's Joe Perilli reports 98% retention in the health captive programs [M8zhsS8szwY @ 01:00:11], while Jeff Christian puts the figure lower at 95% [M8zhsS8szwY @ 01:04:13]. Both numbers are strong, but a three-point gap on the same panel shows the headline figure moves depending on who is doing the counting. The bigger fault line is captive versus level funding. Heads Up Adviser frames captives as working much like level funding, a cash-flow protection product that 'makes me feel good at night,' and says captives may still suit groups of 200 plus [7cqcZyKB5aw @ 00:00:37]. Rich at BeneSmart draws a harder line: level funded fits 25 to 150 lives, captives fit 50 plus but not for owners who want a hands-off arrangement, and level-funded refunds are shared while the model carries spread pricing [WXjUaMhMjcQ @ 00:04:23]. Rich's own case study backs the unbundled route: a 150-life manufacturer paying $35,000 a month for three drugs saved $215,000 on Rx in year one after the move. The alternative playbook follows from that split. A group that wants cash-flow protection without unbundling anything can live inside level funding, while a group willing to pick its own TPA, PBM, and stop-loss vendor lineup is the buyer the captive crowd is actually built for.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The traditional-market trap behind most captive conversations is the laser. Conner Strong & Buckelew reports stop-loss carriers lasering claimants in year two and pushing increases upwards of 50 percent [f50x6TivjJ4 @ 00:01:50]. The Paro health team on Self-Funded says stop-loss rate caps of 50% plus are only going up, and positions the captive as the route to better reinsurance terms, no lasering, and lower caps [8nD9sJwgMdE @ 00:03:15]. Heads Up Adviser names the casualty: a group that was hit with some giant laser and forced back to fully insured may value a captive regardless of size [7cqcZyKB5aw @ 00:01:08]. Ross & Yerger frames the captive counter-offer as no new lasers in perpetuity plus renewal rate caps [zzL4UNZNaFo @ 00:03:22]. The economics check runs against the traditional side. A group that self-funds on a standalone stop-loss policy, takes a large claimant, and then gets lasered has lost the protection it bought at exactly the moment it needed it. Retreating to fully insured means walking back into a product with no data and with taxes and carrier profits built in. The practical move is to pin down lasering and rate-cap terms before renewal, because that is the specific protection every practitioner here sells the captive on.

Captives do not remove bad years; they relocate them. CRI's Chief Business Officer describes the look-back analysis: compare a member's historical large claims against its bucket, and a member that exceeds it is looking at an assessment year, while surplus becomes an equity position [BQVeLgnIx8A @ 00:15:20]. Surplus years are where the dividends and distributions come from; deficit years are where the member writes a check. Ross & Yerger puts the cycle bluntly: four of five years are good and one or two are bad [zzL4UNZNaFo @ 00:04:11]. Self-Funded's risk-layer breakdown says a captive can go as low as 20 lives, but smaller groups face claims volatility, and above about 1,000 lives the cost advantage is less obvious [6st-3ujjXM4 @ 00:02:18]. The claim sizes feeding those bad years keep climbing. On the Christopher Martens panel, the speakers cite gene therapy claims at $3.5M for hemophilia, $3M, and $2.8M [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:38:09]. Those severities are why the Omega structure stacks unlimited specific coverage above the captive layer, and why a small member's real exposure in a bad year is the assessment rather than the headline claim. The operator read: underwrite the downside year first, because the dividend history only describes the good four out of five.

The rest of the failure list is operational. On the Martens panel, the joining warning is explicit: captives run on a common anniversary with short or long first contracts, and a group should not move mid-year while claims are accruing to the deductible [M8zhsS8szwY @ 01:06:08]. The Paro health team flags the employer who treats stop-loss as a commodity and buys over 12 months as not a fit [8nD9sJwgMdE @ 00:05:41]. That buyer misses the point the member-owned structure is built on: skin in the game, plus reserves that can lower deductibles or fund premium holidays. Phil Helloka of Complete Captive flags the trap inside block-rated programs: the good group charged over its own risk turns into a 'perpetual winner' who becomes unhappy, and ultimately you want individual underwriting [PERxwpJ2Rnk @ 00:09:48]. The economics check from the Martens panel shows why fit matters: on $1M of spend, a 20% increase costs $200K fully insured versus $40K when it lands on stop loss alone [M8zhsS8szwY @ 00:57:40]. Berkley has been fronting the same program for 13 years, and the panel's instruction is plain: do not shop the captive every year. Groups that shop the captive yearly, join mid-cycle with claims running, or accept a blended block rate without asking throw away the exact advantage they came for.
