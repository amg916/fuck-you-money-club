# Chapter 08: Brokers, Commissions, and the Broker of Record Letter

*Broker value and selection criteria, commissions including MEC plans, when to fire your broker, building a competitive benefits program, and BOR letter mechanics and myths across benefits and P&C lines*

## Executive Summary

Benefits and commercial brokers agree the employer pays its broker through commission built into the premium, around 5% or 3 to 6% of premium, and that percentage pay rises with every renewal while persistency bonuses reward clients staying put. Benefits Blake cites a $178 median per enrolled person per year and about $30 more commission per $1,000 of extra premium, then shows how to force disclosure of direct and indirect pay of $1,000 or more and pull Form 5500 Schedule A filings for free. Evolved Benefits pitches MEC commissions of 12 to 25%. On broker of record letters, practitioners agree a BOR fires the incumbent, takes five to ten business days, and gives the incumbent a rescind window, but split on whether commission or flat-fee models and free due diligence or paid proposals best align broker and client.

## The Practitioner Consensus: Who Pays the Broker and What the BOR Actually Does

The benefits practitioners start from the same blunt fact: the employer is already paying the broker, it just never sees the invoice. Benefits Blake splits broker pay into two buckets, direct commission that is 'bundled right into your rate' and indirect money the carrier pays the agency, and says most employers have no idea what they pay because 'you just don't see the line item' [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:01:56]. Benefits On The Fly says the same thing from 25 years of selling: most owners have 'absolutely no idea how much their broker is actually making,' and the dominant model is commission rather than fee [50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:00:31]. Butch Zemar adds the mechanical reason clients treat brokers as a free commodity: premiums are filed with the state with commissions already incorporated, so they are built in whether or not an agent is used [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:00:42]. Rodney Mattos frames the fully insured buyer's position as 'one premium, one lever, one renewal conversation,' with the advisor's pay sitting inside the same bill as carrier administration and profit [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:01:11]. The baseline prerequisite all four share: before judging whether a broker is worth it, convert the hidden commission into a dollar figure you can actually argue about.

The second point of agreement is that percentage pay rewards the broker when the client loses. Rodney Mattos, who sat at carrier golf outings and incentive dinners early in their career as a producer, says the unspoken rule was 'keep the client happy, keep the premiums flowing,' and that a 10% premium jump lifts a percentage commission 10% with 'no extra effort required' [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:02:59]. Benefits On The Fly admits the same discomfort from inside the commission model: they could justify their 5% with HR support and claims advocacy, yet 'I still kept making more money every time my client was hit with a premium increase' [50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:03:55]. Benefits Blake isolates the sharpest lever, persistency compensation, which pays the agency more 'when clients stay put. Not when they do better' [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:06:46]. Butch Zemar confirms that at the top end, enough volume unlocks retention bonuses that create 'a financial incentive for the insurance broker to not shop plans,' though they say those land at big shops, not small and mid-sized agencies [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:02:50]. None of these speakers call the industry crooked; Blake's line is that you do not need conscious bad intent for an incentive to work against somebody, you just need the wrong incentive to exist year after year.

On the broker of record letter, the property, casualty, aviation and malpractice brokers converge on identical mechanics. Beyond Business Insurance defines it as a letter that 'essentially fires your current agent and hires another agent': the client signs it on letterhead, it goes to the carrier, and in usually five to ten business days the new agent is assigned, because carriers work with only one agent at a time [rnj58CEkGiE @ 00:00:48]. BWI Aviation Insurance's Matt White, who has watched hundreds of these letters over 11 years, says the carrier then gives the current broker five business days to obtain a rescinding letter before the policy transfers [LDwFFXhIu_U @ 00:01:19]. Strategic Builders-Dev calls that window 'the tick tock': the current agent gets five days to hold the request and call the client to rescind, so expect an uncomfortable call [79EPdRvCp3A @ 00:01:50]. eQuoteMD's Tim Ryan puts it at five to ten days and adds that the letter only applies to the specific carrier named, not to any other company in the market [IeLvLNXVKEo @ 00:03:18]. The shared rule: sign a BOR only to fire the incumbent or change who holds access to a market, never as a casual favor to get a quote.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The commission math is simple enough to run on a napkin, and the practitioners want owners to run it themselves. Benefits On The Fly's formula: take the monthly premium check, multiply by 5%, then by 12. Their worked case is 50 enrolled employees at $500 a month, a $25,000 monthly check or $300,000 a year, with $1,250 a month flowing back to the broker through the carrier [50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:01:29]. Rodney Mattos uses the 3 to 6% base commission range ProPublica reported in 2019: on a $1 million plan that is $30,000 to $60,000 a year, and a 10% premium increase lifts it 10% [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:02:59]. Benefits Blake scales it to a 250-life fully insured group paying $2 million at 5%, a $100,000 commission; a 10% renewal pushes premium to $2.2 million and the broker to $110,000, so the employer eats a $200,000 problem while the advisor collects a $10,000 raise for less work [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:08:03]. Blake then shows why self-funding can cut the broker's pay: the premium base collapses to stop-loss premium and admin fees because claims money stays in the employer's account, so the honest recommendation and the profitable one point in opposite directions [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:09:35].

The second toolset is disclosure plus public filings. Benefits Blake says that under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, brokers on ERISA group health plans are covered service providers under Section 408(b)(2) and must disclose in writing any direct or indirect compensation of $1,000 or more, reasonably in advance of signing, renewing, or extending, for any contract since December 27, 2021 [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:11:22]. Blake's ask: named dollar figures, not 'commissions vary,' and the last three years if you have never seen one. Enforcement is soft, with no filing and no penalty schedule, but a missing disclosure makes the contract unreasonable and creates prohibited transaction exposure. The free side-channel is the Form 5500 Schedule A, which reports commissions and fees paid to agents and brokers; Blake pulls it on the EFAST2 search site in about two minutes and points out every other employer's filing is public too, so you can benchmark peers your size in your area [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:15:54]. The catch: Schedule A runs about a plan year behind and misses agency-level indirect money, so treat it as the floor, not the ceiling [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:17:00]. Rodney Mattos adds the one question to put to any advisor, themselves included: how does your pay change if we stay, if we cut, or if we switch [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:06:27].

On the commercial side, Max Revenue's Micah lays out how a producer earns a BOR without asking a stranger to sign anything on day one. The sequence: build rapport, ask why the prospect took the meeting, have them rate their insurance experience on a 1-to-10 scale and say what would make it a 10, ask when they last went to market, then give a 30-second to one-minute explanation that underwriters release one quote to one agent per year, so whoever arrives first blocks everyone else [vTPBeVqiUY4 @ 00:17:48]. The pivot is the house-sale analogy: competitors scrap multi-agent quoting and hire the one agent who will market and stage them best, an approach Micah says has saved their clients an average of 17% [vTPBeVqiUY4 @ 00:22:14]. The ask is a no-cost review of premiums, coverages, and a marketing plan that does not tip off the incumbent, and Micah is upfront that about 70% of the time it simply confirms the client is in a good spot [vTPBeVqiUY4 @ 00:24:01]. Butch Zemar describes the paid version for benefits: charge for the proposal, credit it back if the broker wins the business, and write in performance-based wording so a broker who does not perform does not get paid [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:21:58].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Price the Current Broker in Dollars
   - Convert the embedded commission into a monthly and annual figure.
   - Multiply the monthly premium check by 5% and then by 12; on 50 employees at $500 a month that is a $25,000 check and $1,250 a month to the broker [Benefits On The Fly — 50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:01:45].
2. Force Disclosure and Pull Schedule A
   - Request the 408(b)(2) statement and download your own Form 5500 Schedule A before the next renewal meeting.
   - Ask for named direct and indirect figures, plus the last three years if never received; take two minutes to pull the 5500 and five minutes to read Schedule A, then watch how the request gets handled [Benefits Blake — m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:19:26].
3. Run Due Diligence Before Any BOR
   - Have the challenger review premiums, coverages, and a marketing plan without tipping off the incumbent.
   - The review answers whether you are overpaying, overexposed, and how to look more attractive to underwriters, with no time suck and no ruffled feathers with the current agent [Max Revenue — vTPBeVqiUY4 @ 00:23:23].
4. Execute the BOR With Eyes Open
   - Confirm the carrier's BOR rules, sign on letterhead, and plan for the transition period.
   - Some carriers refuse midterm BORs or any BOR at all [Rob Freeman | Insuring The Built Environment — C_YjzkmKog0 @ 00:01:17]; the outgoing agent keeps commission through the end of that year while the new agent effectively works for free until the new policy [Beyond Business Insurance — rnj58CEkGiE @ 00:03:34].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over how the advisor should get paid. Benefits On The Fly ran the standard 5% commission play for years, showing prospects they were already paying their broker and listing HR support, automated onboarding, compliance and claims advocacy to justify the check, then abandoned it because every premium increase raised their own income: 'I was winning while my client was losing' [50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:03:55]. Their replacement is a flat fee billed directly to the client, with every commission, bonus and incentive disclosed. rodneymattos frames the same fault line: base commissions of 3 to 6% rise in lockstep with premium, while the per-employee flat fee many advisors now charge does not grow with your rate [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:02:59]. Butch Zemar pushes from the other side. On a 10-life group the agency collects roughly $3,600 a year, but proposal, enrollment and service work priced at $300 an hour totals about $3,900, so the broker is in the hole before technology, HR help or wellness programs are counted [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:15:29]. Their fix is a hybrid: charge for the proposal, credit it back against fees or commissions on a win, and write performance-based language so a broker who does not deliver gives up pay [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:22:13].

Practitioners also split on whether commission keeps a broker carrier-neutral. Lee Soo, a licensed broker interviewed on The HR Hub, argues group commissions are pretty standard across carriers, so the broker gets paid wherever the business lands, while a carrier selling direct only pushes its own plan; they concede volume bonuses exist for brokers with a lot of business at one carrier [d5PvJwohdPU @ 00:02:43]. Benefits Blake says neutrality breaks at the agency level: overrides, growth bonuses and persistency bonuses pay more when clients simply stay, and moving a sound group to self-funding collapses the premium base and can cut the broker's pay [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:09:35]. Butch Zemar places retention bonuses with the big shops, saying they create a financial incentive not to shop plans, while small and mid-size agencies collect level commissions [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:02:50]. Evolved Benefits runs a different playbook altogether: MEC plans pay 12 to upwards of 25% monthly versus about 5% on major medical, and on a 200-employee group with only 8 or 9 on major medical, MEC enrollment of 50 to 80 lands commissions on par with or above the major medical check [H0MJJJNfYdo @ 00:02:23]. They pitch it as covering employees who could never afford even the leanest bronze plan.

The broker of record letter splits practitioners into camps. Beyond Business Insurance calls it a great tool with three legitimate uses: firing your agent, letting a new agent into a carrier your current agent already blocked, or letting your current agent reclaim a market a shopper reached first, and insists you never need to sign anything just to get quotes [rnj58CEkGiE @ 00:02:47]. BWI Aviation Insurance is stricter, calling 'sign this BOR and I can get you a better rate' the worst and most misleading way to use the letter [LDwFFXhIu_U @ 00:00:16]. Strategic Builders-Dev only requests a BOR when the prospect says they are unhappy with their agent, and tells a well-placed client to stay where they are [79EPdRvCp3A @ 00:00:40]. Max Revenue skips the letter as an opener entirely. Micah pitches free due diligence on premiums, coverages and underwriter feedback, a review agencies have billed at $5,000 to $15,000, then frames the decision like hiring one real estate agent to sell a house; they claim an average 17% savings and admit roughly 70% of reviews simply confirm the client is in a good spot [vTPBeVqiUY4 @ 00:24:01]. Their own label for the method is plain due diligence dressed up under the BOR name.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is invisible math. Benefits Blake runs it on a 250-life group paying $2 million a year at 5% commission: $100,000 to the broker. A 10% renewal lifts premium to $2.2 million and the broker's check to $110,000, so the employer inherits a $200,000 problem while the advisor collects a $10,000 raise for less work, because accepting a renewal is easier than changing carriers [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:08:03]. rodneymattos shows the same shape inside the medical loss ratio: on a $1 million plan up to $150,000 can go to everything besides care, and a 10% increase lifts that allowance to $165,000 [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:01:57]. They also report carrier bonuses of up to $150,000 for a single group, some tied to keeping the employer with that carrier at renewal rather than lowering cost, and admit sitting through the carrier golf outings and incentive dinners as a young producer [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:03:16]. Benefits On The Fly flags the behavioral tell: a broker you rarely hear from all year who pops up only at renewal, to make sure you renew so they keep getting paid, is a sign to start shopping for a different broker [50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:02:02].

The second trap is assuming disclosure law protects you. Benefits Blake notes that brokers expecting $1,000 or more in direct or indirect compensation must disclose it in writing ahead of a renewal, yet enforcement is thin: a 2021 field assistance bulletin, no filing, no penalty schedule, only prohibited transaction exposure if the disclosure never happened [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:12:51]. The reasonableness call belongs to the CFO who signs off, not the broker, so an employer who never received the statement is sitting on an unmet fiduciary obligation [m68BBrL5G18 @ 00:15:17]. Blake also cools the lawsuit hype: four December 2025 class actions named brokers beside LabCorp, Community Health Systems, United Airlines and Allied Universal over voluntary benefit commissions of 22 to 40%, but the Johnson and Johnson and Wells Fargo health plan cases were each dismissed on standing twice. Benefits On The Fly turned full commission and bonus accounting into a selling point, pitching it as keeping clients compliant with their CAA fiduciary requirements [50kdf1lBaO8 @ 00:05:24]. rodneymattos adds the reading discipline: the disclosure should land with whoever is responsible for the plan before you sign, and you read the bonuses, not just the commission [bmIls2hM4uk @ 00:05:56].

BOR execution carries its own landmines. BWI Aviation Insurance warns that when a second broker gets you to sign a BOR to unlock carriers the first broker already approached, the second broker takes over every quote the first broker worked up, and these letters tend to create more issues than they solve [LDwFFXhIu_U @ 00:02:04]. Their rule of thumb: research reviews beforehand, pick one great broker, and let only that broker shop the market. Rob Freeman warns that some carriers refuse midterm BORs and some refuse BORs entirely, so owning the choice of broker does not guarantee the carrier will honor a switch [C_YjzkmKog0 @ 00:01:37]. Beyond Business Insurance spells out the economics: the outgoing agent keeps commission through the end of that year, while the new agent is essentially working for free until the new policy takes effect [rnj58CEkGiE @ 00:03:47]. On the selling side, Butch Zemar describes employers who collect one broker's research and proposal, then hand it to their current broker to execute, which is why they now charge for the proposal and credit it back if they win the business [2DrBScDIwrk @ 00:21:32]. Their line to employers: you can take the proposal, they just need to get paid for that time.
