## Executive Summary

Litigators, benefits attorneys and PBM reformers agree that the 401(k) fee-suit playbook is being ported onto self-funded health plans. The Johnson & Johnson complaint is the template: loyalty and prudence claims built on PBM pricing data that CAA disclosures now expose. The street numbers are stark. Teriflunomide cost $81 to acquire, the plan paid $10,239, and Cost Plus sold it for $28.40. Generics are relabeled as specialty to move reimbursement from AWP minus 87 percent to AWP minus 12 percent. Settlements run $8-15M before fees. Operators answer with documented RFPs, liability caps, short lock-ins, item-level data and claimant-level analytics. Practitioners split on how rigid that process must be. Seyfarth's defense bench calls the steps options, says a committee is not required, and rejects pass-through pricing as the legal standard. Compliance advisors push committees, insurance, bonding and attestations.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Health plans are the new fiduciary battlefield, and the people who litigate and defend these cases agree on why. At Seyfarth Shaw, Sam frames the shift bluntly. 401(k) and 403(b) fee suits are roughly 20 years old and that market is saturated. The plaintiffs' bar is now turning to group health plans, armed with data that CAA 2020 disclosures push into the open, and plan sponsors themselves have started suing their own TPAs [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:04:12]. Frier Levitt's panel calls the Johnson & Johnson complaint the first case of its kind: employees suing their own employer over PBM mismanagement. The panel draws a straight line back to the retirement-plan fiduciary suits that came before it [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:03:09]. Justin Burgess opens his compliance briefing on the same note. Group health plan litigation is spiking, he says, and fiduciaries have to monitor fees, PBM fees above all, exactly the issue in the J&J suit [Bq89alxy8qQ @ 00:00:01]. Eric Dailey of Multnomah Group supplies the procedural accelerant. ERISA class filing rates are high, and after Northwestern the burden in these fights lies with the defense [_QhwEj0_Hrg @ 00:00:01]. Every camp reads it the same way. The retirement-plan playbook already exists, the disclosure data now exists, and self-funded health plans with large drug spend sit directly in its path.

The legal spine of these claims is equally settled across the speakers. Ben at Seyfarth Shaw anchors health plan exposure in two ERISA duties, exclusive benefit and prudence. Prudence is measured against the prudent-expert rule, not against what a well-meaning amateur running HR might have done [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:19:25]. Frier Levitt's panel compresses the same idea into loyalty and prudence. It points operators to paragraph 7 of the roughly 80-page J&J complaint, where the core allegations are concentrated, and treats that paragraph as the map for what plaintiffs will copy next [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:05:01]. Mike Hill of Total Control Health Plans settles the scope question. Self-funded medical, dental and vision plans are ERISA plans. The J&J class action names both the company and its benefits committee over the PBM and the consultant, not just the vendor [FvXAV4cqPRg @ 00:00:24]. John Barl, on The Alliance webinar, ties the duty to cash: fees must be disclosed, and Johnson & Johnson and Wells Fargo have both been sued over health plan fees [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:24:14]. Taken together, these four speakers treat a self-funded sponsor as a fiduciary over every dollar routed to TPAs, PBMs, brokers and consultants. A plaintiff will ask two questions about that money: did it serve participants, and would a prudent expert have paid it.

The baseline prerequisites are a written paper trail and a funded backstop. Justin Burgess lays out the floor. Form a committee, train it, document how vendors were selected, watch for hidden fees, and bring in ERISA attorneys. Then back the whole structure with fiduciary insurance and bonding [Bq89alxy8qQ @ 00:00:51]. Ben at Seyfarth Shaw agrees on training, delegation, documentation, audits and market checks, but he pushes back on rigid formalism. He tells sponsors the items are options rather than a checklist and that a committee is not required, while warning that these suits look back six years [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:57:22]. Barl's contract point is the hardest edge of the consensus. Without a written contract there is no ERISA Section 408(b)(2) exemption, or as he puts it, 'handshake agreements not going to cut it in this situation' [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:23:16]. On the claims side, Medilink Global describes what a TPA is supposed to deliver once that paperwork exists: provider empanelment, eligibility checks, letters of guarantee, 24x7 support, and end-to-end adjudication and payment [Tvw5ziI8ANo @ 00:01:12]. The sponsor owns the process even when the TPA runs the claims. With a six-year lookback, an undocumented vendor decision made today stays within a plaintiff's reach for years.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The money mechanics start with how PBMs actually get paid. Ben at Seyfarth Shaw walks through the models. Spread pricing means the PBM buys at $10 and sells to the plan at $20. Pass-through pricing adds a per-employee-per-month fee. Then there is rebate retention, plus the less visible cuts from pharmacy ownership and shared-savings arrangements [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:27:30]. Frier Levitt's panel names the current profit engine: generics relabeled as specialty drugs. Those drugs are reimbursed at AWP minus 12 percent instead of AWP minus 87 percent on molecules that cost $1 to $3 a pill, and exclusivity is steered to PBM-owned specialty pharmacies [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:09:14]. Mike Hill gives the headline number from the J&J complaint. Teriflunomide carries an average pharmacy acquisition cost of 91 cents per 14-milligram tablet, or $81 for a 90-day supply. The plan paid $10,239, a 12,403% markup [FvXAV4cqPRg @ 00:02:29]. Pramod John benchmarks the same drug at $28.40 through Cost Plus against roughly $10,000 under the plan, a gap of about 300 times [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:24:16]. For an operator, that arithmetic is the audit. When one molecule shows acquisition cost, transparent retail price and plan-paid price that far apart, the plan has the exact fact pattern plaintiffs are now pleading. Generic and specialty definitions in the PBM contract decide which of those three prices the plan ends up paying.

On the vendor side, John Barl on The Alliance webinar gives the most granular procurement script. Send the RFP to three to six vendors and allow about a month for responses. Summarize the responses in a spreadsheet for a benefits committee to review. Review template contracts from the three finalists, score each vendor on a 1-to-10 scale, and write down why the winner was chosen [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:37:33]. His negotiating benchmarks are specific. Typical liability caps run $50K per error, $1M per claim, or four times the last 12 months of fees, and he treats those as the starting ranges to argue from [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:38:42]. On term length, he accepts a one-year lock-in followed by 60 to 90 days' termination for convenience, and he calls a five-year lock-in risky [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:39:37]. Frier Levitt's fiduciary checklist covers the PBM side of the same desk. Send a CAA letter demanding consultant compensation, audit rebates and spread pricing, and review the formulary and MAC pricing [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:44:54]. Each step leaves a dated record: a scored spreadsheet, finalist contract redlines, a compensation letter, an audit report. Together they turn the abstract prudence duty into a file of evidence. That file shows a process, which is what the Seyfarth and Alliance speakers keep returning to as the core of any health plan defense.

The third mechanic is data, and the speakers agree that sponsors work blind without it. Pramod John says PBMs refuse to give plans their own data. That is why his action steps start with getting item-level pricing, then allowing fills at Cost Plus and other pharmacies, hiring a non-conflicted consultant, and exploring transparent models [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:47:45]. Self Insured Reporting sells the plumbing for that work. It offers more than 100 TPA and PBM integrations and loads a plan's data in under a week, so the sponsor is not waiting on a vendor to send reports [Ci7LnsZjk2g @ 00:00:52]. The AHealthcareZ presenter teaches how to read the output. Decide what you want to see first, then look at claimants rather than claims, by quarter or year. 5% of members drive 50% of costs at more than $100K each, and 20% drive 80% at more than $20K, so the intervention sweet spot is the $20K-$100K band [UQkzQUPP8JE @ 00:02:00]. In a 100-employee plan spending $1M, that means five people spend about $500K and fifteen people about $300K. His facility example sets the stakes: one dialysis patient cost $1M a year, equivalent to $10M in sales at a 10% margin [UQkzQUPP8JE @ 00:07:19]. Out-of-network surgery centers are his next target.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split runs between defense-side and plaintiff-side readings of the J&J pharmacy complaint. Sam at Seyfarth Shaw rejects the plaintiffs' framing outright: pass-through-only pricing is not the legal yardstick, reasonableness is, and the headline '10 times' gap against uninsured cash prices does not by itself make a breach [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:31:32]. Frier Levitt's panel reads the same document as a market reset. Jonathan Levitt and Pramod John say flatly that the standard of care just went up, and Pramod expects plan sponsors to end up signing compliance attestations the way 401(k) committees and SOX filers already do [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:18:50]. On the cross-subsidy defense, the camps collide head-on. Frier Levitt predicts J&J will argue cherry-picking, offsetting rebates and plan totality, then counters that anyone claiming rebates subsidize overpriced generics has to prove it with line-level pricing [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:33:46]. Pramod's numbers push the same way: Humira nets about $30,000 to $35,000 a year after rebates, while a Cost Plus biosimilar runs about $8,400, roughly a third of that net cost [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:37:02]. Operators sitting on a PBM contract have to pick a posture before plaintiffs pick it for them: defend on aggregate reasonableness, or assume plaintiffs win the line-item fight and price every script accordingly.

Practitioners also disagree on how close the threat actually is. Mike Hill at Total Control Health Plans frames the J&J case as an awareness event rather than a wave of imminent suits, noting the class is not yet certified and the case may be dismissed [FvXAV4cqPRg @ 00:02:49]. Seyfarth's Ian builds the dismissal path in detail: standing under a Supreme Court defined-benefit pension case, cost savings that never flow dollar-for-dollar to participants, a plaintiff who did not plead she paid more, and a complaint built on 40 drugs out of thousands [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:39:07]. The Class Action Weekly Wire reports the opposite outcome on a health-plan theory. In the Goodyear tobacco surcharge case, plaintiffs who had actually paid the $100-a-month surcharge cleared standing on a pocketbook injury, and Judge Boyko kept the case alive past a motion to dismiss, though the 10/8 ruling was only a partial win [NtoIMU293PI @ 00:03:54]. Multnomah Group's Eric Dailey sits with the pessimists on the defense side: filing rates are high, after Northwestern the burden lies with the defense, and settlements of $8 to $15 million before fees are heading the wrong direction for fiduciary insurance [_QhwEj0_Hrg @ 00:01:39]. Plan the defense budget around the Goodyear outcome, not the hoped-for dismissal.

On governance mechanics, the playbooks diverge on how much structure a sponsor needs. Justin Burgess treats a formal committee as step one: form it, train it, document vendor selection, watch for hidden fees and bring in ERISA attorneys [Bq89alxy8qQ @ 00:00:51]. Seyfarth's Ben pushes back on rigid templates, calling his recommended practices options rather than a checklist and stating that a committee is not required [EwDJFTGZkoE @ 00:57:22]. The Alliance's John Barl lands in the middle: run an RFP to three to six vendors, score them one to ten, have a benefits committee review the spreadsheet, document why the winner won, and remember the cheapest vendor is not required [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:37:33]. Frier Levitt attacks the scoring metric itself, arguing discount guarantees are the wrong questions to be asking and pushing item-level pricing and a non-conflicted consultant instead [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:54:25]. The practical difference is paper weight. Burgess and Barl want a documented committee trail on every vendor pick; Ben treats documentation as one tool among several, alongside training, delegation, audits, market checks and identifying every vendor fee; Frier Levitt cares less about the trail than whether the numbers being scored are the right numbers. Whichever playbook a sponsor runs, Ben's six-year lookback means the paper trail has to reach back that far.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The money trap practitioners keep hitting is the relabeled generic. Mike Hill walks the teriflunomide line from the J&J complaint: pharmacies acquire it at 91 cents per 14-milligram tablet, so a 90-day supply costs $81, yet the plan paid $10,239 for the same prescription, a 12,403% markup [FvXAV4cqPRg @ 00:02:29]. Frier Levitt explains the mechanism behind numbers like that: generics get relabeled as specialty, reimbursed at AWP minus 12% instead of AWP minus 87%, which works out to $1 to $3 a pill, and exclusivity is steered to PBM-owned specialty pharmacies [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:09:14]. Pramod John, Vivio's CEO, prices the same drug at Cost Plus at $28.40 against roughly $10,000 under the plan, about 300 times the transparent price [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:24:16]. Getting out late is expensive too, because plans that carve out specialty pay seven-figure penalties [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:38:45]. The economics check is blunt and lives in the contract. If the contract lets the PBM define what counts as a 'generic specialty' drug and route it to its own pharmacy, the overcharge hides inside a definition rather than inside the headline discount guarantee, and every renewal signed without fixing that definition stacks another year into the six-year lookback window plaintiffs use.

John Barl's contract traps at The Alliance are the most operational warnings any self-funded sponsor will hear. A handshake deal kills the exemption: without a written contract there is no ERISA 408(b)(2) protection, and prohibited transactions can carry a penalty of about 100% of the amount at issue [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:23:16]. Leverage evaporates the moment a vendor knows it won the RFP, so limitation of liability, indemnification and data access have to sit in the RFP before anyone responds; typical vendor caps run $50,000 per error, $1 million per claim, or four times the last 12 months of fees [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:40:26]. Lock-ins are the next trap: one year is acceptable with 60 to 90 days of termination for convenience after that, five years is risky and ten is, in Barl's words, kind of too long. The January CAA FAQ made sponsors responsible for gag clauses in downstream contracts they never see, which Barl calls a really bad result from an employer perspective [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:51:20]. Frier Levitt shows why the data-access term is not optional: PBMs refuse to give plans their own data, and Vivio was named in the J&J complaint without ever being contacted [jpLEKhmPpLs @ 00:47:45].

The administrative failures are quieter but just as expensive. In the Goodyear case, the Quit for Life program offered retroactive reimbursement, but the flyers omitted it, and the complaint alleges more than $17 million in surcharges collected from 2020 to 2025 [NtoIMU293PI @ 00:02:54]. Goodyear did win part of the fight, since collecting the surcharge was held a settlor function and plaintiffs alleged no plan loss, but the claims that survived turned on the wellness-rule disclosures. Barl's version of the same trap is eligibility drift: keep an ineligible long-term employee on the plan, let that person run up $1 million in claims, and the stop-loss carrier can deny them, which is why he wants the plan document amended and carrier sign-off obtained before covering anything outside it [2VQyGj0Z_oY @ 00:20:40]. On the provider side, Maritza Duran warns that TPA effective dates are staggered and can take several months, so a startup dental office can open out of network and lose visibility and foot traffic [uSNmcAr95UQ @ 00:03:46]. AHealthcareZ's economics check closes the loop: one dialysis patient costing $1 million a year equals $10 million in sales at a 10% margin, which is why report time belongs on claimants in the $20,000 to $100,000 band rather than on raw claim counts [UQkzQUPP8JE @ 00:07:19].
