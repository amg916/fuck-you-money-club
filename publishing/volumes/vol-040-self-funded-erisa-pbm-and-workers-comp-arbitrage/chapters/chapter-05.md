# Chapter 05: Pharmacy Benefit Managers: Following the Money

*PBM money-flow mapping, formulary tiers, rebates, spread pricing basics, PBM 'games,' industry consolidation and scrutiny, transparency claims, and the economics of owning a PBM*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Pharmacy benefit managers sit between plan sponsors, drugmakers, wholesalers and pharmacies, and every practitioner here agrees the money is made in the gaps between contracts the buyer never sees. Three PBMs, CVS Caremark, Express Scripts and OptumRx, control roughly 80 to 85 percent of the market, run the formulary auction, and own mail-order and specialty pharmacies. The street math is consistent: brand rebates priced as a percentage of list (PBM retention estimated at 20 to 45 percent), generic spread pricing ($6 billed, $2 paid to the pharmacy, 30 cents acquisition cost), and one worked example extracting $175 gross from a single $1,000 specialty script. Practitioners split on remedies: transparent pass-through PBMs and carve-outs versus legislated delinking and fiduciary duties, with ERISA preemption already knocking back state reform in Oklahoma.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The baseline every speaker builds on is leverage through scale. KFF Health News traces how PBMs began in the late 1960s as claims processors and, after the FTC stepped in and drugmakers like Merck and Eli Lilly spun off their PBM holdings, consolidated until three players, CVS Caremark, Optum Rx and Express Scripts, control about 80 percent of the market [P21Zb_OySIg @ 00:01:43]. Dr. Eric Bricker of AHealthcareZ puts the figure at 85 percent and flags both the PBM layer and the three-wholesaler layer (McKesson, AmerisourceBergen, Cardinal) as oligopolies where the plan sponsor has very few options [vT0NNXYjQ_Y @ 00:04:36]. Tyler Dutchover explains why employers hand over the keys anyway: a 100-person company with four insulin users gets zero discount from a manufacturer, while a PBM negotiating for millions of prescriptions extracts major concessions [ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:04:08]. Vault of Profit frames the same moat from the owner's side: a PBM managing 1 million lives gets laughed out of the room with zero discounts, a PBM managing 100 million lives holds commercial life or death over a manufacturer's balance sheet, and no HR director will take the call unless the card works at 60,000 retail counters on day one [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:04:13]. Scale is the entry ticket; everything else is built on top of it.

The second point of agreement is that the formulary is an auction and the rebate is the bid. STAT lays out the mechanic: drugmakers want tier 1 or tier 2 placement because that is where volume lives, so they offer the PBM a rebate, and the more expensive the list price, the higher the PBM's rebate tends to be [27INQuNycLA @ 00:02:27]. CancerCare states the incentive flatly: drugs with higher list prices can offer larger rebates, which can push a higher-priced drug into a preferred slot even when a lower-cost drug would be cheaper for the patient or the plan [f8oI5KtxoXM @ 00:01:56]. Audio Articles cites manufacturer rebates to PBMs climbing from $39.7 billion in 2012 to $89.5 billion in 2016 and notes that because rebates are calculated as a percentage of list price, high-deductible patients paying on list get hit harder [Cyz7QbHNjKc @ 00:02:11]. Tyler Dutchover runs the selection game with four insulin bids: the obvious lowest-price option loses, and the PBM picks Pharma Company 3 at a $300 sticker because it carries the biggest rebate, a $70 check split between PBM and client [ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:05:50]. The consistent read: the PBM's revenue rises with list price, so the auction rewards inflation rather than cheap net cost.

The third consensus pillar is that the money hides in contracts the buyer never sees. Benefits On The Fly sums up the three profit channels as spread pricing, retained rebates, and complex contracts where hidden fees and multiple revenue streams make it hard to track where the money actually goes [0ga6iJF0H1I @ 00:00:36]. Kendra Roberson of Faegre Drinker, speaking on the American Health Law Association podcast, defines spread pricing as the PBM charging the plan more than it pays the pharmacy and keeping the difference, and flags the Senate PBM Reform Act as proposing to prohibit it outright [bO0c-bD-CEA @ 00:13:15]. Audio Articles adds why it survives: the payment schedules PBMs generate for pharmacies are kept confidential from health plans, so the employer cannot compare what it paid against what the pharmacy received [Cyz7QbHNjKc @ 00:03:28]. Then comes vertical integration. Dr. Glaucomflecken's sketch has the insurer, the PBM and the pharmacy all turn out to be United Healthcare operating as Optum and Optum Rx, with every customer required to use the in-house pharmacy and the conglomerate boasting of 200 billion dollars a year [5rw4kNHNZyk @ 00:01:22]. The operator baseline: assume the PBM has a second customer, a pharmacy it owns, and a contract written so you cannot audit either.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start with the brand rebate, because every speaker who runs the numbers lands on the same retention trick. Dr. Eric Bricker's illustrative case in AHealthcareZ: a $400 monthly brand, a $100 negotiated rebate, the PBM keeps $75 and tells the employer it secured a $25 rebate, which Bricker argues is really a commission dressed up as a discount [-xH2R8NZ4pU @ 00:13:37]. Bricker flags the figures as invented to show the principle, but the incentive holds: higher list prices mean bigger rebates and a bigger retained slice. Vault of Profit uses a $1,000 specialty carton: the manufacturer sends a $400 rebate per carton, the PBM announces it is passing 80 percent back, the employer receives $320 and the PBM keeps $80 as a rebate retention fee [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:07:26]. Tyler Dutchover's insulin version shows the disclosure gap: the client is told it got a $50 rebate on a $300 drug, but the real check was $70 and the PBM quietly pocketed $20 [ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:07:26]. RxPreferred adds the formulary-exclusion angle: Tom's plan refuses a $20 generic antacid but covers $148 Nexium, the manufacturer pays a $50 rebate with $10 to the plan and $40 to the PBM, so the plan spends $138 instead of $20 [15IQO_jTMUM @ 00:03:22]. Same structure, four price points.

Spread is the second engine, and the numbers get sharper on generics. Bricker walks a generic with a $30 AWP: the PBM negotiates an 80 percent discount so the plan pays $6, pays the pharmacy a $2 maximum allowable cost (MAC), and keeps $4, while the pharmacy's actual acquisition cost, the NADAC, may be just 30 cents, leaving the pharmacy $1.70 [-xH2R8NZ4pU @ 00:16:24]. Bricker's punchline: AWP, the anchor for that 80 percent discount, stands for 'ain't what's paid', a number two quiet companies arguably make up. RxPreferred's spread game is blunter: the PBM pays the pharmacy $10, invoices the plan $100 plus a $2 admin fee, and nets $92 on one fill without the client knowing [15IQO_jTMUM @ 00:01:17]. Vault of Profit runs it on the $1,000 specialty script: the patient pays a $30 copay, the PBM pays the pharmacy $850, bills the employer $940, and captures a $90 spread; stack the $80 rebate retention and a $5 admin fee and the PBM extracts $175 gross without touching the box [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:06:29]. Tyler Dutchover's insulin example: employer billed $250, pharmacy paid $200 take-it-or-leave-it under threat of network removal, a $50 spread on top of the $20 hidden rebate cut [ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:07:50].

The third layer runs through owned pharmacies. RxPreferred's repackaging game: the PBM pulls the plan and the member into its mail-order pharmacy with deeper discounts, a waived admin fee and lower copays, then repackages the drug and assigns its own AWP. The Lipitor fill that costs AWP $441 minus 15 percent plus a $2 fee, $376.85, at retail becomes AWP $684 minus 20 percent with no fee, $547.20, by mail, which RxPreferred calls technically legal but not ethical [15IQO_jTMUM @ 00:02:19]. Rujul Desai of Covington & Burling, on the American Health Law Association podcast, describes the specialty-pharmacy version: the PBM tells manufacturers to give larger discounts at its own pharmacy and lower rebates, because rebates must be passed along but pharmacy discounts stay with the owner [bO0c-bD-CEA @ 00:15:51]. Vault of Profit adds the back-end squeeze on independents: post-adjudication direct and indirect remuneration fees, where an automated audit months later claws back thousands from future remittances with no appeal, because the master contract was the only way to accept cards from 80 percent of the town [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:10:55]. The counter-model SmithRx's Alan Pannier pitches is a PBM that owns no pharmacies or supply-chain assets and shows plan sponsors pharmacy contracts, manufacturer rebates and true drug cost through its Partner and Member Portals [KB9tHMlyhyA @ 00:00:55].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Map the seven players and both flows
   - Draw plan sponsor, plan, PBM, manufacturer, wholesaler, pharmacy and patient, then trace money one way and drugs the other.
   - Bricker's standard is that a benefits operator can reproduce the PBM money-flow chart on a napkin and explain it in plain English before negotiating anything [AHealthcareZ - Healthcare Finance Explained — vT0NNXYjQ_Y @ 00:01:08].
2. Demand full manufacturer-payment disclosure
   - Require the PBM to disclose rebates plus formulary, market penetration and bonus payments, and push for 100 percent pass-through.
   - Benefits On The Fly's first move is partnering with a transparent PBM that passes 100 percent of manufacturer rebates and discounts to the plan sponsor, then using fee-discovery tools to surface what clients actually pay [Benefits On The Fly — 0ga6iJF0H1I @ 00:01:57].
3. Audit billed price against pharmacy paid price
   - Ask for what the PBM paid the pharmacy versus what it charged the plan, and benchmark generics against MAC and NADAC instead of AWP.
   - Expect resistance: Dutchover reports PBMs can simply refuse, having written clauses calling the real drug cost a confidential trade secret, which is itself the signal that spread is in play [Tyler Dutchover — ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:09:52].
4. Restructure toward specialty control and flat fees
   - Concentrate on specialty spend and move to models with zero spread, zero retained rebates and flat per-script admin fees.
   - Vault of Profit reports new competitors promising zero spread pricing, zero retained rebates and flat $5 administrative fees per script, the exact model large employers are defecting toward [Vault of Profit — 351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:12:47].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — How Practitioners Pry Open the PBM Black Box**

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fault line is whether the PBM toll is worth paying at all. STAT frames two camps directly: PBMs work with hospitals and insurers, carry a lot of people to negotiate for, and can push better prices for everybody, yet nobody outside the deal knows how much of the rebate reaches the consumer and the PBM could be pocketing a larger share [27INQuNycLA @ 00:02:47]. KFF Health News lands on a blunt yes and no: PBMs have helped keep some drug prices and premiums lower, but consumers do not see the savings directly, may be limited in drug choice, and prices for some drugs keep going up [P21Zb_OySIg @ 00:03:28]. Audio Articles adds the strongest pro-PBM data point, reporting that the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services found larger negotiated rebates helped lower drug prices and slow spending growth over the last three years, while still flagging the percentage-of-list incentive [Cyz7QbHNjKc @ 00:01:17]. Benefits On The Fly takes the hard opposite line for employers: PBMs are just middlemen that evolved into major players profiting at your expense, and businesses with the right strategy are cutting pharmacy expenses by 30 to sometimes 50 percent [0ga6iJF0H1I @ 00:01:40]. Operators should treat the savings pitch and the extraction pitch as two sides they must price, not pick on faith.

The pass-through numbers themselves do not agree. Dr. Eric Bricker of AHealthcareZ pegs the share of manufacturer rebates passed to the plan at roughly 55 to 80 percent, with the PBM keeping 20 to 45 percent, and warns the manufacturer also pays formulary, market penetration and bonus payments outside the rebate line [vT0NNXYjQ_Y @ 00:03:31]. Audio Articles cites a study showing pass-through rising from 78 percent in 2012 to 91 percent in 2016, then immediately notes many small insurers and employers say they do not receive that share [Cyz7QbHNjKc @ 00:03:06]. Vault of Profit models an 80 percent pass-through on a $400 rebate, which still leaves the PBM $80 retained per carton before spread and admin fees are counted [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:07:26]. The American Health Law Association panel explains why headline percentages mislead: Rujul Desai describes a narrowing and narrowing of the definition of rebate to create other pockets of revenue, so even a 100 percent pass-through mandate turns on how rebate gets defined [bO0c-bD-CEA @ 00:14:31]. The playbook split is clear: one camp audits the rebate percentage, the other audits every manufacturer payment stream, including discounts booked at a PBM-owned specialty pharmacy.

The biggest structural argument is whether to stay bundled, carve out, or go with a transparent independent. AHealthcareZ notes that if you are fully insured your PBM is tied to your carrier, but a self-funded plan can carve out the PBM, use a small independent, or even run a competing insurer's PBM, such as UnitedHealthcare medical with CVS Caremark or Express Scripts pharmacy [-xH2R8NZ4pU @ 00:12:22]. On specialty carve-outs, Bricker calls the question highly controversial, pointing to an OptumRx paper arguing a specialty carve-out PBM is a bad idea against a Pfizer presentation to the Dallas Fort Worth Business Group on Health arguing it is a good one [vT0NNXYjQ_Y @ 00:08:30]. SmithRx's Alan Pannier argues the opposite of scale-through-ownership: SmithRx owns no pharmacies or supply chain assets, which the company frames as what aligns incentives with plan sponsors and patients [KB9tHMlyhyA @ 00:00:55]. Vault of Profit argues the economics run the other way for anyone building a PBM, insisting you need at least 5 million covered lives before negotiating your first rebate contract and your own mail order and specialty network to capture the therapies driving 70 percent of pharmacy spend [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:16:01].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most repeated firsthand trap is paying a fictitious price. RxPreferred walks through Tom's fill: the PBM paid the pharmacy $10, invoiced the plan $100 plus a $2 admin fee, so the plan paid $112 and the PBM kept $92 without its client's awareness [15IQO_jTMUM @ 00:01:17]. AHealthcareZ shows the generic version: a $30 AWP discounted 80 percent looks like a $6 win, but the PBM pays the pharmacy a $2 maximum allowable cost and the national average drug acquisition cost is 30 cents, which is why insiders say AWP stands for ain't what's paid [-xH2R8NZ4pU @ 00:16:24]. Vault of Profit runs the brand math on a $1,000 list specialty carton: the employer is billed $940, the pharmacy gets $850, and the $90 gap is spread taken with zero additional labor [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:06:06]. Tyler Dutchover adds the insulin case where the employer is charged $250, the pharmacy is told to take $200 or leave the network, and the PBM pockets $50 on top of a $20 rebate skim, because neither side can see the other's contract [ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:07:39]. The check is simple: demand the pharmacy-paid amount per claim, not the AWP discount.

The rebate engine fails the payer by rewarding the expensive drug. RxPreferred's rebate game has Tom's plan refusing a $20 generic antacid but covering $148 Nexium; of a $50 manufacturer rebate, $10 goes to the plan and $40 returns to the PBM, so the plan spends $138 instead of $20 [15IQO_jTMUM @ 00:03:03]. AHealthcareZ calls the structure what it is: on a $400 brand with a $100 rebate, a PBM that keeps $75 and passes $25 is collecting a commission, which means it wants more prescriptions of higher-cost drugs and wants prices to go up [-xH2R8NZ4pU @ 00:13:37]. Tyler Dutchover plays the former drug company CEO testifying before Congress that bringing a product with a low list price gets you punished financially with no uptake, because everyone in the supply chain makes money on a higher list price [ufeHEzWD1Cc @ 00:08:46]. CancerCare spells out who eats it: the list price at the counter is often far higher than what the PBM or plan pays after rebates, which drives the copay and the out-of-pocket hit on high-deductible plans [f8oI5KtxoXM @ 00:02:31]. The economics check is net cost after rebate per therapeutic class, not the size of the rebate check.

The third trap lives in PBM-owned pharmacies. RxPreferred's repackaging game lures plan and member into mail order with deeper discounts and waived copays, then reassigns its own AWP: Lipitor at retail runs AWP $441 less 15 percent plus a $2 fee for $376.85, while repackaged mail order runs AWP $684 less 20 percent for $547.20 [15IQO_jTMUM @ 00:02:01]. The National Community Pharmacists Association's fill-my-pockets PBM character brags about forcing patients into a mail order pharmacy the PBM owns, lousy service or not, and says not even your pharmacist can do anything about it [lbfUr3FiuyU @ 00:01:40]. Rujul Desai of the American Health Law Association flags the self-dealing play: a PBM tells manufacturers to give larger discounts at its own specialty pharmacy and lower rebates, because rebates must be passed along but pharmacy discounts are kept [bO0c-bD-CEA @ 00:15:51]. Vault of Profit describes the pharmacy-side kill shot, retroactive direct and indirect remuneration fees clawed back months after dispensing through automated audits, with no court of appeal and no alternative network [351RCg-LQ6I @ 00:10:55]. Any model that routes specialty volume through an affiliated pharmacy needs a per-claim price comparison against retail before signing.
