# Chapter 02: The Level-Funding Playbook: Quoting, Renewals, and Plan Redesign

*First-case preparation, broker step-by-step questions, long-term fully insured vs. level-funded planning, common pitfalls, renewal review checklists, small-business benefit options, direct primary care, and exposing who profits from rising premiums*

## Executive Summary

Level-funding operators from Security Health Plan, American Trust Administrators, BeneSmart and Benefit Experts converge on one model: fixed monthly payments split across administration, stop-loss and a pre-funded claims account the employer owns, with unused dollars refunded after run-out. They agree it fits healthy, stable groups that can commit to a full 12-month contract and want the upside a fully insured carrier keeps. Street mechanics are concrete: IHQs at 5 to 10 minutes per employee, quarterly wage filings, census with SIC and zip, Milliman Curve pharmacy underwriting, SHP specific stop-loss of $10,000 to $20,000 with 125% aggregate, and a 66% refund rate. Plan redesign levers include regional networks or RBP at $9 versus $25 access fees and DPC diverting about $40 PMPM. Practitioners split on surplus splits, IHQ thresholds and which carriers quote the smallest groups.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who covered the mechanics describes level funding the same way: a self-funded plan dressed in fully insured clothing. Security Health Plan's Garrett Johnson and Erin Long frame it as "a hybrid of a traditional fully insured plan and a self-insured plan," built from three monthly cost layers (administration, specific and aggregate stop-loss, and maximum claims pre-funding) with unused claim dollars refunded after run-out [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:02:19]. American Trust Administrators lays out the identical skeleton: a claims account, an admin bucket covering TPA, broker, PPO access and RBP repricer, and stop-loss, paid in level monthly installments with a true-up at year end, where "the claims account is the employer's money" [QBs9tuVEIhw @ 00:04:16]. BeneSmart's Rich strips it down further, calling it "simply a self-funded plan that is fully funded," assembled from four parts (TPA, network, PBM and stop-loss) and stressing that "your costs will not vary throughout the year" [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:08:13]. Benefit Experts pitches the same trade to small-business owners: steady monthly costs with a refund available if claims run low, a refund they tag as "Possible, not promised" [-KHMJD6TdmM @ 00:00:37]. The shared operating premise is ownership. The employer funds its own claims bucket, keeps what it does not spend, and caps the downside with stop-loss instead of renting coverage from a carrier that keeps every surplus dollar.

The practitioners agree just as hard on who should stay out of level funding. Tom Stein, CEO of the family-owned ATA, says "level funding is not for groups that have above average claims experiences," and he flags high ER utilizers, owners selling within one to two years, and seasonal or layoff-prone workforces as bad bets, because "level funding is a long-term solution" [dgpkKOJBv_s @ 00:01:22]. Security Health Plan's sweet spot matches: healthy ACA, grandfathered or transitional groups, or smaller large groups using level funding as a stepping stone to full self-funding, typically 10 to 100 enrolled employees excluding family members [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:03:58]. ATA's broker-question video names the only two legitimate disqualifiers as too many claims or an inability to commit to a 12-month contract [ise8qdroICE @ 00:01:42]. The farm-analogy episode adds the time-horizon test: "you need to know that you're going to be in business for the next 12 months before level funding, which is a self-funded program, starts to make sense" [8scqEaAolto @ 00:02:16]. The consensus filter is three questions asked before any quote gets pulled: is the group healthy, will it be around and intact for at least a full contract year, and does the owner actually want to keep the upside rather than hand it to a carrier.

The renewal is the trigger every speaker builds around. ATA aims its pitch at employers with 5 to 100 employees staring at a 20% fully insured increase, opening with one question, "Have we looked at level funded?", and noting the product has "been around for three and a half decades" while many brokers skip it "simply because it's a lot more work for them" [ise8qdroICE @ 00:00:59]. BeneSmart reports fully insured carriers raising rates 10 to 15% and says 45% of employers in the 5 to 200 life space have already left for level funded or self-funded plans [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:05:10]. Jennifer Shafer of JS Benefits Group refuses to treat the carrier's number as final: "we don't believe your employee benefit strategy should simply be about accepting the renewal your insurance carrier gives you," and she reviews plan design, claims, population and funding options instead [6P2sMVFtCtk @ 00:00:37]. ATA's verdict on the fully insured side is blunt: "All of the upside is retained by the insurance provider, the carrier" [8scqEaAolto @ 00:01:44]. The operator takeaway is consistent across the room. A big renewal is the opening to re-market into level funding, and ATA's instruction for a broker who will not run those numbers is to go find another broker.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Quoting starts with paperwork, and every underwriter on the panel wants the same packet. ATA's first-case checklist is the quarterly wage and tax report, enrollment forms and IHQs collected under a boss-set Friday deadline, plus census, SIC code and zip code, all routed through a general agent out to carriers, TPAs, pricers and MGUs [E2z3UzEl2Ds @ 00:01:14]. ATA's broker video budgets IHQs at "5 to 10 minutes from each one of your employees" and uses the quarterly wage filing to validate who actually belongs on the plan [ise8qdroICE @ 00:02:28]. Security Health Plan underwrites with Milliman's Curve pharmacy tool, which requires a full member census, layers on medical applications that Erin Long admits are a "tough pill to swallow," and pulls 24 months of claims experience where it exists [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:14:19]. BeneSmart's Rich works the larger end of the market: he submits the current carrier invoice, renewals, MLRs and high-cost claimant reports, and reserves IHQs for groups under 25 [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:16:20]. ATA's rule for the whole process is that "level funding rewards honesty and it punishes shortcuts," and that clean submissions for underwriting produce better plans and better renewals long term, so shaded or incomplete IHQs come back to bite the broker at the very first renewal.

Pricing turns on stop-loss and on how the surplus gets split. Security Health Plan quotes specific stop-loss between $10,000 and $20,000 and aggregate at 120 to 125%; its own specific limit steps by contract count, with $10,000 for 10 to 25 contracts, $15,000 for 26 to 50, and $20,000 for 51 to 100, while aggregate stays fixed at 125 [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:19:02]. SHP's worked refund example pre-funds $120,000 of claims, spends $95,000, and returns $15,000 to $25,000 depending on a 60 to 100% refund rate; its current rate is 66% of unused claim dollars after run-out. ATA pushes brokers to ask "What's my surplus?" and to target 100% retention over time, stair-stepping toward it if needed [ise8qdroICE @ 00:05:09]. ATA's time-horizon matrix ties the split to tenure: a 50% surplus split, which it calls sharecropping, may fit a 1-year to 18-month horizon, while 2 to 3-plus years calls for 100% [8scqEaAolto @ 00:04:50]. On the carrier side, BeneSmart lists minimums of Cigna 25, UHC 5 and BCBS/Anthem 10, says Aetna will level fund down to two lives in certain markets, counts roughly 15 unbundled TPA partners, and treats admin and stop-loss fees as negotiable every year [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:12:35].

Plan redesign is where the renewal savings get manufactured. Security Health Plan runs level funding on a fixed SPD with 12 preset designs per network, while true self-funded designs flex within ERISA and ACA rules [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:18:24]. ATA's network play is to question whether a group even needs a national network like Cigna or Aetna: a tight regional network or RBP tends to carry "better discounts" and lower access fees, $9 a month against $25, and a knee surgery at $22,500 locally versus $25,000 nationally adds up to tens of thousands of dollars a year on a 50-life group, at the cost of fewer hospitals [ise8qdroICE @ 00:04:18]. BeneSmart stacks steerage, direct contracts, RBP and DPC on top of the funding change, and uses the savings to push zero or disappearing deductibles [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:06:35]. Milliman actuary Fritz Busch, on Hint Health's panel, puts a number on the DPC lever: against an $85 fee, diverted claims are worth about $40 PMPM and avoided services about $25, covering roughly 80% of the fee before quality and health-improvement value are counted, and he calls DPC a slam dunk for high-cost groups [5ztwBEIpJjs @ 00:19:47]. Busch also tells operators not to build a pro forma straight off those study figures, so the DPC math belongs in the pitch as directional support.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Surplus splits draw the sharpest line between carrier and TPA playbooks. Security Health Plan frames the refund as a dial: on $120,000 pre-funded with $95,000 used, the $25,000 left over returns $15,000 to $25,000 at a 60 to 100 percent refund rate, and SHP itself currently pays back 66 percent of unused claim dollars at the end of the run-out period [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:08:16] [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:15:46]. American Trust Administrators treats anything short of full retention as a negotiating target. Their broker script tells employers they are entitled to their claims data and should aim for 100 percent surplus retention over time, with stair-stepping toward that number acceptable [ise8qdroICE @ 00:05:09]. The same firm's farm analogy brands a 50 percent split as sharecropping, since unused crop should stay in your own granary, yet it concedes that a 50 percent surplus can fit an owner with a one-year to 18-month horizon, while two to three years or more should command the full 100 percent [8scqEaAolto @ 00:04:12]. The operator read is blunt: the refund percentage is a contract term to match against how long the owner expects to stay in business, and it belongs on the table at every quote and every renewal rather than accepted as a fixed feature of the product.

Practitioners split on how heavy the underwriting file should be. American Trust Administrators makes individual health questionnaires non-negotiable, budgeting five to ten minutes per employee and pairing them with quarterly wage and tax filings that validate who actually belongs on the plan [ise8qdroICE @ 00:02:28]. Their pitfalls video adds that IHQs are how the plan gets priced to the reality of that group. Rich at BeneSmart runs a lighter file built on the carrier invoice, renewals, MLRs and high-cost claimant reports, reserving IHQs for groups under 25 [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:16:20]. Security Health Plan sits between the two: it pulls Milliman curve pharmacy data off a full member census, uses 24 months of claims experience where it exists, and still requires medical applications that the team admits are a tough pill to swallow [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:14:19]. Carrier floors diverge as well. Rich lists Cigna at 25, UHC at 5 and BCBS or Anthem at 10, and says Aetna will level fund all the way down to two in certain markets, while SHP writes 10 to 100 enrolled employees, excluding family members [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:12:35]. The practical consequence: the same census can be quotable at one carrier and dead at another, which is why ATA routes submissions through a GA to carriers, TPAs, pricers and MGUs.

The ladder from fully insured to self-funded is contested too. Security Health Plan pitches level funding to healthy ACA, grandfathered or transitional groups, and to smaller large groups as a stepping stone to self-funding [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:03:58]. American Trust Administrators sizes level funding at 5 to 250 lives and self-funding at 125 lives and up depending on CFO sophistication, flags a $500,000 cash call on a 250-life self-funded group, and says flatly not to self-fund below 100 lives [QBs9tuVEIhw @ 00:08:36]. Other operators never lead with level funding at all. Benefit Experts lays four options side by side: a small group plan where the business sets its premium share, a level-funded plan for relatively healthy teams whose refund is possible, not promised, an HRA with a fixed monthly allowance, and payroll-deducted voluntary benefits that can cost the company nothing [-KHMJD6TdmM @ 00:00:37]. Gusto's self-serve flow recommends small-group, level-funded and health-allowance plans by location and headcount and claims small group prices are set by law and don't vary by broker, while on the level-funded side ATA warns that brokers dodge the product because it is more work and tells owners to find another broker if theirs refuses to look [z_Qpt9fQLWg @ 00:01:03]. Benefit Experts starts every engagement with budget and people before showing a single option.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is leaving early. American Trust Administrators states it is not a good idea to leave a level-funded plan early, because stop-loss will not honor a broken contract [TKRoJd6Glxw @ 00:01:20]. Tom Stein frames it as a 12-month contract that no group should terminate off of, and screens out owners planning to sell within one to two years plus seasonal or layoff-prone workforces [dgpkKOJBv_s @ 00:03:35]. ATA's long-term comparison sets the bar plainly: you need to know you will be in business for the next 12 months before level funding starts to make sense [8scqEaAolto @ 00:02:16]. Runout is the second shock. Contracts are expressed as 12/18 or 12/24, and under a 12/24 a group that runs well in year one still has that money sitting in accounts for an additional 12 months; ATA's example is a $115,000 surplus that does not reach the employer until a year later [TKRoJd6Glxw @ 00:05:38]. The same video notes savings sit outside the benefit trust and can fund something like a work truck, which makes the delay real money an owner is waiting on. Show the runout math before signature or the owner will expect a refund check at month thirteen.

Late claims and admin drag are the operational bottlenecks. ATA reports an MRI billed four years after the fact that turned into a negotiation, and says that conversation happens a couple of times a year across its book of business [TKRoJd6Glxw @ 00:02:39]. The same firm lists the paperwork load that comes with the plan: 1095-Bs, PCORI and an annual 5500. Speed is the other trap. ATA opens its first-case walkthrough by admitting this is not as fast as fully insured and warns brokers not to oversell speed to the client [E2z3UzEl2Ds @ 00:04:11]. Rich at BeneSmart wants enrollment done 30 days before the start date and reports seeing it squeezed to seven days, then describes the post-launch mess: providers confused by the logo on the new ID card, fixable through education [UOjbo9Dprhw @ 00:10:38]. Design freedom is also narrower: Security Health Plan's level-funded groups choose from 12 preset designs per network under a fixed SPD, while self-funded employers get flexible designs within ERISA and ACA rules [8FR_2qIAL34 @ 00:18:24]. Budget the calendar for paperwork, provider education and a 30-day enrollment window before quoting an effective date. ATA's rule for every surprise on the case is to share the bad news early.

Underwriting dishonesty and bad-risk groups blow up the economics fastest. Tom Stein is direct that level funding is not for groups with above-average claims experience and flags high ER use as a disqualifier [dgpkKOJBv_s @ 00:01:22]. ATA's case-prep rule is that level funding rewards honesty and punishes shortcuts, or in harder terms, rewards the honest and punishes the liars; clean submissions produce better plans and better renewals long term [E2z3UzEl2Ds @ 00:02:31]. The vendor-side numbers need the same scrutiny. In the Hint Health panel, Milliman actuary Fritz Busch prices an $85 DPC fee against roughly $40 PMPM in diverted claims and $25 in avoided claims, about $68 in total, leaving a $17 gap that quality value closes to break-even, and explicitly warns against building a pro forma from those figures [5ztwBEIpJjs @ 00:20:18]. Dave Chase adds the broker-side check: Health Rosetta found up to 17 undisclosed revenue streams the employer didn't know about, and the fix starts with getting access to claims data, by demand letter if needed [nrL0pYx-hQo @ 00:22:04]. Clint Flanigan of Nextera separately warns about commission-paid brokers. Tom Stein's last check is behavioral: if the client doesn't know it is their money, part of the value of level funding is taken away.
