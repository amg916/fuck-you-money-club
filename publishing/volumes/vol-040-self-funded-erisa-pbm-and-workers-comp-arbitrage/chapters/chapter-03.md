# Chapter 03: Stop-Loss Insurance: Specific, Aggregate, and the Contract Traps

*Specific vs. aggregate coverage, aggregate corridors, aggregating specific deductibles, lasers, MGUs, reimbursement mechanics, filing stop-loss claims, and who pays the million-dollar claim*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Stop-loss practitioners agree on the foundation: it is a reimbursement contract owned by the self-funded employer, split into specific coverage for catastrophic individual claims and aggregate coverage capping total plan spend. Spencer, Pete Dorne, and the Self-Funded Sketchbook put 80 to 90% of premium into the specific layer, while aggregate is cheap sleep insurance built on expected claims plus a 25% corridor that pays out for under 3% of groups. The street mechanics turn on numbers: a $600,000 laser on a $150,000 spec quietly adds $450,000 of exposure, a $50,000 ag spec trades roughly dollar for dollar against premium, and unbundled PBM and TPA reporting can hide a breached deductible entirely. The fault lines run between carrier laser leverage and ag-spec counter-moves, paying for experience refunds versus taking a premium cut, and who files claims when BUCA ASO setups never send them.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who walks through stop-loss starts in the same place: it is a reimbursement contract bought by the self-funded employer, not by employees, and it comes in two layers that usually get bought together. Spencer of Self-Funded frames self-funding as the employer saying it will absorb and manage the risk itself, then buying specific and aggregate coverage to mitigate and predict that risk, noting that outside true jumbo employers the vast majority purchase both together [4cndcrJWUGg @ 00:01:29]. Self Insured Reporting states flatly that stop-loss covers the employer rather than the employees, with specific protecting against any single large claimant and aggregate capping the total claims the employer pays in a period [HuaDk3XBOrc @ 00:00:39]. Andrew and Don of This STUFF is complicated call reimbursement the universal truth from which everything else flows, because claims lag of 30 to 45 days, sometimes multiple months, means a claim has to be paid, measured, and processed before anyone can be repaid [q36fieahkDs @ 00:01:33]. Pete Dorne and Spencer Smith at ParetoHealth reinforce it: the employer owns the risk and writes the check, and the carrier reimburses the dollars above the deductible after the fact, which is why advance funding gets treated as a separate enhancement rather than the default [4VhWXTCGo74 @ 00:01:24].

On where the money goes, the numbers line up across speakers. Spencer puts 80 to 85% of stop-loss premium on the specific layer because the underwriter is trying to predict the wildly unpredictable individual catastrophic claim, and notes the contracts theoretically carry an unlimited maximum, so a $20 million claim still gets picked up above the deductible [4cndcrJWUGg @ 00:03:19]. In the Self-Funded Sketchbook the figure climbs to 85 to 90% or more depending on group size and deductible, with specific described as low frequency, high severity risk: a hemophiliac joining the plan or a premature baby parked in the NICU for three or four weeks [adwLQHPv5YI @ 00:02:21]. Pete Dorne confirms 85 to 90%, and adds the reason it is getting worse: one of ParetoHealth's stop-loss carrier partners saw a 45% increase in the size and severity of large claims, million-dollar-plus, in a single policy year, with claims that ran $250,000 five years ago now landing at $500,000 or $600,000 [4VhWXTCGo74 @ 00:02:06]. Tyler Dutchover lands on the same logic in plain English: because nobody can predict a three-month NICU stay or a coronary bypass, specific is the more expensive of the two coverages [b2StkpG4yOY @ 00:02:34].

Aggregate gets the opposite treatment: cheap, predictable, and rarely triggered. Spencer calls it sleep insurance, built by taking expected claims of $1 million and adding a typical 25% buffer to set a $1.25 million attachment point, with a hit on maybe two to three percent of contracts and coverage usually capped around $1 million, though some BUCAs offer unlimited [4cndcrJWUGg @ 00:05:27]. Pete Dorne derives the attachment point the same way, prior-year net claims plus trend plus a 25% aggregate corridor that sets the maximum not-to-exceed bucket on small claims, places expected claims at roughly 80% of attachment, and puts aggregate hits at less than 3% of all self-funded groups [4VhWXTCGo74 @ 00:08:54]. Spencer's corridor episode adds the kill shot on why it is cheap: with a 25% corridor the underwriter has to mispredict by 25% before aggregate pays, which takes bad data, a mistake, a genuinely terrible claims year, or an underwriter getting too aggressive on the attachment to win the business [Dy4S9Ie5mEM @ 00:05:16]. Tyler Dutchover runs it at 125% of expected claims, sometimes slightly higher, and positions aggregate as protection from death from a million cuts across the whole workforce rather than one catastrophic claimant [b2StkpG4yOY @ 00:03:37].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Run the math the way the practitioners do. Tyler Dutchover's model: a 150-person group expecting $1.5 million in claims sets a $75,000 specific attachment; the instant one person hits $75,001 the carrier pays 100% of the rest, so a $1 million NICU baby costs the employer $75,000 and the carrier reimburses $925,000 [b2StkpG4yOY @ 00:01:26]. The group's aggregate cap at 125% lands at $1.87 million; in a brutal year that hits $2.5 million, the carrier covers the final $630,000, and the critical interaction is that the $75,000 the employer paid on the NICU claim counts toward the aggregate limit while the $925,000 the carrier paid does not [b2StkpG4yOY @ 00:04:05]. Spencer's base case is identical in structure: a $100,000 specific deductible, a $125,000 claimant, a $25,000 reimbursement, and claims can be medical only, Rx only, or combined across the policy period [4cndcrJWUGg @ 00:02:45]. Pete Dorne adds the monthly reality underneath it: the TPA bills the employer every month, the employer funds whatever utilization came in, and those swings, the EKG effect, are the employer's to carry unless year-end claims clear the attachment point or the group bought an aggregate accommodation [4VhWXTCGo74 @ 00:07:45].

Lasers are where a clean-looking renewal hides the real bill. Spencer's illustration: everyone on the plan carries a $100,000 spec, but one known high-cost claimant gets a $250,000 laser, so the employer now owns another $150,000 of risk on that person alone, and on a $300,000 claim the carrier reimburses only $50,000 [hiWm3Y4k6SI @ 00:03:17]. Carriers do it because a predictable claimant, say someone on a $20,000-a-month drug, would breach the deductible with not enough premium in the case to stay profitable, and in extreme cases they exclude the person entirely [hiWm3Y4k6SI @ 00:05:25]. Ralph Weber of Benefit Whisperer shows the trap at renewal: premium moves from $84,000 to $89,000, a single-digit increase nobody has seen in years, but a $600,000 laser on one claimant against a $150,000 spec means a $500,000 claim leaves the employer an extra $350,000, and up to $450,000 of added exposure if it passes $600,000 [6E1eObzSNLc @ 00:01:05]. Spencer's counter-move from the ag spec episode: when a small laser sits close to the spec, like $125,000 over a $100,000 deductible, offer a $50,000 ag spec in exchange for dropping it, and the carrier will often accept [VatFfWseBbI @ 00:07:34].

The ag spec is the most aggressive premium play taught. Spencer credits Scott Campbell of AmWINS with the gift-card analogy: add a $50,000 aggregating specific deductible and the carrier most often cuts premium by $50,000, but that money can only be spent on the shared layer of claims sitting above the regular spec [VatFfWseBbI @ 00:01:42]. The bucket math: claimants run $25,000 and $10,000 over a $100,000 spec, filling $35,000; a third claimant $45,000 over contributes only the $15,000 needed to fill the $50,000 bucket, and the remaining $30,000 spills to the carrier for reimbursement, with one person able to blow through both layers alone [VatFfWseBbI @ 00:05:04]. Spencer's pitch is heads you win, tails you break even: no spec claims means $50,000 saved, a full bucket means $50,000 paid against a $50,000 premium cut, and it beats an experience refund that charges roughly 5% for a chance at 10% back [VatFfWseBbI @ 00:08:18]. Once reimbursements are flowing, Andrew and Don put carrier turnaround at 10 to 12 business days about 90% of the time after submission, stretching to 15 to 20 business days at year end when filings pile up [q36fieahkDs @ 00:04:47].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Set the specific deductible and aggregate corridor
   - Choose the per-person corporate deductible and confirm the corridor and monthly aggregate factor before the policy period starts.
   - Budget monthly somewhere between expected claims and maximum liability, using the monthly aggregate factor that flexes with headcount, and confirm whether the corridor is 25%, 20%, 15%, or a 10% level-funded corridor [Self-Funded — Dy4S9Ie5mEM @ 00:02:07].
2. Audit the renewal for lasers before signing
   - Pull the stop-loss contract and check every named laser against the standard specific deductible.
   - Ralph Weber's workflow: go to whatsinyourcontract.com, pick administrative services agreement, PBM, or stop-loss from the drop-down, upload the file, enter an email, and run check my contract before you renew [Benefit Whisperer — 6E1eObzSNLc @ 00:01:28].
3. Assign exactly one claim filer up front
   - Decide whether the TPA, the broker team, or a GA files stop-loss claims, and lock it in at setup.
   - Get a monthly data dump, file feed, or reporting portal access from the administrator, and pick one entity so there is no duplicate filing, no reporting disconnect, and no finger-pointing later [Self-Funded — AM2DB13I88c @ 00:04:01].
4. Merge medical and Rx and track every claim to payment
   - Make sure the carrier receives both medical and PBM claim reports, then monitor each filing through reimbursement.
   - Read your reports, know when a claim is going to be filed and when it has been filed, and understand what eligibility and plan document steps the carrier needs to release the money [This STUFF is complicated — q36fieahkDs @ 00:07:39].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Every practitioner agrees specific coverage absorbs most of the premium, but the split they quote differs. Spencer of Self-Funded puts roughly 80 to 85% of stop-loss premium on the spec side because the carrier is trying to predict the wildly unpredictable individual catastrophic claim [4cndcrJWUGg @ 00:03:19]. Pete Dorne of ParetoHealth answers 85 to 90% when asked the same question on the ParetoHealth channel, confirming spec is where the real dollars sit [4VhWXTCGo74 @ 00:01:52]. The corridor is where playbooks diverge most. Spencer calls a 25% buffer the most common self-funded corridor, yet reports 20% and 15% corridors in the market and 10% corridors on level funded contracts, which means the underwriter only has to miss by that smaller margin before aggregate pays [Dy4S9Ie5mEM @ 00:04:18]. Tyler Dutchover models the aggregate attachment at 125% of expected claims, sometimes slightly higher, so a 150-person group expecting 1.5 million in claims carries a 1.87 million aggregate cap, and a brutal 2.5 million year triggers a 630,000 aggregate payout [b2StkpG4yOY @ 00:03:37]. The operator takeaway: corridor width is a quoted variable, not a constant, and a tighter corridor moves the point where the cheap aggregate layer actually starts paying money back to the employer.

The cash-flow playbook splits between pure reimbursement and advance funding. Andrew Kikenbball of This STUFF is complicated calls the reimbursement structure the universal truth everything else flows from, since a claim has to be filed, adjudicated and paid, with claims lag running 30 to 45 days and sometimes multiple months [q36fieahkDs @ 00:01:33]. Lauren at Roundstone Insurance pitches the opposite experience: on a million-dollar claim against a 100,000 deductible, the employer pays the first 100,000 and does not have to pay and wait to be reimbursed, because the captive can advance it for groups that are not comfortable putting the cash out, which matters for the 50 to 300 employee sweet spot Roundstone targets [FPn0kt8qe6U @ 00:06:14]. The Benefits Group describes stop-loss the same way for level funded and self-funded plans, saying the carrier advances money into the claims account so a million-dollar claim never drains it past the 50,000 specific deductible [7-gc1zHvZbc @ 00:01:34]. PeopleKeep draws the hard line on who should skip the model: a mostly older or family-heavy workforce runs a higher claim frequency, and that employer may decide waiting for reimbursements is not worth the initial expensive claim payouts and look at alternatives to self-insured plans [87JIyLFot-o @ 00:03:19].

On lasers, two Self-Funded playbooks compete. Spencer's baseline is straight negotiation: get the carrier to pull the laser back, or if they dig in, extract some premium to offset it, and treat it as part of the game, since anyone self-funded long enough has likely had a laser imposed or at least proposed on a quote [hiWm3Y4k6SI @ 00:04:54]. Spencer's preferred counter is the ag spec swap. With a 100k spec and a 125,000 laser, the move is to throw on a 50k ag spec, take the 50,000 premium cut, and drop the laser; when the numbers are that close the stop-loss carrier often accepts, though not every underwriter will agree [VatFfWseBbI @ 00:07:34]. Spencer then attacks the experience refund directly: a carrier offering a possible 10% dividend for good claims experience will charge around 5% for it, so why pay five percent to potentially get ten back when an ag spec lowers premium without charging for the privilege [VatFfWseBbI @ 00:08:18]. Roundstone runs a different reward model, redistributing unused premium at the captive layer, and Lauren describes the best part of account management as calling a group or broker to say a percentage of premium is coming back [FPn0kt8qe6U @ 00:09:29]. One camp banks the upside as a lower premium up front; the other pays it out after the year closes.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap reported is the laser buried inside a renewal that looks friendly. Ralph Weber of Benefit Whisperer walks through a stop-loss renewal moving from 84 to 89,000, a single digit increase nobody has seen in years, with one word hidden in the contract: a 600,000 laser on one identified claimant against a 150,000 specific deductible [6E1eObzSNLc @ 00:00:29]. If that claimant runs 500,000 in eligible claims, the policy never kicks in and the employer is on the hook for an extra 350,000; once claims pass 600,000, the extra exposure reaches 450,000 [6E1eObzSNLc @ 00:01:05]. Spencer of Self-Funded explains the mechanics behind it: a claimant who is sick or projected to be sick represents known risk the carrier cannot absorb and still stay profitable, so that person gets a 250k spec while everyone else sits at 100k, leaving the employer funding another 150,000 of risk tied to a single name [hiWm3Y4k6SI @ 00:03:17]. The worst version is an outright exclusion, rare but used when someone poses millions of dollars of risk to the plan and no premium load could absorb it, at which point the carrier carves that person out of coverage entirely [hiWm3Y4k6SI @ 00:05:25]. Every laser moves dollars back onto the employer, never the other way.

Claims filing is where brokers get burned. Spencer of Self-Funded reports seeing a really big errors-and-omissions claim when someone at a broker shop dropped the ball and did not file a stop-loss claim in a timely manner, handing the carrier an opportunity to deny it [AM2DB13I88c @ 00:01:10]. The root cause is structural: with a BUCA ASO and a third-party stop-loss carrier, the ASO overwhelmingly is not sending claims to the carrier, and from Spencer's time at Sun Life, brokers would call demanding a 300,000 reimbursement only for underwriting and the claims department to respond, what claim [AM2DB13I88c @ 00:02:56]. Unbundled PBM setups add a second blind spot: a member with 10,000 in medical and 100,000 in prescription claims totals 110,000 against a 100k spec, but if only the medical reporting package reaches the carrier, nobody, including the broker, knows a stop-loss claim exists [AM2DB13I88c @ 00:05:35]. Don McCully of This STUFF is complicated puts a number on the pattern, saying that when they entered the space nine out of 10 E&O claims came from stop-loss, and pointing out that stop-loss is filed as a property and casualty product rather than accident and health [q36fieahkDs @ 00:02:25]. The exposure lands on whoever was supposed to be watching the feeds.

Contract wording and plan documents create the quieter losses. Andrew Kikenbball and Don McCully warn that the stop-loss contract does not always say what it means because reimbursement runs off the plan document, a separate contract, so operators should hunt for plan document amendments and a plan mirroring clause stating whatever is in the document is in the contract [q36fieahkDs @ 00:03:23]. PeopleKeep adds that a stop-loss carrier may have policies that refuse reimbursement for certain claims, and a misunderstanding can produce significant liability, so the document needs to be examined with the insurer or broker before the plan period begins [87JIyLFot-o @ 00:01:46]. On the aggregate side, Spencer flags that a 25% miss usually means bad data, an outright mistake, a genuinely bad claims year, or an underwriter getting way too aggressive on the attachment point to win the business [Dy4S9Ie5mEM @ 00:05:34]. Roundstone's claims team shows what unreviewed payment costs: a long hospitalization billed intermittently and then billed again for the entire stay put about a million dollars in front of them twice, and Lauren admits they would not have believed it a couple of years earlier without seeing it firsthand [FPn0kt8qe6U @ 00:22:30]. Somebody has to read the bills, not just pay them.
