# Chapter 07: Reference-Based Pricing and Direct Contracting

*RBP vs. PPO and commercial claims pricing, Medicare-plus pricing, chargemasters, negotiation best practices, surprise-billing interaction, member advocacy, RBP costs, and direct employer–provider contracts*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree reference-based pricing is just a different way to pay a claim inside a self-funded or level-funded plan: build up from Medicare instead of discounting down from a hospital chargemaster. Quoted savings run 20 to 40 percent, and ClaimDOC reports a 300-employee agricultural group cutting plan spend 41.4 percent, from $819 to $480 per employee per month, with zero known balance bills. The street mechanics: pay facilities the greater of Medicare plus 25 or cost plus 20, clinically audit every inpatient bill, pre-sign physicians through outreach that wins 95 percent acceptance, and cut single-case agreements on the rest. Operators split on posture, from Montana's arm-twisting that cut hospital pay to 200-220 percent of Medicare, to pay-and-defend versus negotiate-and-settle, while one producer reverted a client to PPO within months over balance-bill complaints.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who prices claims for a living lands on the same core mechanic: reference-based pricing is not a plan, it is a different way to pay a claim. Lester Morales frames the PPO as top-down, starting from a hospital's billed charge and shaving a discount, while RBP is bottom-up, starting from Medicare and paying a percentage above it, with both routes answering the same question of what the plan reimburses for a knee surgery or an MRI [xVuFXA-gN8g @ 00:05:18]. Omar Arif of ClaimDOC goes further, calling the product network replacement rather than RBP, and pointing out that every big carrier already reference-prices out-of-network claims and already pays physicians Medicare plus a markup, so RBP is baked into every group plan in the country [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:22:42]. Tom Stein of American Trust Administrators strips the chargemaster down to a restaurant menu that only the uninitiated believe is real, and says RBP discounts that menu entirely and keys off what the hospital accepts from its biggest payer, the government [D3AuT-Ki3nw @ 00:02:52]. Mike Kupa of Remodel Benefits calls the savings 'literally just math': egregious providers sitting inside carrier contracts simply get paid less, and that gap is where the employer's money comes back [vCzjI43ZWVI @ 00:03:26].

The savings bands cluster tightly across operators who run the numbers independently. Kupa tells employers to expect 20 to 30 percent on medical spend, sometimes more, after years of implementing and maintaining RBP plans [vCzjI43ZWVI @ 00:00:49]. Arif says ClaimDOC has historically promised 25 to 35 percent of overall spend depending on geography and plan structure, and that its 2022 book was tracking into the high 30s or low 40s against Kaiser Family Foundation figures, measured on true cost per employee and per family rather than on discount percentages [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:37:45]. The funding prerequisite is just as uniform. Howard Deihl of The General Agency states RBP contracts are only written self-funded or level-funded, never in the fully insured market, are medically underwritten, and in the level-funded version unused claim funds get refunded to the employer, so low utilization stacks extra savings on top of the rate cut [FPcS3pupwwA @ 00:05:33]. Ryan of Holloway Benefit Concepts confirms underwriting and risk analysis still apply, pitches the model squarely at small and mid-sized businesses, and reports two clients on RBP whose claim costs and overall premiums dropped two years in a row [2dsXkvZasDU @ 00:01:14]. Deihl adds that RBP coinsurance can run anywhere from 50 to 90 percent, but the single out-of-pocket maximum is the number operators sell on.

The third consensus point is that RBP fails without member education and advocacy wrapped around it. Rhett Robbins says employees can visit virtually any provider but need to confirm in advance that the provider accepts RBP pricing, with a third-party advocate stepping in to negotiate any billing dispute, and folds direct contracting with preferred providers into the same toolkit [8begy-typh4 @ 00:01:24]. Benefits Studio names the two catches every buyer has to price in: without a contract a hospital can balance bill the employee for the rest of its charge, and some hospitals refuse to take part at all, which leaves real gaps in access [noY8cLblMi4 @ 00:01:31]. Arif is blunt that nobody saves a health plan 20 to 40, sometimes 50 percent without disruption, and that the fix is telling clients up front what will break and how it gets fixed, then repeating it in a constant drip of communication all year [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:08:48]. Marty Makary, in a clip run by both Imagine360 and ELAP, supplies the macro case operators lean on when the noise starts: hospital prices have no orbit, they need to be anchored to something, and reference-based pricing is that anchor, with the added promise of reining in surprise billing and ensuring claims are paid at a fair amount [U6ZwMY7UtFU @ 00:02:01].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The rate engine is where operators make or lose the deal. ClaimDOC prices every facility claim two ways, Medicare plus a 25 percent markup and the hospital's own self-reported cost plus a 20 percent markup, then pays whichever is higher; the cost figure comes from the cost-of-services report every Medicare hospital must file with CMS, which Arif argues is fully loaded because hospitals use it to set their own government reimbursement [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:24:54]. The math runs line by line, so the plan can defend every payment as fair and reasonable, and Arif, self-described as aggressive, says a flat-Medicare program a broker touted on LinkedIn would scare even Arif [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:48:56]. Kupa's worked example shows where the spread lives: a $10,000 billed procedure with a 62.5 percent carrier discount pays $3,750, roughly 250 percent of Medicare; Medicare itself pays $1,500; an RBP plan at 150 percent pays $2,250, an effective 77.5 percent discount [vCzjI43ZWVI @ 00:01:46]. Deihl gives the carrier comparison operators quote against, with insurers averaging about 140 percent of Medicare for hospital services and 120 to 125 percent for physician services, higher or lower depending on the carrier [FPcS3pupwwA @ 00:00:41].

Access is engineered before the first claim drops. ClaimDOC's Pave the Way program pulls the consultant's top utilization report, lets members nominate their own doctors during open enrollment and all year, then calls each office with a fixed script: here is how we pay, how fast we pay, the payer ID, what the card looks like, and will you keep charging the $25 copay; that outreach lands about a 95 percent physician acceptance rate [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:12:17]. Facility care is left to flow through normal pre-certification: Arif estimates roughly 85 percent of outpatient procedures clear with no friction, eight or nine of every ten flagged cases settle through a ten-person provider relations team run by a former hospital executive, leaving one or two percent that need a delay or a redirect to another facility [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:21:38]. In markets locked by one dominant system, the plan B is contracting freestanding ambulatory surgery centers, while ERs stay covered because the Consolidated Appropriations Act bars them from balance billing the member [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:38:40]. Eric Bricker's direct-contracting stack mirrors this: bundled or discounted fee-for-service rates, an independent TPA loading a client-specific network, zero out-of-pocket benefit design, and a navigation service [xcu6bvj22fc @ 00:02:50].

The back end is a claims-defense machine. Arif relays the ClaimDOC CEO's estimate that the clinical team of nurses and coding experts scrapes 25 to 30 percent of the dollars off any inpatient stay before repricing even starts [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:33:00]. The marquee catch is ICU unbundling: Arif cites Stanford billing up to $80,000 a day for what should be an all-in per diem, and COVID-era claims where oxygen, ventilator, ECMO and even nursing were split out until bills hit $23 million and tripped PPO outlier clauses, turning a presumed 50 percent discount into 15 percent [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:35:26]. Every audited facility claim goes to the hospital by certified mail with appeal instructions and the hospital's own self-reported financials; if the provider later bills the member, a notice goes to that same address stating the check was cashed and the plan paid per the plan document. ClaimDOC itself is paid a percentage of billed charge, capped per claim, and Arif says experienced stop-loss carriers grant ClaimDOC decrements that more than offset the fee against a fixed-PEPM vendor [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:34:41]. Deihl's carriers go one step further on collections, mailing providers a letter telling them not to report the member while a claims negotiation runs [FPcS3pupwwA @ 00:07:38].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fault line is the multiple itself. Ryan at Holloway Benefit Concepts sells plans where every claim is paid at a higher percentage of Medicare, typically 150% to 200%, and pitches it as an exit from back-door, no-transparency carrier-hospital negotiations [2dsXkvZasDU @ 00:00:24]. Omar Arif at ClaimDOC runs a tighter, line-by-line formula: facilities get Medicare plus 25% or the hospital's own self-reported cost plus 20%, whichever is higher, and that floor is what lets ClaimDOC defend every payment as fair and reasonable. Arif says a broker moving a client to flat Medicare would scare even an operator as aggressive as Arif [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:48:56]. Howard Deihl at The General Agency puts the incumbent baseline on the table: carriers are reimbursing about 140% of Medicare for hospital services and 120% to 125% for physician services, with some higher and some lower depending on the carrier [FPcS3pupwwA @ 00:00:41]. Tom Stein at American Trust Administrators attacks from the opposite flank, arguing that paying 50% more than the state pays means small business is getting fleeced, while hospitals counter that government underpayment forces private plans to subsidize them [D3AuT-Ki3nw @ 00:03:28]. Same chassis, different price anchors, and each one moves acceptance, savings, and defensibility in a different direction.

Negotiation posture splits practitioners even harder. Arif lays out the two primary ways to run RBP: pay and defend, where the plan pays what it judges fair and defends the claim, or negotiate and settle, which can be better for a member in certain scenarios but sets a bad precedent and dilutes plan assets. ClaimDOC keeps both weapons plus single case agreements and direct contracts under one roof, with legal, provider relations, and member services in-house [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:29:51]. Dr. Eric Bricker at AHealthcareZ argues rational analysis alone did not seal the deal in Montana: Marilyn Bartlett's analysis found Medicare covered 92% of hospital costs, yet the agreement only closed after arm twisting, including threats to publish commercial payment rates and the personal cell phone numbers of hospital board members, CEOs, and CFOs [gpm3vtKEf5U @ 00:03:19]. Bricker's direct-contracting playbook runs on volume instead of threats: a concentrated workforce gives the employer steerage as leverage at the table with independent physician practices and ambulatory surgery centers [xcu6bvj22fc @ 00:01:02]. Carrie Nixon at Healthcare Solutions Connection reports Boeing going straight to two local accountable care organizations in Puget Sound, where 30% of employees chose the preferred partnership option in year one with an 8.5 out of 10 satisfaction rating [ypiI6d2Bnkk @ 00:01:53].

The fee model and the rollout architecture are the third split. Arif defends ClaimDOC taking a percentage of billed charge, capped per claim, over a flat fee: on a 200-life case where nobody hits the hospital for six months, the employer pays $30 PEPM to a fixed-fee vendor or nothing to ClaimDOC, and stop-loss carriers that know the model hand out decrements [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:34:13]. Benefits Studio counters that there is no public price list, every TPA and RBP vendor fee is quoted, and Willis Towers Watson warns repricing fees can eat into the saving [noY8cLblMi4 @ 00:01:12]. On architecture, Arif rejects RBP ever becoming the norm and expects a hybrid with PPOs; ClaimDOC already runs dual-option enrollments, and on the first one six employees picked the United Healthcare plan while 350 picked ClaimDOC at a lower monthly premium [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:55:41]. Adam Rousseau of the Fiat Group, speaking on Partners MGU, reads surprise billing rules as a huge opportunity rather than the end of RBP and sees RBP eventually becoming an RBP network built on direct contracting, while host Vince Lewis reports a producer forced back to a conventional PPO within months [YEFRBSmT3IE @ 00:03:08]. One camp bets on pure network replacement; the other wants a network to fall back on.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The fastest reversal on record is a full unwind. Vince Lewis at Partners MGU took a call from a producer whose January RBP client had to convert back to a conventional PPO just a few months in, because employees kept complaining about balance bills from the local provider and requests for cash upfront before treatment; Lewis calls it a morale issue at best and a public relations nightmare in the media [YEFRBSmT3IE @ 00:00:29]. Arif traces the stigma to early vendors that ended up in litigious fights with hospitals or acted as repricers that slammed a plan in to make a sale with no advocacy or education, and admits deals have gone sideways on ClaimDOC groups too when the team could not get access to HR and rank-and-file employees [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:08:26]. Micro-Dyn spells out the mechanism: when providers reject the benchmark rate, the patient can receive a balance bill for the difference [Hz5wBrKavxY @ 00:01:14]. Deihl adds the collections trap, where billing companies historically dinged credit right away and sent accounts straight to collections, so the carriers The General Agency works with mail hold letters to providers while a claim is under negotiation [FPcS3pupwwA @ 00:07:38].

Access friction is the second bottleneck, and it is measurable. Arif estimates about 85% of outpatient facility claims flow through with no problems before care, leaving roughly 15% with friction; eight or nine of every ten of those get worked out, which leaves one to two percent of procedures where the surgery slips a week or the doctor has to move it to another facility. The nightmare version is a provider calling at 7 p.m. Central to secure a contract for an 8 a.m. surgery the next day [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:22:14]. In markets controlled by one dominant system, often rural, ClaimDOC contracts with as many freestanding ambulatory surgery centers as possible and leans on the Consolidated Appropriations Act for emergency rooms, which cannot balance bill the member or turn care away, a fallback the provider relations team calls plan B [A__ZFq9k8Ds @ 00:38:56]. Benefits Studio flags the same catch: some hospitals refuse to take part, which can leave gaps in access [noY8cLblMi4 @ 00:01:49]. Bricker warns that direct contracts stall without navigation, because employees end up getting lost, and notes that getting a major ASO to administer one has been super hard, so independent TPAs build client specific networks instead [xcu6bvj22fc @ 00:04:03].

The economics checks start with the bill itself. Arif describes hospitals unbundling ICU charges that are supposed to be an all-in per diem, billing oxygen, ventilator, ECMO, and even nursing separately on COVID stays, so claims that should have been billed at a fraction of that came in at $23 million and tripped an outlier clause that turned a supposed 50% PPO discount into 15% [mPaz9R2yMrs @ 00:35:10]. Micro-Dyn warns that under RBP coding errors can multiply into bigger financial losses than under contract pricing [Hz5wBrKavxY @ 00:01:34]. Deihl keeps quotes honest: a demographically older 29-employee group priced at $334 per employee on RBP against $678 on the PPO, booked as 202% savings, but most of the time the agency sees anywhere from 15% to 70%, and these plans are medically underwritten [FPcS3pupwwA @ 00:04:56]. Stein calls the EOB headline a trap of its own, where a hospital bills $96,000, the administrator touts a $40,000 discount, and the chargemaster number was never what anyone expected to collect, an opacity that benefits every party except self-funded plans [D3AuT-Ki3nw @ 00:06:10]. Measure cost per employee per month and real dollars paid, not discount percentages.
