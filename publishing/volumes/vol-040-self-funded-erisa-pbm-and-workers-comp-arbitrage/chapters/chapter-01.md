# Chapter 01: Fully Insured vs. Self-Funded vs. Level-Funded: The Funding Architecture

*Premium mechanics, claims funds, fixed admin costs, surplus refunds, and why double-digit renewals push small and mid-size employers off fully insured plans*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners across benefits brokerages, stop-loss carriers and healthcare finance channels converge on one architecture map: fully insured is guaranteed cost where the carrier keeps every unspent claims dollar, self-funded is pay-as-you-go with stop-loss backstops, and level-funded is the hybrid that bills a fixed monthly amount split into admin, stop-loss and a claims fund with surplus refunds. Street numbers include 60-70% of self-funded spend landing in claims, carriers loading level-funded rates roughly 25% above expected cost, surplus splits of 50-80%, and level-funded penetration jumping from 10% to 45% of lives in 50-500 employee groups. Disagreements center on where the size cutoffs sit (25, 30, 45, 100 lives), whether carriers quietly keep level-funded surpluses, and how hard medical underwriting, lasers and renewal spikes bite after a bad claims year.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same baseline: fully insured is a guaranteed-cost product where the carrier owns the upside. Spencer Smith of Self-Funded frames it as prepaid cost, with the carrier projecting claims, calculating administrative charges, collecting taxes and baking its desired profit margin into one premium, so the employer spends that full amount no matter what happens in the claims bucket unless enrollment drops significantly [VPeObJ32l7M @ 00:00:49]. Matt Roy of Blue Ocean Benefits & Consulting puts it bluntly: you pay a premium, 100% of it goes to the carrier, and the carrier keeps it regardless of what the claims are [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:00:58]. Tyler Dutchover runs the street version of that math: a business paying $300,000 in premium whose young workforce only burns $100,000 of care watches the carrier keep every penny of the $200,000 difference, gets no refund or thank-you, and then gets higher rates the next year [ExcUj28jzzc @ 00:01:16]. Brian Calvert of Health Insurance Doesn't Have to Suck sums up the trade as maximum predictability, maximum cost, noting the model works as a starting point under 50 employees but starts leaving money on the table as a group gets larger and more stable [y9n2wYoPMYw @ 00:01:46].

The second point of consensus is what level funding actually is under the hood. Dr. Eric Bricker of AHealthcareZ calls it a crossbreed: a fixed monthly premium to the carrier, like fully insured, that funds claims plus stop-loss insurance plus an administrative fee plus the PBM, with a refund or credit possible if claims come in low and stop-loss covering the year if claims run hot [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:00:20]. Tyler Dutchover and Brian Calvert describe the identical three-bucket split, admin fee, stop-loss premium and a claims fund that pays everyday doctor visits and prescriptions, with leftover money coming back as a refund or credit [ExcUj28jzzc @ 00:02:16] [y9n2wYoPMYw @ 00:04:06]. The practitioners also agree that self-funding is less of a cliff than its name suggests. Matt Roy repeats a line from an old rep that a self-funded plan is really a fully reinsured self-funded plan, because individual and aggregate stop-loss cap exposure, and only very big companies take 100% of the risk, something Roy says you will not see under a thousand lives [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:00:30]. That shared stop-loss floor is why most speakers treat the three models as points on one dial, trading predictability against how much unspent claims money the employer keeps.

Speakers also line up on who belongs in which model, and the real dividing line is claims risk. Bricker says the number one driver of claims is age, so young groups sitting in fully insured should generally go level funded, while older, high-claims groups are left holding the bag; brokers now describe the fully insured market as essentially a high-risk pool, and Bricker warns that if you don't know who the sucker at the poker table is, it's probably you [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:08:05]. Tyler Dutchover calls the same dynamic a death spiral, healthy groups exit, the community pool gets sicker, rates rise again, and points to Connecticut as a place where it has already happened [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:07:13]. Olivia of Allstate Benefits adds that four out of five years groups perform better than expected, so the self-funded or level-funded employer reaps that margin instead of the fully insured carrier, which also hands the group very little data on providers, claims spend or drug usage [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:05:35]. Jennifer Shafer of JS Benefits Group turns that into the baseline prerequisite: the question is not whether level funding is cheaper but whether it fits the group's demographics, claims history and risk profile [qE34fv4L2qU @ 00:01:13].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The level-funded rate is built, not guessed, and the speakers expose the formula. Matt Roy says the carrier calculates what it needs for admin, individual stop-loss, aggregate stop-loss and claims, then usually charges about 25% more than it expects the plan to cost and hands over one flat number that looks like a fully insured premium, with some or all of the overage split back at year end depending on the contract [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:05:15]. Olivia of Allstate Benefits mirrors that figure from the stop-loss side: the aggregate is set around 125% of expected claims, spread over 12 months, and if a major claim hits in January before the fund is built, Allstate advances the money and recoups it as the fund fills [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:16:41]. Tyler Dutchover's 40-employee model runs $30,000 a month, $6,000 to admin, $8,000 to stop-loss and $16,000 to the claims fund, totaling $192,000 a year; a healthy year using $92,000 leaves $100,000, of which the employer gets 50 to 80% back [ExcUj28jzzc @ 00:03:06]. In the Mediocre Corp case, a $500,000 fully insured quote becomes a $470,000 level-funded cap at $39,000 monthly, with $120,000 for admin and stop-loss and $350,000 in claims reserve that technically belongs to the company [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:02:13].

Stop-loss is the engine every architecture except fully insured runs on, and the attachment points decide how much risk the employer actually carries. Matt Roy's worked example: with a $30,000 individual stop-loss, a $100,000 hospital claim costs the employer the first $30,000 and the carrier the next $70,000, and even a $2 million claim only costs the employer $30,000 [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:02:49]. Aggregate stop-loss is the second layer, adding up every claim below the individual cap, so a $5,000, a $10,000 and a $50,000 claim capped at 30 total $45,000 toward the aggregate; Roy notes a group can hit the aggregate with nobody hitting the individual layer, though it rarely happens [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:04:09]. Olivia says the specific deductible is the employer's choice, as low as 10 or 20 thousand or as high as 40, 50 or 100 thousand, with the aggregate deductible capping total group spend [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:07:12]. Marc Combs of Self Insured Reporting frames the same two risks in plain terms: specific risk is one employee hit by a bus with a half-million-dollar claim, reinsured at 50,000 up to 500,000 depending on size, and aggregate risk is everybody getting the bird flu at five grand apiece [5PxkF9WUCd0 @ 00:07:44].

The vendor stack is where level-funded and self-funded split hardest. Bricker says level-funded buyers are stuck with the carrier's PBM, Cigna means Express Scripts, Aetna means CVS Caremark, United means OptumRx, and the carrier's stop-loss, while a self-funded group can carve out the PBM or buy stop-loss from Berkeley or Tokio Marine [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:01:18]. Olivia says Allstate runs bundled level-funded and traditional self-funded deals through Allied Benefits as TPA on about 95% of groups, with Meritain in some regions, quotes level-funded as three line items, admin, stop-loss premium and claims funding, and will sell standalone stop-loss to very large groups that want their own TPA and PBM [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:11:13]. Getting a level-funded quote at all means underwriting: Warp reports some carriers require every employee to complete an Individual Medical Questionnaire taking three to five minutes each, non-IMQ options leave rates a shot in the dark, and brokers dodge level funding because chasing every IMQ is more work for the same job [XHSDGFk6y94 @ 00:02:08]. Marc Combs pushes the last lever: commissions run around 5% of the fully insured dollar, and after about 15 years teaching this Combs argues brokers should be fee-based, paid by the hour like accountants and attorneys for known, visible work [5PxkF9WUCd0 @ 00:05:13].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Strip the premium dollar
   - Break the current fully insured renewal into premium tax, commission, carrier retention and claims before shopping alternatives.
   - Marc Combs maps a fully insured dollar at about 2 cents premium tax, about 5 cents commission, a carrier retention slice and 70 to 75 cents in medical and pharmacy claims, and says self-insuring shrinks tax and commission while cutting into carrier profit [Self Insured Reporting — 5PxkF9WUCd0 @ 00:01:28]. Brian Calvert pegs carrier overhead, margin and profit at 15 to 20 cents of every fully insured dollar [Health Insurance Doesn't Have to Suck — y9n2wYoPMYw @ 00:03:04].
2. Size-match the architecture
   - Place the group on the headcount ladder before requesting any level-funded or self-funded quote.
   - Calvert's ladder: under 10 employees fully insured, 10 or 15 to 100 the level-funded sweet spot, over 100 self-funded [Health Insurance Doesn't Have to Suck — y9n2wYoPMYw @ 00:06:49]. Allstate sends traditional self-funded deals to underwriting at 100 lives, going smaller only with level-funded claims data in hand [Maximize Asset Protection — hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:08:46].
3. Run the underwriting sprint
   - Push every enrolling employee through the medical questionnaire fast and screen the census for high-cost conditions.
   - Warp clocks the IMQ at three to five minutes per employee, with everyone required to finish it on IMQ carriers [Warp — XHSDGFk6y94 @ 00:02:08]. Tyler Dutchover warns underwriters can decline an older group with conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or hemophilia, or set the claims reserve so high the plan costs more than fully insured [Tyler Dutchover — m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:05:13].
4. Lock stop-loss terms and refund mechanics
   - Set the specific deductible, confirm the aggregate corridor, and read the surplus split and renewal provisions before signing.
   - Olivia sets the specific deductible anywhere from 10 or 20 thousand up to 100 thousand, with the aggregate around 125% of expected claims as the hard ceiling [Maximize Asset Protection — hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:07:43]. Jennifer Shafer insists on knowing the contract, stop-loss coverage, renewal provisions and financial exposure, and says never renew automatically [JS Benefits Group — UhT4cGnMRpY @ 00:01:12].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over where level funding sits on the ladder. Dr. Eric Bricker treats it as a halfway house the carrier still milks: he has run self-funded groups as small as 45 employees and says self-funding 120-employee groups is fairly common in the South and Midwest [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:04:30]. Olivia of Allstate Benefits pitches level funding as the stepping stone, with traditional self-funded only going to her underwriters at 100 lives unless a smaller group arrives with clean claims data from a prior level-funded year [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:08:46]. Brian Calvert draws the cleanest map: under 10 employees start fully insured, 10 or 15 to 100 is the level-funded sweet spot, and past 100 self-funding becomes worth exploring once claims history is stable [y9n2wYoPMYw @ 00:07:03]. Floors drift by seller. Matt Roy says Cigna, Aetna and UnitedHealthcare write level-funded contracts down to 25 lives depending on state, while noting nobody under 1,000 lives carries 100 percent of the risk alone [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:03:05]. Jennifer Shafer flags roughly 30 employees as the evaluation trigger, and David at Imago Dei opens the conversation at 10. Across the four, the floor moves with whoever is selling, and Olivia's rule holds: prior level-funded claims data is what earns a sub-100 group a self-funded look.

Nobody agrees on the size of the prize. Warp says its own level-funded quotes have produced 60 percent plus cost savings [XHSDGFk6y94 @ 00:00:48], while Bricker cites a Milliman study pegging level-funded savings at about 6 to 12 percent of overall cost [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:06:23]. Tyler Dutchover's Mediocre Corp model sits at the low end: a $500,000 fully insured quote against a $470,000 level-funded cap, paid at $39,000 a month [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:02:13]. Olivia flatly refuses to throw out a number, saying it depends on coverage, network and what the group wants, and frames the move as a strategy play more than a dollar play [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:13:08]. The surplus split is just as contested. Dutchover's other walkthrough says the carrier hands back anywhere from 50 to 80 percent of unused claims money depending on contract, as cash or lower rates. Matt Roy says the carrier pads its flat price about 25 percent above expected cost and then returns some or all of the leftover. Bricker's live Dallas case got neither: a 60 percent loss ratio, no credit, no refund, flat renewal. Read every contract for the split, because three practitioners report three different refund realities on the same product.

Practitioners even disagree on what self-funded means. PeopleKeep defines it as the employer assuming total financial risk and forgoing stop-loss entirely, citing KFF data that 20 percent of covered workers at small organizations sat in self-insured plans in 2024 [o5eOH2AXJAs @ 00:01:46]. Matt Roy says the opposite: no medical group plan is truly self-funded because it is always reinsured, a fully reinsured self-funded plan in his old rep's words [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:00:30], and Calvert says self-funded plans almost always carry stop-loss. The control argument splits too. Bricker warns level funding locks you into the carrier's stop-loss and PBM, so Cigna means Express Scripts, Aetna means CVS Caremark and United means OptumRx, while true self-funding lets you shop stop-loss with Berkeley or Tokio Marine and carve out pharmacy [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:01:18]. Olivia's Allstate sells standalone stop-loss to groups that want their own TPA and PBM, but she says that appetite rarely shows up below a couple hundred to thousands of lives [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:12:38]. PeopleKeep pushes a fourth exit altogether, a standalone HRA with a fixed monthly allowance that rolls over and reimburses more than 200 expense types, and Imago Dei says the right answer for some employers is a different strategy altogether, such as direct primary care.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is assuming a good year pays you. Bricker's Dallas broker had a 120-employee, 240-member group run a 60 percent loss ratio on roughly $1.4 million in premium at about $15,000 per subscriber; the carrier refused any credit or refund and simply held the renewal flat, pocketing about $560,000 that a self-funded group would have largely kept [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:04:09]. The bad year is worse. Dutchover's Mediocre Corp blows through its $350,000 claims reserve to $430,000, stop-loss covers the $80,000 overage, and total spend stays capped at $470,000, but the next renewal lands with a significant increase and possibly a laser that pushes one sick employee's specific stop-loss attachment to $200,000 [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:06:25]. Warp confirms that a significant medical claims year will hit level-funded renewal rates hard and that the group must actively manage its stop-loss [XHSDGFk6y94 @ 00:01:04]. Dutchover's escape hatch costs money: the fully insured community pool must take the group back with no health questions, but at 10 percent or more above the old rate, so $500,000 becomes roughly $550,000 [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:06:50]. Price that fallback before signing year one, since neither the good-year refund nor the bad-year renewal is locked in.

The second wall is underwriting friction before you ever see savings. Warp reports some carriers require every employee to complete an Individual Medical Questionnaire that takes three to five minutes each, a grind across hundreds of employees, while non-IMQ quotes leave rates a shot in the dark; level funding is also unavailable in some states [XHSDGFk6y94 @ 00:02:08]. Warp says that workload is exactly why many brokers never raise the option. Dutchover adds that an older workforce with a few chronic cases such as rheumatoid arthritis or hemophilia can get a group declined outright, or quoted with a claims reserve so high that level funding costs more than staying fully insured [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:05:25]. On traditional self-funded, the danger is liquidity. Olivia warns that a couple of massive claims early in the year can force the employer to pay the full aggregate amount within a couple of months, which is why the route skews to larger groups with cash on hand [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:17:30]. Level funding softens that, since Allstate advances money when the claims fund runs short in month one and recoups it over later deposits. Matt Roy adds a back-end trap: when a self-funded contract ends, somebody still has to pay the claims that arrive afterward [D23XjGg7o_g @ 00:06:56].

The structural failure is the fully insured death spiral. Bricker says brokers now describe the fully insured market as essentially a high-risk pool, with one broker facing an 80 percent renewal and another relieved to take 30 percent, because young, low-claim groups keep leaving for level funding [wshiRRBh4Gs @ 00:08:30]. Dutchover reports the same spiral and says it has already played out in Connecticut [m5BLhBDIZZ4 @ 00:07:25]. If you are fully insured and cannot spot the sucker at the table, Bricker's line is that it is probably you. The overpay is real on the ground: Mario of Maximize Asset Protection paid UnitedHealthcare about $1,200 a month for a healthy family of four that he says never used the plan once, a $64,000 lesson he blames on buying out of fear instead of knowledge [hqPZ5l67B4A @ 00:20:45]. Marc Combs of Self Insured Reporting closes the loop: no one pays your claims but you. A fully insured group that pays $1 million in premium and runs $2 million in claims gets that money clawed back at renewal, and he calls post-ACA loss-ratio optics a shell game where a 75-cent payout can hide money flowing back to the carrier [5PxkF9WUCd0 @ 00:09:30].
