# Chapter 09: CAA Compliance: Gag Clauses, Disclosures, and Plan Filings

*Gag clause bans and attestations, broker and consultant compensation disclosures, transparency provisions, new fiduciary obligations, contract review, and Form 5500 filing and late-filing penalty relief*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Benefits lawyers, brokers and compliance shops converge on one message: the CAA makes every ERISA health plan sponsor, fully insured groups included, a fiduciary who can delegate tasks but never liability. The street playbook is to strip gag clauses from carrier, ASO and PBM contracts, attest every year, force brokers and consultants expecting more than $1,000 to disclose direct and indirect compensation in dollars and cents, and terminate holdouts within 90 days after a missed 30-day disclosure window. The numbers set the stakes: $100 per day per employee in penalties, a Florida school system that expected about $195,000 in provider pay and found roughly $4 million, and DFVCP cutting late Form 5500 penalties from $2,739 to $10 a day. Practitioners split on the attestation date (December 27 versus December 31), on the bill's length (1,100 versus 6,000 pages), and on whether vendor payments can ever lower a sponsor's fees.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on this beat starts from one premise: the employer is the fiduciary, and that status sticks whether anyone signs up for it or not. Tony Sorrentino of HPFID, a CPA and attorney who coaches brokers through the rollout, puts it bluntly: "delegate responsibility you can never delegate liability if you don't do this it's on you" [I7g6idLkgrc @ 00:29:13]. Benefits lawyer Michael Flanagan goes further, warning that a person does not even need to be aware they are a fiduciary to carry the exposure, because functional fiduciaries are created by what they do, not by what the paperwork says, and a claims administrator stays a fiduciary even when its contract disclaims the role [9hPiS9wv_F4 @ 00:07:55]. Jamie Greenleaf of OneVision tells sponsors that at the end of the day they are the fiduciary unless their vendors are signing off that they are fiduciaries, and the liabilities lie with the sponsor [ks-F1VjDtk4 @ 00:18:05]. Jason Andrade of the Mahoney Group lands the line that resets small-group conversations: fully insured employers are now fiduciaries too, so sitting behind a carrier policy is no longer a hiding place [l5tGs_zQ8xA @ 00:04:02]. The working baseline is identical across all four voices: name the fiduciaries, run a documented decision process, and assume every gap in vendor oversight lands on the sponsor's own balance sheet rather than on the vendor that caused it.

The second point of agreement is that gag clauses are dead and the sponsor has to prove it every year. Chad Burdo of HUB Midwest East explains the old play: insurers and PBMs restricted what pharmacists could tell members, so a member could be bound to a $15 negotiated rate when a cheaper option existed; now plans must review insurer and PBM contracts and "attest every single year by December 31st" that those contracts are compliant [JY_hbh9Kxrc @ 00:01:42]. Julia of Berger Montague takes the fight upstream into self-insured ASO agreements, where gag language blocks the employer from its own claims and price data and therefore from its duty to monitor and confirm that costs are reasonable [WBl8smrz_3E @ 00:00:53]. Clear Value Benefit Compliance adds that contracts with providers, networks or TPAs cannot block de-identified claims access, provider quality data, or sharing with business associates, with attestations due by December 31 of each year going forward [wEKxSVkV2NA @ 00:01:20]. Mel DeWeese tells employers directly that they carry a new stated fiduciary responsibility to vet that those clauses have actually been removed from their health insurance plans [k0FZkQKH4SQ @ 00:07:35]. Nobody in this group treats the attestation as optional paperwork; it is the annual receipt proving the sponsor's data pipe is open and being watched.

Third, the practitioners agree the CAA ports the retirement-plan fee playbook straight into health benefits. Karen of Berger Montague ties it to 408(b)(2): fiduciaries were "asleep at the switch" on healthcare fees, and the new broker and consultant rules mirror the 401k regime that let sponsors judge whether service agreements were reasonable and avoid prohibited transactions [3ZIAPoIvjvc @ 00:00:41]. Mark Fisher of Advanced Benefits states the trigger plainly: the disclosure and transparency rules hit all ERISA plans that pay any party more than a thousand dollars a year [twKQWqVJ4zc @ 00:00:32]. The Leapfrog Group panel uses the same $1,000 line for expected direct or indirect compensation and applies it to brokers and consultants alike, which puts every renewal relationship under the microscope [QU7rxyEAEjQ @ 00:10:51]. On enforcement, the shared fear is plaintiffs rather than Washington. Jason Andrade concedes regulators "certainly could be" breathing down a sponsor's neck, but rates participant lawsuits as the more likely hit, and frames the upside as finally seeing exactly what the plan pays for and which brokers deliver the best value for their fees [sIp9x3Y9ePw @ 00:00:33]. The common conclusion is that the same disclosures that create exposure also hand a sharp sponsor the leverage to renegotiate, rebid, or fire an overpaid intermediary.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The gag-clause workflow the Berger Montague panel teaches is sequenced and unforgiving. Rule one: "you can't extend any contract with someone who won't remove the get clause" [WBl8smrz_3E @ 00:01:38]. If a sponsor is stuck mid-contract with a holdout, Julia's sequence is to document the refusal in writing, tell the DOL, and then escalate: the employer "might need to file a lawsuit" either to get the information it is entitled to or to terminate the contract for breach and seek damages [WBl8smrz_3E @ 00:02:12]. The annual attestation then explains any clause that could not be removed, and the panel's tone is that this is not optional: you better do it, and do it now. Karen pushes past the paperwork into the money: once the data flows, audit the claims, because claims data surfaces what no disclosure will, such as somebody being paid twice for an operation on the same day for the same person, cross-plan offsetting, and improper claim reductions [WBl8smrz_3E @ 00:03:01]. On the review step itself, the Miller Johnson team tells sponsors to read the carrier and ASA agreements and request an attestation from the TPA or carrier rather than relying on assumptions about who handled it [WVU2CeENchc @ 00:24:42]. The operating logic is simple: no data access, no renewal, and a written record of every refusal.

The broker-compensation mechanics come with hard clocks. The Leapfrog Group panel spells out the escalation: if a covered service provider does not deliver the disclosure "within 30 days," the sponsor has to notify the Department of Labor and then terminate the contract within 90 days [QU7rxyEAEjQ @ 00:11:35]. Galpin flags the indirect money hiding in plain sight: an insurer advertising bonuses of up to $150,000 per employer group, and brokers being offered Hawaii trips for placing business [QU7rxyEAEjQ @ 00:29:17]. Mark Fisher of Advanced Benefits warns that brokers may be earning fees on every single pharmacy script, and that certain large brokerage firms run coalitions that hide these fees, so even simple plans pay brokers thousands of dollars the employer never sees; the question that matters is what services those dollars actually buy [twKQWqVJ4zc @ 00:00:50]. The OneVision case study shows the scale of the leak: a Florida school system expected about $195,000 in service-provider compensation and found roughly $4 million over four years once the numbers were surfaced [ks-F1VjDtk4 @ 00:17:25]. Practitioners treat the disclosure request as the opening move of a fee audit, not a formality, and push sponsors to compare every dollar against the services delivered before signing any renewal or extension.

The filing side runs on two engines: RxDC reporting and Form 5500. Mel DeWeese walks the RxDC build file by file: a P-file carrying plan name and number, plan year dates, members as of December 31, and the issuer, TPA and PBM names and EINs, then D-files D1 through D8 covering premium and life-years, spending by category, the top 50 most frequent brand drugs, the top 50 most costly drugs, the top 50 by spending increase, Rx totals, rebates by therapeutic class, and rebates on the top 25 drugs, plus a response narrative [k0FZkQKH4SQ @ 00:09:29]. For D1 life-years, the working shortcut is to "add all 12 numbers together and then divide by 12" [k0FZkQKH4SQ @ 00:11:19]. Foundation Benefits flags the 5500 gotcha that catches mid-size groups: a 125 to 150 employee shop may have only 75 to 80 on medical, but an employer-paid LTD or basic life plan with 110 enrolled still triggers the filing [R-MOuJrCG9Q @ 00:01:00]. When a filing is missed, HR Service, Inc. runs the DFVCP math: $2,739 per day per plan outside the program versus $10 per day inside it, capped at $1,500 for small plans and $4,000 for large plans [K_Kmaqdjrns @ 00:09:00]. That spread is the entire case for self-reporting fast.

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The calendar itself is contested ground. Tony Sorrentino of HPFID, a CPA and attorney, dates the CAA to December 27, 2020 and tells brokers the gag-clause attestation covers every ERISA plan down to two lives, due December 27, 2022, with under 5% of brokers ready and the filing looking backward over 2020 to 2021 data [I7g6idLkgrc @ 00:28:09]. Chad Burdo at HUB Midwest East runs a different clock: review insurer and PBM contracts, then attest every single year by December 31st that those contracts comply [JY_hbh9Kxrc @ 00:01:42]. Clear Value Benefit Compliance pushes the first attestation out to December 31, 2023, reaching back to December 27, 2020, and pegs every later filing to December 31 of each year [wEKxSVkV2NA @ 00:01:03]. Even the size of the law splits the room. Sorrentino counts roughly 1,100 pages with 91 pages on fiduciary duties, while OneVision co-founder Jamie Greenleaf describes about 6,000 pages with 90 on healthcare [ks-F1VjDtk4 @ 00:00:22]. The operator play is to calendar the earliest date any practitioner names, run the contract review before it, and treat December 31 as the recurring annual drop-dead line rather than betting on a single figure, because an attestation filed late against any of these clocks is still a missed filing.

Where the hammer falls is the second fault line. Sorrentino expects DOL may make an example of a Fortune 50 or 100 company but names participant lawsuits as the bigger fear, and when host Spencer Smith floated the 87,000 new IRS agents as the enforcement engine, Sorrentino's answer was "I really don't know" [I7g6idLkgrc @ 00:39:12]. Jason Andrade of The Mahoney Group concedes regulators from Washington certainly could be breathing down a sponsor's neck, yet ranks plaintiffs' suits as the likelier hit [sIp9x3Y9ePw @ 00:00:33]. On the Ameriflex panel, Andrade splits from Ameriflex counsel on who gets sued: Andrade is skeptical of employer-versus-carrier actions because carriers carry no fiduciary duty, while counsel reports suits surviving the 30- and 60-day marks and emerging within the last six months [l5tGs_zQ8xA @ 00:16:01]. Penalty math diverges too. Sorrentino runs $100 per day per employee, which turns a 1,000-employee group into $100,000 a day, while Greenleaf works the "per belly button" framing on covered lives, so a 300-employee plan with 450 covered members prices out on 450 heads, and still calls class actions the bigger fear [ks-F1VjDtk4 @ 00:23:52]. Model exposure on covered lives, not headcount, and build the paper trail for a plaintiffs' bar before worrying about an audit letter.

When a vendor stonewalls, the playbooks fork. The Leapfrog Group's session lays out a statutory clock: if a broker or consultant does not deliver the disclosure within 30 days, notify the Department of Labor and terminate the contract within 90 days [QU7rxyEAEjQ @ 00:11:35]. Julie on the Berger Montague compensation panel runs a softer front end, requesting the missing disclosure in writing first and reporting to DOL only if the sponsor is still ignored, while assuming every covered service provider must disclose until guidance says otherwise [3ZIAPoIvjvc @ 00:04:14]. On gag-clause holdouts the same firm goes harder: Julia says never extend a contract with a holdout, document the refusal and tell DOL mid-contract, then sue for the data or terminate for breach and seek damages [WBl8smrz_3E @ 00:02:12]. The fee debate is just as split. Galpin at Leapfrog argued vendor payments might lower fees, Thompson shot back that there are no free lunches, yet the same session holds that paying more is not wrong if value is delivered, and Michael Flanagan at Walsh Insurance frames prudence as an evaluation based on value rather than lowest price [9hPiS9wv_F4 @ 00:38:24]. Pick one escalation ladder and write it into the vendor file before the renewal, not after the refusal.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The Form 5500 is where groups bleed cash on technicalities. Foundation Benefits flags the classic gotcha: a 125 to 150 employee shop with only 75 to 80 on medical assumes it is under the line, but an employer-paid LTD or basic life plan with 110 enrolled still triggers the filing, and the government penalizes each line separately [R-MOuJrCG9Q @ 00:01:00]. HR Service, Inc. puts the unmitigated number at $2,739 per day per plan, versus $10 a day under DFVCP capped at $1,500 for small plans and $4,000 for large, with an SPD wrap collapsing several plans into one filing so one $4,000 cap applies instead of one per plan [K_Kmaqdjrns @ 00:09:00]. The same team warns that penalties start the day after the original due date, not the extension date, and lists the errors they see: plan-year confusion, counting dependents or COBRA wrong, assuming the broker or TPA files, and lumping health in with the 401k filing [K_Kmaqdjrns @ 00:10:11]. Emparion's retirement-side trap is a cash balance plan plus a solo 401k that together pass $250,000, such as $150,000 plus $110,000, and slip through the cracks when each plan needed its own 5500-EZ [yGr9BaJ2hJ4 @ 00:00:56]. Every one of these is a counting error, not a strategy error.

The money leak most sponsors never see is indirect compensation buried inside vendor contracts. Greenleaf cites a Florida school system that expected roughly $195,000 in service-provider pay; the actual figure ran about $4 million over four years [ks-F1VjDtk4 @ 00:17:25]. At Leapfrog, Galpin described an insurer advertising bonuses of up to $150,000 per employer group and brokers being offered Hawaii trips, and Fogerty found a 64-page Ameriprise disclosure that on page 22 shields representatives from knowing how they are paid [QU7rxyEAEjQ @ 00:29:17]. Mark Fisher at Alliant Idaho warns that brokers may earn a fee on every pharmacy script, that certain large brokerage coalitions hide these fees, and that even simple plans pay brokers thousands of dollars the employer never knew about [twKQWqVJ4zc @ 00:00:50]. Disclosures alone do not close the gap. Karen on the Berger Montague panel says claims data surfaces what paperwork will not: someone paid twice for an operation on the same day for the same person, cross-plan offsetting, and improper claim reductions, none of which a sponsor can monitor without the raw data [WBl8smrz_3E @ 00:03:01]. If the disclosure total looks clean but nobody has pulled claims, the economics check has not happened, and the sponsor is signing off blind.

The quietest trap is assuming someone else holds the liability. Michael Flanagan at Walsh Insurance warns that a person does not even need to know they are a fiduciary to be one, that personal liability attaches, and that a claims administrator is a fiduciary even when its contract disclaims the role [9hPiS9wv_F4 @ 00:07:55]. Sorrentino of HPFID puts it in one line for brokers: you can delegate responsibility but you can never delegate liability, and if the attestation is not done it is on you [I7g6idLkgrc @ 00:29:13]. Miller Johnson adds the contract reality for self-funded plans: the TPA administers, the plan sponsor stays ultimately liable, and the only way to legally shift the financial obligation when it is the TPA's fault is an indemnification clause [WVU2CeENchc @ 00:34:53]. Mel DeWeese reports the RxDC side is already reversing assumptions, with fully insured carriers mailing letters asking employers to file the D1 premium and life-years file themselves, and stresses that it is the employer's fiduciary responsibility to make sure every form gets done [k0FZkQKH4SQ @ 00:03:25]. Read every carrier letter, check every vendor contract for indemnification language, and stop treating the TPA as a liability sponge, because the bill lands on the sponsor.
