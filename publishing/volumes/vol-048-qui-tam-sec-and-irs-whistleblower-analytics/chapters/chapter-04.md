## Executive Summary

Practitioners from Foley & Lardner, Seyfarth Shaw, Epstein Becker Green, Reed Smith and Analysis Group converge on one picture: DOJ is centralizing FCA enforcement, healthcare dominates the money, and data miners now drive a large slice of the docket. FY2025 recoveries topped $6.8B, with $5.7B from healthcare, roughly 1,300 qui tams filed, and data miners behind more than 45% of qui tams since FY2024. The street mechanics are concrete: the FOCUS initiative demands correlations tethered to falsity and scienter, Rule 9(b) is the first motion-to-dismiss weapon against algorithmic complaints, CID signatures reveal case value, and a civil self-disclosure pilot prices repayment at 1.25-1.6x. Disagreement centers on filing counts, cyber recovery totals, relator share ranges, and whether DOJ will actually use its dismissal power against weak declined cases.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: Central Command, Healthcare Money and the Rise of Data Miners

Enforcement is being pulled toward Main Justice and pointed at healthcare. Erica Sibley Bondson and Leah Brownlee Taylor of Epstein Becker Green walk through the new National Fraud Enforcement Division, which absorbed the criminal Tax section, the Health Care Fraud Unit and MGCF, with civil components possibly added later; Leah expects top-down, centralized, hands-on control from Main Justice rather than scattered U.S. Attorney discretion [rxUll524o5w @ 00:03:00]. Jennifer Seraphin of Seyfarth Shaw confirms that Directive 26-12 makes healthcare the top priority, backed by strike forces in New England and on the West Coast and a dedicated fraud division inside the DC U.S. Attorney's Office [QLVKZRh_ArE @ 00:16:20]. Jonathan Porter of Husch Blackwell, a DOJ alum, reports staff being moved off antitrust and securities work and onto healthcare files, and the episode closes with the blunt line that healthcare enforcement is coming [FO7NfNf-r0k @ 00:00:30]. The money backs the staffing: the Foley & Lardner panel says 84% of the dollars the government recovered last fiscal year came from healthcare, inside a record-breaking $6.8B year [J1-aE0W1Eu8 @ 00:09:37]. For an operator, the baseline assumption is simple: healthcare claims sit at the center of the target map, and the decisions about them increasingly come from a central command that sets targets before local offices ever touch a file.

Every serious panel agrees the docket is being fed by data, not just insiders. Analysis Group's panel reports that almost all FCA cases historically came from insider whistleblowers, then DOJ officials from 2019 through 2022 started saying they were using big data to find payment anomalies, with an ultrasound statistical-outlier case and four PEMF electro-acupuncture cases cited as proof [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:08:09]. Epstein Becker Green puts a number on the relator side: data miners have filed more than 45% of qui tams since FY2024, and a new whistleblower program pays $10-30,000 per tip [rxUll524o5w @ 00:16:24]. Tom Sutis and David Bender of Reed Smith describe an upsurge of non-insider whistleblowers using public data and AI tools, driven by DOJ priority, newly public data and the relator's share of the recovery, with healthcare as the main hunting ground [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:03:44]. Relator counsel Shawna Itri of Seeger Weiss traces the fuel back to CMS releasing claims data in 2011, and lists data-driven settlements of $280M and $98M plus Integra settlements of $61M and $188M [SKjmFHwe2Rk @ 00:33:33]. The consensus mechanic: public claims data plus AI tools plus a 15-30% relator share turns outsiders into relators who never worked a day inside the target company.

The same practitioners agree on the ceiling: data flags anomalies, but it does not prove fraud. The Reed Smith pair put it flatly, correlation does not equate to causation, and the scienter or knowing element is what separates an overpayment from fraud [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:11:53]. Analysis Group's panel adds that data cannot prove scienter under the FCA knowingly standard, let alone the higher anti-kickback intent standard, and that outputs are vulnerable to the data-generating process, such as how clinicians order tests [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:23:24]. The proof is the Integra Med Analytics line: Epstein Becker Green notes the Fifth Circuit dismissed the Baylor Scott & White suit in 2020 because obvious alternative explanations existed, and Analysis Group adds the Ninth Circuit did the same to the Providence suit, even though DOJ did intervene in an Integra nursing-home RUGs case [rxUll524o5w @ 00:18:26]. On the defense side, Josh Drew holds that traditional compliance still works, with an effective reporting line, internal investigations and remediation at the top of the list, because whistleblowers usually raise concerns internally first [zF0bNkCTI_Q @ 00:10:23]. The baseline prerequisite for anyone mining claims or defending against miners is identical: a statistical outlier must be married to a falsity theory and hard evidence of knowledge.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The newest rulebook for algorithmic cases is DOJ's FOCUS initiative, announced at the end of April 2026, short for fraud oversight through careful use of statistics, which sets guardrails on FCA data mining [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:03:06]. The Reed Smith team explains why it exists: reduced DOJ staff are facing record 2026 filings, so FOCUS is meant to winnow the pile, and what DOJ will accept is a reliable correlation tethered to legal analysis of falsity and scienter rather than a raw statistical spike [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:16:39]. Speed is built into the review: Jennifer Seraphin reports the Shumate memo sets a 60-day seal review with a 120-day maximum, and describes two intake tracks, vetted data miners on one side and interagency or OIG referrals on the other [QLVKZRh_ArE @ 00:23:51]. On the pleading battlefield, Rule 9(b) heightened particularity is the first motion-to-dismiss defense against data-built complaints, a higher bar than an ordinary nonfraud civil case, and the AHLA panel points to Hanagan v. Amazon in the Second Circuit as requiring scienter facts under Rule 9(b), offsetting the subjective-knowledge standard of SuperValu that otherwise makes cases harder to kill early [U_YfcwE9PJk @ 00:20:16]. The street read: a miner who files a bare outlier chart is feeding a dismissal motion.

The modeling playbook comes from Analysis Group's panel. Builders choose between supervised machine learning trained on known fraud and unsupervised outlier and association-rule detection, while deep learning and tree ensembles are flagged as black boxes that raise bias concerns [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:15:28]. Models are screening tools followed by deeper human review; the panel urges CRISP-DM as the workflow, treating model flags like whistleblower complaints, and balancing false positives against false negatives with an F1 score [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:44:42]. The panel's sharpest line is to start with simple things first and not get cute with machine learning, which tracks with how Integra Med Analytics built its cases on simple statistics claiming 1 in 100,000 odds [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:27:54]. AI now runs on both sides of the caption: Sutis and Bender report relators using AI to draft complaints, defendants using AI to dig up counterexamples showing a pattern of compliance, and some courts requiring counsel to certify AI use [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:20:36]. The compliance flip is the same toolkit pointed inward: pharma should mine call patterns, HCP payments and prescriptions for outliers, and DOJ in the Walmart opioid matter pointed at the company's own wealth of dispensing information.

The response numbers are where operators make or lose money. Michael Podberesky of McGuireWoods, ex-DOJ Civil Fraud, reads the signature first: a local U.S. Attorney signals a matter under $10M, while the Civil Fraud Director in Washington signals $10M or more, and the statutory clock runs 20 days for documents and as little as 7 days for oral testimony [JXEl43HAAuo @ 00:09:41]. Tori Young of Seyfarth Shaw prices the exit: the civil self-disclosure pilot runs to April 22, 2027 with repayment at 1.25-1.6x and no factual admissions, CIA, CMPs or revocations, against typical settlements near 2x, with the AKS repayment base set at remuneration rather than claims paid [QLVKZRh_ArE @ 00:30:43]. On the bounty side, relators take 15-30% plus a new tipster track of $10-30,000, while Evan Gotlab of DarrowEverett puts the share upwards of 35% and walks a $20M claim to roughly $6-7M before attorneys take about a third; Gotlab adds that once DOJ is involved, settlements come within 6 months, which compresses the whole fight [5LsJY2Ss1SI @ 00:09:30]. Penalty math is the multiplier that makes mining pay: per-claim penalties stack across thousands or millions of claims, and as Reed Smith puts it, a lot of claims means a lot of penalty [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:09:35].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Triage the Government Demand
   - Read the subpoena or CID, identify the signer, and calendar the 20-day and 7-day clocks.
   - Podberesky's sequence is read it, check the signer for the $10M signal, retain counsel, contact the prosecutor within one to two days to narrow scope, then run a rapid internal investigation [McGuireWoods LLP — JXEl43HAAuo @ 00:09:02].
2. Lock Down People and Data
   - Bring in counsel early, control who knows about the investigation, and preserve IT data.
   - The AHLA CID workflow is counsel early, only truthful statements, controlled knowledge of the investigation, IT preservation, internal investigation and remediation [American Health Law Association — U_YfcwE9PJk @ 00:22:01].
3. Run the Data Back at Yourself
   - Mine call patterns, HCP payments and prescriptions for outliers, then review flags by hand.
   - Analysis Group's panel treats models as screening tools followed by deeper review, with strong human intervention rather than reliance on machine learning alone [Analysis Group — y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:35:20].
4. Price Disclosure Against the Fight
   - Compare the 1.25-1.6x pilot repayment against roughly 2x settlements and forfeiture exposure.
   - Tori Young prices the civil pilot at 1.25-1.6x with an April 22, 2027 deadline, while Epstein Becker Green notes pre-charge seizure risk now factors into the disclosure decision [Seyfarth Shaw LLP — QLVKZRh_ArE @ 00:30:43].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split on the defense side is whether DOJ will actually cull weak qui tams before they reach a courtroom. Foley & Lardner relays Brenna Jenny signaling that DOJ will lean on its dismissal authority more often [J1-aE0W1Eu8 @ 00:08:46]. Reed Smith's Tom Sutis, speaking three months into the FOCUS initiative, calls it too early to tell but still expects DOJ to use the dismissal power more, while fellow panelist David Bender bets on a wave of declinations instead, which leaves relators litigating without the government at the table [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:25:19]. Seyfarth Shaw's Teddy Arnold reads the new Justice Manual language, that DOJ will in each case assess dismissal of declined qui tams against the seven Granston factors, and advises clients to temper expectations rather than plan around a government rescue [QLVKZRh_ArE @ 00:06:49]. The ABA Criminal Justice Section panel names the political brake behind that caution, pointing to Grassley as a reason DOJ stays reluctant to dismiss relator suits [w2SmaDc-C4M @ 01:15:11]. The operating split is stark: one camp budgets for DOJ clearing out weak, data-mined filings at the gate, while the other camp budgets for declined cases grinding forward with relator counsel in control.

Practitioners also split hard on whether data-mined relators are a durable business or a niche that courts keep swatting down. Epstein Becker Green's panel reports that data miners have filed more than 45 percent of qui tams since FY2024, while a FOCUS initiative launched April 30 now puts guardrails on statistical cases [rxUll524o5w @ 00:16:24]. The American Health Law Association panel frames the same docket very differently, listing data-mining cases as only a sliver of current activity next to medical necessity, Stark, anti-kickback and PPP matters [U_YfcwE9PJk @ 00:27:48]. Analysis Group's panel walks through Integra Med Analytics, which built one-in-100,000 simple-statistics claims, only to see its DRG suits dismissed by the Fifth Circuit against Baylor Scott & White and the Ninth Circuit against Providence over an obvious alternative explanation, yet DOJ intervened for the first time in an Integra nursing-home RUGs case [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:29:43]. First Healthcare Compliance's Shawna Itri, relator counsel at Seeger Weiss, cites data-driven settlements of $280 million and $98 million plus Integra settlements of $61 million and $188 million [SKjmFHwe2Rk @ 00:33:33]. The working takeaway from both camps: raw outliers die at the pleading stage, while statistics paired with facts on the ground get paid.

The third fault line is posture once a CID or subpoena lands: cooperate fast, fight on the merits, or settle early. McGuireWoods' Michael Podberesky, ex-DOJ Civil Fraud, warns that the standard is a preponderance of the evidence and that a mud-throwing style toward DOJ is ineffective, and pushes contacting the prosecutor within one to two days to narrow scope and arrange rolling production [JXEl43HAAuo @ 00:13:04]. The ABA Criminal Justice Section panel openly disagrees on litigation tactics, debating whether to depose government witnesses at all, summed up in one line: you don't know what they're gonna say [w2SmaDc-C4M @ 00:17:42]. On DEI certification exposure, Epstein Becker Green's Leah Brownlee Taylor rejects the reflex to fold, telling defendants to assess scienter and materiality first and that they need not rush to settle [rxUll524o5w @ 00:26:38]. DarrowEverett's Evan Gotlab, ex-DOJ and a white-collar chair, frames the trial threat from the other direction: in more than 15 years, Gotlab has never seen a False Claims Act trial, settlements typically land within six months of DOJ involvement, and no settlement closes without the whistleblower's sign-off [5LsJY2Ss1SI @ 00:21:08]. The split is less about law than about leverage: who controls timing, witnesses and the final number.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest trap practitioners report is treating a DOJ declination as a win. Foley & Lardner puts FY2025 qui tam settlements at $5.3 billion, with $2.3 billion of that coming out of declined cases, and states flatly that a declination is not a pass [J1-aE0W1Eu8 @ 00:07:51]. McGuireWoods' panel frames the other side of the ledger: the government declines three-quarters of the time but recovers the vast majority, 90 percent of its money, from the 25 percent it joins, so the intervened minority is where the real hammer falls [JXEl43HAAuo @ 00:16:24]. The penalty math compounds either way. Reed Smith's panel on HB Litigation News warns that data-mined complaints stack per-claim penalties across thousands or millions of claims, and a lot of claims means a lot of penalty [mNX2NY20ks0 @ 00:09:00]. DarrowEverett's Evan Gotlab supplies the small-company endgame: treble damages turn $100,000 into $300,000, and an engineering firm billing false hours settled for $4 million, closed its doors, and left the relator with roughly $900,000; in Gotlab's words, if it's a smaller business, it could lead to the business closing [5LsJY2Ss1SI @ 00:11:28]. Declined, intervened or settled, the bill arrives; the only variable is how many claims sit underneath it.

Data-driven and sampling cases also fail in ways that cost the government and relators real money. The ABA Criminal Justice Section panel catalogs judgment reversals: a $347 million Salas rehab judgment vacated after more than 1,000 surveys turned up few violations, alongside roughly $1 billion in judgments reversed overall [w2SmaDc-C4M @ 00:14:54]. In AseraCare, a sample of 233 patients found 124 unsupported, but after an eight-week trial, a new trial and summary judgment entered sua sponte, the defense won, and the government put up no clinicians to counter a 5 to 10 percent normal rate of clinical disagreement [w2SmaDc-C4M @ 00:35:42]. Analysis Group's panel explains the mechanics: prediction is not causation, models are hostage to the data-generating process such as clinician test-ordering, and data alone cannot prove scienter under the FCA knowingly standard, let alone the higher anti-kickback intent standard [y9nEr_YUXBY @ 00:23:24]. First Healthcare Compliance's Shawna Itri reports the harshest reversal for data-mining relators: DOJ dismissed an LLC's suits against 38 pharma companies because of its false pretenses calls, and Integra's case was dismissed in May 2020 and affirmed by the Fifth Circuit [SKjmFHwe2Rk @ 00:34:16].

The remaining failure modes are self-inflicted by targets. DarshanTalks warns that a partial or reactive disclosure handed DOJ a road map, and tells deal teams to check targets for unresolved OIG audits and just dig deeper [BXNGLzF0b-E @ 00:05:34]. McGuireWoods' panel describes private equity exposure that barely existed three to four years ago and now rises when a sponsor takes a purchase-price concession for a known problem without remediating it, with a coordinated relators' bar targeting deep pockets [JXEl43HAAuo @ 00:03:30]. Husch Blackwell's Jonathan Porter and Brandon Hall shut down a common compliance shortcut: reporting a payment through Open Payments does not cure an anti-kickback willfulness problem, while some reps still tried creative perks like whiskey tastings and lakehouse weekends [FO7NfNf-r0k @ 00:08:08]. Seyfarth Shaw's Teddy Arnold flags the newest trap: under the draft GSA AI clause the government owns prompts and outputs, contractors must certify deletion, and Arnold's fix is human review of AI outputs, because leaning on the tool is just not going to be a defense [QLVKZRh_ArE @ 00:46:37]. Each of these traps converts an ordinary business decision into evidence a prosecutor can read as knowledge rather than compliance.
