## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Silence before filing is the first rule every intake lawyer hammers. James Kaufman at Levin Papantonio tells relators flatly not to go to the newspaper or the press, because publishing the information strips its value to the government and risks blowing the public disclosure bar, at which point the relator is no longer the original source [xH5IpDAiP5c @ 00:00:36]. The same team ties that to first-to-file: going public raises the odds someone else files first and blocks the relator in line, and host Peter Muggie calls the urge to shout it from the rooftops the absolute worst thing a relator can do [xH5IpDAiP5c @ 00:01:36]. Jason T. Brown of Brown, LLC repeats the race logic at the close of the firm's case-selection breakdown, warning that the first whistleblower to file generally blocks everyone behind them and that another day someone else may file the case [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:09:15]. Brown also says the worst thing a whistleblower can do is act impulsively, talk to the wrong person, or expose themselves before knowing their rights [Cz4DaWsS-xM @ 00:05:25]. The firm extends that discipline to the internet: case-specific questions about a real-life scenario should never be left publicly in comments, because doing so can only compromise the case, so relators should email or call counsel privately instead [N4uuM9uvRYY @ 00:06:35].

The second point of agreement is that a relator must hold firsthand, provable knowledge, not a hunch. Beasley Allen states that to be a whistleblower you have to have some first-hand knowledge about what is actually occurring, and notes the typical relator is a bookkeeper, a nurse, or someone dealing directly with services involving the federal government [dnDB-BkTNpk @ 00:01:01]. Sanford Heisler Sharp McKnight frames it as knowledge plus traceability: the whistleblower must trace the wrongdoing in a way that is believable, credible, and verifiable, whether through documents, emails, internal traffic discussing how dangerous the scheme is, or superior knowledge [zvfb6_cVFWg @ 00:02:37]. Brown, LLC asks first whether the caller is an insider or an outsider, defining the insider as someone who saw the meetings, has the emails, and can point to where the bodies are buried, adding that insiders usually clear the public disclosure bar while outsiders often do not [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:01:45]. Ron Chapman of Chapman Law Group lands on the same pleading reality: because fraud is so easy to allege, the relator must supply the what, when, where, how, and who, and the relator's most important job is producing enough facts for a disclosure statement [4cRTBx7ikiY @ 00:03:08].

Third, every practitioner agrees the economics run on government money, contingency, and secrecy. Tony Munter of Price Benowitz reduces a good case to two elements: it must plainly scream out as fraud, and some government agency must have lost money, because a wild fraud between two securities firms without government money is not a traditional False Claims Act case [IX4WhZTmPRI @ 00:01:07]. Munter also explains why contingency is the only workable fee model: the relator pays nothing up front, even after being fired with no money, and since the sealed case can be discussed with no one except counsel, the relator has to marry the lawyer and cannot be worrying that every phone call costs money [KMtkKgsZzRo @ 00:00:43]. Cohen Milstein confirms the seal mechanics, stating that only the whistleblower, counsel, the court, and the government know a case has been filed, and that the firm works fully contingent, collecting a fee only when the relator receives an award [skGozAS0va8 @ 00:00:39]. Gary Martin Hays walks the same sequence for a Medicare billing specialist being told to bill for services never performed: find a lawyer, have the attorney research the evidence, file under seal so even the defendant is kept in the dark, then let the Department of Justice investigate and decide whether to intervene [64dIGOXUwoc @ 00:05:05].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Brown, LLC runs intake like an underwriting desk. Jason T. Brown says the firm turns down the overwhelming majority of cases and has said no to millions of dollars in offered fees, because a whistleblower case is a years-long war [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:00:00]. The damages screen is explicit: as a general rule the firm wants fraud against the government exceeding $10 million in actual damages, measured pre-trebling, against a solvent defendant, but damages and liability sit on a sliding scale, so a billion-dollar fraud can justify hard proof problems while a smaller case with a smoking-gun document can beat a giant case built on inference [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:02:58]. The third filter is where most cases die: facts versus theories. 'I think my company is defrauding Medicare' is a call; patient files, billing codes, dates, and the director's email ordering upcoding is a case, because Rule 9B particularity gets complaints dismissed every week [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:04:15]. The remaining filters are character, geography, and program: the firm refuses callers who are mean to intake staff or demand to speak only with Brown, weighs circuit splits and how aggressive each United States Attorneys Office is, and concentrates on bounty programs. Stephen Teller's first move is simpler: have the insider describe exactly how the government is getting ripped off [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:04:16].

Building the package is document-driven. Stephen Teller of Teller Law asks the insider to describe the fraud, then gathers whatever paper or electronic records help explain it and drafts a couple of long letters plus a complaint that fits the False Claims Act, all handed to the Department of Justice and filed under seal without serving the defendant [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:04:32]. Ron Chapman shows what the disclosure statement should carry using incident-to billing: a practice can bill 100 percent for a nurse practitioner or physician assistant who would normally draw 85 percent, but only if the physician is present in the office, so a biller who sees the doctor managing staff by telephone or text message has a scheme worth documenting [4cRTBx7ikiY @ 00:03:41]. The statement then gets built from calendars, billing records, medical records, and a list of people with intimate knowledge who might testify, because the US attorney uses it to decide whether to intervene [4cRTBx7ikiY @ 00:04:34]. Brown, LLC adds the construction rules: build a timeline, separate what you know from what you suspect, identify who directed the conduct, preserve lawful evidence already in your possession, and never destroy, alter, or hide anything or take privileged or unauthorized documents [CZeUV80NaAA @ 00:07:40].

Once filed, the clock is slow and the seal is the operating environment. Teller reports the seal typically runs 6 months at a time, with the government asking the judge for more, so cases stay sealed 8 months, 14 months, or a couple of years, and many Teller Law cases finish two or three years after filing; even the fast ones take almost a year [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:08:23]. During that window the Department of Justice contacts the client agency, issues civil investigative demands for documents, written answers, or depositions, and occasionally has relators wear recording devices [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:09:24]. At the end of the seal the government intervenes or declines; frequently it has already negotiated a settlement, intervenes, and the case is dismissed, while a declined case can still be litigated by the relator, sometimes over government opposition. Brown, LLC describes the partial seal lift that often precedes settlement: the government asks the relator and counsel to consent to sharing a redacted complaint with the defendant to hear its position, and even without consent the judge typically defers to the government as the true party of interest [N4uuM9uvRYY @ 00:03:00]. Brown adds that experienced defense counsel start suspecting a hidden case when government demands keep circling the same subject [N4uuM9uvRYY @ 00:05:09].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split among these practitioners is over who actually gets to be a relator. Jason T. Brown of Brown, LLC opens intake by sorting insiders from outsiders: an employee, billing manager, or compliance officer who saw the meetings and holds the emails usually clears the public disclosure bar, while outsiders often do not and get underwritten far more skeptically [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:02:26]. Yet in a separate session the same firm stresses that nowhere in the False Claims Act does a relator need to be an employee, listing board members, consultants, patients, data miners, statistics quants, and competitors as live categories [39Q-yYGWPVg @ 00:01:00]. Beasley Allen draws the line tighter, insisting a relator must hold first-hand knowledge of what is actually occurring, and says the typical relator is a bookkeeper, a nurse, or someone dealing directly with services involving the federal government [dnDB-BkTNpk @ 00:01:01]. The outsider lane stays open but narrow: Brown points to PPP cases built by cross-checking ProPublica headcount and revenue against corporate filings, yet says entities formed solely to litigate on prescribing-propensity data drew backlash, and the firm wants to see a little bit of the flame, not just smoke, before it signs on [39Q-yYGWPVg @ 00:08:24].

The second fault line is how much the relator should do before counsel is engaged. Ron Chapman of Chapman Law Group describes the insider actively looking around, gathering information, and assembling calendars, billing records, medical records, and lists of willing witnesses that the attorney turns into a disclosure statement [4cRTBx7ikiY @ 00:04:34]. Brown pushes the opposite way: do not go gather documents on your own, and confirm with an attorney that you have lawful, ethical access before forwarding anything [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:04:28]. Elsewhere, Brown warns that the instinct to gather evidence gets people fired for probing around, or criminally charged for touching information they had no lawful right to access [Cz4DaWsS-xM @ 00:06:48]. Stephen Teller adds a modern wrinkle: do not run your documents through AI or have it build a summary before calling the lawyer, because it raises confidentiality concerns and can create discovery problems later [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:06:42]. The practical synthesis is sequencing. Chapman's document-heavy playbook and Teller's intake, where the client describes the fraud and then starts gathering paper and electronic records with counsel, both work once a lawyer is already steering what is collected and how. Get the order wrong and, as Brown puts it, the wrong first move can make a bad situation worse.

Practitioners also split on which cases are worth the war. Brown runs a hard filter: the firm generally wants fraud above $10 million in actual, pre-trebling damages, a solvent defendant, and a sliding scale where a smoking-gun document can carry a smaller case while a billion-dollar fraud can justify hard proof problems [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:03:29]. Tony Munter reduces selection to two questions: does the case plainly scream fraud, and can you name the government agency that lost money, with public disclosure, original source, and limitations issues treated as important but secondary to that core [IX4WhZTmPRI @ 00:00:51]. On fees there is no real dispute about structure, only emphasis. Munter calls the contingent fee the only way to run these cases because the seal leaves the relator with one person to call and nobody should ration phone calls to counsel [KMtkKgsZzRo @ 00:01:37]. Brown frames the same contingency as the reason for selectivity, since the firm funds every case with its own money and years. Cohen Milstein likewise only receives a fee if the client receives a whistleblower reward. On timing, Teller says the longer the better, because a long seal means the government believes the case is worth the effort, while even fast cases run almost a year [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:12:14].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The fastest way to kill a qui tam is to go loud. Levin Papantonio's James Kaufman says the urge to get the word out can blow the case: going to the newspaper or press dumps the information into the public, strips its value to the government, and risks the public disclosure bar that ends original-source status [xH5IpDAiP5c @ 00:00:36]. The panel calls it counterintuitive: the instinct to shout it to the rooftops to save people money or get them out of harm's way is the absolute worst thing you could do. Publicity also raises the odds that someone else files first and blocks the relator in line. Brown frames the same clock bluntly: the first whistleblower to file generally blocks everyone behind them, and another day someone else may file your case [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:09:15]. Seal discipline continues after filing. Munter warns that once a case is under seal the relator cannot tell anyone except the lawyer, which is why Munter says you have to marry your lawyer at that point [KMtkKgsZzRo @ 00:01:03]. Brown adds that posting case-specific questions in public comments can only compromise the case, and that experienced defense counsel will start suspecting a hidden False Claims Act action once government demands keep circling the same subject [N4uuM9uvRYY @ 00:06:35].

Relators with dirty hands face a different failure mode. Brown says big sources bring big problems, and that once a whistleblower's own conduct can be questioned the strategy flips from chasing an award to protection. Under the False Claims Act, a relator who planned and initiated the violation can see the award reduced, and one convicted of criminal conduct arising from it is dismissed from the civil action and receives nothing [CZeUV80NaAA @ 00:02:12]. The biggest misconception Brown flags is immunity: a whistleblower submission is not an immunity agreement, and the reward track and the criminal cooperation track are two different things that can overlap and sometimes conflict [CZeUV80NaAA @ 00:05:24]. Brown's six-rung ladder runs from minimal involvement through knowing participant, manager or approver, planner or initiator, and finally obstruction, meaning destroyed evidence, altered records, lies, or interference with investigators, with exposure rising at every rung and with every dollar of direct benefit. Teller flags a quieter version of the trap: settle a related retaliation claim carelessly and the relator might accidentally settle the False Claims Act case out from under the government, though Teller says it is rare [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:06:26]. Brown adds that HR will ask why the employee waited a year or two and treat that delay as grounds for termination [Cz4DaWsS-xM @ 00:07:46].

Past the publicity trap, cases fail on proof and on the relator. Brown says the facts-versus-theories gap is where most cases die: a caller who thinks the company is defrauding Medicare has a theory, while patient files, billing codes, dates, and the director's email ordering upcoding make a case, and courts dismiss complaints every week under Rule 9B when the relator is strong on suspicion and weak on specifics [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:04:41]. Chapman makes the same point from the healthcare side, calling the disclosure statement extremely important because the US attorney uses it to decide whether to intervene [4cRTBx7ikiY @ 00:04:48]. Credibility is the second filter. Brown says the government vets the whistleblower before it vets the defendant, that a shifting story or a vendetta will be felt by a jury and by the Department of Justice, and that the firm has walked away from probably hundreds of millions in potential fees over difficult clients, including callers rude to intake staff [Hb6Ogl9Fm8I @ 00:05:56]. The economics are unforgiving. Brown says more cases fail than succeed even after the firm takes them, and Teller says even fast cases take almost a year, with seals renewed six months at a time, and quite a few of Teller's cases finish two or three years after filing [SiATzxJHy2Y @ 00:08:23].
