## Executive Summary

Relator counsel, a former EDVA U.S. Attorney, a DOJ fraud-section alumna and contractor-side defense firms converge on one point: since DOJ's October 2021 Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative, a cybersecurity gap stops being a fixable contract defect and becomes a knowing false claim the moment an invoice goes out. Three theories drive the cases: deficient cyber products or services, misrepresented controls such as NIST 800-171 and SPRS certifications, and late incident reporting. The economics are brutal: treble damages, per-claim penalties quoted from about $11,665 to $28,619, relator shares of 15-30%, and settlements ranging from Georgia Tech's roughly $1M to Aerojet Rocketdyne's $9M. The pipeline is insider-fed, led by overworked security officers who say nobody listened. Practitioners split on whether a misrepresented security posture is automatically material, whether a breach is needed to prove materiality, and how hard relator counsel should push the government during the seal period.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same premise: the False Claims Act is, in Julie Bracker's words, the federal government's 'primary tool for addressing the knowing misuse of taxpayer funds,' and since October 2021 DOJ has pointed that tool straight at contractor cybersecurity [j_QEFJG-ON4 @ 00:01:33]. Thompson Coburn's panel dates the Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative to that October 2021 announcement, enforcing 31 USC 3729 against knowing cybersecurity violations, in a year when FCA recoveries topped $5.6B, more than $5B of it from healthcare [CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:05:25]. PilieroMazza's cyber team is blunt about the design: the initiative does not invent any new cybersecurity requirements; it uses the FCA to enforce the ones already sitting in your contracts and to deter noncompliance [OqheRWaihEo @ 01:08:36]. Blue Ocean Law Group frames the before-and-after in operator terms: before 2021 a vulnerability was a fixable fine, after 2021 it is fraud, because 'the crime is the lie' [4mXSodsYqr4 @ 00:03:00]. The consensus takeaway is that cyber exposure now rides on representations rather than on incidents. A contractor that certifies controls it does not actually run, then keeps billing against those certifications, has handed both DOJ and any disgruntled insider a ready-made theory that requires no stolen data and no breach report. That is the baseline every speaker builds on.

On the theories of liability, the speakers are unanimous. Jones Day lays out the three uses DOJ announced: knowingly failing to comply with cybersecurity standards, knowingly misrepresenting controls such as encryption claims or incident-response plans, and knowingly failing to timely report cyber incidents [ah8GhXnbe-8 @ 00:00:57]. Bracker maps the same trio onto contractor paperwork, treating every NIST and DFARS certification made since January 1, 2018 as a potential misrepresentation and arguing that an inaccurate SPRS score carries a duty to correct, with 'no patience' for riding out the three-year recertification cycle [j_QEFJG-ON4 @ 00:08:40]. The scienter line is equally settled. Thompson Coburn stresses that the knowledge standard means actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance or reckless disregard, a lower bar than the CFAA hacking statute, yet one that still excludes innocent mistake, mere error or negligence [CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:12:17]. PilieroMazza's Matt Feinberg and Camilla Hundley compress it into one line: 'a mere mistake or accident' is not enough, but recklessness is [ZyG_Zu7OvIg @ 00:04:45]. For an operator, the practical read is that ignorance stops being a shelter once warning signs exist. Deliberate ignorance and reckless disregard sit inside the definition, so a company that declines to look at its own security posture, then invoices on the strength of that posture, has walked into the standard rather than around it.

The third pillar of consensus is that the invoice, not the breach, is the trigger. Daniel Strauss of Cordatus LLP, speaking on CMMC LaunchPad, describes two liability paths: representations made at proposal or award, covering 8(a) status, 51% veteran ownership and cyber status, and an implied re-certification riding on every invoice, which the government reads as a license to claw back every contract dollar [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:05:21]. Blue Ocean Law Group lands in the same place, warning that simply sending an invoice while noncompliant can be a false claim and calling the Act 'the government's number one weapon for fighting fraud' [4mXSodsYqr4 @ 00:01:21]. The speakers also agree on who pulls the trigger. Jones Day flags the overworked, under-resourced information security officer as 'somebody who could legitimately turn out to be a whistleblower' [ah8GhXnbe-8 @ 00:03:26]. Former DOJ fraud-section assistant director Sarah Mlan, interviewed by Federal News Network, confirms the pattern from the government side: in cyber matters it is in-house cybersecurity professionals with extensive information who come forward, typically because nobody listened to them [ovf7HOfeGQ0 @ 00:06:38]. The baseline prerequisite is therefore cultural as much as technical. The internal security complaint that management shrugs off is effectively the first draft of a sealed qui tam complaint, written by the person who knows exactly where the gaps sit.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The unit economics are where contractors get blindsided. Zack Trilliger, the former EDVA U.S. Attorney who now runs a firm's DC office, walks the math: damages triple, so $5M becomes $15M, and penalties of $14K to $28K attach to each claim, including every invoice on an IDIQ, before suspension and debarment even enter the picture [-2BTi30xXrA @ 00:07:24]. Daniel Strauss pushes the base number higher, explaining that on a $1M contract carrying $100K of profit the government's position is that the full $1M is owed regardless of profit, and treble damages turn it into $3M [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:49:01]. PilieroMazza's cyber panel runs the same multiplier on a $10M contract to reach a $30M recovery claim [OqheRWaihEo @ 01:07:40]. Matt Feinberg then layers the per-invoice penalty on top: 60 invoices means 60 violations, a $150K billing error balloons to roughly $475K at about '23,000 a pop,' and a $7M HUBZone contract can generate a whistleblower share of roughly $7M on its own [ZyG_Zu7OvIg @ 00:46:22]. Trilliger's own label for this exposure is 'extinction level events for certain companies.' The operator lesson is to model risk per invoice rather than per contract, because the penalty count scales directly with billing frequency, and monthly invoicing on a multi-year IDIQ quietly stacks dozens of separate violations onto a single underlying compliance gap.

The procedural clock is long and lopsided. Bracker notes that the statute gives the government 60 days to investigate, which is barely enough to assemble a team and interview the relator; extensions run from three months to a year, the longest seal Bracker has carried lasted over ten years, and the average case runs two to three years from filing to resolution [j_QEFJG-ON4 @ 00:14:44]. On the government's investigative toolkit, Trilliger reports civil investigative demands for both documents and testimony, one of them reaching back seven years across all records, with 30-day or 14-day turnarounds and a public motion to compel waiting for anyone who ignores them [-2BTi30xXrA @ 00:14:47]. The relator economics run on a sliding scale. UAP Legion breaks out 15-25% when DOJ intervenes and 25-30% when it declines, so a $300M recovery pays the relator between $45M and $90M, with the first-to-file rule rewarding whoever reaches the courthouse first [Y9DOny35SW0 @ 00:13:09]. Sarah Mlan adds that relators collect up to 30% plus attorney's fees and that 'you don't actually have to have a lot of information' to qualify [ovf7HOfeGQ0 @ 00:06:04]. Low evidentiary entry cost, a first-to-file race and a percentage-of-recovery payout mean the relator pipeline stays full, and a contractor should assume any known gap is already being documented by someone inside the building.

The live-fire mechanics center on SPRS scores, notification windows and self-disclosure. Strauss gives the concrete play for a score that collapses from 110 to -200: update SPRS and fire an FYI email to the contracting officer now, then move to formal notice once a minimum score becomes mandatory, partly so a competitor cannot report you first [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:14:03]. On incident reporting, Jones Day tells contractors to pull the actual notification timeframe from each contract, because it may be vague, such as 'reasonable time' or 'without undue delay,' or hard, like the GDPR's 72-hour deadline [ah8GhXnbe-8 @ 00:02:05]. Thompson Coburn adds the DFARS overlay: rapid incident reporting tied to NIST 800-171, plus preservation of anything related to the incident for at least 90 days [CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:22:47]. Self-disclosure carries a measurable discount; Bracker cites Verizon coming forward on its own and settling at a multiplier of roughly 1.5 rather than the full treble [j_QEFJG-ON4 @ 00:12:07]. Strauss reports the same dynamic from the defense chair, where going first let the client control the narrative and lowered the settlement. The working rule is to treat SPRS as a living certification, pre-map every contract's reporting deadline, and assume that whoever tells the contracting officer first sets the terms of the conversation.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Map every certification you are making
   - Build a contract-specific checklist of every cyber clause and representation tied to each award.
   - Pull FAR 52.204-21 and its roughly 26 safeguards, the DFARS clause requiring NIST 800-171, and DFARS 252.204-7021 CMMC tiers into one register [Thompson Coburn LLP — CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:17:58]; then designate a compliance employee, run internal audits and keep contract-specific checklists [PilieroMazza PLLC — ZyG_Zu7OvIg @ 00:39:01].
2. Keep SPRS live and tell the contracting officer
   - Update SPRS the moment the score moves and notify the contracting officer in writing.
   - On a drop from 110 to -200, update SPRS and send an FYI email to the contracting officer, escalating to formal notice once a minimum score is mandatory, and close POA&M gaps inside 180 days [CMMC LaunchPad — GkIQylusGaw @ 00:14:03].
3. Clock every incident against the contract
   - Pre-map each contract's notification window and lock incident data on discovery.
   - Identify whether the contract says 'reasonable time,' 'without undue delay' or a hard 72-hour deadline [Jones Day — ah8GhXnbe-8 @ 00:02:05], and preserve everything tied to the incident for at least 90 days [Thompson Coburn LLP — CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:22:47].
4. Run the CID and subpoena playbook
   - Freeze destruction, engage outside counsel and open a channel with DOJ early.
   - On a subpoena or CID, don't panic, call counsel and halt document destruction [PilieroMazza PLLC — ZyG_Zu7OvIg @ 00:44:22]; designate a point of contact, issue a litigation hold and line up IT resources [WilliamsMullen — NdqYGSN5BYc @ 00:54:27]; then stop any ongoing problem, investigate internally and engage DOJ early [Cape Endeavors, Inc. — -2BTi30xXrA @ 00:29:21].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is when to fix a bad SPRS number. Julie Bracker, a relator-side attorney on ClearanceJobs who has practiced only FCA work since 2005, asserts a duty to correct an inaccurate SPRS score now, has “no patience” for waiting, and expects the defense bar to lean on the 3-year recertification cycle instead [j_QEFJG-ON4 @ 00:08:40]. The CMMC LaunchPad panel with Cordatus LLP's Daniel Strauss runs a tiered play: when a score drops from 110 to -200, update SPRS and send the contracting officer an FYI email, then switch to formal notice once a minimum score becomes mandatory, partly so a competitor cannot report you first [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:14:36]. Strauss adds that voluntary disclosure has been eliminated, disclosure is now mandatory, and going first let the client control the narrative and lowered the settlement [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:55:12]. Theodore Watson goes further, telling contractors who spot a potential FCA issue during performance to report it immediately, “over and beyond any mandatory disclosure requirements,” because paying counsel beats a tarnished reputation [uJurzK0qLLU @ 00:01:55]. Bracker's own scoreboard backs the early-mover camp: Verizon self-disclosed and received a multiplier of about 1.5 rather than the full treble every speaker describes.

The second fault line is whether a cyber gap is fraud or just a contract problem. Blue Ocean Law Group's narrator takes the hard line: promising security and failing to deliver is fraud even if no data is stolen, no breach needs to be proven, and “the crime is the lie” [4mXSodsYqr4 @ 00:00:48]. WilliamsMullen's defense panel runs the opposite playbook: a mere breach of contract is not a False Claims Act violation, so the civil defense goal is to convert the claim into a breach of contract and win summary judgment, and after Escobar, years of payment despite known noncompliance is a strong materiality defense [NdqYGSN5BYc @ 00:30:12]. PilieroMazza PLLC's Part 1 panel sits between them: defective glue is not an FCA violation but falsified testing is, and some product disputes look like “a contract dispute question, not a false claim question,” yet DOJ in certain regions has started to “grasp at those issues” [OqheRWaihEo @ 00:54:52]. On CMMC LaunchPad the split played out live: Strauss ties materiality to what the contract says, while attendee Joe and law student Leslie argued a misrepresented security posture is material, even though Leslie reports contracting officers treat CMMC as a “check in the box” [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:22:27].

Practitioners also read the enforcement machine differently. Bracker openly opposes relator counsel who want to hold the government's “feet to the fire” on investigations that, by Bracker's count, average 2 to 3 years from filing to resolution [j_QEFJG-ON4 @ 00:35:03]. Former EDVA U.S. Attorney Zack Trilliger, on Cape Endeavors, frames DOJ as reloading rather than retreating: a May Criminal Division memo lists procurement fraud as a top priority, Inspectors General carry on DOGE work, and the whistleblower pipeline is unaffected by resource reallocation [-2BTi30xXrA @ 00:34:02]. Federal News Network's guest, a former assistant director in DOJ's fraud section, undercuts the idea of a dedicated cyber squad: there is no separate cyber fraud unit, just a civil fraud section of about 130 lawyers partnering with U.S. Attorney's offices and agency cyber experts who are “essentially the clients” [ovf7HOfeGQ0 @ 00:07:48]. ASI Education's hosts attack the compliance regime itself, floating a “hot take” that incumbents want CMMC kept as a barrier to entry and arguing the fix is to make CMMC a CLIN so contractors simply price to it, while phase two sits paused since July [95bEAYfWTiY @ 00:12:20].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first economics check is that resolution costs can dwarf whatever the gap saved. Thompson Coburn's panel walked through CHS, which paid $930,000 plus $531,691 toward the relators' fees, while the relators themselves collected $172,050 against $474,018 in reimbursements [CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:28:02]. Their verdict: “the cost of the settlement far exceeded the original benefit to CHS” [CNlX3Qfdluw @ 00:31:03]. Aerojet Rocketdyne shows the declined-case trap: DOJ declined to intervene, the case kept going, summary judgment was denied, and the company settled for $9 million on the first day of trial, which Daniel Strauss on CMMC LaunchPad guesses compares to a possible $100 million ask [GkIQylusGaw @ 01:01:14]. Strauss also pegs Georgia Tech at about $1 million for having no SSP, and Trilliger notes the investigation alone can run years and cost millions while the government offers no settlement number upfront. Trilliger has seen defendants fabricate SPRS scores and submit blank documents, and warns “this is something you do not want to screw around with” [-2BTi30xXrA @ 00:29:06]. Thompson Coburn's defense menu reflects that math: run an internal investigation, seek declination, offer refunds, avoid parallel proceedings, and push for the broadest release.

The second trap is assuming exposure tracks profit. The CMMC LaunchPad panel walks the math: on a $1 million contract with $100,000 of profit, the government's position is that the contractor owes the full $1 million, or $3 million once trebled, “regardless of profit” [GkIQylusGaw @ 00:49:01]. PilieroMazza's Matt Feinberg and Camilla Hundley show how fast that compounds: 60 invoices are 60 violations at roughly $23,000 “a pop,” a $150,000 error becomes about $475,000, and a $7 million HUBZone contract produces a whistleblower share of about $7 million [ZyG_Zu7OvIg @ 00:46:22]. Small-business status claims carry their own multiplier: PilieroMazza's Part 1 panel explains that deemed-certification and presumed-loss rules set damages at three times the gross contract value plus about $25,000 per invoice, with settlements of $48.5 million for a pass-through and $1.75 million for failing to recertify after an acquisition [OqheRWaihEo @ 00:20:46]. Trilliger converts the abstraction into extinction risk: $5 million in damages becomes $15 million, penalties of $14,000 to $28,000 attach to each claim including every IDIQ invoice, and suspension and debarment can follow, “massive penalties like extinction level events for certain companies” [-2BTi30xXrA @ 00:07:24].

The third failure mode is the ignored insider. Jones Day flags the overworked, under-resourced information security officer as someone who “could legitimately turn out to be a whistleblower” [ah8GhXnbe-8 @ 00:03:26]. Federal News Network's former DOJ fraud official confirms the pattern from the inside: in cyber cases, in-house security professionals holding extensive information are the ones coming forward saying “nobody listened to me,” and a relator does not need much information to qualify for up to 30% plus fees [ovf7HOfeGQ0 @ 00:06:38]. PilieroMazza's Feinberg and Hundley tell contractors to take every internal complaint seriously because the complainer can become a qui tam relator and competitors can file too, while Bracker urges giving complainants a written resolution [ZyG_Zu7OvIg @ 00:06:20]. Process mistakes compound the damage once DOJ arrives. Trilliger has seen a CID demand seven years of all records, turnaround runs 30 or 14 days, and ignoring one draws a public motion to compel [-2BTi30xXrA @ 00:14:47]. WilliamsMullen's panel adds that people must preserve documents and not destroy or modify them, and that D&O and E&O coverage costs something but only exists if you ask for it.
