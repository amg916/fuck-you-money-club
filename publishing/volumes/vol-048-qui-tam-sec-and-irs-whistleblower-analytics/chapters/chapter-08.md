## Executive Summary

Customs-fraud qui tam practitioners agree the False Claims Act squarely reaches tariff evasion, and that the attack surface is CBP Form 7501: origin, HTS code, description, and declared value. Tycko & Zavareei's Jon Tycko maps the schemes: undervaluation via dual invoicing, misclassification, transshipment, sham substantial transformation, omitted assists, and de minimis structuring. These run against Section 301 tariffs of 25% and AD/CVD orders reaching hundreds of percent, with only 1-2% of containers inspected. The street math: $2M evaded across 25 shipments becomes roughly $6.5M in liability, relators collect 15-30% (about 17-18% typical), seals average 18 months, and wins take 2.5-3 years. Beyond customs, PPP affiliate concealment, grant charging, and state FCAs paying up to 50% widen the field. Disagreement runs on scienter, intervention odds, and whether state policy enforcement belongs under the FCA.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The customs bar agrees on one thing first: the False Claims Act covers tariff evasion, full stop. John Tio, who has run roughly 15 customs cases since 2012, puts it flatly that "there's no question that the false claims act covers Customs fraud" [MTKHmo8s-kc @ 00:27:22]. Jon Tycko explains the hook: customs cases run on the reverse false claims provision, which needs only a false record and knowing falsity, and importers cannot keep their "head in the sand" [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:04:06]. The false record almost always sits on CBP Form 7501, where four fields carry the lie: country of origin, product description, HTS code, and quantity or value. Shipping Solutions anchors the stakes with a $26 million penalty upheld on appeal against an importer that labeled Chinese goods as Taiwanese to dodge antidumping duties [EIHwzCe4zZM @ 00:00:06]. Brown, LLC reduces the field to two dominant plays: stripping certain basis costs out of dutiable value and falsifying country of origin, typically by drop-shipping China-tariffed goods, roughly 25% exposure, through a third country [XXtgvEhkvo4 @ 00:00:54]. Tax liability itself is carved out of the federal FCA and routes to IRS or anti-money-laundering programs, so the customs relator works duties, not income tax. Every scheme the practitioners describe ultimately resolves to a provably false line on an entry form.

Second point of consensus: the government cannot see this fraud without insiders, and tariff spikes are manufacturing more of it. Tio cites statistics that only 1% or maybe 2% of all containers get inspected, and warns that origin fraud is "very hard to detect without whistleblowers" because CBP has little visibility into supply chains [MTKHmo8s-kc @ 00:21:23]. Steve Teller describes the incentive engine: sudden tariff hikes catch importers who picked price points under the old rates, and that "really increases the incentives for them to be dishonest" [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:00:48]. Jason T. Brown predicted more customs cases built on circumvention of China tariffs and said the government wants to "make an example" of drop-ship schemes dressed up with a superficial or cosmetic change [5fDm1yVZ4cM @ 00:00:41]. Tycko says the relator pool is wide open: individuals or entities, insiders or outsiders, competitors, even trade associations, and foreign relators are allowed [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:16:41]. Tio adds that higher tariffs hand cheaters a competitive edge, and names competitors, industry insiders, and employees as the relators who surface it. The operator read is simple: the people closest to sourcing, pricing, and logistics hold the evidence CBP lacks, and every new tariff layer widens the gap between honest and cheating importers.

The consensus carries past the docks into relief loans, grants, and state statutes. Brown says viable PPP cases center on borrowers that concealed affiliates to slip under the 500-employee or $10 million cap [5fDm1yVZ4cM @ 00:03:09]. Cheryl Burch of USC's Institute for Creative Technologies tells research administrators they are exposed when they process a grant expense they know does not benefit the project or submit a proposal with inaccurate statements, because the Act reaches "a false statement that leads to a false claim" [oh1aiSdEJHY @ 00:00:53]. Rebecca Ferdick of Husch Blackwell flags that state FCAs vary widely: California pays relators up to 50% in non-intervened cases against 25-30% federally, some states cover claims to political subdivisions, and some reach tax claims [_n3NJXxKz_8 @ 00:03:13]. Across all of it, the knowledge standard is the shared baseline. Shipping Solutions defines "knowingly" to include actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance, or reckless disregard, and notes the whistleblower takes a share of the recovery [EIHwzCe4zZM @ 00:01:15]. Minnesota's statute, about 15 years old and not limited to Medicaid, carries roughly $27,000 per claim, and its AG already chased false COVID rental-assistance claims. For an operator, that means customs, PPP, grants, and state money all run through the same knowing-falsity filter.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The unit economics are concrete. Tycko walks a model case: $2 million in evaded duty across 25 shipments converts to roughly $6.5 million in liability once treble damages and per-shipment penalties stack [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:25:04]. Relator share runs 15-25% when the government intervenes and 25-30% when it declines; he pegs 15% of $6.5 million at "$925,000," though the arithmetic lands near $975,000, and says awards average "about 18%" [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:29:15]. Teller's field number is tighter: about 17% is typical, the realistic band is 15-25%, and anything above 20% is hard to get; one cable-company recovery of $40 million paid the relator 16%, stated as $6.5 million and described as "really life-changing money" [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:12:30]. On the relief-loan side, PilieroMazza's Matt Feinberg prices penalties at $11,665 to $23,331 per claim and shows a $5 million contract turning into $15 million in treble damages [eIBPl3LNUXQ @ 00:09:59]. Feinberg also reports that 25-40% of FCA cases originate with the government itself. Fees shift to the defendant, and most relator counsel work on contingency, so the relator fronts no hourly bill. Tycko's customs scoreboard for 2011 to 2023: 43 settlements, 42 of them relator-driven, more than $220 million recovered, and more than $20 million paid out in rewards.

Execution hinges on surviving procedure. Tycko's public disclosure trap is the first filter: raw, unanalyzed data is generally not treated as a public disclosure, which matters for anyone building a case from import data [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:17:20]. Filing is "a secret filing" under seal; the statute says 60 days, but the seal averages about 18 months, and breaking it risks losing the reward or getting the case dismissed [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:20:33]. During that window the government can intervene, decline, or dismiss over the relator's objection, and successful cases take 2.5 to 3 years or more. Brown warns that first-to-file rules can leave later filers with nothing, and that relator-versus-relator disputes get "ugly" and are often settled quietly [5fDm1yVZ4cM @ 00:09:45]. Teller's pre-filing sequence for insiders: research the issue first, raise it with your boss if the goal is to fix it, and call FCA counsel if the boss turns defensive; calling an employment lawyer early is "a great idea," and complaints should be documented strategically, from an informal "crayon" note to a formal HR escalation [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:14:41]. Tycko counts only about 500 specialist lawyers nationwide in this space, and names the importer of record, overseas suppliers, downstream retailers, and owners as potential defendants.

Operators also price the alternative channels. Tycko says CBP's e-Allegations route, which needs Form 4623, and EAPA claims, limited to AD/CVD and directly affected parties, cap rewards at $250,000 and operate as a CBP "black box" compared with a qui tam [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:34:59]. On the state side, Husch Blackwell's Todd G. walks a DC tax FCA where a Bitcoin investor allegedly evaded about $25 million through false residency claims, including false W-2 addresses, and the matter settled for $40 million, then adds New York's 2021 Fanatics matter, more than $1.5 million over undercollected sales tax; the firm also cites a Massachusetts runway case that settled for $3 million with nearly $1.5 million more recovered federally [_n3NJXxKz_8 @ 00:14:04]. For financial fraud, Tycko & Zavareei lists at least five avenues: the FCA, SEC, CFTC, DOJ banking, and Treasury/BSA, each paying a percentage of actual recovery under its own procedures, with few reports reaching an award, so submissions must be compelling [azRmbEExKOw @ 00:00:23]. The National Desk reports a new $25 million whistleblower program pitched with a "1% commission" and $260 billion in uncovered fraud, but "just reporting suspected fraud won't automatically get you a check": payment requires a conviction or significant enforcement action [yWSbhn3NK9w @ 00:01:55].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how much intent a case needs. K2 GRC sells the FCA as a near strict-liability hammer: treble damages plus an inflation-linked per-claim penalty that, in their telling, applies "even if it was an accident" and even to information submitted "unknowingly" [BgDdBSZpEjc @ 00:01:00]. The customs specialists run a tighter standard. Shipping Solutions defines "knowingly" as actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance, or reckless disregard [EIHwzCe4zZM @ 00:01:15]. Jon Tycko of Tycko & Zavareei builds customs cases on the reverse false claims provision with two required elements, a false record and knowing falsity, and stresses that importers cannot put their "head in the sand" [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:05:42]. Steve Teller draws the line that matters inside tariff-classification work: misstating what an item actually is counts as fraud, while choosing between two plausible HTS categories sits closer to a regulatory question [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:02:46]. For a data-mining relator the playbook difference is real. K2 GRC's framing would treat every coding error as a payday, but the litigators who actually file customs cases want evidence of deliberate conduct, reckless disregard, or a willful blind spot. A file built on a defensible judgment call between two codes is the weakest pitch an operator can bring to relator counsel.

Payout expectations diverge by channel and by lawyer. Tycko quotes the federal bands as 15 to 25 percent when the government intervenes and 25 to 30 percent when it declines [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:26:11]. Teller is more guarded about the top of the range: roughly 17 percent is typical, the band runs 15 to 25, and getting above 20 percent is hard [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:12:30]. Rebecca Ferdick of Husch Blackwell points to the state-level outlier, California, which pays relators up to 50 percent in non-intervened cases against 25 to 30 percent federally [_n3NJXxKz_8 @ 00:03:13]. The administrative routes sit at the other end of the spectrum. Tycko describes CBP's e-Allegations and EAPA routes as a "black box" that caps rewards at $250,000, with EAPA confined to AD/CVD and directly affected parties [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:34:59]. That cap is the pivot for route selection. A relator sitting on a multi-million-dollar evasion pattern leaves most of the upside on the table by going administrative, while the qui tam route keeps the reward tied to treble damages and per-claim penalties. The trade is a capped, opaque administrative payout against a sealed, multi-year fight, with fee-shifting and contingency counsel absorbing the cost.

Forum, constitutionality, and tax coverage are all live fights. John Tio, appearing on Fraud in America, flags the Island Industries ruling in the Ninth Circuit, where the court may require customs FCA claims to be filed in the Court of International Trade, yet Tio still files Ninth Circuit cases [MTKHmo8s-kc @ 00:26:42]. Robert Salo of Akin Gump, a former DOJ Civil Fraud lawyer on the American Health Law Association panel, reports that the "zaurov" decision held the qui tam provisions violate the Appointments Clause, and that defendants now raise the argument routinely and move to quash CIDs [xaaGMrYhE60 @ 00:12:25]. Tax is the third split. Brown, LLC tells customs whistleblowers that tax liability is carved out of the FCA and routes to the IRS or anti-money-laundering programs instead [XXtgvEhkvo4 @ 00:00:22]. Husch Blackwell's Todd G. sees the state side moving the opposite way, citing a DC Bitcoin investor who allegedly dodged about $25 million in tax through false residency claims in a case that settled for $40 million, and arguing it "just makes too much sense" for governments to chase tax evasion through FCA mechanisms [_n3NJXxKz_8 @ 00:16:36]. Read them as filters: federal customs files run through qui tam, state tax files need a statute that reaches tax, and Appointments Clause motions add delay.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Procedure kills more relator files than weak facts. Tycko warns the seal runs 60 days by statute but averages about 18 months, and breaking it risks losing the reward or getting the case dismissed [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:20:33]. The public disclosure bar is the next tripwire, though Tycko notes raw, unanalyzed data is generally not treated as a public disclosure, which is exactly what keeps a data-mined import case alive when the relator supplies the analysis [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:17:20]. Jason T. Brown of Brown, LLC flags the race condition: first-to-file rules can leave later filers with nothing, and the resulting disputes are "ugly" and often settled quietly [5fDm1yVZ4cM @ 00:09:45]. Tycko & Zavareei's financial-fraud briefing adds the volume problem: few reports lead to awards, so the submission has to be compelling [azRmbEExKOw @ 00:01:57]. Put together, the operating rule is speed plus silence plus analysis. File before a competitor or a co-worker beats you to the courthouse, say nothing outside counsel while the seal holds, and hand the government a worked theory instead of a pile of shipping records. A relator who leaks, waits, or dumps unprocessed data gives defendants three separate exits before the merits are ever reached.

The employee-relator path carries personal risk that the lawyers describe from live cases. Teller recounts an extruded-aluminum AD/CVD matter where a bike-rack maker owed about $1 million but disclosed only about $400,000; the client objected, was let go, and the company ended up paying about $3.4 million [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:05:33]. Teller's fix is sequencing: research the issue, raise it with the boss if the goal is to fix it, and call an employment lawyer early rather than after the termination letter [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:14:41]. Evidence quality is the other trap. Matt Feinberg of PilieroMazza, arguing from the defense side, points to a case built on secondhand happy-hour gossip that was dismissed [eIBPl3LNUXQ @ 00:58:01]. Inside universities, Cheryl Burch of USC's Institute for Creative Technologies lays out the slower internal path for research administrators: get an email from the researcher justifying the charge, and if it still doesn't make sense, share it with the departmental or central campus representative [oh1aiSdEJHY @ 00:02:00]. The common thread is documentation before confrontation. A relator who escalates without paper, or who relies on what colleagues repeated at the bar, hands the defense an easy dismissal and exposes their own job with nothing to show for it.

Run the arithmetic yourself before pitching counsel or a client, because even the practitioners' own figures drift. Tycko prices a 15 percent share of an example $6.5 million liability at "$925,000" [A2nRu5da_9k @ 00:26:11]; 15 percent of $6.5 million is about $975,000. Teller describes a $40 million cable-company recovery paying 16 percent, stated as $6.5 million, and calls it "really life-changing money" [QNzd1e6e8UE @ 00:13:09]; 16 percent of $40 million is $6.4 million. Feinberg's defense math shows the downside a defendant prices in: penalties of $11,665 to $23,331 per claim on top of treble damages [eIBPl3LNUXQ @ 00:09:59]. Michael Sanchez of Compliance Mitigation anchors the realistic outcome with WorkRite's GSA MAS pricing case, which settled for $7.1 million and paid the relator $1.27 million [3kDd1dqYkWY @ 00:09:33]. That works out to roughly 18 percent, landing right on Tycko's historical customs average. Model payouts at 15 to 18 percent of the actual settlement, not of the theoretical treble number, and assume the check arrives years after filing, not months. Anything built on the 25 to 30 percent declined-case band or California's 50 percent ceiling is upside, not the base case.
