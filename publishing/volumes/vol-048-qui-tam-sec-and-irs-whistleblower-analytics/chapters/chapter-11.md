# Chapter 11: The IRS Whistleblower Program: Form 211 to Award Check

*Section 7623(b) thresholds and award percentages, Form 211 vs. Form 3949-A, a line-by-line Form 211 walkthrough, the start-to-finish process, offshore-account cases, IRS agent whistleblowers, and practitioner debate over whether the program works*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Former IRS agents, whistleblower counsel and tax controversy litigators agree on the core deal: Form 211 is the only route to an award, payouts run 15 to 30 percent of proceeds actually collected, and nothing pays until the target exhausts every appeal. The working floor is more than $2 million in tax in dispute plus $200,000 of gross income for individual targets, but Brown says the IRS rarely moves below a likely $10 million collection. Winning files are insider-sourced, documented, current and packaged as a narrative memo with enabling-party, witness and document appendices. Timelines run seven to ten years, longer if litigated. The split: Brown and Sullivan want big splashes, while Green and Skarlatos say their billion-dollar claims never paid and the sweet spot is $10 million to $50 million of simple tax evasion.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who walks the program lands on the same payout math. The IRS Whistleblower Office pays 15 to 30 percent of proceeds collected and attributable to the tip, and only after the target has exhausted every appeal right and can no longer file a refund claim [i4GnKgGXiMw @ 00:01:04]. Emily Stibil of Phillips and Cohen describes that band as mandatory for eligible claims, with the final award amount set by the Whistleblower Office itself [fQss6bE5tKc @ 00:00:34]. Former IRS agent Mike Sullivan states the collection rule bluntly: if the money is not collected, the tipster does not get a nickel, so chasing a broke target is wasted time [41ejxu80Qtg @ 00:02:09]. The second point of agreement is that Form 211, the Application for Award for Original Information, is the only door to a payout, and the filing has to be right the first time. Jason T. Brown warns that calling a tip hotline or the local office, or going to the FBI or Secret Service, can get a tipster treated as a confidential informant or cooperating witness with a little cash thrown their way, but leaves zero eligibility for the real award even if the government recovers hundreds of millions [RuNrDW46nKY @ 00:01:41].

The floor numbers repeat across firms. Stibil and Brown both put the line at more than $2 million in tax, penalties, interest and additions in dispute, plus gross income above $200,000 in at least one year at issue when the target is an individual [fQss6bE5tKc @ 00:01:27]. Brown stresses that the $2 million is tax liability, not unreported income: hide $2 million of income at a roughly 50 percent effective rate and the liability is only $1 million, short of the bar. Clearing the threshold does not make a case workable. Brown says that even at $2 million the IRS is unlikely to move unless the file is blatant, well documented and likely to collect $10 million or more, against deep-pocketed targets with collectible assets, willful conduct and a whistleblower who is an insider [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:01:52]. Dean Zerbe of the National Whistleblower Center ranks insiders first, naming the senior bank official, the tax department and the accountant, and adds that the IRS wants conduct three years old at most for an individual and five or six for a corporation, ideally still running [O45E7FrG_mM @ 00:02:01]. Sullivan's version is that the IRS wants a whale splash that makes headlines and pleases the commissioner, not a couple of bait fish [41ejxu80Qtg @ 00:03:10].

The third consensus is that the IRS will not do the tipster's homework or forgive a thin file. Law Minded guest Alina says the IRS receives countless tips every year, so a claim built on something overheard at a restaurant is speculative, and even well-substantiated claims often go uninvestigated, which is why the firm screens out ineligible or unsupported cases before filing [sNHauc7rutQ @ 00:07:51]. A CPA, lawyer and Northeastern academic interviewed by Boston 25 says the informant essentially has to spoon-feed the dirty details, giving the IRS an idea of what taxes are missing, how it came about and what the amounts may be, so the whistleblower does all the digging [mfEL3LienPQ @ 00:01:56]. The same report estimates that 90 percent of claims are found useless and puts the reported average payout at about $224,000, because the IRS is too busy to run down every lead [mfEL3LienPQ @ 00:02:27]. Nobody sells speed either. Eric Green and Bryan Skarlatos put the average paying case at eight to ten years, and cases the IRS drags into court at twelve to twenty years minimum; Skarlatos says that, given the timeline, any new case had better be simple and easy [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:07:15].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The form itself is short; Teach Me! Personal Finance walks it as a single page split into Section A on the target and Section B on the claimant [WA3dtUQT9f8 @ 00:07:29]. Line 1 flags a new or supplemental submission, with any prior claim number entered; line 2 takes the last four digits of the target's taxpayer ID; lines 3 through 5 take the name with aliases and related taxpayers, the address and a date of birth or approximate age, and the IRS will work with partial data. Line 6 names any IRS employee previously told, line 7 checks the violation type across income, employment, employee plans, government entities and exempt organizations, line 8 states the facts and why they break the law, and line 9 explains how the information was obtained. Lines 15 and 16 lay out the tax owed by year and dollar amount, with room for at least five tax years [WA3dtUQT9f8 @ 00:12:44]. The claimant must sign personally under penalty of perjury even with a Form 2848 power of attorney on file, and the package is mailed to the Whistleblower Office [WA3dtUQT9f8 @ 00:03:53]. Alina warns that the 211 looks strikingly like a report-only form with no award, a common but devastating mix-up [sNHauc7rutQ @ 00:06:25].

The 211 is a wrapper; the winning asset is the narrative behind it. Zerbe calls the online form a check-the-box document and says the narrative is what the IRS is really reading, starting with chapter-and-verse specificity on the taxpayer and a plain-language account of the scheme [O45E7FrG_mM @ 00:09:59]. Zerbe then bolts on three sheets: an appendix of every enabling party, meaning the accounting firm and law firm helping the taxpayer; a list of who else can verify the story, where they are located and what they will likely say, preferably people the IRS can reach inside the US; and a document sheet naming each key record, who controls it and where it sits [O45E7FrG_mM @ 00:11:19]. Brown typically files a comprehensive written memo alongside the 211 walking the IRS through facts, law and documents, because the IRS wants cases silver-plattered, not a quick value meal that gets discarded [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:06:49]. Sullivan's agent-side version is a really nice letter that someone else polishes, backed by dossiers sometimes inches thick so the reviewing agent does no work; three or four pages of he-said-she-said went straight into the garbage when Sullivan was an agent [41ejxu80Qtg @ 00:03:49].

The numbers the IRS needs are two predicate facts: what the target reported and what the true liability was. Brown's model case is a company telling the government it makes $2 million a year while the second set of books, the one hidden from the government, shows $5 million buried through sub-corporations and offshore accounts, a $3 million annual deficit stated as hard figures with the math shown, not guesswork [RuNrDW46nKY @ 00:08:47]. For a clean criminal case, Green walks the file to a local IRS Criminal Investigation special agent first, since the 211 asks whether anyone at the IRS has already seen it; one agent told Green they did not like the idea of people in Utah making the call, and CI has interviewed Green's clients [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:20:30]. Skarlatos agrees CI is more used to informants than the civil side, but stresses lodging the 211 before CI acts, or there is no award [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:22:37]. After filing, Brown reads the yearly update codes: no action means little, referred to audit is slightly promising, and referred to collections is the ding-ding-ding signal that liabilities were found [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:08:07].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the target on dollars and collectability
   - Compute tax liability in dispute, confirm individual gross income, and confirm collectible assets before building anything.
   - Clear more than $2 million in tax, penalties, interest and additions in dispute, plus more than $200,000 gross income in at least one year for individual targets [The Anti-Fraud Coalition — fQss6bE5tKc @ 00:01:27]; Brown adds the IRS rarely acts below a likely $10 million collection.
2. Gather evidence the lawful way
   - Assemble internal emails, spreadsheets, both sets of books, returns, bank statements, contracts, invoices and messages showing intent.
   - Brown lists these as the evidence the IRS acts on and warns that taking documents you are not authorized to have can backfire, get you fired or get you arrested [Brown, LLC - A Premier Whistleblower Law Firm — tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:04:57].
3. Build the 211 plus narrative package
   - Complete Form 211 Sections A and B, attach a plain-language narrative memo, and add enabling-party, witness and document sheets.
   - Zerbe appends a sheet of enablers, a list of who can verify the story and where they are, and a document sheet naming each record and who controls it [Kohn Kohn & Colapinto — O45E7FrG_mM @ 00:11:19]; sign under penalty of perjury and mail it to the Whistleblower Office.
4. Route, lodge and monitor
   - For a clean criminal case, present to IRS CI first, lodge the 211 before CI acts, then request yearly status and read the update codes.
   - Skarlatos says to go to IRS CI first in the right case but lodge the claim with the Whistleblower Office before CI takes action, or there is no award [Where IRS Problems Get Solved — ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:22:37].

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to file Form 211 solo or behind counsel. Emily Stibil of Phillips and Cohen says flatly that you don't need a lawyer to file a tip, positioning an attorney as a way to navigate the process and craft the tip so it heightens the likelihood of success [fQss6bE5tKc @ 00:00:34]. Jason T. Brown pushes much harder: you can fill out the form yourself, but Brown strongly discourages it, predicting a self-filed claim will probably hurt the case and go into the circular file, while conceding that certain people have recovered without an attorney [RuNrDW46nKY @ 00:12:28]. Alina of Oberhide PC sells counsel as a credibility signal, arguing the IRS has reason to believe law-firm submissions are genuine because the firm has already vetted eligibility and substantiation and walks away from cases that fail either test [sNHauc7rutQ @ 00:08:46]. Brown's intake desk shows what the solo route costs: many matters arrive after someone already submitted their own Form 211 and wants it salvaged, the firm reviews whether it can even clean up the mess people generally make on their own, and it declines on the spot when it can't [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:06:18]. The working read from the counsel camp is blunt: a botched first filing is treated as close to unrecoverable, because you only get one first bite at the apple.

Case-size doctrine splits into two camps. Former IRS agent Mike Sullivan preaches whales only: the IRS wants a big splash that can make the newspapers and please the commissioner, not a couple of bait fish on top of the water, and a small or undocumented claim is not worth anyone's time [41ejxu80Qtg @ 00:03:10]. Bryan Skarlatos answers from twenty years of whistleblower practice that bigger is not better, reporting many billion-dollar claims filed in a career and none of them paid, because a massive claim drags the IRS into a very complex process [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:12:43]. Skarlatos expects hundred-million-plus cases to hit headwinds from complexity and well funded taxpayer defenses, and puts the sweet spot at 10 to 50 million dollars, or a tighter 10 to 25 million, while warning that a five or six million claim may not justify a ten-year wait for a 20 to 30 percent award once professional fees are counted [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:16:01]. Eric Green backs the middle path with paid results: the cases that actually paid were small, simple income tax evasion matters the government could make and plead out, and Green notes the DOJ side has no patience for complicated RICO-type builds either [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:13:20]. Sullivan's whale doctrine and the Skarlatos sweet spot agree on one thing only: tiny cases die in the queue.

Routing is the third fault line. Sullivan says IRS Criminal Investigation chases conviction rates and doesn't care if it collects a nickel, wanting the target behind bars, which is exactly why small payroll or unreported-income gripes go nowhere with that unit [QxVllUQ7IRk @ 00:01:50]. Skarlatos explains that CI is far more used to working with witnesses and informants than the civil side, and agrees with Green's practice in the right case: bypass the Whistleblower Office, go directly to IRS CI, then lodge the claim with the office before CI acts, because skipping that filing forfeits the award entirely [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:22:37]. Brown hedges the same play, warning that a criminal referral might accelerate a case but can increase the risk your identity is revealed or create criminal exposure, a fate some other IRS complainants have already suffered, so it is a strategy reserved for a clean whistleblower working it through counsel [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:03:58]. Dean Zerbe sets a lower proof bar than the criminal-first crowd: you just need to show the target owes tax, and proving fraud is a nice bonus rather than a requirement [O45E7FrG_mM @ 00:17:40]. The criminal-first operators trade exposure risk for speed and traction, while the civil filers accept slower, quieter handling in exchange for keeping the whistleblower out of the blast radius.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is filing in the wrong lane. Alina warns that Form 211 looks strikingly like another form that just reports information with no award attached, calling the mix-up a common but devastating mistake that practitioners see constantly [sNHauc7rutQ @ 00:06:25]. Sullivan draws the same line from the agent's side: Form 3949-A is just a report for someone who screwed you or whose employer failed to take out withholding, while the whistleblower program is something a lot bigger [41ejxu80Qtg @ 00:00:35]. Brown adds that calling a tip hotline or the local office makes you ineligible for an award, and going to the FBI or Secret Service may get you treated as a confidential informant or cooperating witness who gets a little cash thrown your way but no big IRS award [RuNrDW46nKY @ 00:01:41]. Even inside the right form, mechanics kill claims: a wrong filing leaves you dead in the water or delayed a couple of years, and an attorney or accountant who holds the information through that professional role and fails to work it out ahead of time can sit in a taint process for three to five years, half a decade lost before anything happens [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:06:05]. Every one of these losses is locked in on filing day, long before any agent reads a word.

The second trap is the whistleblower becoming the target. Brown warns that taking documents you are not authorized to have, especially from an employer, can backfire, get you fired, or get you arrested, which is why counsel should rule on what is usable before any collection starts [tQIkb01vCTw @ 00:05:16]. Green reminds callers that being the whistleblower is no get-out-of-jail-free card, pointing to the UBS whistleblower who collected 104 million dollars only after serving 40 months in prison for participating in the scheme [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:30:36]. Brown recounts that in one of the biggest recoveries, in excess of a hundred million, the first firm's filing reportedly made the whistleblower the subject of the criminal investigation, and a second firm had to rehabilitate the claim [RuNrDW46nKY @ 00:13:05]. Green has watched the same dynamic from the defense table: in one audit the whistleblower was the former bookkeeper, the audit showed the client was arguably skimming money and so was the bookkeeper, and the criminal case went away once the facts turned muddy [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:30:01]. The pattern across all four accounts is identical: personal participation, an unauthorized document grab, or a sloppy first filing hands the target's lawyers a weapon and can flip the reporter into the defendant's chair.

The third trap is economic: a winning case can still pay little or nothing. Sullivan states that if the money is not collected you do not get a nickel, so going after a broke target with no collection potential is wasting your time, because the IRS has few agents and only wants the big fish [41ejxu80Qtg @ 00:02:09]. Green spells out the downside math: you only collect what the government collects, so if a billion-dollar target gambles everything away and settles through an offer in compromise for a hundred bucks, that is the base for your percentage, while clients doing fraud-penalty math in their heads need to come back to earth [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:14:54]. Skarlatos adds that the Whistleblower Office waits until every last cent is collected and then waits two more years for the refund-claim statute of limitations to expire before processing, so a case that collects 90 percent in year one can still sit for years over the final 10 percent [ruRW_hNSDYk @ 00:32:51]. Boston 25 News reports the base rate: an estimated 90 percent of claims are found useless, and the average payout is reportedly about 224,000 dollars [mfEL3LienPQ @ 00:02:27]. Price every claim against those numbers, and against a decade of carrying costs, before spending a single day building the file.
