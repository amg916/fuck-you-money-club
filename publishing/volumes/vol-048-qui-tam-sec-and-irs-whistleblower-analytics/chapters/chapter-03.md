## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the qui tam lifecycle is won or lost under seal. The 60-to-90-day statutory window routinely stretches past 120 days and sometimes to about five years. DOJ intervenes in only 21 to 24 percent of cases, and a declination reflects cost rather than merits. Street mechanics center on a privilege screen before any document reaches the government, targeted evidence instead of folder grabs, and 'boxed fruit' packages of evidence, draft subpoenas, and org charts. The numbers are concrete: $11,665 to $22,331 per claim on top of treble damages, 15 to 30 percent relator shares, and DOJ dismissals climbing from about 6 to about 25 a year. Mediators settle release scope before money and keep fees out of lump-sum offers. Relator counsel split roughly 50/50 on whether to negotiate fees alongside the merits.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner treats the seal as the opening battlefield of the qui tam lifecycle. Amanda Roach of the Colorado U.S. Attorney's Office describes a seal of at least 60 days that serves three purposes. Partial lifts are available, and under a Fourth Circuit rule a relator may discuss the allegations but not the action [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:40:25]. Rich Hayes, EDNY Civil Deputy Chief, says 60 days is 'not a lot of time to figure out whether there's been a multi-billion dollar fraud perpetrated against the government.' He also reports that courts are allowing less time to decide intervention [kcNFrNC2BIg @ 00:46:46]. Bill Nettles, U.S. Attorney for South Carolina, works the 90 days under seal hard. His office issues CIDs and takes depositions while it decides whether it wants to get in the case [yiUS5Zf9HRI @ 00:19:18]. On the relator side, Mary Ann Hamilton of Miller Law Group says the statutory seal reads 90 days but in practice is never under 120 days. She has watched seals run a year or two, and in one matter about five years [H0ay64A0XXA @ 00:09:43]. The operator consensus is blunt. Budget for a multi-year sealed phase, treat every extension as routine, and package the case so the government can act inside a compressed window that keeps getting tighter.

The second point of agreement is that the relator pool has widened far beyond the disgruntled insider. McGuireWoods' FCA Insider team lists insiders, competitors, and CMS data miners as live relator categories. The team also notes that courts threw out suits brought by a defendant's former counsel [J3hHvouRce8 @ 00:02:59]. Allison Hawthorne of Beasley Allen previews panels on corporations and professional data miners, including the split between 'true corporate relator versus corporate fiction.' Her panel also covers drug manufacturers suing competitors and the relator coalitions formed because these cases 'can be very expensive' [ujkR1nAOen8 @ 00:00:42]. The 2024 FBA panel featuring DOJ Civil Fraud assistant director Ed Crook and Constantine Cannon's Michael Roniker widens the list further. It adds customers, patients, clients, third-party vendors, consultants, and industry experts who mine publicly available data in ways DOJ 'may not have the capability or or means to do' [aZd6GqRekxQ @ 00:03:21]. Hamilton confirms that the insider requirement bends in customs cases brought by competitors [H0ay64A0XXA @ 00:17:52]. Every one of these channels still runs through the same qualifying hurdles: first-to-file, public disclosure, and anonymity, including Doe relators. For an analytics-driven operator, outsider data mining is a recognized relator lane, but it lives or dies on clearing those bars.

Third, practitioners agree that intervention is the lever that moves money, and that neither declination nor dismissal is a merits verdict. McGuireWoods puts intervention at 21 to 24 percent of cases. Over the same period, qui tam filings doubled from the 350-to-400 range in 2008 to 782 last year [J3hHvouRce8 @ 00:09:07]. Hayes insists declinations reflect cost and recoverable dollars rather than merits. He notes that some complaints are 'three pieces of paper' alleging billion-dollar fraud, and that relators have won large judgments after declination [kcNFrNC2BIg @ 00:42:21]. Husch Blackwell's Tanner Cook and Jonathan Porter stress that DOJ dismisses cases for abuse that undermines the purpose of the False Claims Act, not for bad facts or weak pleading. Weak cases are left for courts to resolve at the pleading stage or at summary judgment [XAVzMsuvKNE @ 00:02:08]. The Phillips & Cohen panel anchors the stakes: 85 percent of recoveries come from whistleblower cases and only 15 percent from government-initiated matters [5twGfbHBnJs @ 00:19:33]. The baseline prerequisite follows directly. The relator's package has to be strong enough to win intervention, or the relator and counsel must be resourced enough to survive a declination and litigate the case themselves through discovery.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The unit economics start with the penalty stack. McGuireWoods' FCA Insider team prices each false claim at $11,665 to $22,331 on top of trebled damages, which makes claim count the first input in any exposure model [J3hHvouRce8 @ 00:04:29]. The payout curve then depends on who carries the case. Husch Blackwell's Tanner Cook notes that relators can collect up to one-third of the recovery in non-intervened cases. That pull is strong enough that some interviewees in existing investigations file duplicate suits of their own, which is the conduct targeted by the Granston memo's 'parasitic' dismissal category [XAVzMsuvKNE @ 00:09:49]. In intervened cases, relator counsel Sarah Frazier explains that a share of 20 percent or more requires extraordinary measures, such as wearing a wire for the government [vHZhDg8Ml14 @ 00:12:18]. Blake Percival's USIS case shows the result. The background-investigation dumping fraud against OPM ended in a settlement of about $30 million, and Percival collected 20 percent, about $6 million, within a statutory range of 10 to 30 percent [FopC1sCa2y8 @ 00:56:47]. The working sequence is to count the claims, multiply by the per-claim penalty, treble the damages, and then discount the total for the years the case will spend under seal before any check clears.

Evidence intake is where the craft lives. Leslie Skillin of Getnick & Getnick runs a three-step screen. First, ask whether any document was sent to, from, or copied to an attorney, and whether an attorney was present on a recording. Second, segregate those materials and hire outside ethics counsel to review them. Third, leave crime-fraud determinations to the court on in-camera review [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:05:30]. Hamilton's rule is never to take documents a client would not normally access and to stay targeted. She also tells clients to avoid work email and work machines, and to fall back on detailed notes when the only access runs through company systems [H0ay64A0XXA @ 00:10:36]. The Phillips & Cohen panel says the winning delivery is 'boxed fruit': evidence, a draft subpoena, and an org chart handed to the government [5twGfbHBnJs @ 00:44:29]. Percival's proof was a single flash drive of spreadsheets. It showed that USIS reviewer headcount could not physically process the volume of investigations submitted, and that capacity-versus-throughput analysis exposed the dumping scheme [FopC1sCa2y8 @ 00:36:36]. Taken together, the mechanic is surgical extraction plus a government-ready roadmap, never a bulk folder grab. A clean, narrow package arrives faster and survives the privilege fights that follow.

Settlement is a sequencing game. Mediator Steve Altman, a veteran of more than 40 FCA mediations, settles the scope of the release before any money talk. He tells parties not to offer a lump sum that includes fees, exchanges mediation memos with the other side, and notes that DOJ may allow 60 days to resolve administrative remedies [vHZhDg8Ml14 @ 00:46:04]. King & Spalding's Mike Paulus shows why the fee rule matters. In a $10 million pot carrying $1.5 million in fees, the client might have settled at $8.5 million. He also reports that relator counsel split about 50/50 on negotiating fees alongside the merits [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:50:12]. Kristen Nedwick of the Texas Medicaid Fraud Control Unit warns that multistate deals can require up to 53 separate state agreements. States take up to 60 days to return signed agreements, and states with §1909-compliant statutes pocket an extra 10 percent of the Medicaid share [vHZhDg8Ml14 @ 00:34:31]. DOJ's Sarah McLean adds that cooperation credit is likely to mean about a 1.5x multiplier [vHZhDg8Ml14 @ 00:56:59]. The operator order of operations is release scope, then damages, then fees, then the state paperwork. Mixing those steps is how two-day mediations collapse over whether a number included fees.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The seal clock is the first fault line, and practitioners read it differently depending on which side of the docket they sit. Bill Nettles, U.S. Attorney for South Carolina, treats the seal as a working window: "we have 90 days under seal to decide whether we want to get in the case or not," and inside that window the office issues CIDs and takes depositions [yiUS5Zf9HRI @ 00:19:18]. Mary Ann Hamilton of Miller Law Group calls the statutory number a fiction: the seal is never under 120 days, has run a year or two, and in one matter ran about five years [H0ay64A0XXA @ 00:09:43]. Rich Hayes, EDNY Civil Deputy Chief, attacks the clock from inside government, calling 60 days "not a lot of time" to figure out whether a multi-billion dollar fraud occurred [kcNFrNC2BIg @ 00:46:46]. Hayes adds that courts are allowing less time to decide intervention, which squeezes the government further. Amanda Roach of USAO-Colorado frames the seal as at least 60 days serving three purposes, with partial lifts available and a Fourth Circuit rule that a relator may discuss the allegations but not the action [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:40:25]. Budget for Hamilton's reality, not the statute's headline number.

The second split is over what DOJ dismissals and declinations actually signal. Jonathan Porter and Tanner Cook of Husch Blackwell report DOJ dismissals jumping from around six a year to around 25, yet insist "DOJ isn't looking to dismiss weak cases"; dismissal targets abuse that undermines the statute, and weak cases are left for pleading or summary judgment [XAVzMsuvKNE @ 00:22:44]. The McGuireWoods team hears a different story on the ground: DOJ attorneys say the Granston memo "didn't change anything," but in practice DOJ has told relators' counsel to dismiss [J3hHvouRce8 @ 00:42:18]. Rich Hayes rejects reading declination as a merits verdict at all: declinations reflect cost and recoverable dollars, some complaints are "three pieces of paper" alleging billion-dollar fraud, and relators have won large judgments after the government walked away [kcNFrNC2BIg @ 00:42:21]. The Phillips & Cohen panel describes a far harsher funnel, about 700 cases filed a year with the government intervening in about 15, well below the 21 to 24 percent intervention rate McGuireWoods reports [5twGfbHBnJs @ 00:44:44]. Porter and Cook add a tactical warning for defendants who want DOJ to kill a case: arguing "we're right" fails, and the request has to fit one of the seven Granston categories.

Fee handling inside global settlements splits the bar almost down the middle. Mike Paulus of King & Spalding reports relator counsel split about 50/50 on negotiating fees alongside the merits, and runs the math: a $10M pot with $1.5M in fees means the client might have settled at $8.5M [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:50:12]. Leslie Skillin of Getnick & Getnick, on the relator side, pushes back that this is not an issue in intervened cases [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:54:51]. Mediator Steve Altman, with more than 40 FCA mediations on the books, takes a hard line the other way: do not offer a lump sum that includes fees, and settle the scope of the release before agreeing on money [vHZhDg8Ml14 @ 00:47:11]. Paulus floats the opposite fix, saying that putting fees into the shared pot "might" resolve the conflict, while DOJ's David Wiseman recalls a two-day mediation that ended in a dispute over whether the number included fees [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:59:21]. Paulus ties the conflict to Rules 1.2, 1.4, 1.5, 1.7 informed written consent, and 1.8, plus written allocation among multiple relators, with the panel stressing that any offer from opposing counsel must be promptly relayed to the client. Pick a lane before mediation, not during it.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive self-inflicted wound is the evidence grab. In Cafasso, the relator took 11 GB, the NDA counterclaim was upheld, and $300,000 in fees was awarded; the account of how it happened is damning on its own: "i just grabbed the whole folder um she didn't read the files she just read the file names" [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:11:12]. Mike Paulus confirms defense clients pursue counterclaims for improper access, and the panel flags that "rummaging" is a factor and that the CFAA criminalizes unauthorized access, so the better play is telling the government where documents sit so it can subpoena them [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:09:12]. Privilege is the second trap: David Wiseman warns that privileged productions delay DOJ, force taint review, and can disqualify the attorneys assigned [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:07:36]. Mary Ann Hamilton draws the same line from the relator side: never take documents you would not normally access, be targeted, avoid work email and work machines, and never feed case data to AI because it is not privileged, can be subpoenaed, and gives wrong legal answers [H0ay64A0XXA @ 00:10:52]. Hamilton's verdict on overreach: it "can very quickly undermine the legitimacy of a case and it can get you in trouble all on its own."

Seal breaches and settlement paperwork are where money leaks after the case is already won. The ABA panel stresses that "it's not an automatic dismissal if the relator breaches the seal," but the bill still comes due: in Bibi v. Wells Fargo, DOJ recommended $2.7M, the share above the 25 percent minimum, and the court ordered $1.6M [JN4iipJiHZM @ 00:48:18]. Defendants hit the seal problem too; Mike Paulus says insurers create a catch-22, so Paulus seeks an extra partial lift. Sarah Frazier warns that a common reason for a government veto is too much of the settlement allocated to the retaliation claim, and the panel says to tell DOJ about any retaliation claim in advance [vHZhDg8Ml14 @ 00:06:14]. Exit paperwork is the employee-side trap: the McGuireWoods team notes FCA claims cannot be released, but the recovery can, meaning a departing employee who signs can lose the right to share in any recovery [J3hHvouRce8 @ 00:55:28]. Blake Percival lived that trap: severance was $42,000 with a fine-print waiver of whistleblower rights, and their lawyer's advice was "don't sign anything" [FopC1sCa2y8 @ 00:31:22]. Refusing that $42,000 kept the path open to the roughly $6M, 20 percent share Percival later received.

The economics check is time, and every practitioner who has lived a case reports it running longer than planned. Blake Percival filed a sealed qui tam on July 1, 2011 through Beasley Allen after being fired in May 2011, then sat through 60-to-90-day continuances for years; a June 2013 hardship settlement attempt was derailed by the Snowden hearings, and DOJ intervened only on October 30, 2013 [FopC1sCa2y8 @ 00:38:39]. The firing alone cost six figures in pay, a $20K bonus, an SUV and an Amex. The settlement came to about $30M, with Percival taking 20 percent, about $6M, against fraud that ran across 665,000 investigations and a Wall Street Journal estimate of seven point six billion dollars if fully prosecuted, and Percival reports being blackballed in the industry afterward [FopC1sCa2y8 @ 00:56:47]. On the litigation side, a DC Circuit case ran 12 years, with three years of discovery and a million documents, before dismissal at summary judgment [kcNFrNC2BIg @ 00:06:14]. The McGuireWoods team tracks Ruckh, where a $340M verdict was vacated before $85M was reinstated in June 2020 [J3hHvouRce8 @ 00:24:35]. The defendants' lever there was materiality: they "moved for judgment as a matter of law arguing that none of their uh purported misstatements were material."
