# Chapter 05: Medicare and Medicaid Billing Fraud: Schemes, Red Flags, and Reporting Paths

*Improper billing vs. FCA liability, upcoding and illegal coding, DME fraud, impossible-hours telehealth billing, sham storefronts, patient-initiated whistleblowers, long-term-care exposure, physician risk, Senior Medicare Patrol prevention, and what happens after you report*

## Executive Summary

Whistleblower counsel, defense lawyers, investigators, and fraud educators converge on one playbook: systemic billing fraud by providers, not a neighbor misusing benefits, is what pays under the False Claims Act, and the deciding element is knowing conduct, including reckless disregard and deliberate ignorance. Insiders win cases because the government already holds the claims data but lacks state of mind, and emails are the top proof. Street numbers: relators take 15 to 30 percent; intervened cases average about $13 million versus $3 million declined; damages run up to treble plus per-claim penalties. Schemes run as assembly lines: call centers selling signed prescriptions, signers paid $12 to $15 per file, labs billing $463 million. Practitioners split on whether to report internally, on per-claim penalty ranges, and on how much the government finds fraud through data analytics without any relator.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The whistleblower bar draws a hard line on who can actually cash a case. Jason T. Brown, a former FBI special agent who now runs Brown, LLC, defines actionable Medicare fraud as an entity knowingly submitting false claims, keeping overpayments it knows it is not owed, or paying kickbacks, while a beneficiary misusing benefits gets no False Claims Act avenue and can only be reported to the local U.S. Attorney's office or FBI [faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:01:22]. He repeats the point in a shorter Q&A: callers who want to report a neighbor collecting benefits will not trigger government action or a percentage, whereas an insider who knows of systemic upcoding, kickbacks, or billing for services not rendered may be entitled to a share [nDO81fwnTqg @ 00:01:24]. Dr. Bill Cushing lists the same core menu from the coding side: billing for services never provided, upcoding to a more expensive procedure, worthless services, false implied certifications, and failing to return an overpayment within 60 days [FCOkO5_92ZE @ 00:01:03]. The nurselyf compliance breakdown ties kickback and Stark violations into the same bucket, noting the Stark law applies even without intent to defraud while the Anti-Kickback Statute carries fines, jail time, and exclusion [T-h0f2YLPec @ 00:01:37]. The operator takeaway: screen for systemic, provider-side billing before spending a dollar on a case.

Every lawyer in the lineup agrees that good-faith error is not the trap; mindset is. Stephen Teller, with more than 25 years in whistleblower and retaliation work, says the shortest answer to where a mistake becomes fraud is the state of mind of the people making it, and that recklessly allowing mistakes is not a mistake either [0w534uBbqVQ @ 00:01:32]. Tom Barnard of Baker Donelson, presenting to long-term care operators, breaks knowing into three tiers: actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance where a provider tells its billing company to just submit without questions, and reckless disregard, which he calls the most common, such as an untrained coder picking codes at random or a system that bills every encounter the same way by default [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:10:16]. He adds that specific intent to defraud is not required. Surgeon and health executive Scott Ellner lands in the same place, warning physicians that intent is not an excuse and that defensively ordering MRIs can fall under reckless disregard [eIMINUxFlVI @ 00:08:17]. Barnard also flags the reverse false claim: discovering a coding error that inflated revenue, then sitting on the money instead of repaying it, converts an innocent overpayment into exposure [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:08:22]. For a relator, that means proof of who knew, and when, matters more than proof of a wrong code.

The consensus on why insiders get paid is blunt. Brown says that even though people assume big brother is everywhere, the government needs insiders, and he pegs last year's False Claims Act settlements and judgments above $2.9 billion, with roughly $580 million flowing to whistleblowers in aggregate [faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:02:45]. Barnard explains the gap those insiders fill: the government has access to all the claims data but cannot see state of mind, so a relator who can say they sat in the meetings and read the emails where everyone knew the rule and broke it anyway gets prosecutors over the final hurdle [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:13:04]. Asked what the number one way to prove knowing is, he answers emails and poor business hygiene in emails, ahead of witness testimony [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:41:20]. Teller describes his clients as the billing staff and coders on the front line who push back on an instruction they think is wrong; when an owner cannot or will not explain the instruction and instead shuts the employee down, he treats that as a clue that something is wrong [0w534uBbqVQ @ 00:09:55]. Barnard warns operators of the flip side: relators are often recently fired or ignored employees, and the first question he asks a client hit with a case is who left and whether they complained.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The coding-level schemes are small per line and huge at volume. Teller says upcoding can be as minor as billing a slightly longer visit every time, $20 here and $50 there, which adds up across a year at a large organization; unbundling splits a hospital night that pays $1,000 as a bundle into nurse visits, injections, and pills billed at $1,500 [0w534uBbqVQ @ 00:02:26]. His incident-to pattern is a clinic billing mid-level providers at the doctor's rate while the doctor goes on vacation, drops to half time, and finally sells the practice, which he calls fraud once the doctor is absent [0w534uBbqVQ @ 00:04:06]. He also flags fudged qualification scores, where a provider coaches a patient to answer a depression questionnaire as if more depressed so Medicare or Medicaid pays for treatment. Ellner points the spotlight at Medicare Advantage risk coding, where patients are upcoded with comorbidities that cannot be substantiated in order to pull larger capitated payments, and cites a relator who received $8 million in one such case [eIMINUxFlVI @ 00:06:52]. He says the Justice Department will use AI, deep learning, and neural networks to find upcoding patterns in Medicare Advantage [eIMINUxFlVI @ 00:16:50], and that level five 99215 visits get scrutinized. Those are the codes an insider should map first.

The telemarketing-to-lab pipeline is the most fully documented street mechanic. Per the appeals court record walked through by Money vs. The System, call centers cold-called beneficiaries offering free braces, filled out prescription forms themselves, had a licensed provider sign, then sold the signed prescriptions to suppliers for hundreds of dollars per beneficiary [QrUTZJlJrbQ @ 00:01:42]. Nurse practitioner Elizabeth Hernandez signed thousands a month, often within seconds, then pivoted to genetic tests and pandemic telemedicine, billing more than 24 hours of video visits in single days and about $111 million total. Physician assistant Colby Joyner was paid $12 to $15 per file, signed orders for some 607 beneficiaries while speaking to about 20, and those signatures drove more than 14,600 tests and over $10 million in claims [QrUTZJlJrbQ @ 00:11:03]. At the top, LabSolutions submitted more than $463 million in claims, Medicare paid over $187 million, and owner Minal Patel personally took over $21 million, hiding kickbacks in contracts labeled advertising [QrUTZJlJrbQ @ 00:13:42]. In South Florida DME, OIG agent Omar Perez Aybar says fraudsters buy lists of patients and doctors, bill from open Wi-Fi, and that about 90 percent of local DME companies are fraudulent; Jesus Garces used one storefront to bilk $48 million in a year and buried $2.5 million in 12 PVC pipes [OqK_6Nor98U @ 00:02:09].

Detection mechanics come from both ends of the claim. Ray Walker of the Oklahoma Medicare Assistance Program, which runs the Senior Medicare Patrol grant, tells beneficiaries the Medicare summary notice arrives every 3 months, while a MyMedicare.gov account shows charges in near real time so suspect lines can be reported faster [BLKbLs-2Y2I @ 00:31:22]. He explains why urinary catheters became a favored phantom item: low cost, single use, needed several times daily, so fraudsters bet CMS would ignore them in favor of high-ticket claims [BLKbLs-2Y2I @ 00:25:47]. The James Hoyer case shows a patient can be the relator: a slip-and-fall patient received two unrequested shipments of pain meds and scar cream, called to stop a third, got pushback, and the government says Age Vital's owners collected more than $37,000 in Medicare payments for unwanted meds, ending in a settlement of more than three-quarters of a million dollars [whq5-7zwAGE @ 00:00:33]. On the government side, Barnard describes analytics that flag a pharmacy sitting at the 100th percentile for one drug versus same-size peers, all scripts signed by one doctor, which triggers kickback questions, and he says more cases now start this way without any relator [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:45:21]. The race is real: data can surface the same pattern a relator is still sitting on.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Triage the Tip
   - Confirm the fraud is systemic and provider-side, not a beneficiary misusing benefits.
   - Brown says reports about a neighbor wrongly receiving benefits are not something the government acts on under the False Claims Act and pay no percentage, while insider knowledge of upcoding, kickbacks, or billing for services not rendered can [Brown, LLC - A Premier Whistleblower Law Firm — nDO81fwnTqg @ 00:01:24].
2. Lock Counsel, Then Lawful Evidence
   - Retain a whistleblower firm offering a free confidential consultation, then preserve lawfully accessible records.
   - A qui tam cannot be filed pro se because the relator sues on behalf of the government [Brown, LLC - A Premier Whistleblower Law Firm — 7two8gR6AAY @ 00:01:46]; prioritize emails, which Barnard calls the number one way knowing gets proven [Baker Donelson — -Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:41:20].
3. File Under Seal With a Disclosure Statement
   - File the complaint in federal court and serve the relator disclosure statement on DOJ and the U.S. Attorney.
   - Barnard says the statute forces the government to investigate enough to decide; the initial 60-day seal is usually extended in six-month increments, investigations often run two to three years, and six years of conduct is the typical lookback window [Baker Donelson — -Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:14:19].
4. Navigate Intervention and the Share
   - Plan for both outcomes: government takeover or a declined case the relator litigates alone.
   - If declined, the relator can keep suing and a judgment pays 25 to 30 percent, while intervention pays up to 25 percent based on relator contribution [Baker Donelson — -Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:36:46]; Brown notes intervened cases average about $13 million versus $3 million declined [Brown, LLC - A Premier Whistleblower Law Firm — faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:07:11].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Brown, LLC's Jason T. Brown draws the hardest line on routing. Walk a tip straight into the local U.S. Attorney's office or FBI and you need no lawyer, but you collect no whistleblower award; run it as a qui tam and counsel is mandatory because pro se relators are barred, with the payoff climbing to as much as 30 percent of what the government recovers [faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:04:47]. Brown also narrows the target list. A neighbor collecting benefits they should not receive is illegal, but the government is not really going to act on it under the False Claims Act and the reporter gets no share, while an insider sitting on systemic upcoding, kickbacks, or billing for services not rendered may be entitled to a percentage [nDO81fwnTqg @ 00:01:24]. The Senior Medicare Patrol camp runs a completely different playbook built for beneficiaries, not bounty hunters. Ray Walker of the Oklahoma Insurance Department tells anyone who spots a strange charge from a provider they actually saw to call that provider first, since honest mistakes get fixed, and only then bring it to the Medicare Assistance Program, which helps report it if it turns out to be fraudulent [BLKbLs-2Y2I @ 00:32:24]. The SMP DME crew routes every billing concern straight to the local SMP office, with no relator share anywhere in the picture [ZkOLz_FNG3c @ 00:02:39].

The money math on intervention is where the lawyers diverge. Brown argues relators should want the government in: the share drops to as much as 25 percent when DOJ intervenes versus 30 percent when it declines, but he puts intervened cases at roughly 13 million dollars in average settlements against about 3 million for non-intervened cases, so a smaller slice of a bigger pie wins [faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:07:11]. Tom Barnard of Baker Donelson, speaking from the defense chair, paints a colder picture of what declination usually means: the vast majority of cases end when the government finishes investigating, finds no merit or no interest, and the relator voluntarily dismisses rather than litigating alone [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:23:31]. Barnard also flags the government's own incentive to cut relators out entirely. He calls them homegrown cases built on data analytics, where the 20 to 25 percent that would go to a whistleblower stays with the government and no sealed-complaint deadline is running [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:44:03]. Even the quoted percentages wobble from speaker to speaker. Bill Cushing pegs the intervened-case range at 15 to 25 percent [FCOkO5_92ZE @ 00:03:12], Barnard puts non-intervened judgments at 25 to 30 percent, and Brown quotes 15 to 30 percent as the overall band relators should expect.

The knowledge standard splits practitioners on how hard a case really is. Stephen Teller of Teller Law says the shortest answer to mistake-versus-fraud is the state of mind of the people billing, and he warns that pure medical-necessity cases are a tough sell: a nurse who disagrees with a doctor about one procedure lands in a battle of experts over a one-off [0w534uBbqVQ @ 00:04:39]. Surgeon-executive Scott Ellner, on Kevin Pho's show, takes the opposite emphasis for physicians: you do not have to demonstrate intent, because reckless disregard and deliberate ignorance both count, so defensive MRIs ordered to dodge malpractice claims can land in the crosshairs [eIMINUxFlVI @ 00:07:32]. The nurselyf breakdown pushes further on self-referral, saying the Stark law applies even when there is no intent to defraud at all [T-h0f2YLPec @ 00:01:37]. On private payers the split sharpens again. Teller says the False Claims Act reaches only the government portion of a mixed bill, with California and, he believes, Illinois running private-insurance whistleblower programs, and he admits he has never found a way to feed his Medicare cases to Kaiser or Blue Cross [0w534uBbqVQ @ 00:11:54]. Ellner frames commercial insurers as running their own fraud units, with private parties able to bring cases against physicians too.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Brown's sharpest warning is that the evidence hunt itself can blow up the whistleblower. Collecting billing statements, emails, or records you are not lawfully entitled to can bring about your own termination or put you in criminal trouble, which is why he insists on calling whistleblower counsel before making any report [faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:05:35]. He adds that running concerns up the internal compliance chain a lot of times results in termination, and that sitting on knowledge is just as dangerous: only the first to file has rights, and if the feds figure it out themselves, they may treat you as part of the problem instead of part of the solution [faQOCnnY-QI @ 00:09:35]. Self-reporting without counsel carries its own exposure, because the government may view the reporter as implicated, especially anyone who profited from the scheme [7two8gR6AAY @ 00:00:35]. Barnard confirms the procedural trapdoors from the defense side. A relator convicted for the very bribes they paid cannot recover, a second filer alleging the same conduct is knocked out by the first-to-file bar, and anyone lifting allegations from newspapers or public filings runs into the public disclosure bar unless they are the original source [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:39:41]. Patience is the final cost, with Brown calling a one-year rocket docket the rare exception to multi-year sealed investigations.

The criminal side shows how lopsided the economics run for the people holding the pen. Money vs. The System walks through physician assistant Colby Joyner, paid 12 to 15 dollars per file to sign genetic-test orders for 607 beneficiaries he had mostly never spoken with, generating over 10 million dollars in lab claims while he personally earned about 17,600 dollars and drew 72 months in prison [QrUTZJlJrbQ @ 00:12:26]. Nurse practitioner Elizabeth Hernandez pulled over 1.66 million dollars in kickbacks and direct Medicare payments, then received 20 years, with the Eleventh Circuit affirming even after finding the judge wrongly gave prosecutors 10 extra minutes of closing argument [QrUTZJlJrbQ @ 00:16:30]. Her tells were textbook: orders dropped right after Operation Brace Yourself and again after Operation Double Helix, she reported her own provider number stolen to cover her tracks, and she began deleting texts once Medicare racked up hundreds of complaints and unfulfilled record requests [QrUTZJlJrbQ @ 00:08:41]. CNBC's ride-along shows the same brazenness at street level. DME operator Jesus Garces billed 48 million dollars in a single year from a nondescript storefront, wrapped roughly 2.5 million in cash inside PVC pipes buried under his home, and took 12.5 years [OqK_6Nor98U @ 00:03:46].

Even when the system catches something, the bottlenecks are real. Florida special agent in charge Omar Perez Aybar told CNBC that agents simply cannot keep up with the volume and need far more investigators, against a backdrop where HHS spent only 2 cents of every 100 dollars on oversight while returning 12 dollars per enforcement dollar [OqK_6Nor98U @ 00:08:25]. Walker explains why cheap disposables slip through: fraudsters picked urinary catheters precisely because low-cost, single-use items stayed under the radar until beneficiaries reading their statements flagged them to counselors [BLKbLs-2Y2I @ 00:25:47]. On the provider side, Nellie Miranda traces how a payment suspension alone can gut an operation. Tapestry Health, serving roughly 80,000 Medicare beneficiaries, filed Chapter 11 after CMS froze certain payments, while the government alleged it stopped cooperating with document requests and the company insisted it cooperated fully [b8VkfUJZguw @ 00:05:37]. Barnard adds the settlement trap that catches defendants who think a refund ends the matter: you cannot get a release without paying a multiplier, cooperative settlements land closer to two times singles, and self-disclosure only earns credit if it is made before you learn of any investigation [-Vss5I_7dHM @ 00:32:59].
