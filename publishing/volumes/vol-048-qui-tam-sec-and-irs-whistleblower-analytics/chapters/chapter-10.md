# Chapter 10: Dodd-Frank Protections and the Expanding Financial Bounty Programs

*SOX and Dodd-Frank anti-retaliation, the SEC's first retaliation action, CFTC derivatives-fraud rewards, AML/sanctions whistleblower awards and FinCEN's proposed rules, DOJ's corporate whistleblower incentive pilot, and Treasury's $30,000 tip rewards*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Bounty lawyers, former agency insiders and defense counsel converge on one formula. Voluntary, original information that leads to more than $1 million in sanctions pays 10 to 30 percent across the SEC, CFTC and AML programs, and related-action awards stack on top. CFTC whistleblower office director Raagnee Beri reports that roughly 42 percent of FY2024 CFTC enforcement matters involved whistleblowers. The street mechanics turn on clocks and channels: a 120-day window for compliance staff, Sarbanes-Oxley's 180-day retaliation deadline versus Dodd-Frank's six years, anonymity only through counsel, and a FinCEN maximum-award presumption proposed at $15 million. Newer lanes include DOJ's tiered pilot and Treasury's flat $30,000 fraud portal. Practitioners split on reporting internally first, on company counter-incentives, on Treasury's discretionary payouts, and on FinCEN's confidentiality and bankruptcy loopholes.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The payout math is the one thing every bounty lawyer on the circuit repeats without variation. Whistleblowers International pitches the CFTC program as paying anywhere between 10 and 30 percent of whatever the government collects off a tipster's information. The collections must exceed $1 million [nGZvKSKSh-Y @ 00:01:48]. Chris Erman of Phillips & Cohen is the former director of the CFTC whistleblower office and a 14-year veteran of SEC enforcement. Erman adds the related-action kicker: if a second agency brings a successful case on the same information, the tipster is eligible for another 10 to 30 percent of that agency's sanctions [dyn9ni2HPDU @ 00:00:23]. Foley & Lardner's Ken Weiner traces the SEC side to Section 21F of the Exchange Act. Dodd-Frank added that section in July 2010, and it forces the SEC to pay awards on voluntary, original information that leads to collections of at least $1 million [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:14:27]. Robert Eckard maps the same 10-to-30-percent band onto the money-laundering program. There, Treasury pays on collected sanctions from Bank Secrecy Act actions exceeding $1 million [tLBsvqESK6o @ 00:01:51]. Four separate shops give one formula: original tip, million-dollar floor, ten-to-thirty cut.

The second point of agreement is that the money dies on paperwork. Whistleblowers International tells tipsters to file a Form TCR (tip, complaint or referral) and to strictly follow every CFTC filing procedure. Mistakes lead to a denial of the reward [nGZvKSKSh-Y @ 00:02:08]. Brian Mahany of Mahany Law is blunter. His firm constantly hears from people who wrote a letter to an agency or called a hotline and never got paid. If you fail to follow the procedural rules, there are no rewards and no exceptions [BueLAsaxhHM @ 00:03:40]. The asset being sold is original information. Weiner breaks the SEC definition into three tests: the information must come from independent knowledge or independent analysis, must not already be known to the SEC, and must not come exclusively from public sources [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:18:34]. Tony Munter frames what the tipster actually gets. In a False Claims Act qui tam, the relator stands in the government's shoes and files in court. The SEC program gives no independent right of action: you hand the agency a tip, the Commission decides whether to pursue it, and the harm runs to defrauded investors rather than the government [tW8oVBiMkLg @ 00:00:45]. Packaging the tip is the whole game.

Anonymity runs through counsel, and the bar agrees on that structure. Whistleblowers International says the CFTC grants anonymity to whistleblowers represented by a lawyer [nGZvKSKSh-Y @ 00:02:56]. Eckard confirms that a represented AML tipster may submit anonymously to FinCEN. Treasury and DOJ staff are barred from disclosing information that could reasonably reveal the tipster's identity, except to a defendant in a public proceeding [tLBsvqESK6o @ 00:04:13]. Poppy Alexander of Whistleblower Partners explains why counsel is required. Lawyers carry ethical obligations and certify to the agency that the filer is who they say they are. That certification is a backstop against AI-generated spam, stolen identities, and filers who think they know more than they do [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:33:47]. On retaliation, the Garrison, Levin-Epstein team draws the baseline every employee needs. Sarbanes-Oxley protects internal reports to a supervisor or compliance department under a contributing-factor standard. Dodd-Frank protection attaches only when the concern goes directly to the SEC, and reporting internally to a manager or compliance is not enough [ft81gWsFd2M @ 00:02:02]. The consensus prerequisite is sequencing: get counsel, pick the reporting channel deliberately, and only then move. The Garrison team warns that early choices about who to report to and when decide which protections attach.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Timing is where the money is won or lost, and the clocks are specific. Weiner walks through the SEC carve-out for compliance officers. People whose principal duties are compliance or internal audit are normally excluded from awards. Once 120 days have passed since a supervisor, the chief compliance officer, the chief legal officer or the audit committee received the information, they are free to go to the SEC and stay bounty-eligible [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:23:52]. A second clock protects internal reporters. If an employee reports through company channels and contacts the SEC within 120 days, the SEC credits the employee when the company later hands over the same information or its investigation results [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:25:13]. Weiner also reports what the plaintiff side actually tells clients. On a substantial issue, most whistleblower attorneys would not advise reporting to the company first, though some advise reporting internally and to the SEC at the same time [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:27:50]. The retaliation clocks run differently. Sarbanes-Oxley gives only 180 days from the retaliation to file. Dodd-Frank allows up to six years and potentially double damages, but only if the report went directly to the SEC [ft81gWsFd2M @ 00:01:32].

The money-laundering lane has been rebuilt from scratch. Mahany notes that the old Bank Secrecy Act rewards were capped at $150,000 and were completely discretionary, which is why very few AML rewards were ever paid [BueLAsaxhHM @ 00:00:40]. Eckard lays out the fix. The Anti-Money Laundering Whistleblower Improvement Act, signed December 23, 2022, sets a 10 percent minimum award. It extends coverage to sanctions violations under IEEPA, the Trading with the Enemy Act and the Kingpin Act. It also expressly lets compliance and audit staff and non-US citizens collect [tLBsvqESK6o @ 00:01:15]. Poppy Alexander describes how FinCEN routes tips. They arrive by email or online form and get farmed out to OFAC for sanctions, to the OCC and Federal Reserve for bank issues, and to DOJ. FinCEN's whistleblower office still controls the award, so the tipster must keep FinCEN informed of every conversation with OFAC or anyone else [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:21:08]. Andrew Feller adds the speed lever. FinCEN proposed a presumption of the maximum 30 percent award with a $15 million threshold, against $5 million at the SEC. The CFTC estimated that roughly 80 percent of its historic awards would have qualified under its own similar proposal [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:12:18].

The newest bounty lanes stack on top. Matt Kelly explains that the DOJ Criminal Division pilot pays up to 30 percent of net proceeds after asset forfeiture. In his example, a $300 million settlement with $100 million going to victim restitution leaves the tipster's 30 percent calculated on the remaining $200 million [bFRmUwE-_F4 @ 00:01:27]. Tom Fox flags the tiers: 30 percent of the first $100 million, 5 percent of anything between $100 million and $500 million, and nothing clear above that. The pilot is scheduled to run three years [bFRmUwE-_F4 @ 00:18:25]. Kelly also names the targets: FEPA bribery of foreign officials, FCPA cases at non-public companies outside SEC reach, and health care fraud. These are the gaps where no awards program existed before [bFRmUwE-_F4 @ 00:02:07]. At the retail end, Treasury Secretary Bessent announced a $25 million fraud whistleblower incentive fund. It offers a $30,000 reward for information leading to a conviction or enforcement action, submitted through a single centralized portal at america.gov/fraud that replaces scattered agency tip lines [FnUiQJNaMn8 @ 00:04:10]. The spread is enormous: one lane pays a flat $30,000, while the others pay percentages of nine-figure settlements.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the record and retain counsel
   - Write down what was seen and when, then retain whistleblower or employment counsel before any report goes out.
   - The Garrison team tells employees to document what they saw and when and to talk to an employment lawyer before the complaint [Garrison, Levin-Epstein, Fitzgerald & Pirrotti — ft81gWsFd2M @ 00:02:17]. Counsel also unlocks anonymous filing at the CFTC [Whistleblowers International — nGZvKSKSh-Y @ 00:02:56].
2. Pick the channel and start the clocks
   - Decide between internal, direct-to-agency or simultaneous reporting, and calendar the 120-day and retaliation deadlines.
   - Compliance staff become eligible 120 days after leadership receives the information, and internal reporters keep credit if they reach the SEC within 120 days. Most whistleblower attorneys skip reporting internally first on substantial issues [Foley & Lardner LLP — 7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:27:50].
3. File the formal tip with a curated package
   - Submit through the formal channel (Form TCR, or FinCEN's email address or online form) with targeted evidence rather than a data dump.
   - Whistleblowers need to file a Form TCR and strictly follow the CFTC's filing procedures, because mistakes lead to denial of the reward [Whistleblowers International — nGZvKSKSh-Y @ 00:02:08]. Privileged material is held back until a taint process exists [Fraud in America — R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:31:26].
4. Cooperate, keep the award office informed, then claim
   - Assist every agency that picks up the tip, keep the award office informed of each contact, and file the award submission after the enforcement action.
   - After an enforcement action, the whistleblower submits an award claim to FinCEN, which manages the award even when OFAC or other agencies ran the case, so the tipster keeps FinCEN informed of every contact [Fraud in America — R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:22:24].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — How the bounty bar actually packages a winning tip**
> 
> Former agency insiders and whistleblower counsel converge on curation, proof of what leadership knew, contemporaneous notes, and sustained cooperation as the levers that move an award.
> - Do not dump terabytes on the agency. The government wants the good material, and anything potentially attorney-client privileged should be held back until a taint process or segregation is set up, because it can taint the whole investigative team [Fraud in America — R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:31:26].
> - Point FinCEN at data it already holds. Banks self-report so much that the agency often lacks only the key showing where to look, and showing that senior leadership knew about the problem is what tipsters most often leave out [Fraud in America — R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:14:54].
> - Write down what you saw and when, and talk to an employment lawyer before making the whistleblower complaint. Early choices about who to report to and when dictate which protections apply [Garrison, Levin-Epstein, Fitzgerald & Pirrotti — ft81gWsFd2M @ 00:02:17].
> - Keep feeding the agency after the first tip. In a $37 million SEC award, the whistleblower's ongoing, extensive and timely assistance exposed the full context that the company's own self-disclosure left out [Compliance Podcast Network — bFRmUwE-_F4 @ 00:14:00].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over where the first report goes. Josh at Garrison, Levin-Epstein lays out the fork: Sarbanes-Oxley protects a purely internal report to a supervisor or compliance department, but the employee gets only 180 days to file a retaliation complaint, while Dodd-Frank offers more damages, potentially double, and a six-year window, yet only kicks in when the concern goes directly to the SEC [ft81gWsFd2M @ 00:01:32]. Ken Weiner at Foley & Lardner reports that most whistleblower attorneys he talks to would not advise a client sitting on a substantial issue to tell the company before the SEC, though some would advise reporting to both simultaneously [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:27:50]. Tom Fox and Matt Kelly argue internal reporting is far from dead: Fox puts the share of SEC whistleblowers who reported internally first at 85 to 90 percent, and Kelly says a solid majority of employees want to report inside because they want the company to do well [bFRmUwE-_F4 @ 00:10:39]. Andrew Feller calls FinCEN's proposed handling of internal reporting a mixed bag, flagging the operator reality that the internal mechanism may be flawed or the person receiving the report may be complicit in the wrongdoing [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:17:20].

Steve Pearlman and Ruben Guttman go head to head on what bounties do to the workforce. Pearlman expects two types of whistleblowers to come out of the woodwork: the private-attorney-general type acting in good faith with religious zeal, and the disgruntled employee who sees the writing on the wall, holds dirt on a supervisor, and wants leverage for a sweeter deal or a richer severance package [eSiH7Rgnhe4 @ 00:03:30]. Pearlman reports companies responding by developing mechanisms to compete with the 10 to 30 percent bounties, some trying to monetize an incentive to keep the complaint internal and others enhancing performance evaluations for good-faith fraud reduction [eSiH7Rgnhe4 @ 00:05:40]. Guttman calls that a stunning admission, arguing that where a company like Pfizer went through three integrity agreements and kept violating the law, or an Enron-style situation, there is no viable internal compliance program to work with, so the call is case by case [eSiH7Rgnhe4 @ 00:06:47]. On the corporate defense side, Lisa Noler at Foley tells companies to use the internal investigation to build a credibility file on the whistleblower, the government's primary witness, and to tell prosecutors the witness will get killed on cross-examination [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:38:27].

The program rulebooks themselves split on who gets paid and how. Ken Weiner walks through the SEC's exclusion of employees whose principal duties involve compliance or internal audit, escapable mainly through a time exception: once 120 days have passed since the supervisor, chief compliance officer, chief legal officer, or audit committee received the information, the compliance official is free to go to the SEC and stay bounty-eligible [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:23:52]. Robert Eckard counters that the AMLA reward program imposes no limitation on people who learn through compliance or audit duties and expressly authorizes compliance personnel, with non-US citizens eligible too [tLBsvqESK6o @ 00:03:53]. The newest tier splits hardest. Bessent, speaking for Treasury, pitches a $25 million fund paying up to $30,000 to anyone whose information leads to a conviction or enforcement action, filed through a single portal at america.gov/fraud instead of a maze of tip lines [FnUiQJNaMn8 @ 00:04:10]. Vincent Romo's crew questions the 1 percent commission framing, notes Trump said he would personally decide who gets paid when several people claim the same tip, calls the setup subjective rather than clear-cut, and asks how many cases have actually been prosecuted after the photo ops [G7YHankdf9E @ 00:01:22].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common kill shot is procedural. Brian Mahany says his firm constantly hears from people who wrote a letter to an agency or called a hotline and never got paid, and the rule is blunt: fail the procedural rules and there are no rewards and no exceptions [BueLAsaxhHM @ 00:03:40]. Whistleblowers International gives the same warning for the CFTC: file a Form TCR, strictly follow every filing procedure, because mistakes lead to denial, and anonymity is available only to whistleblowers represented by a lawyer [nGZvKSKSh-Y @ 00:02:33]. Poppy Alexander of Whistleblower Partners attacks the FinCEN draft's demand that the first tip be certified complete, since nobody knows what complete means; the playbook is to bring the good stuff instead of terabytes, hold back anything possibly attorney-client privileged until a taint process is set up, and expect gaps when filers work without counsel, such as missing proof that the CEO knew [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:31:39]. Chris Erman of Phillips & Cohen adds the capacity constraint: the CFTC has limited resources and can only pursue a limited number of investigations, which is why packaging the information the best way possible matters [dyn9ni2HPDU @ 00:02:24].

Collection risk is the economics check most filers skip. Andrew Feller reports that the SEC does not define collected proceeds to include amounts distributed in bankruptcy, so if the company goes bankrupt before the SEC collects its sanctions, in all likelihood there is no payout, and FinCEN's draft left that same loophole open [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:24:19]. Feller warns the risk clusters in companies and industries with a higher likelihood of bankruptcy after an enforcement action, exactly the targets FinCEN should want reported, and it will get fewer of those reports unless the hole is fixed [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:25:00]. Poppy Alexander flags the million-dollar covered-action threshold: actions can be combined only if they resolve at substantially the same time, yet civil, criminal, individual, and corporate resolutions are sometimes separated by years, and she points to the SEC's approach as the fix [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:36:33]. Matt Kelly delivers the hardest number: the process is arduous and long-drawn-out at the best of times, you probably will not get $37 million, and Kelly has seen people whose careers went into the ditch collect about 800 grand six years later, not much to carry someone in their mid-40s or 50s into retirement, which is why Kelly calls this a high-risk thing people do [bFRmUwE-_F4 @ 00:17:32].

Identity exposure and evidence-gathering are where filers get burned. Sandy Wier at Foley warns that once a whistleblower is debriefed, the information becomes subject to the SEC's routine uses, including sharing with other agencies and Freedom of Information Act responses, so it is probably not going to stay confidential [7vp44tFe6hs @ 00:04:13]. Feller calls language in the FinCEN draft allowing whistleblower information to be shared with defendants to facilitate settlement one of the biggest concerns, and says if it stays in the final rule, the whistleblower bar will have very interesting conversations about whether to use the program at all [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:26:08]. The draft's disqualification for evidence obtained in violation of any state or federal law is the other trap: Poppy Alexander points to one-party versus two-party recording laws and California's civil action for illegal recordings, which gives companies an incentive to push a DA toward charges [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:28:53]. Feller cites a software engineer who left Goldman Sachs with code, not as a whistleblower, yet the firm apparently convinced New York prosecutors to bring a criminal trade secret case, showing the incentives companies have to press trade-secret and intellectual-property theories [R3h4J-EAtro @ 00:30:03].
