## The Practitioner Consensus

The common starting point is a sale that leaves money after the debt, not a promise that every foreclosure produces a check. Attorney Jack describes tax and mortgage auctions where the final bid exceeds what was owed, leaving funds potentially recoverable by the former owner or heirs. Their firm discovered unclaimed balances while handling probate, wills, trusts, and other county matters, then began researching and pursuing recoveries. Dreams & Dollars sharpens that explanation: authorized costs and competing interests matter, and only some sales leave a recoverable remainder. Overage Acquisition makes the same point from the payout side: the surplus shown on a list is not automatically the former owner's money. Their $45,000 surplus and $9,000 owner-payout illustration describes claims ahead of the owner consuming the difference. The shared business mechanic is therefore locating a possible balance, establishing who can claim it, and following the distribution process. Jack's account explains why outreach has value: counties, trustees, and clerks often fail to reach displaced owners or their families. Dreams & Dollars adds the counterweight that a list supplies a lead, not proof of an available balance. Those are compatible positions: finding the person and verifying the money are both parts of the work. State selection comes after identifying which kind of money is involved. Matt Joffrey at Surplus Funds Secrets separates Texas property-tax foreclosure proceeds, deposited in the court registry, from mortgage foreclosure proceeds held through the trustee process. Their explanation puts the tax claims under sections 34.03 and 34.04, while explicitly rejecting application of that same machinery to mortgage cases. Dreams & Dollars makes a broader three-way distinction: mortgage foreclosure surplus, tax-sale overages, and state unclaimed property are separate systems with different offices and paperwork. Attorney Jack similarly describes legal filings in some states and work with private trustees in others. The shared prerequisite is knowing where the funds actually sit before sending a package anywhere. Dreams & Dollars names the clerk, sheriff or appointed trustee, tax collector or treasurer, and state unclaimed-property office as different research destinations. Their method begins with the sale, the debt, the docket, and the distribution record, then checks whether money remains. Joffrey also starts with confirming that funds still exist and have not been consumed by other liens. Neither approach treats a statewide label as enough to identify the correct claim route. The sale type and custodian remain separate questions throughout the investigation. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Money Making Juggernaut's Florida research starts with Real Foreclosure and its auction calendar. On tax sales, they compare the third-party sale amount with the opening figure; on mortgage foreclosures, they look at the final judgment. The parcel identifier leads to the property appraiser, where they locate the former owner and property address. They then search the county civil case by case number, read the event history, and open the disbursement certificate to identify the remaining surplus. Existing claims and additional motions are part of that review. On the tax-deed side, they describe county portals with surplus sorting, notices, and claim forms, plus Real TDM documents and property-information reports for lien research. Dreams & Dollars extends the same records-first approach into distribution orders, clerk accountings, identity matching, bankruptcy, and probate. Overage Acquisition adds searches by parcel number, address, and the previous owner's full legal name, then separate federal-tax-lien and judgment checks. PACER is their named tool when federal judgments may be involved. The actual toolkit is therefore auction results plus the case file and recorded interests, not an auction spread standing alone. Each speaker uses additional records to test what the initial lead appears to offer. Money Making Juggernaut [FIgAVvrCcC8 @ 00:00:54]; Dreams & Dollars: The Everyday Entrepreneur [Zpz5fpHax1w @ 00:03:45]; Overage Acquisition [V0TgTN8wjyM @ 00:03:08].

For Texas tax excess, Overage Acquisition gives a court-specific sequence: file in the district court that ordered the sale, under the original tax-case number, rather than sending the claim to the tax collector or county clerk. They describe notice to every party in the original case at least 20 days before the hearing, and a Harris County situation requiring 60 days when the original clerk notice is unavailable. Their assignment model is a purchase: wait at least 36 days after the proceeds enter the court registry, use a written signed assignment, avoid in-person or telephone solicitation, and pay at least 80% of the claim on assignment day. They put the assignee's release ceiling at 125% of the purchase payment. Matt Joffrey independently describes the non-attorney fee prohibition and the attorney ceiling of 25% or $1,000, whichever is less. Joffrey also discusses assignments with immediate 80% payment, while distinguishing mortgage opportunities from the tax-sale restrictions. Overage Acquisition calls insufficient upfront capital a reason this tax-assignment model may not fit an operator. Both accounts reject importing a routine percentage recovery fee into these Texas tax cases. The payment structure is part of case selection, not an afterthought after signing. Overage Acquisition [F9-rceUCqYE @ 00:02:17]; Surplus Funds Secrets [uyZ7HIGnVzE @ 00:04:57].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Attorney involvement produces the clearest split. Lenny the Lawyer-Live argues that recovery after a sheriff sale requires an attorney in almost every state, stresses state-specific guidelines, and says not every attorney knows surplus practice. Their selection questions include actual experience with the case type and who handles complications. Attorney Jack warns about agreements with non-attorneys and emphasizes the firm's ability to navigate filings and trustees, yet also says an attorney is not always required. Dreams & Dollars takes a narrower DIY position: simple cases can be handled directly, while estates, liens, or multiple heirs make assistance more useful. Money Making Juggernaut supplies an actual alternative rather than only an opinion: the Lee County tax-deed entity claim was filed without an attorney after collecting the required company and authority documents. That presenter also acknowledges other recoveries can involve an attorney or probate. These are different service models and different descriptions of case complexity, not one shared attorney rule. Lenny sells specialized representation; Jack describes research and legal execution; Dreams & Dollars allows simple direct claims; the Lee County example demonstrates an operator-managed administrative filing. The disagreement remains visible without turning one successful collection into a claim about every state or sale type. The timing accounts also resist a single Florida countdown. Lindsay & Jed gives the shorthand that Florida has one year and says the clock begins when the sale closes. Ben Haynes distinguishes an owner's foreclosure claim from a subordinate lienholder's claim. In that explanation, the much-discussed 60 days concerns subordinate lienholders, while the owner should generally act within a year of the certificate of disbursements because funds can move to the state and the case can close. Haynes says this transfer is not necessarily automatic on the anniversary. Money Making Juggernaut instead discusses a tax-deed review delay: 120 days after the surplus notice, with claims submitted before review and lienholders required to act within that window. Overage Acquisition adds that some Florida post-auction deadlines can be as short as 30 days, without identifying one universal claimant category for that statement. The speakers therefore describe different triggers, interests, and procedures. Their figures should stay attached to those descriptions: sale-date shorthand, an owner's certificate-based timing concern, a tax-deed notice-based waiting period, and a warning about local filing windows. Haynes's account of late transfers and Juggernaut's instruction to file during the waiting period both oppose using a processing delay as a reason to postpone preparation. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Haynes's Broward County litigation shows why competing debt totals do not finish the analysis. They represented the former owner in a case with about $252,000 in surplus. The foreclosure complaint dated to 2011 and identified a city lien of about $671; additional city code and utility claims accumulated over the following 14 years to more than $1.9 million. Haynes argued that only the lien identified on the face of the complaint qualified for the subordinate-lienholder treatment at issue. The later liens remained outstanding, but were not named encumbrances in that complaint. Haynes reports that the trial judge agreed and that the city said it would appeal; the prediction of winning an appeal was still a prediction. Overage Acquisition's competing-lien playbook complements this dispute: request a written payoff, compare it with recorded amounts, and inspect added fees, especially HOA collection costs and attorney charges. Dreams & Dollars likewise puts the docket and distribution record ahead of assumptions about the list. The firsthand Broward result is not that liens disappear whenever they are large. It is that the complaint's contents and the particular claim to the surplus determined the trial-level fight, despite a much larger asserted debt balance. Haynes Law Group [Uj-vrJWHKQo @ 00:00:43]; Overage Acquisition [V0TgTN8wjyM @ 00:04:09]; Dreams & Dollars: The Everyday Entrepreneur [Zpz5fpHax1w @ 00:03:45].

The recurring operational failure is a package that looks complete to the sender but does not match the receiving office. Overage Acquisition says they have worked Florida cases and seen agents hit these traps: Miami-Dade's specific form cannot be replaced with another county's form, Broward calls for identity and relationship attachments, and notarization wording or signing requirements can differ. Rejection or inactivity may become visible only when the operator follows up weeks later. Their identity warning is equally concrete: finding someone with the same surname near the property is not enough; cross-reference the deed, county records, property-appraiser database, and skip-tracing results before outreach. Dreams & Dollars reports repeatedly seeing wrong-office inquiries, stale lists, skipped lien or estate research, and sensitive documents sent to unverified recipients. Its contact check is to find the government office independently rather than trusting the telephone number on a letter. Haynes adds firsthand consequences of delay: they have seen funds sent to the state and cases closed after owners waited too long. The combined bottleneck is not simply filling blanks. It is matching the claimant, evidence, form, office, and calendar, then following the claim instead of assuming submission means movement.
