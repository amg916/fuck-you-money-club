## The Practitioner Consensus

The repeatable operation starts with a work pattern, not another random search for a surplus list. Eric Richardson tells operators to identify their states and counties, learn when those places hold sales, track results, organize the opportunities in a spreadsheet, and send the records for skip tracing. The record includes the owner's name, property address, amount owed, sale amount, potential surplus, parcel number, and case number. Richardson also wants a repeatable closing sequence: obtain the email address, send sale proof and company information, and follow up rather than treating one conversation as the finish line [k9f1r7jg4nA @ 00:01:43]. Spencer Vann arrives at the same demand for deliberate execution through a student's stalled start. The student moved between tax, state, and mortgage funds, then changed locations; months passed without settling on a niche and location. Vann says to pick both and complete a couple of deals before reconsidering the choice [OvHZIY5JYBY @ 00:02:52]. Unclaimed CEO adds preparation to that routine. Joe describes coaching people who locate a name and amount, then immediately start dialing to hit a hundred-person target. Joe objects that those callers have not prepared the details needed for the person to know, like, and trust them [c56x3h_z_XE @ 00:01:04].

The speakers also converge on checking whether the apparent opportunity can actually pay. Sean Bougie says a tax list can carry an outdated tax-office name rather than the last owner entitled to the funds. Bougie also says mortgages, equity lines, and liens can stand ahead of the former owner, so a list and a phone number are not the whole business. Bougie's research partner submits the list and debt research on a worksheet before the processing team takes over [Y7MXP9nRGuA @ 00:02:02]. Richardson attacks a related shortcut: assuming the difference between the winning bid and opening bid establishes a surplus. An opening bid may instead reflect part of an appraised value or an amount chosen by a trustee. Richardson tells operators to check the amount owed and include additional fees and interest when examining mortgage foreclosures [pZt-fAltsNk @ 00:00:53]. Jordan Tejeda Marketing shows how much filtering Surplus Rocket performs before a lead reaches the calling queue: 2,165 processed leads became 254 viable leads over 30 days, with a $20,000 surplus threshold. Jordan explains that the threshold accommodates attorney compensation, operator compensation, and fairness to the claimant after fees. The dashboard distinguishes processed leads from the smaller set Jordan considers worth pursuing [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:01:09].

Support and handoffs are part of the operating system, not just something purchased when a file becomes difficult. Charlie's Surplus Systems walkthrough lays out a seven-day action plan covering business setup, lists, surplus verification, owner and lien verification, skip tracing, contact, follow-up, and paperwork. Operators can activate an SOP as a dedicated checklist and return to it under their saved checklists. Charlie describes adding new SOPs when users ask how to perform a task that is not already covered [fsPM6ribYtI @ 00:02:36]. Surplus Funds List puts the handoff inside a case record: schedule the homeowner's requested callback, update the client portal, generate signature documents, print details for an attorney, and upload documents received from the homeowner. The caller can see loan and lien information while speaking [lOnYUxDdOdI @ 00:04:10]. Full Circle's office-hours host describes the people behind a larger operation: five assistants, ten AI agents, and five callers, with FollowUpBoss used to keep track of the work. The host calls that CRM expensive but emphasizes having something manageable for calls and records. These operators describe specific places for tasks, documents, questions, and team activity rather than leaving the whole recovery in one person's memory [c2vik3KCA5g @ 00:38:45].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Surplus Funds List demonstrates the daily CRM loop from intake through follow-up. Import outside leads into the workspace or select database leads and add them without downloading and reimporting files. Filter by sale type, source, status, state, county, stage, tags, proceeds, and sale date. The dashboard separates new leads, contacted leads, documents sent, signed cases, and claims filed; it also summarizes team calls and messages. Estimated revenue uses the cases being worked and the fees charged on them [lOnYUxDdOdI @ 00:01:06]. During outreach, the case view supplies homeowner details and contacts. The walkthrough shows removing numbers marked do-not-call before dialing, then using case-linked calling, text, email, and physical-letter options. A requested callback becomes a scheduled follow-up rather than an informal promise [lOnYUxDdOdI @ 00:03:15]. Charlie supplies the support layer for operators who cannot yet run those tools confidently: a fast-track trainer, help articles with videos, live chat, individual coaching, and a 60-minute technical call described as carrying no additional charge. Four weekly Q&A sessions cover technical and business questions. Charlie also offers trained virtual assistants, with preparation in the overage business and professional phone conversations, rather than describing the hire as an unexplained shortcut [fsPM6ribYtI @ 00:00:31].

Jordan's Surplus Rocket routine begins with vetted leads sorted newest first. Call-round tags record how many outreach rounds a lead has received, while the lead's serial number retrieves a record after newer opportunities bury it. A one-to-five-star score represents the platform's assessment of complexity and recovery speed. The record distinguishes living and deceased owners, single and multiple owners, and judicial and non-judicial recoveries [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:03:09]. Once Jordan closes a case, the claims portal carries it through case review, pending documents, pending filing, claim submission, response, release, funds received, and completion. Jordan's Alaska entity example required identification front and back plus the signed service agreement before attorney submission. The law firm sends updates every 30 days to the claimant and Jordan, with the JV partner included when applicable [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:13:01]. Richardson's scaling training assigns the surrounding work to specific roles: researchers find opportunities and contact information, callers work the phones, a closer supplies a second voice, and an organizer maintains PDFs, documents, county follow-up, and outstanding issues. Richardson names virtualstaff.ph for research assistants at $4 to $5 an hour and Fiverr for cold-caller recruitment. The team must learn the work, not merely receive access to the software [Hzupqw4cjhI @ 00:12:32].

Attorney recruitment has a concrete pitch in Richardson's playbook. Search for a surplus funds attorney, real estate attorney, or foreclosure attorney in the claim's state. Richardson also names Free Consultation Attorney and lawyer.com as places to upload a case so attorneys can respond. The upload should identify the property, amount owed, judgment or delinquent taxes, sale amount, equity, and whether the claimant is the former owner or an heir. The value proposition is that the recovery business markets, contracts with clients, checks liens and ownership, supplies contact details, and brings continuing business [zrj6RA8N9HA @ 00:02:26]. Charlie complements that approach with attorney resources by state and a flat-fee negotiation walkthrough, including how to open the discussion and respond to a high quote [fsPM6ribYtI @ 00:04:44]. Jordan offers a different handoff: book an appointment with one of three closers, transfer claimant details and relevant notes, and let the closer handle closing and processing. In Jordan's $10,000 living-case fee example, the lawyer receives $2,500; the remaining $7,500 is divided into $2,250 for the closer and $5,250 for the partner. Jordan separately describes lawyer shares of 25% for living cases and 30% for deceased cases [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:17:21] [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:26:24].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Texting produces a direct disagreement. Sean Bougie says operators need mail as well as calls and flatly says texting does not work. Bougie also rejects withholding the source of the money until a contingency agreement is signed, arguing that secrecy sounds like an inheritance scam and breeds distrust. Bougie instead explains what created the funds, uses attorneys, and offers good-faith money at signing as part of the firm's approach [Y7MXP9nRGuA @ 00:03:09]. Richardson describes the opposite experience with text follow-up. Using Excess Equity CRM, Richardson runs an initial email and text, waits a day, follows up, waits another day, and continues to a final reach-out. Richardson says most responses come on the second or third text, not necessarily the first [pZt-fAltsNk @ 00:02:36]. The disagreement also reaches purchased lead supply. Bougie criticizes lists distributed to hundreds of people as worthless, while Jordan openly promotes a shared lead platform and says there is enough business for everyone [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:09:01]. Richardson's saturation argument emphasizes organized auction tracking, a defined marketing plan, and follow-up; Richardson even describes successful operators contacting ghosting prospects from other phone numbers. That is Richardson's aggressive follow-up account, alongside Bougie's preference for calls, mail, disclosure, and upfront good-faith money [7REA1e46Yvo @ 00:03:52].

Geographic breadth splits the playbooks just as clearly. Richardson's scaling training asks whether the business will work one state or the nation, then invites operators to pursue tax overages, mortgage overages, and state unclaimed funds. The next step is assembling researchers, callers, closers, and organizational support so one person's capacity does not limit the whole operation [Hzupqw4cjhI @ 00:01:41]. Jordan presents an attorney network covering 45 states and a mortgage-foreclosure platform that supplies vetted, skip-traced opportunities. Jordan's own routine is newest-first vetted leads, not a single-state restriction [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:01:28]. Vann goes the other way for beginners: pick one niche and one geographic location, with state funds recommended as the easiest starting point. Vann's student example shows repeated switching consuming months before any settled execution [OvHZIY5JYBY @ 00:02:52]. Joe likewise tells operators not to bounce from state to state. Learn the chosen state's rules and work there; if the process is too daunting, make an actual move rather than continuing to bounce. Joe allows that beginners may initially test different places, but argues that one state can keep an operator busy for years. The contrast is between Richardson's multi-market staffing plan, Jordan's network-supported coverage, and Vann's and Joe's insistence on concentrated early execution [c56x3h_z_XE @ 00:03:18].

Attorney use and partnership scope are not interchangeable offers. Richardson calls attorneys valuable for deceased-owner claims, probate or summary administration, interpleader, counties that resist third-party companies, and claimant confidence. Richardson prefers a contingent arrangement but acknowledges that an unfamiliar attorney may request an upfront retainer [zrj6RA8N9HA @ 00:00:39]. Joe explicitly avoids attorneys and multiple-owner recoveries. Joe describes past attorney relationships as unpleasant, with attorneys either collecting payment for signing and submitting documents or trying to take over. Joe prefers recoveries completed with their own team [c56x3h_z_XE @ 00:06:30]. Bougie's partnership starts earlier in the process than a signed-client handoff: the partner obtains the list, researches debt, and submits a worksheet; Bougie's team locates people, calls and mails, negotiates, and hires the attorney and notary. Bougie contrasts that with programs requiring the partner to locate, contact, and contract the claimant before processing begins [Y7MXP9nRGuA @ 00:03:44]. Foreclosure Recovery Inc pitches attorneys a different role entirely: introduce former clients while the company verifies eligibility, files claims, and manages correspondence. The company advertises a 10% referral fee, typically $800 to $8,500 per case, with no upfront cost or ongoing work. Those are the company's stated referral economics, not Jordan's closer-and-lawyer split [pBzLGn7xg9U @ 00:00:31].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Payout expectations can break the operation even after the client signs. Richardson says not to leave employment immediately: counties do not pay overnight, and the student-lessons playbook recommends building about six months of deals in the pipeline first. That discussion describes a usual 30-to-90-day wait after filing [DK8BPV7WVvI @ 00:01:47]. Richardson's faster-payment discussion separates fund types and waiting periods. Florida tax-sale claims are described as paying in roughly 30 to 45 days after the initial 120-day period; Florida mortgage foreclosures are described without that same waiting period. Richardson recounts both fast and slow Texas recoveries depending on the trustee, and says probate or restoring a dissolved company can add work before payment [9Tua3eW94Og @ 00:02:29]. Foreclosure Recovery Inc advertises an average twelve-week recovery timeline for its service [pBzLGn7xg9U @ 00:01:11]. Jordan's economics example is about dividing an eventual fee: $10,000 becomes $5,250 for the partner after the stated lawyer and closer deductions. Jordan also presents a $3,000 upfront annual promotional offer and a $450 monthly plan reduced to $350 for the first three months through the described promotion. Jordan pitches those costs against a future recovery, while Richardson's examples describe why getting the recovery money can take time [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:26:09].

Automation has firsthand failure reports, not only enthusiastic demonstrations. Full Circle's host says ChatGPT is not an asset-recovery expert out of the box and that the team's AI hallucinates on small details. One example is an agent inventing a rule against quoting the available amount because the person was deceased. The team builds its own rules and SOPs into agents, combines models, and still repeatedly corrects the results [c2vik3KCA5g @ 00:01:19]. Joe takes a much more enthusiastic position, calling AI a personal assistant for writing, understanding laws, and structuring the business, and describes expanding the AI section of their course [c56x3h_z_XE @ 00:11:17]. Jordan demonstrates an industry-specific assistant answering a Texas divorce question. Jordan already knew the answer depended on title and the divorce decree, then suggests using the assistant's answer in an email or during a call [-bhX4mtOKPs @ 00:10:08]. The Full Circle host also describes a separate verification workload that persists alongside automation: scheduling the claimant and notary while checking everything learned from the client. Every case sent to the attorney carries a retainer in that operation, including a bad case. The host therefore insists on verifying information before sending it, because the retainer is still paid when the underlying case is wrong [c2vik3KCA5g @ 00:33:15].

The recurring failures are often ordinary execution failures with expensive consequences. Richardson says the phone still produces a racing heart, then describes making calls around a financial-adviser job and trying to contact 100 to 200 phone numbers each day. Richardson's response to fear is preparation and repetition, not waiting to feel fearless [TJMvg6ZeN5Y @ 00:00:47]. Joe describes a similar problem from earlier door-to-door knife selling: anticipating rejection killed their drive before the conversation. For recovery calls, Joe practiced with their spouse about 22 times rather than taking an unfamiliar script straight to strangers [c56x3h_z_XE @ 00:02:05]. Full Circle reports another bottleneck after substantial preparation: an attorney receives detailed materials but wants to study an unfamiliar argument independently, and the situation stretches into three weeks. The same host recounts being hung up on while proposing foreclosure help to an owner with two properties, substantial equity, and liens; the owner would not engage [c2vik3KCA5g @ 00:25:39]. Vann's student who kept changing niches supplies the opposite failure pattern: lots of learning and switching, but months without a settled market. Vann's correction is to focus long enough to complete deals. These accounts describe fear, resistance, unfinished learning, and slow handoffs inside real operations, rather than attributing every stalled file to market saturation [OvHZIY5JYBY @ 00:02:52].
