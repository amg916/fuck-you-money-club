## The Practitioner Consensus

A claimant's first question is often why a stranger found money that the claimant never heard about. Israel Miller opens by acknowledging the exact reaction to an unexpected text about a property the recipient no longer owns. Miller explains surplus as the difference between the debt behind an auction and the higher sale price, then explains why notices can miss former owners: the letter goes to the foreclosed residence, or its legal language gets set aside. Miller's offer is research, paperwork, and the claim process on contingency, with nothing charged upfront and a percentage collected only after recovery. Nick Fullmer describes the same basic business: monitor sales, identify an overage, find the former homeowner, and help reunite that person with the funds. Fullmer also points to notices sent to the lost property as a reason owners remain unaware. Richardson's answer to distrust is not a faster pitch. The first call establishes identity, confirms the correct person, and asks for an email address so the claimant can inspect the details independently. The common operating position is specific: explain where the money came from, explain why the message is unexpected, and show what the recovery service actually does before asking the person to trust it. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Bougie's search sequence starts wide: first and last name plus the property address, initially leaving out the middle name and directional components such as northwest or southeast. If the match fails, tighten the search or change the address. The deed may show a mailing address different from the property, particularly when the purchase was an investment or for someone else. The tax assessor's card is another source of a separate notification address. Bougie repeatedly asks for these records because they provide additional reference points for finding the correct owner. Bougie also checks returned mail for 'forwarding order expired'; the return can include the destination address that had previously been used. For live contact through another person, White Pages Premium is Bougie's reverse-address option: a renter still at the property may supply the former owner's contact details. Wilkerson's bulk alternative is Batch Lead Stacker, quoted at about forty dollars monthly and fifteen cents per skip trace. Wilkerson says it does not charge for no-hit files or duplicate leads and remembers identifying details across uploads. The workflows differ in scale, but the actions are concrete: prepare the identifying records, search against more than one address when needed, and retain useful information from failed delivery rather than treating every returned envelope as the end of the search. Richardson budgets two to three hours for a solo operator, describing two hours of calls and one hour of research. Manual dialing produces about fifty calls in the stated daily routine; the software-assisted target is 150–200 leads. Richardson names Batch Dialer and PhoneBurner as alternatives charging roughly one hundred to one hundred thirty dollars monthly per seat, then describes Excess Elite's built-in dialer without an additional seat charge. Leads must be imported in the correct format. The workflow leaves a voicemail when possible and sends a text suited to the recipient: a former owner receives a property-related message, while a relative receives a relationship-confirmation message. Richardson separately reports changing outreach after a DNC lawsuit, filtering DNC-listed contacts, not using voicemail drops or auto dialers, and using a one-number-at-a-time power dialer. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The data-buying disagreement is explicit. Bougie says to get a real service and quotes one dollar fifty to six dollars per search even in bulk, depending on depth. Free services and penny searches are precisely what Bougie rejects. Vann does not require every beginner to start at the expensive end. Vann puts True People Search, Fast People Search, and basic White Pages at one end, with TLO and IDI Core toward the other, and asks whether time or money is the limiting resource. A person with more time and less money may start with free searches or lower-priced services such as BeenVerified; someone with less time and more capital may move up. Vann also says access requirements can take weeks and recommends speaking with the provider before creating a business entity, so the required documents are understood. Wilkerson's choice is different again: bulk Batch Lead Stacker data, with no-hit and duplicate exclusions central to the economics. These practitioners are not quoting one universal market price or naming one unanimous winner. Bougie emphasizes deeper claimant research, Vann emphasizes entry-point tradeoffs and onboarding friction, and Wilkerson emphasizes recurring batch preparation and the charges avoided when lists overlap or a search produces no telephone number. SurplusFundsRiches [bt6FqwHwtBY @ 00:00:13]; Spencer Vann [ZTs6XQ9lr-c @ 00:03:51]; Surplus Funds 101 [xqXetexqH30 @ 00:03:24].

Objection handling divides between immediate proof, staged resistance handling, and aggressive persistence. Richardson says pressure fails when someone calls the offer a scam; send evidence and give the claimant room to verify it. Wilkerson calls many objections buyer questions in disguise, but treats the first complaint as something to ignore while continuing the presentation. On its second appearance, Wilkerson acknowledges it, answers briefly, then drops and diverts. The third appearance becomes a true objection: repeat it, explain in detail, ask for remaining questions, and tie down the answer before moving on. Church educates first, presents the service, and follows people who say they will claim independently. Church describes asking to check back in three or four weeks, then says the callback still happens even if the person says not to. Richardson draws the opposite line on an explicit stop: stop and put the person on the do-not-call list that day. This is a direct disagreement, not merely a different tone. Church also distinguishes ordinary moved-account owners from people discovering inherited accounts, saying the latter more often use the service. Richardson accepts that people can file themselves in simple cases and argues for the service through paperwork, deadlines, and difficult claims rather than denying that option. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Richardson's DNC account is a reversal grounded in a lawsuit, not a theoretical warning. Richardson says calls went unscrubbed from the start of the business in 2018 until about September 2024 because the requirement was not understood, and says most skip-tracing providers were not performing a DNC check. The contact concerned an entity owed surplus and its registered agent, whose phone number was publicly available. Richardson believed the business-to-business context supported the contact, yet the other party filed a federal case and brought a class action. Richardson stresses the cost of defending the case regardless of who was right. The forty-thousand-dollar figure is the attorney's described potential cost of fighting through court and depositions, not a reported settlement payment. Richardson reports paying three thousand dollars for specialist training, then describes DNC filtering, no voicemail drops, and one-at-a-time power dialing. Luketic also reports being sued over an offer-check letter sent through ordinary direct mail, before explaining the move toward inbound pre-foreclosure leads. Bougie adds a separate data-handling burden: providers may require annual testing, a verifiable business location, and secure handling of regulated information. Bougie describes destroying regulated material with a cross-cut shredder or a destruction company when disposing of the file, rather than simply discarding it intact.
