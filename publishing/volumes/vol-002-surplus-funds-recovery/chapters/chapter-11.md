# Chapter 11: Beyond Foreclosure: State and Bankruptcy Funds

*Fund-type comparisons, California investigator agreements, North Carolina finder-fee restrictions, federal bankruptcy searches, recovery frameworks, and a bankruptcy claim case study.*

## Executive Summary

State property, foreclosure surplus, and bankruptcy distributions share a recovery business, not a single claim process. Eric Richardson, Unclaimed Funds Finder, and Darius distinguish forgotten accounts, sale proceeds, and undelivered bankruptcy payments. Scott White starts with availability and entitlement; Richardson uses court records to explain the money before contracting. David Wilkerson demonstrates California's investigator agreement and reports a 10% fee limit. Pierce Law Group describes different North Carolina timing and fee restrictions. Bankruptcy operators use the federal locator, PACER, skip tracing, and deposit records. Richardson's guest reports a claim slightly above $34,000 at a 20% fee, paid after three or four months. Operators disagree over disclosure, preferred niches, and payment control; court backlogs and reluctant claimants complicate the faster-payout pitch.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with the event that created the money, not the size of the advertised opportunity. Eric Richardson at Money Making Juggernaut describes state property as forgotten savings, insurance payouts, utility overpayments, and other payments companies turn over when nobody claims them. Their foreclosure comparison concerns the excess paid at a foreclosure, rather than an abandoned financial account [kuCEYN64vtQ @ 00:00:25]. Unclaimed Funds Finder makes the same distinction and sharpens the custody question: foreclosure proceeds may sit with a court, sheriff, trustee, or county office, while forgotten financial assets commonly run through a state property program. Their description of surplus includes mortgage debt, taxes, liens, and sale costs before any remaining balance [NDXBOlfVC5E @ 00:00:50]. Darius at ACE SURPLUS describes a third origin: a bankruptcy distribution that cannot reach its intended recipient. An uncashed check becomes void and returns as unclaimed money; the intended recipient can be a debtor or creditor, including an individual or company [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:00:28]. These speakers agree on separating the fund types before pursuing the paperwork. Their accounts describe different reasons for the separation between owner and money, different offices holding it, and different records establishing the recovery.

The shared business is research, contact, documentation, and eventual distribution, not simply finding a name beside a balance. Scott White of FundSleuth calls the first stage Discover: establish that money remains available and understand the rules governing third-party recovery before moving into deeper research. Detect then identifies the entitled party, competing priorities, and estate questions. White specifically warns against working money that is no longer available [J6DNZZLasK0 @ 00:00:56]. Spring Porter of Spring Solutions, LLC describes the same working components across state and bankruptcy cases: create the working list, skip trace, contact people by telephone or mail, provide documents, and wait for officials to process the claim. Payment comes at the end in both businesses [mZSZf1u7A70 @ 00:02:05]. Richardson adds a practical bankruptcy prerequisite: understand the docket well enough to explain why someone can receive money after a closed bankruptcy. Their sequence is to check whether a claim already exists, understand how the funds arose, locate the person, market the service, obtain the contract, and submit the claim [EyQovsqVOnE @ 00:02:31]. White's framework and Porter's comparison both place the research and documentation work ahead of the final payday.

Several operators connect stronger conversations to a specific explanation of the money. Richardson's state-funds example uses the reporting bank, reporting year, dormant check amount, registered-finder status, and stated percentage to make the opening understandable. Richardson also searches existing surplus clients for state property and reports helping one client recover another $12,000 from an overlooked savings account [kuCEYN64vtQ @ 00:04:17]. Unclaimed CEO's Joe favors state funds for beginners because the conversation usually meets less resistance than calling someone who recently lost a home. They describe smaller amounts but quicker wins, while emphasizing that the business still takes time to develop [doqCC8csJa0 @ 00:01:32]. David Wilkerson at Surplus Funds 101 applies the same additional-account idea inside California work: search the claimant's name again and collect other accounts at the same time. Their agreement walkthrough includes an additional-account selection and signatures for that purpose [QbqHu-FTlPY @ 00:04:22]. Richardson and Wilkerson therefore expand a relationship through another actual account search, while Joe focuses on the easier conversation around state money. None of those descriptions turns every fund into foreclosure surplus; each starts from the particular account or event the operator can explain.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Wilkerson's California workflow starts with the state-drafted investigator agreement, not a substitute contract. They enter the claimant and investigator names, the owner's address as reported to the controller, the reporting company, property type, and amount. For the property reference and property ID fields, Wilkerson reports using the same number in both without a payment-stopping objection [QbqHu-FTlPY @ 00:01:05]. The current mailing address belongs in a different place from the historical reported address. Wilkerson checks where the claimant actually lives, supplies the telephone number from their own records, and leaves the signature and Social Security entry for the notary appointment. They obtain copies before submission so they have the information needed for later state inquiries [QbqHu-FTlPY @ 00:05:36]. Joe at Unclaimed CEO separately describes California registration as mailing a simple form and receiving an approval email, contrasting it with additional steps they describe for Texas [doqCC8csJa0 @ 00:06:07]. Wilkerson's payment arrangement uses the investigator's registered address because California sends that investigator a separate check. They say they use an LLC tax ID but explicitly express uncertainty about whether one is required [QbqHu-FTlPY @ 00:08:01].

For bankruptcy discovery, Held demonstrates the free Unclaimed Funds Locator as a public-name search. Enter at least three characters in the creditor or debtor field; leave the court blank for participating districts together, or select a particular court. Held uses a fictional creditor name and does not complete the CAPTCHA, so the demonstration returns an incorrect-CAPTCHA message rather than a successful search result [Q5-VYr50NM0 @ 00:00:44]. Richardson and Will Whitman describe a second layer beyond the locator: sort the database by amount, filter by court, and then investigate the case. Whitman's example narrows attention to Northern District of Georgia claims between $10,000 and $20,000; Richardson describes weekly additions and sorting newer leads above $10,000 [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:10:25]. Their record-retrieval tool is PACER. They describe creating an account, opening the relevant bankruptcy records, retrieving the notice of deposit, and checking whether another claim was submitted or paid. A prior claim might concern only part of the money, so Richardson does not automatically discard the lead when a filing appears [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:14:34]. Held says any resulting claim belongs with the particular court, and describes Form 1340 as only a template [Q5-VYr50NM0 @ 00:01:28].

Richardson's paid-tool route is Bankruptcy Surplus: weekly skip-traced leads, contact information, supporting documents, and a CRM for calls, texts, emails, and other outreach. Their description includes relatives' contact information for individual claimants and the deposit proof already attached to the lead [91o6wv-g6D0 @ 00:03:58]. In the webinar, Richardson quotes $300 per month for that system and describes transferring a selected lead out of its public database view when the operator initiates the filing process. The operator then receives processing updates and communicates with the filing team [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:26:51]. Their guest's paid case used a more direct acquisition channel: telephone calls. The guest says they and Kim researched the bankruptcy events so they could explain where the money came from, including answering a claimant family's question about property-sale proceeds. Richardson describes the recovered claim as slightly above $34,000; the guest reports an agreed 20% fee [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:05:47]. Porter describes another payment mechanic: their bankruptcy process directs the funds to the recovery business, which distributes the client's portion, with an electronic-transfer option into the business bank account [mZSZf1u7A70 @ 00:03:53]. These are the operators' different acquisition, processing, and payment arrangements.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The preference split is explicit. Joe at Unclaimed CEO favors state funds for new operators because smaller, less resistant conversations can produce earlier wins. They contrast that with larger tax-surplus deals, more skeptical former homeowners, and the need for a tighter script. Joe says two or three state deals may equal one tax-surplus deal [doqCC8csJa0 @ 00:05:23]. Porter reaches a different commercial conclusion from their own Georgia recovery. They found the state process easy to complete online and say they would not have paid a third party for it. Bankruptcy's original documents, court order, certificate of service, and occasional hearing make that service more attractive to Porter [mZSZf1u7A70 @ 00:04:41]. Payment control also separates their preferences. Porter dislikes state arrangements where the client receives the money directly and the operator still has to obtain their fee. Wilkerson describes a California arrangement that answers that concern differently: the state cuts the investigator a separate check at the registered address [QbqHu-FTlPY @ 00:08:20]. Porter's objection is therefore not Wilkerson's reported California payment experience. The operators favor different mixes of easy filing, convincing the claimant to hire help, and receiving the service fee through the process they use.

The disclosure playbooks pull in opposite directions. White's Deploy stage includes deliberately controlling information before contracting. They say not to give away everything that lets the prospect go around the operator, and to structure agreements strategically so the operator retains an interest after signing if the client changes their mind [J6DNZZLasK0 @ 00:04:03]. Richardson's guest emphasizes being upfront and transparent because earlier training had produced poor results. Their successful bankruptcy conversations explain the docket's sequence of events and the source of the leftover funds, rather than keeping that explanation vague [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:00:29]. Richardson's webinar goes further into proof-led outreach: send an introductory email with the deposit document, company website, and business email identity [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:12:04]. NLP Sales presents a client-service variation, promising a transparent process, understandable communication, and updates through the recovery [VwDsj48KI80 @ 00:01:26]. White is addressing contract protection and the temptation to bypass the finder; Richardson and the guest emphasize credibility through a specific explanation. NLP Sales emphasizes how the service feels to the client. These are distinct approaches to disclosure and trust, rather than a shared instruction about exactly how much to reveal before signing.

The broad bankruptcy pitch also contains narrower qualifications. Richardson describes one national site and sometimes frames the recipient around the creditor, including cases where the creditor and debtor names coincide [7WZONOtYbjc @ 00:06:30]. Whitman's webinar explanation says both debtors and creditors can have unclaimed funds, and that an auction-generated overage is not the base scenario: an intended distribution may simply never reach its recipient [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:32:55]. Darius likewise describes debtor refunds from overpayments and liquidation proceeds, alongside undelivered creditor payments. Their locator description estimates 90% court coverage and directs people to contact missing courts [daCZb9lQvnc @ 00:04:22]. State contracting has an equally important jurisdiction split. Pierce Law Group describes North Carolina's general first-24-month restriction, the stated attorney exception, fees often limited to the lesser of $1,000 or 20%, and possible registration and investigator licensing requirements [Uxc6KOSzdLc @ 00:00:29]. That is not the same agreement setting as Wilkerson's California walkthrough. Richardson's simplified national-search and fund-category descriptions, Whitman's fuller recipient explanation, Darius's missing-court qualification, and Pierce's state-specific restrictions remain distinct. The speakers do not describe one contract, one recipient role, or one fully identical procedure across these recovery lanes.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The paid bankruptcy case puts actual waiting time beside the faster-payment pitch. In the webinar, Richardson describes an ideal 30-to-60-day payout and says the process should not require six to eight months unless probate or additional issues intervene [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:28:30]. Their guest reports three or four months on the completed New York South branch claim. The process included application review, a Zoom call with the court to verify the applicants, another decision interval, and then the check. The guest describes individual processing stages of 21 to 35 days [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:08:13]. The same guest had filed four cases, but only one had paid. Two Texas cases were taking longer because, according to the guest, one person was working the large bankruptcy matter and the backlog was six months [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:02:30]. Porter independently says payout time varies by district and state [mZSZf1u7A70 @ 00:03:23]. The guest's Illinois claim had been accepted, with a hoped-for approximately $28,000 company payout on $142,000, but that was still anticipated money. The interview separates a paid case, pending cases, and an accepted case awaiting the expected payout.

A big balance does not automatically produce a willing client. Richardson's guest reports at least three contacted prospects still procrastinating, including claims of $232,000 and $65,000. Their follow-up points out that the records still show no filing and offers help again [waps0YmW8BI @ 00:05:47]. Joe at Unclaimed CEO reports a coaching conversation with a new operator who had registered the business, hired a virtual assistant, spent money, and still closed nothing. The operator was frustrated with ineffective skip tracing and burnout. Joe's response is to establish a repeatable working process before hiring someone to repeat it [XdU7CVOntKk @ 00:02:36]. Joe also admits starting an earlier business with a Gmail address and later recognizing the credibility problem when requesting personal information and exchanging documents [XdU7CVOntKk @ 00:08:45]. White's framework separates Discover, Detect, Deploy, Deliver, and Distribute because strength in one stage does not finish the rest [J6DNZZLasK0 @ 00:05:48]. These accounts locate the bottleneck in actual operations: an identifiable owner may delay, research may fail to create contact, and staffing may scale an ineffective process. The guest's successful cold calls and Joe's staffing warning describe different stages, not interchangeable shortcuts to a payment.

Bad records and incomplete paperwork create their own stops. Richardson says they and Whitman encountered a false bankruptcy claim. Their response is to inspect what was filed, whether it was paid, and whether it covered only part of the deposit, rather than assuming either that every listed dollar remains available or that any prior filing ends the opportunity [NxCoaATrtq4 @ 00:15:32]. Held's on-camera search stopped at an incorrect-CAPTCHA response because the CAPTCHA was not completed; no actual claim was filed in that demonstration [Q5-VYr50NM0 @ 00:01:06]. Wilkerson's address check addresses another concrete failure point: a claimant's historical property record may not be the address where the payment should go [QbqHu-FTlPY @ 00:05:36]. Pierce Law Group describes agreements becoming void and unenforceable when signed too early, priced too high, or missing disclosures and notarized signatures. They also identify heirs, estate authority, disputed ownership, and competing claims as situations where a simple template may not carry the case [Uxc6KOSzdLc @ 00:00:00]. These accounts preserve the difference between a search attempt, an available deposit, an enforceable recovery agreement, and money actually delivered. Each speaker identifies a particular point where an apparently straightforward recovery can stop.
