## Executive Summary

Alfred Taren, Eric Richardson, and Nakia describe the same operating chain: find surplus, locate the entitled person, earn their trust, and move the claim. Richardson teaches free county lists and free skip tracing, with contingency fees commonly described as 15%–30%. Sean reports roughly $300,000 recovered and $80,000 in fees across 12 deals; Matthew reports eight contracts and approximately $60,000 expected, not collected. Their playbooks diverge on preparation, marketing, staffing, and payment control. BizMove builds around AI and a website; Taren puts calls ahead of branding. Deceased-owner work ranges from deed-and-death-date analysis to attorney-led probate. Anthony's missing-ID problem, Fullmer's late-arriving first-year income, and Shawn Bougie's 46-email probate case show why signed agreements, recovery totals, and spendable earnings are different milestones.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The common business is a locating-and-filing service, not a property purchase. Alfred Taren divides each deal into research, conversation, and filing: identify the sale, confirm the surplus, find the person, earn enough trust to help, and move the paperwork. They call the conversation the business because research and filing can be systematized or handed off. Taren also separates foreclosure and tax-sale surplus from forgotten bank accounts and utility deposits held as state unclaimed property [8-8koApqnYA @ 00:01:59]. BizMove describes tax overages as sale proceeds above taxes and fees, with the recovery service locating, notifying, and assisting former owners for a fee [01plOr9pFjQ @ 00:00:22]. Surplus Funds Academy adds the distribution side: identify cases through foreclosure records and court documents, pursue the claim process, then distribute funds to the entitled parties, which can include former owners, lienholders, or other creditors [8Rn25K08ct4 @ 00:01:26]. Nakia's working sequence is equally direct: find the money, inform the former owner, assemble what the claim requires, and retain the contingency fee for that service. Their own research uses a paid platform, but their explanation places the service in the research, contact, and assistance rather than in owning the underlying property [c50hZiiw2FU @ 00:03:57].

A beginner does not have to buy a national lead package before making contact. Eric Richardson's zero-dollar route starts with county websites, searching surplus funds, excess funds, and excess proceeds lists, then calling or emailing counties when necessary. They acknowledge that not every county supplies a free list and that free skip tracing is not always the most efficient route. TruePeopleSearch supplies possible phone numbers, emails, and addresses; the next task is to explain the service accurately by phone [sK5PqCLSzzg @ 00:00:40]. Taren starts with a few counties, the actual county claim forms, and enough confirmed properties to understand the process. Their starter list is only 20–30 names, followed by an opening that sounds natural rather than robotic [8-8koApqnYA @ 00:08:00]. Richardson's separate no-money walkthrough specifies matching the former owner's name and city before searching, including checking whether a county lists last names first. It then moves through outreach, an agreed percentage, notarized documents, and mailing [1fbTnvEjC7M @ 00:02:24]. Both routes put public records and actual contact ahead of an elaborate operation. Richardson permits pen-and-paper tracking while recommending a CRM; Taren postpones the LLC until there are live prospects and money on the way rather than making entity formation the first productive milestone.

Repeated contact and a working claim pipeline recur more consistently than any single software choice. Matthew tells Nick Fullmer that their minimum target is one contract sent each week, with additional contracts pursued even after that target is reached. Their working sequence is phone call, text, letter, then another pass, organized through sections in Evernote. They describe concentrated work blocks rather than treating occasional outreach as a complete business [vd__NHmQYgM @ 00:13:03]. Christopher likewise puts the conversation at the center, reporting a target of at least 100 calls daily while admitting that trustee and attorney work sometimes interrupts it. One claim above $200,000 came through a relative and a returned voicemail; discarding those indirect contacts would have discarded the connection [uhyVFTeepew @ 00:02:57]. Nakia identifies a different version of the same stoppage: people spend hours, days, and months consuming information without starting. Their remedy is a plan, accountability, and a clear next step [c50hZiiw2FU @ 00:00:42]. Richardson adds that waiting for the first payment before pursuing another claim stops the pipeline from developing. Court schedules can leave the first month, or even the next, at zero despite work having been completed. Their instruction is to keep stacking claims every week while earlier files move through the system [lFoZDRAGylo @ 00:01:49].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start the research at the county and carry it through to entitlement, not just a promising sale price. Richardson searches surplus funds list, overbid list, overage list, and excess proceeds list; their ChatGPT instruction asks for links to counties with lists rather than merely county names. They also track auctions and request sale results or funds held in the court registry [R8B06ovsDXI @ 00:20:59]. BizMove proposes Octoparse or ParseHub for collecting owner information, sale dates, and surplus amounts, and gives a ChatGPT prompt for a Python script extracting names and amounts from a tax-sale PDF [01plOr9pFjQ @ 00:02:35]. Richardson's validation sequence then checks that the sale actually closed to a third-party bidder, checks judicial dockets for an existing claim, and searches for liens, second mortgages, or home equity lines. They distinguish Propwire's mortgage information from fuller lien reports and describe county-recorder searches under individual or entity names, not addresses [R8B06ovsDXI @ 00:26:24]. Taren pairs this work with reading the county's own filing instructions, especially notarization and original-document requirements [8-8koApqnYA @ 00:08:00]. Richardson also warns against treating every opening bid as the actual debt: the surplus calculation depends on confirming what was owed, and nonjudicial cases require contact with the trustee rather than assuming the county holds the necessary records.

The outreach mechanics are specific enough to run without inventing a polished sales performance. Richardson's webinar favors bullet points: introduce yourself and the company, verify the former owner, ask whether they know about the auction and surplus, explain what happened, describe contingency payment, and send proof. Their packet includes company details, supporting evidence, the agreement, a limited power of attorney, payment directions, and identification, with notarization arranged for the documents [lLLfdrFLXFA @ 00:10:12]. Christopher opens differently: 'Hey Eric, hey, it's Chris,' then starts a conversation instead of leading with a company or service pitch. They emphasize questions about the person's situation [uhyVFTeepew @ 00:11:32]. Sean's Florida operation takes existing surplus leads into Excel, then Google Sheets, skip traces with services such as Skip Matrix or Skip Genie, and follows calls with voicemail, text, and mail. They print letters personally and describe reaching heirs before connecting them with their attorney [1uIxpXmBzuU @ 00:06:43]. BizMove offers another administrative stack: HubSpot CRM or Zoho CRM for contacts, follow-ups, and pipelines, plus AI-drafted follow-up messages [01plOr9pFjQ @ 00:04:32]. These are different implementations of the contact-and-follow-up work the speakers describe, from Sean's manually printed letters to Christopher's phone-first conversations and Richardson's evidence-backed introductory packet.

Price the fee and the work separately, as Richardson does in their $100,000 surplus illustration. A 30% success fee produces $30,000 before notary, filing, attorney, and mailing expenses. Their stated ranges are approximately $75–$100 for the notary, $75–$350 for filing, usually $1,500–$5,000 for attorney work depending on the case, and $50–$100 for FedEx. Attorneys may instead charge a third of the operator's fee or another negotiated structure [R8B06ovsDXI @ 00:17:03]. Shawn Bougie's probate recovery shows a different expense profile: a check slightly above $100,000, an owner share they recall agreeing at 50%, anticipated attorney costs of $10,000–$15,000, and estimated profit of $35,000–$40,000 before the researcher's share [fzvvdAGwCBM @ 00:01:11]. Payment routing is another explicit operating choice. Richardson describes assignment of interest, a payment-direction letter, or an attorney receiving and distributing funds. Where payment must go to the claimant, they describe an attorney escrow service taking $1,000 from the claim [R8B06ovsDXI @ 00:42:26]. Their earlier zero-money route instead has the claimant receive the check and pay a subsequent invoice through PayPal, with Richardson explicitly acknowledging the trust involved [1fbTnvEjC7M @ 00:05:27]. The fee agreement, expense allocation, and actual recipient of the check are separate parts of their deal structure.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The launch order is a genuine disagreement. BizMove calls a professional website critical and proposes Wix ADI or Bookmark, AI-generated homepage copy, CRM automation, and a marketing plan as parts of the startup sequence [01plOr9pFjQ @ 00:03:52]. Taren attacks the familiar LLC, EIN, bank account, website, logo, and CRM checklist when it delays finding leads and making calls. Their claimed credibility comes from knowing the property, sale date, amount, and holder of funds, not the logo [8-8koApqnYA @ 00:05:10]. Nakia's mentorship pitch addresses the gap between information and execution through a 90-day, one-on-one action plan, process work, and building toward the participant's first $10,000 [c50hZiiw2FU @ 00:09:26]. Richardson offers a different support structure: self-directed course materials, a 90-day group program described as 12 weeks with four meetings weekly, and a business-setup option involving a team, CRM, virtual assistant, and CRM manager [lLLfdrFLXFA @ 00:17:11]. These speakers are not prescribing the same amount of infrastructure before outreach. Taren says the first call needs neither a website nor employees; BizMove makes online presentation part of the launch. Nakia emphasizes accountability and next actions, while Richardson's options range from learning independently to managing a prepared system and closing clients. The stated programs differ in delivery as well as in how much operating work remains with the beginner.

Marketing choices also differ in the practitioners' own results. Richardson ranks texting and calling highly, supports mailers, and calls door knocking 'undefeated,' recommending a company flyer, surplus details, supporting state law, and an agreement for the visit [R8B06ovsDXI @ 00:34:20]. Matthew says email has not worked for them and reminds them of scam pitches; their sequence is phone, text, letter, repeated through their Evernote workflow [vd__NHmQYgM @ 00:14:20]. Christopher reports postcard-generated inbound calls and says in-person marketing is not advantageous for their operation: they prefer making calls, including into other states, to driving between prospects [uhyVFTeepew @ 00:10:22]. Anthony's first California deal went the other way. After failed phone searches, social searches, neighbor contacts, and overnight letters, they located the auction buyer and drove to meet the former owner. Anthony reports a surplus of roughly $192,000 and a signed 35% fee after the in-person conversation [gB8lD75eL5I @ 00:05:20]. Anthony later describes calls, voicemail, text, and email as their regular approach rather than routine mailers. Door knocking was decisive in that difficult first case, while Christopher deliberately runs a phone-centered operation. Matthew's rejection of email is their reported experience, not the same ranking Richardson gives the channel or the sequence Anthony continues to use.

Deceased-owner files create another fork rather than one universal probate workflow. SurplusFundsRiches teaches reading the deed before chasing heirs, then comparing death and foreclosure dates. In their Barry-and-Elizabeth example, death before foreclosure transfers the interest under the stated survivorship arrangement, whereas death after foreclosure leaves an heirship issue for Barry's share [1N1Z99kUvRw @ 00:03:38]. Sean deliberately works leftover Florida claims that other operators avoid, often involving probate or someone outside the country. Their operation contacts heirs and uses an attorney found after calling approximately 12 attorneys about contingent work [1uIxpXmBzuU @ 00:08:56]. Bougie's partnership example shows a more layered probate structure: a local frontline attorney, a central attorney, a case manager, and a researcher receiving a share of net rather than the whole fee [fzvvdAGwCBM @ 00:00:18]. Matthew offers a different salvage route when a homeowner lead stalls: identify a judgment creditor and contract with that creditor as claimant. Fullmer reports actually recovering money through a defunct company's surviving connection after researching an old recorded judgment [vd__NHmQYgM @ 00:08:39]. The alternatives are therefore deed-based entitlement research, attorney-led heir claims, researcher partnerships, and creditor recovery. Matthew specifically distinguishes contracting with the creditor from taking assignment of the judgment, which they describe as potentially complex for someone unfamiliar with judgment work.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Signed contracts and impressive recovery checks do not erase the payment gap. Fullmer recalls nearly returning to corrections work during their first year, despite eventually making about $118,000. They say 75% of that income arrived in the final quarter, and describe waits of three, four, or five months after signing. Matthew's approximately $60,000 is an expected result on contracted work, while their other businesses and investments provide an existing paycheck [vd__NHmQYgM @ 00:20:58]. Richardson similarly warns that working in July does not mean receiving payment in July. Their illustrative beginner economics use $5,000 for 60 hours, roughly $83 an hour, alongside an explicit warning that a specific monthly payment cannot be promised from court-driven timing [lFoZDRAGylo @ 00:01:02]. Anthony describes California tax-sale waiting as a year followed by additional months, depending on when the client is signed and when the sale occurred; that delay motivates interest in other states [gB8lD75eL5I @ 00:12:25]. Bougie's probate example also remains an estimate at the partner-payment stage: the check has reached the frontline attorney, but final attorney charges and net profit are not yet settled [fzvvdAGwCBM @ 00:01:29]. Each account distinguishes work completed, funds recovered, fee calculation, and actual cash received rather than treating a signed agreement as immediate income.

Anthony's first deal was not just finding a large number and collecting a signature. They could not find a working owner number, received no response from neighbors, and got nowhere with overnight letters. The auction buyer supplied the breakthrough, but the in-person visit uncovered another obstruction: the claimant had no ID, and the property had no phone service. Anthony drove back toward the freeway to arrange a notary, then later worked through the claimant's mother and sister to obtain identification. They paid for the sister's assistance and DMV paperwork before submission approximately a month and a half later [gB8lD75eL5I @ 00:09:40]. Matthew reports an agreement that had to be withdrawn because the claimant had forgotten another person was already working the case [vd__NHmQYgM @ 00:04:06]. Overage Acquisition describes that same duplicate-claim trap as wasted time, wasted budget, and an angry client; their check is the county's online portal or a call to the office before proceeding [bpxSOg1_xbE @ 00:02:05]. Richardson adds sale-status reversals: a bidder can fail to finish paying, a sale can be vacated, or bankruptcy can intervene after an initially attractive result [R8B06ovsDXI @ 00:26:24]. These operators describe missing documents, unreachable people, existing engagements, and changing sale status as concrete reasons a promising file does not immediately become a payable claim.

Growth exposes coordination work that the headline deal count leaves out. Sean reports about $300,000 recovered, $80,000 in success fees, and five to six hours of direct work per claim, but also says the business ran off and on for roughly a year and a half and that they hired a VA around active-duty scheduling constraints [1uIxpXmBzuU @ 00:04:24]. Richardson's later earnings overview describes that same success story as operating without employees, so the interview's staffing account and the later solo-business description do not match [lFoZDRAGylo @ 00:10:40]. Christopher supplies a different scaling sequence: admin first, then research capacity, then a case manager handling clients, notaries, and attorneys, before expanding sales. They explicitly reject pushing 100 deals through a broken process that fails to update claimants or follow trustees [uhyVFTeepew @ 00:08:05]. Bougie shows the coordination in one probate recovery: 46 back-and-forth emails involving the estate administrator, case management, updates, attorney liaison, and frontline attorney [fzvvdAGwCBM @ 00:00:35]. Sean's quoted claim hours, Christopher's division of work, and Bougie's email trail describe different operating arrangements. Christopher also admits that testing technology slowed their start; their growth account puts a functioning research and follow-up process before adding more salespeople to the front of it.
