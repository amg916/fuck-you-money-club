# Chapter 08: The Surplus-Funds Call: Scripts and Live Examples

*First-call objectives, credible introductions, conversation structure, common calling mistakes, callbacks, and recorded closing examples.*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Eric Richardson, Alfred Taren, David Church, and Bob Diamond put credible information, patient listening, and a concrete next step ahead of a hard opening pitch. Richardson targets an initial call under five minutes; Taren opens with auction results and pauses; Church leads with identifying data. Their follow-up expectations differ: Richardson describes three to ten follow-ups after documents, while Diamond describes two or three conversations plus other communications. The sharper disagreements concern detailed versus curiosity-driven voicemail, immediate closing versus sending proof first, and how much pressure to apply after rejection. Live examples also separate agreement to receive paperwork from completed recovery: Church calls a $16,000 day verbal commissions with contracts still unsigned, and Spencer Vann describes a first-call result as a potential deal.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The opening job is to explain why this particular person is hearing from you, not to perform a generic sales introduction. Eric Richardson of Money Making Juggernaut reduces the first call to four pillars: introduce yourself, verify the former owner, explain what happened and how the company helps, then collect an email or mailing address. The point is to deliver proof rather than stretch a stranger's attention into a long presentation [JJObKyLY5YY @ 00:00:59]. Surplus Funds Secrets describes the same underlying trust problem from the recipient's side: why are you calling, how did you get the number, why would money exist, and does this mean another debt? Their preferred opening identifies funds that may be owed to the person or their family's estate before asking whether it is a good time to talk [HtbOqZK2He8 @ 00:00:30]. Bob Diamond adds that skepticism is expected, not an objection that disappears because the amount sounds attractive. Diamond asks callers to let people move at their own pace and explains the eventual request as written permission and a short notary meeting, with payment dependent on successful recovery. The shared approach is information first, room for questions, and a manageable next action—not an instant demand for trust [NOkg0U0k9Zs @ 00:00:11].

Credibility begins with identifying details the recipient can recognize. Alfred Taren wants the sale date, sale price, and approximate amount owed ready before dialing. Taren's opening concerns the auction results for the property, followed by a request to go over those numbers. The caller then pauses instead of burying the person under explanation [CqlbwGPmv64 @ 00:00:34]. David Church similarly describes a direct introduction built around the person's name, an account, and an address. Church's example is an unclaimed CD account, not a foreclosure surplus; the attention-getting mechanism is the recognizable data, followed by what the service can do for that person. Church explicitly rejects leading with a personal résumé or family story because the recipient wants to know why the call matters to them [alaKJIcbbLw @ 00:00:42]. Richardson's property verification serves another purpose: two people can share a name, so the caller must establish that the person actually owned the relevant property. Richardson follows verification with engaging questions about whether the person knew about the funds or had already been contacted. Those questions make the call a conversation and keep the information in a deliberate order. A familiar name alone is not Richardson's verification, and a confident voice alone is not Taren's evidence [JJObKyLY5YY @ 00:01:28].

Agreement also extends beyond the first conversation: paperwork and callbacks need active attention. David Wilkerson of Surplus Funds 101 says most accounts arrive at contact points three through five, not the initial contact. Wilkerson asks for an email, offers screenshots of the account documentation, and usually aims for a Zoom conversation as the second contact because seeing the other person adds credibility. When someone is already ready for the notary, however, Wilkerson says to close rather than force another appointment [smhFDkvMwkM @ 00:03:54]. Richardson's review of Morgan's call gives a different follow-up measure: after sending documents, expect three to ten follow-ups before signed papers return, while recognizing immediate returns as exceptions. Richardson praises collecting both the email and current mailing address, not merely getting a friendly response [MbdEoM3KDC0 @ 00:04:15]. Diamond describes two or three actual conversations combined with letters, texts, and emails, totaling roughly six or seven communications before signup. Diamond's examples include a reminder before an agreed callback. These are different descriptions of contact activity, not interchangeable counts of cold calls. What they share is a next step that remains visible after the phone disconnects: deliver the material, keep the appointment, and continue the conversation until the person actually acts [NOkg0U0k9Zs @ 00:01:18].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Richardson's working script starts simply: 'Hello, is this John?' Then comes the caller's name and company, the property address, and the question of previous ownership. Richardson anchors the sale event before asking whether anyone has contacted the person about surplus funds, excess proceeds, overages, or remaining funds. The destination is explicit: 'What I'd like to do is send you the details so that you can see this for yourself. What's the best email for you?' Richardson says the first call is for sending proof, not forcing a close [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:06:34]. Taren offers a quieter alternative opening: 'I'm reading about the auction results for the property on Main Street.' After asking permission to review the figures, Taren waits three to five seconds if necessary, then describes the difference between the sale and opening bid as possible funds rather than definite proceeds [CqlbwGPmv64 @ 00:01:26]. Surplus Funds Secrets supplies the transition from explanation to paperwork: describe engagement documents as the step that lets the team begin working on the recovery, then offer a digital or in-person notary at the client's convenience. Their closing question is whether today or tomorrow works best. Each script gives the recipient something specific to answer instead of requiring them to absorb an uninterrupted monologue [i7mng3-o1u0 @ 00:02:39].

Before that conversation, Richardson sorts the phone numbers rather than assuming the first skip-traced result is correct. Call each listed number to identify disconnected lines, cross those out, then text the live numbers with a first-name question such as 'Hello, is this John?' Wrong-number replies remove more candidates. Richardson then runs a call, voicemail, and text sequence, repeating the process and extending it to relatives [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:03:05]. After an email goes out, Richardson texts that it has been sent and calls within ten minutes to walk through it [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:09:03]. Church's desk preparation is more basic but equally concrete: rehearse the script twenty times and keep the Google Voice number visible because Church sometimes starts giving a personal cell number instead. The visible reference also supports answering unexpected questions without losing the thread [alaKJIcbbLw @ 00:03:03]. Spencer Vann keeps the script in front of them even though they wrote it. In the live calling session, Vann also recommends printed leads for crossing people out and noting voicemail or conversation outcomes. The reason is practical recall while moving through the list, not a claim that printed paper replaces every other tracking method. Both callers describe references they actually use while speaking [JjEdRqLg0Ug @ 00:04:45].

The live fee conversations attach the percentage to a service and a next step. Dexter Brown describes a possible recovery around $15,000, then explains a thirty-percent company share and seventy-percent client share. Brown divides the company's share into ten points each for research, locating, and legal work, and says payment occurs only if money is recovered [0FZrtOY2H5w @ 00:04:49]. Brown then offers paperwork delivery or email, a conversation with another family member, and a shared email thread and DocuSign so the participants receive the material together [0FZrtOY2H5w @ 00:07:56]. In Morgan's call reviewed by Richardson, the caller describes about $29,000 available and quotes a normal percentage of twenty-five to thirty, depending on the arrangement. Morgan asks for current contact details and offers overnight documents with return postage, keeping the immediate task on reviewing and returning the packet [MbdEoM3KDC0 @ 00:01:34]. Church's commission-day examples use a different arrangement: ten percent paid to JD Vista from the recovery, with the state paying the claimant directly. Church offers to email forms and start paperwork, including a power-of-attorney situation. These are the speakers' particular fee conversations, not one universal surplus-funds price. The repeated operational move is explaining the arrangement before asking the person to proceed [ieI6zKICpKo @ 00:00:59].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Voicemail is a direct disagreement. Richardson wants a short message containing the caller's name, company, and property address, with no money talk. Richardson argues that curiosity produces callbacks while too much detail can trigger suspicion and blocking [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:03:51]. Vann uses the same withholding approach: an important piece of information about the old property, not an announcement that money is owed. Vann explicitly calls the voicemail a hook [JjEdRqLg0Ug @ 00:03:40]. Church goes the other way in the state-unclaimed-funds session, describing detailed messages that identify the account, address, company, and reason for contact. Church attributes much of the business to those callbacks and describes the returning caller as a warm lead [xqpLsq3JVlU @ 00:50:30]. Yet Church uses less information when facing a screening prompt: say the recipient's name followed by 'it's' and stop, letting curiosity encourage pickup. Church says this recently adopted technique works well and gives a nine-out-of-ten pickup claim during the explanation. That screening tactic is distinct from Church's detailed voicemail practice, and the examples come from a session devoted to state unclaimed funds. The callers do not offer a single shared rule about how much information belongs in every unanswered-call situation [xqpLsq3JVlU @ 00:23:43].

Pace and emotional preparation also vary. Richardson's first-call target is five minutes or less, with the narrow goal of putting proof in the recipient's hands [JJObKyLY5YY @ 00:00:27]. Church describes the 'perfect' conversation as sixteen minutes and credits listening for receiving mailing details, an email, and extensive family information. The conversation then expands into additional unclaimed accounts rather than stopping at the original returned insurance check [MsxVzoHQCNk @ 00:08:08]. Vann emphasizes certainty, directness, and questions that connect a recovery to the person's financial circumstances. Vann's example asks what an extra $20,000 would mean after a pandemic-related income loss, before presenting the service. This is emotional discovery about the recovery, not weather or sports small talk [ZEobF_ck678 @ 00:11:42]. Wilkerson is more explicit about rapport and urgency: move quickly through people who are not deals, then slow down when a collectible account, receptiveness, and need come together. Wilkerson also says not to push a willing signer into a second appointment merely because the usual process includes one. The contrast is therefore not simply short calls against long calls. Richardson defines a proof-first objective; Church follows useful disclosures; Vann develops the personal meaning of recovery; Wilkerson adjusts pace to readiness [smhFDkvMwkM @ 00:04:15].

Family cases expose a sharper difference between conversational shortcuts and questions about authority. Brown's Louisiana call concerns a deceased owner with several children and a large extended family. Brown presents a small succession as cheaper than a regular succession and describes two heirs stating that they are the only heirs they know of. Brown also expressly says the approach should not be used maliciously to cut people out; the cost argument and that warning both belong to Brown's pitch [0FZrtOY2H5w @ 00:02:30]. Taren takes a different line when speaking with relatives: establish whether the person is a spouse, child, or executor, ask whether probate has started, and do not decide on the phone how the family splits the funds. Taren distinguishes being family from having the right to claim [CqlbwGPmv64 @ 00:05:18]. Church describes having encountered relatives who wanted payment even though a spouse had never divorced the deceased person. That experience leads Church to ask about marriage, children, wills, and documentation before making promises about the recipient. In the returned-insurance-check conversation, Church also asks about power of attorney and supporting paperwork. These accounts offer materially different family conversations, with Church and Taren emphasizing questions that Brown's abbreviated succession explanation moves through quickly [MsxVzoHQCNk @ 00:04:16].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Wrong connections and calling friction appear throughout Church's long live session. Church notices California leads would receive a six-in-the-morning Saturday call and moves away from that timing. Later, Google Voice does not connect as expected, prompting attempts from other lines. A supposed family connection also proves wrong: the person does not recognize the deceased account holder, and Church eventually accepts that the association is bad rather than treating the denial as a successful identification [xqpLsq3JVlU @ 00:57:28]. Taren's preparation addresses the timing side directly: use the recipient's time zone, call between eight in the morning and about nine at night, and consider an earlier evening cutoff for elderly recipients. Taren also says to note a request not to call again and stop calling [CqlbwGPmv64 @ 00:00:51]. Richardson finds a different missed-contact trap in Morgan's call: the phone rings about eight times before the person answers. Richardson says ending after four rings would have lost that conversation. The three examples are not the same failure. One is a false association, another is bad timing, and another is abandoning a ringing line too soon. Each comes from an identified event or explicit calling instruction, rather than a blanket claim that every unanswered number is worthless [MbdEoM3KDC0 @ 00:04:57].

Trust can fail after the recipient has already agreed to listen. In Church's skeptical-family conversation, the person describes frightening reports about another recovery company and worries about bank information. Church responds with public records, company and attorney credentials, paperwork, and checks mailed without requesting routing or banking information. Church also admits cutting off a discussion of a sudden death and says more empathy was needed [87zmv7-0D7s @ 00:10:17]. Fee language creates another problem in Church's returned-insurance-check review: saying the state pays the company could sound as though the ten percent is additional money. Church corrects the explanation to say the fee comes from the recovered funds, and reports that another prospect had recently decided to claim independently [MsxVzoHQCNk @ 00:07:30]. Wilkerson warns against trying to establish credibility through phrases such as '100% legit' or 'scam free,' saying they can produce the opposite reaction. Wilkerson also writes notes during calls and transfers them to the CRM afterward, keeping follow-up grounded in the actual conversation. The practical reversals are specific: talking over grief, making payment sound different from its actual source, and using defensive labels that make the caller seem less credible. Church's own example also shows that explaining the opportunity does not prevent a person from proceeding without the locator [smhFDkvMwkM @ 00:08:17].

A promising call and collected commission are different events in the practitioners' own accounts. Church announces $16,000 from four $40,000 opportunities, but immediately identifies the amount as verbal commissions and says contracts still need signing. One opportunity came from a callback, another from follow-up, and two from first-time conversations [ieI6zKICpKo @ 00:00:00]. Vann's live first-call success likewise ends with permission to email information and investigate whether money remains after an earlier collection. At the end, Vann calls the result a potential deal rather than reporting a recovered check [JjEdRqLg0Ug @ 00:05:17]. Church is equally explicit in the skeptical-family review: the person moves from resisting contact to requesting the information, but Church says the deal is still being worked and the outcome is not known [87zmv7-0D7s @ 00:12:27]. Rejection also carries a production cost. Vann recalls an early probate-property cold call ending in an extended angry response; Vann stopped calling for the rest of that day. The later lesson was to celebrate getting through rejection and continue toward people willing to engage. That story concerns Vann's earlier real-estate calling, while the lesson is applied to surplus-funds conversations: do not let one hostile exchange end the work [ZEobF_ck678 @ 00:06:27].
