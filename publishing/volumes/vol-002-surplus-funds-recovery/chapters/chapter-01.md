# Chapter 01: How the Surplus Recovery Business Works

*Tax-sale excess proceeds, owner recovery, the agent's role, and introductory find-contact-claim workflows.*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Ted Thomas, Bob Diamond, Tax Sale Academy, and Overage Acquisition describe the same starting point: an auction generates more money than the debt being collected, leaving excess proceeds that an eligible claimant can pursue. The recovery business connects records, owners, agreements, and claims rather than buying the property. Eric Richardson emphasizes accessible county records and repeatable outreach; Spencer Vann separates data, sales, and processing. Practitioner economics range from free records and inexpensive skip tracing to paid mail campaigns and attorney-led buyouts. Their disagreements matter: Diamond withholds the money's source before contracting, while SurplusFundsRiches favors disclosure and upfront good-faith payments. State selection, creditor priority, mistaken identities, stale lists, and county processing delays determine whether a promising balance becomes a collected fee.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The business begins with excess cash after an auction, not with the recovery agent buying a house. Tax Sale Academy defines excess proceeds as money paid above what the county is owed, including back taxes, interest, fees, and expenses. Its example uses a $1,000 obligation and a $2,500 sale, leaving $1,500. Overage Acquisition uses a larger illustration: Tom owes $10,000 in property taxes, the property sells for $100,000, and the difference is a $90,000 overage. Both explanations separate the auction buyer's acquisition from the former owner's possible recovery. The buyer receives the property; the recovery operator works on the remaining money and the person entitled to claim it. Ted Thomas makes the same distinction with a property worth $100,000, bidding beginning at $10,000, and a sale at $20,000. The buyer may be pleased with the purchase, but Thomas directs attention to the $10,000 above the tax amount. These are the speakers' teaching examples, not reported completed claims. Their shared starting point is an actual sale producing excess proceeds, followed by a separate recovery process. The agent's work concerns that second process rather than bidding, financing a purchase, or reselling the auctioned property. The next shared premise is an information and contact problem: money can exist without the former owner knowing how to retrieve it. Overagesorg describes inadequate notice and owners leaving funds behind; its finder model is to locate those owners and assist with collection for a service fee. Unclaimed CEO explains the practical break in notification: county mail often goes to the last known address, which is the very home the person has lost. The speaker says county offices lack the bandwidth to hunt people down, creating a role for investigators who can find them. Eric Richardson gives a concrete version from a client they helped: a Miami-Dade notice identified $21,215 from a February 18, 2021 tax-deed sale and said the office would hold it for 120 days. Richardson then explains that letters can be addressed to the foreclosed property. This is why the business is more than downloading a balance: the operator must connect the record to a reachable person. Across these accounts, finding the money and reaching its possible claimant are separate jobs. Richardson's example also shows an actual county notice, rather than treating every county as silent or every owner as already informed. The operating sequence is equally consistent: obtain records, identify and locate the claimant, establish a working relationship, secure documents, and pursue the claim. Bob Diamond describes public-record lists carrying an amount, a property address or tax identification number, usually a person's name, and the sale date. Their workflow moves from those records to skip tracing, outreach, a signed contract package, submission, and follow-up. Spencer Vann organizes essentially the same workload into data, sales, and processing. Data includes filtering, a CRM, and skip tracing; sales means getting people to work with the business; processing means turning agreement into signed documents and money. Vann specifically flags the gap between someone saying yes and actually signing. Richardson adds a useful starting condition: choose a county where results are accessible, the claim form has instructions, and existing claims can be checked. Their emphasis is not merely finding an impressive list but understanding how that county publishes results and accepts claims. These accounts put the beginner's foundation in a working records-to-payment process. Diamond's list provides the starting information, Richardson's county screen makes the process navigable, and Vann's three-part framework keeps research, client acquisition, and claim completion from being mistaken for the same task. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start the research with the language the county uses. Diamond's search pattern is county, state, and 'excess funds list'; their examples include DeKalb County Georgia, Hamilton County Ohio, and Broward County Florida. The resulting list gives names and amounts before the operator looks up contact information. SurplusFundsRiches broadens the search vocabulary to overbids, surplus funds, mortgage overages, tax-sale overages, tax-deed overages, excess funds, and excess proceeds. When an online list is unavailable, the channel contacts whoever held the sale and asks by phone or email. Its 'one and done' method uses a list obtained from one county as a sample to show another county what is being requested. Overage Acquisition supplies the parcel-based route: find the county's excess-proceeds list, use parcel numbers to identify previous owners, and then locate those people. Its paid alternative is Excess Elite, described as delivering daily nationwide leads, skip-traced contacts, phone-number checks against the do-not-call registry, and AI analysis of deals, debts, and liens. The channel presents paid data and manual county research as budget-dependent alternatives. SurplusFundsRiches also recommends requesting lists directly because the extra request can reach records other operators are unwilling to pursue. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest disagreement is whether to reveal where the money sits before the claimant signs. Overagesorg explicitly teaches keeping the information secret and securing a contract before divulging it. Diamond reports having tested the opposite approach: after the team disclosed the source, prospects stopped answering, and later checks showed claims being made soon after the calls. Diamond therefore leads with a potentially recoverable refund and withholds its source. SurplusFundsRiches takes the opposing position. Its mailing identifies court-held funds, the foreclosure that created them, the property address, and the amount. The channel argues that vague claims of secret money produce distrust. Its answer to being bypassed is not secrecy but a different offer: an upfront structured buyout, good-faith money, and an attorney handling retrieval. Overage Acquisition also teaches disclosure, telling operators to provide sales results, county lists, or court documents rather than merely asserting that money exists. These are genuinely different sales systems. Diamond ties source protection to an observed circumvention problem, while the disclosure operators tie proof to credibility. The distinction reaches beyond wording: SurplusFundsRiches supports its open-book pitch with money paid to the claimant before final recovery, whereas Diamond describes a contingent-fee invitation without a client deposit. Vann still permits a tax-fund or mortgage-fund start when that is the operator's chosen focus, but repeatedly returns to picking one market and sticking with it instead of continually changing states and niches. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

A list entry is not the same thing as a payable client file. Diamond identifies the wrong person as the team's most common problem: junior instead of senior, or one John P. Smith instead of another. The team then needs the actual owner's participation rather than the signature already obtained. Diamond also reports mortgage companies or other competing claimants appearing and preventing the anticipated recovery. SurplusFundsRiches makes ownership and debt research central for that reason. It checks who owned the property at the sale, whether recorded debt was satisfied, and whether the listed name still represents the person entitled to pursue the proceeds. The channel's numerical example is a $50,000 overage with a $20,000 unpaid HELOC: it describes setting aside the debt and pursuing $30,000 rather than treating the entire balance as the owner's money. Tax Sale Academy independently notes that some states allow former lienholders priority over the former owner. Together these accounts identify two distinct checks: the person must be the right claimant, and the balance may face other claims. Diamond's firsthand identity errors and creditor appearances explain why a reachable name and a signed agreement do not always produce the expected payment.
