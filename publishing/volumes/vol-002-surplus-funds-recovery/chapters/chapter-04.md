## Executive Summary

Eric Richardson, Alfred Taren, and Nick Fullmer agree that a surplus list is only the beginning: check existing claims, identify ownership, and research competing debt. Richardson uses county dockets, tax-deed portals, and recorder searches; Taren adds the foreclosure file, Propwire, the money holder, and current lienholder payoffs. Florida county walkthroughs show why document names and PDF update direction matter. Fullmer reports title-search prices around $100–$150; Richardson describes a wider $60–$500 range. Their approaches diverge on when to spend money and pursue uncertain files. Taren sometimes submits close cases to obtain the actual breakdown. Sade describes a more aggressive Texas owner-claim route when notified junior creditors fail to act, while explicitly retaining the possibility of later debt collection.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The first agreement is blunt: a surplus figure does not tell you whether the file is still open for business or what the former owner can collect. Eric Richardson starts mortgage-foreclosure research with the civil case number, taken from the auction calendar or foreclosure information, and searches the county docket for an existing surplus claim. For tax-sale files, he checks a tax-deed portal or a county list; when neither supplies the answer, he calls or emails the county for unclaimed excess proceeds. Alfred Taren attacks the other half of the problem: the listed amount can already be spoken for by competing creditors, so he checks the actual case or sale file and what has already been paid. The Polk County demonstration makes both checks visible. Its results page shows a sold property and a $45,000 surplus balance, but the researcher still opens the documents tab for claims and the disbursement tab for remaining funds. Richardson's application-status examples also distinguish an application, denial, appeal, and payment rather than treating the dollar amount as the whole story. These practitioners work through the file, not merely through the headline number on a list. The second agreement is that competing debt has to be researched before treating surplus as the owner's payout. Taren describes second mortgages, home-equity lines, HOA claims, judgments, tax liens, and city charges as recurring items. On the mortgage-foreclosure side, his sequence is the foreclosing lender, later liens in order, and then the owner; a loan recorded before the foreclosing loan usually stays with the house rather than drawing from that surplus. On the tax-sale side, he explains that removing a lien from the house can move the creditor's claim over to the money. Sade gives the same basic distinction in a Texas illustration: the tax sale removes the collateral, but the mortgage debt remains, and the lender can seek excess proceeds. Dave at Private Investigator Videos adds that dates alone are not the entire title analysis: government claims, municipal claims, and certain priority liens can behave differently. AFX Research's Texas search route therefore reaches beyond mortgages into tax records, judgments, and commercial UCC filings. None of these speakers reduces the work to spotting the word 'mortgage' and subtracting one recorded figure. Their recurring subjects are the sale type, recording history, creditor priority, and what debt actually remains. Ownership research is the other baseline prerequisite, and the speakers make it more specific than finding a name beside an address. Nick Fullmer says to confirm who owned the property at the time of sale and, in certain states, who was on the mortgage. He singles out Minnesota as a place where, in his account, the surplus belongs to the mortgagor rather than necessarily the person named on the deed. Richardson's county-recorder method begins with the previous owner's name, because that is how he searches recorded liens and then checks the asset tied to each document. Dustin Hahn's show demonstrates another ownership problem: someone claiming to own the whole property may have another person on title, or may actually be another wholesaler. The panel uses county records to expose those mismatches before a contract reaches the title company. Taren adds deceased-owner files to the picture. He says the right to the funds can pass to family, with relationship or heirship evidence and sometimes probate, and suggests an obituary or skip trace to locate them. The practical agreement is to establish whose interest you are researching before presenting a potential recovery to the person who answers the phone. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Richardson's inexpensive lien search starts with a county-provided property information report or owner's and encumbrance report. He says these identify the title holder, liens, and book-and-page references for recorded documents. Without that report, he searches the county recorder under the previous owner's name, then opens the deed and lien documents. Taren starts even closer to the foreclosure: in a court case, the parties named in the lawsuit can reveal other lienholders, and the final judgment usually includes the bank's interest and fees. For a trustee sale, he starts with the notice of sale. He uses NETR Online as a directory to county recording offices and Propwire as a free property-data starting point, while calling its balances estimates. AFX Research separates Texas searches by office: county clerk for recorded property documents, tax assessor-collector for unpaid property taxes, district or county clerk for judgments, and Secretary of State for commercial UCC filings. Rent Own Sell supplies another paid-data option, PropertyShark, and says title companies can perform lien searches for a fee. These are the actual routes the speakers name; the county report, recorded instrument, and estimated property-data balance serve different jobs in their research. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest contrast is not whether junior creditors exist, but what happens when they do not act. Taren's baseline is that the former owner stands behind qualifying creditors, yet he also says missed filing deadlines can move more money to the owner. Richardson makes a more specific Florida timing claim: he describes 120 days on the tax-sale side after the notice notification and 60 days on the mortgage-foreclosure side, while telling viewers to ask the county about the applicable period. Sade develops a more aggressive Texas owner-claim illustration. With $190,000 in excess proceeds and a $100,000 mortgage, the ordinary split in the example is $100,000 to the lender and $90,000 to the owner. Sade says the owner can pursue all the excess if the required procedural steps and notices are followed and the lender fails to intervene. That does not erase Sade's separate warning about the surviving debt: the lender can pursue a personal judgment, and Sade discusses bankruptcy or negotiating a smaller settlement as possible responses. The contrast is a creditor-payment baseline versus an owner pursuing funds after creditor inaction, not an assertion that every tax sale automatically cancels every debt. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The clearest firsthand economics reversal comes from Dustin Hahn's wholesale panel, not from a surplus-recovery payout. A speaker recounts an early deal where the seller reported a $70,000 mortgage. The speaker considered contracting at $100,000 and wholesaling at roughly $120,000, expecting about $20,000. The title company then uncovered a $30,000 child-support lien the seller had omitted, changing the deal. The panel also reports sellers forgetting second mortgages and contracts arriving without every person on title involved. Taren supplies the surplus-recovery version of the bottleneck: files have sat for months because one lienholder had not sent a payoff. Some liens later turn out not to exist anymore, increasing the potential remainder; other files shrink when the actual debt comes back. These accounts are different from Taren's explicit arithmetic illustrations. His $180,000 example falls to $55,000 after the stated second mortgage, HOA debt, and judgment; his $60,000 tax-surplus example produces nothing for an owner facing a $70,000 mortgage claim. The firsthand lesson is hidden debt and delayed payoffs; the worked examples show the economic direction without pretending they are completed recoveries or measured average results. Dustin Hahn [6IAi_pcEgFY @ 00:00:55]; Alfred Taren [LdemHPCk0dk @ 00:06:24]; Alfred Taren [SzfjJzM76ls @ 00:01:26].

Fullmer's demonstrated obstacle is a recorded Medicaid-related lien renewal with no dollar amount. The record confirms an interest but does not answer the question he needs answered: how much competing debt exists. He points to Healthcare and Family Services in Chicago on the document and recommends calling the recording entity with the case number or address to ask whether the lien remains outstanding and what it totals. Taren explains another reason a visible amount can mislead. Interest, penalties, fees, and lender advances for taxes or insurance can accumulate while public records lag; human data entry can introduce errors as well. His money-holder call establishes what is being held, not today's second-mortgage or HOA payoff. AFX Research supplies the reverse problem: a debt may have been paid without the lien ever being officially released. Its response is to contact the lienholder and ensure a release is filed once the matter is resolved. Dave adds that lien age, priority, and possible negotiation deserve inspection rather than assumptions. Together, these speakers identify missing amounts, stale amounts, and unreleased instruments as separate research problems. A document can be real while the economic conclusion drawn from its face is still wrong.
