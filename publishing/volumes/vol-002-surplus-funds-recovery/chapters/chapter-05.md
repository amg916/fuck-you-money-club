# Chapter 05: Mortgage Foreclosures and Sheriff-Sale Recovery

*Judicial and nonjudicial sales, PropertyRadar research, Texas and Georgia workflows, California and New Jersey guidance, and Philadelphia sheriff auctions.*

## Executive Summary

Chuck Panzarella, Bob Diamond, Josh Sternberg, and Lawrence Friscia agree that foreclosure can leave money for former owners, but debt, competing liens, and claim procedures determine what reaches them. Eric Richardson separates county-held judicial cases from trustee-held nonjudicial recoveries, using auction.com, PropertyRadar, and Excess Elite for research and outreach. His Texas example produced a $16,609 check request and a 30% success fee; his Georgia workflow checks auction results against debt records. Diamond reports charging 30–40%, while David Church describes 10–30%. Practitioners differ over attorney involvement and payout speed. New Jersey examples emphasize priority and service; Philadelphia auction operators and lawyers describe settlement deadlines, title exposure, and litigation that can interrupt a sale.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The common starting point is money left after the foreclosure debt is paid, not the fact that a house changed hands. Chuck Panzarella of Consumer Action Law Group illustrates the basic spread with a $300,000 loan and a $500,000 sale, leaving $200,000; reversing those figures leaves nothing to claim. Their actual verification sequence goes beyond that simple example: establish ownership through title, examine foreclosure records, and subtract the liens or loans paid at the sale [gUeG4eQdMy8 @ 00:01:24]. Josh Sternberg makes the additional debt visible in a California example: a $400,000 first mortgage, $100,000 second mortgage, and $50,000 tax lien total $550,000 against a $750,000 winning bid. The resulting $200,000 is what remains after those payoffs, not after the first mortgage alone [z6jQuGWdtDY @ 00:01:19]. Lawrence Friscia gives the New Jersey version: a $280,000 judgment and $340,000 sale create $60,000 in surplus, but the former owner does not receive an automatic payment [i8-qVMepHe8 @ 00:00:48]. These practitioners put the arithmetic beside ownership and payment procedure. Panzarella asks for proof of ownership; Sternberg works through the liens; Friscia distinguishes a surplus from an automatic check. Their examples all begin with a sale that actually brings in more than the stated debt.

The business itself is finding the money, reaching the person connected to it, and moving a claim through the relevant process. Hutch & Holmes names that sequence directly: find, inform, assist. Their starting assignment is to choose one state, establish what service the business will provide, and understand the filing process before chasing leads [L2oKpjOoh20 @ 00:00:36]. Bob Diamond describes the commercial version through a court registry list. The list can contain mortgage overages alongside appellate bonds and support payments, so their team picks out lender-versus-person cases, follows the docket number, and identifies the former owners. The outreach then offers help recovering identified money for a percentage [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:01:27]. David Church explains why that contact can matter: former owners have moved, a mailer arrives weeks later, and they do not know money remains. Their service prepares the paperwork and helps claim it [k0pvzqTug-M @ 00:00:44]. County Assets Recovery similarly describes pulling county records, identifying the rightful owner, building a documented claim, and handling filing and follow-up [siNLN4gsAEA @ 00:00:30]. Across these approaches, the service is not simply handing somebody a list. The speakers describe locating the claimant, explaining the recovery, assembling documents, and staying with the claim through the holder's process.

The first operational split is where the sale and its money are handled. Eric Richardson describes judicial mortgage foreclosures as county or sheriff matters with a civil case, a docket, and records that can show claims, disbursements, or excess proceeds. Their nonjudicial route instead leads to a trustee, often a law firm or large company, that conducts the sale and handles the money [kYJAnAiPgvc @ 00:00:39]. Diamond's mortgage workflow starts later, after the foreclosing creditor and other required items are paid and the excess is deposited with the court. Their research target is the clerk's registry, followed by the individual case [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:00:39]. Panzarella describes contacting the sale trustee and submitting a claim either to that trustee or to the court, noting that funds are generally deposited with the court after two or three months [gUeG4eQdMy8 @ 00:01:47]. The shared prerequisite is identifying the holder and the claim route, rather than treating every foreclosure as a county claim form. Richardson asks the trustee for a disbursement breakdown and its own claim form. Diamond uses the case number to reach the details behind a registry entry. Panzarella connects title and sale verification to the trustee before describing possible hearings and motions. Each route starts with records and ends with a specific claim process.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Richardson's Georgia mortgage workflow begins before the first-Tuesday sale. Create a free auction.com account, select Georgia and foreclosure homes, and heart the properties before the auction so the sale-result emails arrive afterward. Their instruction explicitly excludes bank-owned properties from that search [FJj3hN2KbIg @ 00:08:50]. For a property reported sold to a third-party bidder, Richardson opens foreclosure contact information to identify the attorney or trustee. They then use a free foreclosurebidlist.com account, select the county, and open the property report. In their worked example, the email reports a $427,000 sale, auction.com shows an opening bid around $185,661, and the second report shows a $179,281 remaining balance. Richardson uses the higher number, saying it could include additional interest and foreclosure fees [FJj3hN2KbIg @ 00:10:41]. This research sequence serves the same verification task Panzarella identifies: use foreclosure records to establish funds after loans and liens paid at the sale [gUeG4eQdMy8 @ 00:01:24]. Richardson continues with lien research and additional-mortgage checks rather than stopping at the auction spread. Their example shows a home equity line that was released. After the client signs an agreement, they contact the foreclosure attorney holding the money to verify the surplus and obtain the details needed to pursue it.

PropertyRadar is Richardson's research tool for the nonjudicial information gap. They say auction.com removes trustee information after foreclosure and that sale prices require the email notification, while PropertyRadar lets them retrieve trustee details later [XNxo6xvEJBg @ 00:01:13]. Their property report review covers deed transfers, purchases, loans, liens, transaction histories, and book-and-page references; some documents can also be pulled directly. Although the platform includes skip tracing and contact functions, Richardson says they use Excess Elite for those activities and PropertyRadar for due diligence and trustee identification. They also describe preforeclosure lists and alerts for properties sold to third-party bidders above the published bid. Their most expensive package is recalled as about $200 or $300 monthly, while basic research can use a basic plan [XNxo6xvEJBg @ 00:04:33]. Diamond describes a different research engine: request registry deposits, inspect the lender-versus-owner case, and use the docket to recover full party details [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:01:55]. The two workflows put different records at the center. Richardson needs the private sale handler's identity and the property's debt history; Diamond needs the court deposit and its case. Both move from an initial lead to the documents and people behind the money before describing a completed recovery.

The Texas case supplies Richardson's concrete execution sequence. A student, Joanne, brought a single-family-home lead from Excess Elite; the research checked the sale against mortgage debt and looked for additional obligations. The owners were divorced, so the recovery had to be split, and Richardson says the initially identified surplus was higher. Their first contact was an introductory email with proof, followed by an agreement sent through Rabbit Sign [7AHHRIbWG80 @ 00:02:05]. With the agreement signed, they contacted the trustee, attached that agreement, requested confirmation and a claim form, and supplied the requested company representation letter. The response authorized a $16,609 check request. Richardson reports correspondence from September 9 to September 11, with the check arriving September 12, and less than two hours of work [7AHHRIbWG80 @ 00:03:09]. Their reported 30% fee produced a little less than $5,000. Diamond's court route uses a different document: an attorney-prepared petition telling the sale story, the amount owed, the selling price, and the former owner's request for payment. They describe these petitions as usually six or seven paragraphs [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:03:07]. Richardson's trustee claim form and Diamond's court petition are separate execution paths, with different handlers and different documents.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Attorney involvement is where the operating models visibly diverge. Diamond's service signs the client and brings in an attorney to petition for court-held money; their reason is that the recovery operator cannot represent someone else in court [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:03:25]. Leonard Boyer of Lenny the Lawyer-Live takes a more specialist-centered position in New Jersey, describing precise, technical proceedings and saying this is not something just any attorney handles [dn1b-e6bUyo @ 00:01:13]. Richardson, by contrast, says the Texas case was completed without an attorney on their side. They distinguish a living homeowner's straightforward claim form from a legal motion or filing and describe their role as limited-power-of-attorney representation for inquiry, confirmation, and paperwork [7AHHRIbWG80 @ 00:04:14]. Hutch & Holmes makes the service boundary the first business-design question: determine how a claimant files without an attorney, what documents are required, where the claim goes, what fee and contract rules apply, and when legal work requires an attorney [L2oKpjOoh20 @ 00:01:52]. These are different workflows, not interchangeable instructions. Diamond is describing a court petition for another person; Richardson is describing the trustee's accepted paperwork in one completed Texas case. Hutch & Holmes also explicitly says notary status does not confer authority to represent someone in court.

There is no single payout clock in the practitioners' accounts. Diamond describes their mortgage recovery process as usually three or four months after the client is signed and an attorney petitions for release [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:05:29]. Richardson reports the Texas trustee inquiry producing a check cut within three days, with the case's correspondence and delivery dates illustrating that fast turnaround [7AHHRIbWG80 @ 00:00:55]. Yet Richardson's broader mortgage-versus-tax comparison says judicial mortgage payouts have typically been faster in their experience because a motion reaches a court date, whereas nonjudicial trustees sometimes drag their feet [ZJ74a_7vvzo @ 00:05:46]. Their separate judicial comparison also says some trustees pay faster than counties and may withhold information until 30 or even 60 days after sale. Fees vary just as visibly. Diamond reports charging 30–40%, leaving 60–70% for the former owner. David Church instead describes a 10–30% sliding scale and says very small recoveries may carry a higher percentage [k0pvzqTug-M @ 00:00:57]. The Texas example's 30% fee is a reported case outcome, while Diamond and Church describe their broader pricing practices. The speakers supply both quick trustee payments and slower trustee behavior, alongside a separate months-long court workflow. Their accounts do not settle on one universal speed advantage.

Sheriff sale is not a sufficient label for deciding whether the underlying matter is tax or mortgage. Richardson says to inspect the plaintiff: a county treasurer points toward a tax sale, while a bank points toward mortgage foreclosure [ZJ74a_7vvzo @ 00:03:01]. Philadelphia makes that distinction tangible. Shawn at bid4assets says the platform hosts both tax and mortgage foreclosure auctions for the sheriff, then gives a buyer workflow specifically for tax delinquent and tax lien auctions. Their settlement explanation gives winning bidders until the next business day's close to fund the remainder of 10% of the winning bid plus the 10% buyer's premium, followed by 15 days from auction close to fund the remaining balance [1BmnpBQHhAY @ 00:16:21]. Console Matison LLP describes Philadelphia clients missing a 10-day balance-payment period after a 10% deposit and pursuing litigation to complete the sale [7eeXOhOafjc @ 00:04:12]. Those stated payment windows differ. The platform walkthrough directs bidders to the settlement terms on the sale and individual auction pages; the law firm's account addresses a failed completion and a motion to rescue it. Both concern purchasers' auction obligations, while Richardson's plaintiff check concerns classifying a surplus lead. Neither Philadelphia buyer workflow is presented as a former owner's surplus-claim form.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Richardson's first mortgage-specific trap is treating the opening bid as the debt. They say it can instead be two-thirds of an appraisal, two-thirds of a final judgment, or an amount chosen by the trustee. Their remedy is finding the final judgment or final writ and reviewing the actual amount due [0pjP-Sr15PA @ 00:00:39]. Their next trap is accepting a statement that another creditor swallowed the surplus without checking payment. Richardson requests disbursement history because entitlement alone does not establish that the trustee actually paid that creditor. They ask what happens to the money if the entitled party never claimed it [0pjP-Sr15PA @ 00:02:30]. Sternberg adds two California distinctions. A property reverting to the bank as REO produces no surplus in their explanation because no winning third-party bidder supplied sale money. A second-mortgage foreclosure is different again: the first mortgage remains, and Sternberg says the former owner's debt requires attention even after ownership is lost [z6jQuGWdtDY @ 00:01:57]. Boyer likewise warns that selling above the mortgage balance does not settle availability because other creditors may be owed money [dn1b-e6bUyo @ 00:02:37]. These are the speakers' checks against a misleading gross spread: actual judgment, actual disbursements, the identity of the buyer, and the position of the foreclosing debt.

The file can also fail after money has been identified. Friscia names four recurring New Jersey problems: not serving every party, missing a lien whose holder then objects, lacking authority for a deceased owner's estate, and proposing amounts that do not match the court's records. The last problem can produce a rejected order; all four can delay payment [i8-qVMepHe8 @ 00:02:30]. Their estate explanation says the family usually needs authority from the Surrogate, while remaining funds belong to the former owner only after valid junior liens are addressed [i8-qVMepHe8 @ 00:01:24]. Richardson's Georgia research encounters a different dead end: existing excess-funds lists may include claims already made, and only some counties flag pending claims. They ask prospects whether somebody has contacted them or whether a claim has already been submitted, then use available court records to investigate [FJj3hN2KbIg @ 00:04:38]. County Assets Recovery warns about money eventually moving to the state, whereas Friscia says New Jersey's motion rule sets no fixed deadline but waiting risks competing claims, harder-to-find records, and eventual state transfer [siNLN4gsAEA @ 00:00:30]. The concrete bottlenecks are missing service, missing authority, missing liens, mismatched amounts, and an already-active claim—not simply whether a lead list displays a large dollar figure beside a name.

Philadelphia's buyer-side accounts show failures that occur around completion of the sale itself. Console Matison LLP reports seeing post-sale litigation at their office, including redemption attempts that can leave a purchaser seeking their money back from the city [7eeXOhOafjc @ 00:02:14]. Their clients also encounter missing tax-clearance certificates and missed balance payments. The described rescue route begins with intervention in the underlying case under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 2327, then a motion to complete the sheriff sale; the firm says an LLC needs a lawyer for that filing [7eeXOhOafjc @ 00:04:57]. Shawn's bid4assets walkthrough separately warns that failure to meet settlement terms means default and deposit forfeiture. Their bidder-on-deck process can offer the property to the next-highest bidder, who has 24 hours to accept and 15 days from acceptance to pay [1BmnpBQHhAY @ 00:13:42]. Recovery economics have their own bottleneck: Diamond calls signing people up the hardest part, emphasizing natural skepticism and the time needed to find people willing to accept help [lzU09FRqmhg @ 00:06:30]. The firsthand reports therefore include stalled purchases and stalled client acquisition, not just paperwork. Diamond's reported fee range sits alongside that sales work; the Philadelphia accounts place litigation and lost deposits beside the appeal of auction discounts.
