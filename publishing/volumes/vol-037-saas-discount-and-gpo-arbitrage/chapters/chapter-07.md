## Executive Summary

Operators across healthcare, private-sector, and public-sector GPOs agree on one engine: pool member volume, negotiate tiered pricing nobody could win alone, and get paid by suppliers. Una pitches free membership funded by a small slice of the supplier admin fee, with food savings averaging 18 to 22 percent; Educationleaves cites 20 to 25 percent average savings. Eric Bricker pegs the supplier-paid admin fee at 1.5 to 3 percent of sales and calls it a commission, while Mark Copeland calls the 3 percent the vig. Angelique Beslic breaks down the contract levers: binding letters of commitment at 70 to 90 percent compliance, flexible letters of participation, clinically documented carve-outs, and a 10 to 20 percent sandbox. The split: Una and OMNIA sell second-generation value, while PBM Insider, Beslic, and Copeland warn the fee stream makes the middleman the real winner.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The base mechanic is identical across every operator: a GPO aggregates the purchase volume of many members so each one buys at a price tier it could never reach alone. Una frames it as tapping billions in pooled buying power to land volume discounts members can never get on their own, then stresses the kicker that matters to a CFO: the price breaks arrive without the member spending more money [wRrnU1fw7P0 @ 00:00:24]. Educationleaves repeats the same pitch nearly word for word, adding that group sourcing motivates suppliers to hand members discounted pricing and that businesses save 20 to 25 percent on average across products and services [Nx4UI5uowcw @ 00:01:04]. Hospital Supply Chain Education ties the leverage to headcount, saying a GPO wins volume discounts, bulk pricing, and favorable terms because it represents a large number of hospitals and providers [f5FL9qjTeE0 @ 00:00:59]. MT Purchasing adds the compounding effect: every new member raises collective buying power, letting the GPO constantly renegotiate supplier contracts and push better pricing back to members [e9uItyw_9cI @ 00:00:45]. The operator takeaway is simple: the asset being sold is aggregated volume, and the price a member gets is a function of the whole pool, not its own order size or negotiating skill.

Practitioners also agree on who funds the machine: in most models the supplier pays, not the member who captures the discount. Una runs free membership with no commitments or exclusivities, explaining that built-in arrangements let supplier partners make money, deliver healthy double-digit value to members, and route a very small percentage of the admin fee back to Una [wRrnU1fw7P0 @ 00:01:10]. Eric Bricker of AHealthcareZ puts a number on that supplier-side payment in hospitals: roughly 1.5 to 3 percent of what manufacturers sell to member hospitals, labeled an administrative fee even though, in Bricker's words, it kind of sounds like a commission, with hospitals also paying membership dues to belong to Premier or Vizient [N0B0bUjLrUE @ 00:01:05]. Educationleaves lays out the full menu operators will meet in the market: administrative fees paid by vendors, participation fees paid by buying members, or both, set either as a percentage of purchases or as an annual flat rate [Nx4UI5uowcw @ 00:01:25]. MT Purchasing closes its pitch the same way Una does, offering membership for free with no commitments or purchasing requirements for any business that joins [e9uItyw_9cI @ 00:02:26]. The baseline expectation in the private-sector pitches: members rarely write a check, and supplier margin bankrolls the GPO.

The third point of agreement is that the discount is only half the product; the other half is reclaimed procurement time without surrendering control. OMNIA Partners argues that plugging in a prevetted, benchmarked agreement lets a team skip sourcing the category, move resources to the next one, and accomplish twice as much in the same time with the same headcount, bypassing in some cases hundreds of hours of sourcing work [iaBVYfYH_ro @ 00:00:52]. Una's team insists no GPO locks a member in: the buyer decides whether to run a single contract in one category or hand over the entire AP file and category stack for review [pKhFVbBzkz4 @ 00:00:01]. Una also kills the misconception that everything funnels through the GPO, telling members they keep owning the supplier relationship and every touch point while Una activates pricing on the back end [wRrnU1fw7P0 @ 00:01:49]. Intalere makes the same promise from the healthcare side, stating that GPOs do not sell products themselves and simply work with a member's current suppliers to get the lowest possible prices on items already being purchased [qlmtp85_1-Q @ 00:00:22]. The consensus prerequisite is that the member still has to show up, pick categories, and activate contracts; Intalere puts it bluntly: the more you connect with the GPO, the more you save.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The money flows on a percentage-of-sales rail, and the numbers line up across buyer and seller accounts. Bricker pegs the hospital GPO take at about 1.5 to 3 percent of manufacturer sales, and points out the structural tension: the GPO negotiates prices down while the same manufacturer pays it a cut of everything sold, and commission structures exist to incentivize sales [N0B0bUjLrUE @ 00:01:24]. Mark Copeland, who still sells medical devices and calls the fee the vig, runs the vendor-side math: sell a product to a hospital for $100 while on contract and you owe the GPO $3, and GPOs chase large spends because 3 percent of $100,000 is $3,000 [0F5PDJ2Sxds @ 00:04:55]. Copeland adds the hidden labor: vendors must keep track of sales tracings, submit them to the GPO, and manage the contract paperwork, a process Copeland calls a nightmare [0F5PDJ2Sxds @ 00:04:40]. Una describes the same rail from the member side, with suppliers funding double-digit member value and a very small share of the admin fee flowing back to the GPO [wRrnU1fw7P0 @ 00:01:10]. For an operator sizing a GPO play, the unit economics are blunt: revenue scales with throughput, which is why Copeland says GPOs are really good for gloves, cleaning supplies, and other commodities.

On the member side, the pitch and the paperwork are where the discount actually gets locked in. Una CEO Anthony opens skeptical buyers with a spend comparison: Anthony asks a procurement officer, CFO, or controller how much they run through office supplies, then contrasts a buyer doing a million against Una doing 200 million in the category, and lets that gap set the stage [N9511Ln5CfE @ 00:08:26]. Angelique Beslic then breaks down the two activation instruments in healthcare. A letter of commitment is a signed, binding, system-level agreement committing 70, 80, or 90 percent of category spend to a supplier in exchange for top-tier pricing and rebates, with contract-dictated penalties if compliance slips [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:13:40]. A letter of participation is a click-to-activate option in the GPO dashboard that can run by department or affiliate, with the tier assigned from the monthly spend reports the member already sends [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:14:42]. Carve-outs are a separate lever: in Beslic's Ethicon illustration, an 80 percent suture and endomechanical commitment can slip to 78 percent because a clinically proven bariatric exception, tested and accepted by the vendor, accounts for the other 2 percent [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:17:45]. Beslic stresses carve-outs are documented exceptions, not the leftover non-commitment percentage.

The final mechanics cover the 10 to 20 percent left outside commitment and how buyers squeeze it. Beslic tells members to hold compliance at 80, 80.5, or 81 percent rather than hovering at 78 or 79 without a carve-out, then route the uncommitted slice into trials, secondary suppliers, and third-party overstock resellers such as Surgery Shop, Surgery Stuff, Palm Harbor, and Omni Channel, which sell surplus hospital allocation below GPO tier pricing [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:22:06]. Beslic also pushes members to stack Vizient-style impact standardization rebates on top of ordinary vendor rebates [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:24:25]. Una runs its own trial mechanic: no long-term commitment, a handful of categories, and a 3 to 6 month test where the GPO has to prove itself every day [N9511Ln5CfE @ 00:05:56]. On the higher-ed side, Gonzaga purchasing manager Madison Seipel describes the Opus e-procurement tool through OMNIA Partners, where staff search multiple suppliers at once and pay the contract price with a P-card, which Seipel says gets products to campus faster [fdEV8yBr5eM @ 00:02:16]. Seipel adds that Opus surfaces contracts the university is not yet connected to, so staff can flag a product and have purchasing connect the contract. The pattern across every model: the contract only pays if someone actively activates, tracks, and fills the available tiers.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Map your GPO landscape
   - Calculate what percentage of your contracts sit on GPO paper versus local paper.
   - Beslic says every buyer should know that split, decide whether the GPO is the foundational strategic partner, and check membership in aggregate pharmacy, lab, or med-surg groups chasing deeper savings [Healthcare Supply Chain Hub: Angelique W. Beslic — K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:25:25].
2. Build a category recipe and trial it
   - Shortlist categories that fit, such as office supplies, shipping, or hotel travel, and test a few.
   - Anthony at Una pushes a dietitian approach over a butcher approach, building a plan around categories that fit, then testing a few categories for 3 to 6 months with no long-term commitment [Una — N9511Ln5CfE @ 00:05:56].
3. Choose LOC or LOP per category
   - Run scenario analyses on letters of commitment versus letters of participation.
   - Beslic tells buyers to spot-check product categories to see whether LOPs are leaving tier savings on the table because no commitment was signed [Healthcare Supply Chain Hub: Angelique W. Beslic — K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:27:04].
4. Track compliance, rebates, and admin fees monthly
   - Build compliance and rebate trackers in Power BI or Tableau and review them with suppliers and the GPO account executive.
   - Beslic and guest Heidi warn that volumes get misreported and that GPOs sometimes show a member not even participating in a contract, so check rebates and admin fees with the account executive on a regular cadence [Healthcare Supply Chain Hub: Angelique W. Beslic — K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:30:31].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over who actually pays for the discount. Una pitches membership as completely free, with no commitments or exclusivities, and says the supplier arrangement is built so the vendor still makes money, the member gets a healthy double-digit value, and a very small percentage of the admin fee flows back to Una [wRrnU1fw7P0 @ 00:00:47]. Intalere runs the same free-membership pitch, telling members the more they connect with the GPO, the more they save, across 1,400-plus contracts [qlmtp85_1-Q @ 00:01:02]. Dr. Eric Bricker of AHealthcareZ describes a different hospital-side reality: manufacturers pay Premier and Vizient roughly a 1.5 to 3 percent admin fee on what they sell, and hospitals still pay membership dues on top to be in the group [N0B0bUjLrUE @ 00:01:59]. Educationleaves lays out the three revenue models side by side: some GPOs collect administrative fees from the vendors they oversee, some collect participation fees from the buying members, and some do both, set either as a percentage of purchases or as an annual flat rate [Nx4UI5uowcw @ 00:01:25]. For an operator, the takeaway is that free to the member does not mean free in the chain; the fee rides inside the supplier deal, and the member's job is to confirm the net price still beats what they could get alone.

A second camp questions whether large buyers need the middleman at all. Bricker points to UPMC, which runs its own analytics AI company to scan every contract and flag which GPO arrangements are worth using and which they frankly shouldn't use, and to Intermountain Healthcare, which used to own a GPO, sold it, and decided to just do it themselves [N0B0bUjLrUE @ 00:06:33]. Mark Copeland, the Value Analysis Expert, argues the ground has shifted under the GPO's feet: HCA started HealthTrust as its own GPO and licensed it out, but the typical health system now runs eight to 12 hospitals plus three or four surgery centers and no longer needs that heft [0F5PDJ2Sxds @ 00:03:08]. Jim Lodge, the PBM Insider, goes further on the pharmacy side, saying plan sponsors are likely better off if their PBM avoids GPOs altogether and contracts directly with manufacturers, adding that nobody is probably going to tell you that [uyVsmq0w3FM @ 00:02:25]. Angelique W. Beslic refuses to pick a side but tells buyers to keep asking whether they even need a GPO, noting that some health systems and some suppliers are already dropping out of the GPO game [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:28:25]. Copeland adds that hospitals routinely tell vendors they can beat the GPO price with local pricing anyway.

The third fault line is how much engagement and commitment the GPO model should demand. Insight Sourcing Group says many GPOs take a fit and forget it approach, leaning on suppliers to drive implementation, and its data shows that produces lower compliance rates and a rising risk of pricing inaccuracies, leaving procurement reporting savings it cannot back with financial results [h348wGhEx-8 @ 00:01:06]. OMNIA Partners pitches the opposite: it does not stop once a contract is adopted, and its team keeps pushing members into new contracts to drive second generation savings [iaBVYfYH_ro @ 00:03:31]. Anthony, president and CEO of Una, frames the choice as butcher versus dietitian, where the butcher just wants to sell you meat and close the contract, while the dietitian builds an a la carte recipe category by category, then tests a few categories for three to six months with no long-term commitment [N9511Ln5CfE @ 00:04:14]. Beslic shows the commitment trade-off is real money: a letter of commitment is binding at the system level and unlocks top-tier pricing and rebates at 70, 80, or 90 percent of spend, while a letter of participation is a dashboard click that is flexible but only pays the tier your reported spend qualifies for [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:13:40].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap Beslic describes is the year-end surprise: the vendor walks in and says you did not make the rebates last year, while your own figures showed 81 percent compliance, which is why every committed contract needs monthly compliance tracking with finance and your analyst, ideally in a joint monthly meeting with the supplier [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:23:27]. Heidi, a guest coming from both the distributor and GPO side, adds that volumes sometimes do not get reported accurately, especially when part of the inventory is ordered direct because of a back order or allocation, so tier qualification has to be checked against the reports sent to the GPO [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:29:54]. Beslic has also seen the GPO admit it did not even show the member participating in a contract, meaning no rebate and no admin fees were flowing [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:30:31]. Insight Sourcing Group describes the same leak at the program level: unchecked compliance and pricing inaccuracies put procurement professionals in a tight spot where they have reported against savings targets but may not have the financial results to support it [h348wGhEx-8 @ 00:01:21]. The economics check is simple: savings not reconciled monthly against the GPO spend reports are savings on paper only.

On the supplier side, Mark Copeland calls the GPO listing a trap for small innovative med device sellers. Getting on a contract usually means a 3 percent administrative fee, the vig, so a $100 sale owes the GPO $3, plus sales tracings to submit and tie contracts to manage, which Copeland calls a nightmare, frankly [0F5PDJ2Sxds @ 00:04:22]. Worse, listing next to gloves and cleaning supplies tells buyers you are a commodity, nobody at the GPO sells your product, and walking in with we are on your GPO still earns the reply that you are not approved and must go through value analysis, often followed by an offer of better local pricing [0F5PDJ2Sxds @ 00:05:58]. Copeland has watched sellers show up only to hear the hospital switched GPOs last year and is now on Vizient [0F5PDJ2Sxds @ 00:07:33]. Bricker reports the structural version of the same complaint: GPOs get accused of pay-to-play shelf space, where manufacturers pay or offer much larger discounts to win placement, leaving small and newer manufacturers without the volume or budget to get on the shelf [N0B0bUjLrUE @ 00:05:24]. The net effect is that a GPO contract can raise a small seller's cost of sale without adding a single approval.

The incentive trap runs through every fee model. Jim Lodge reports that PBM-owned GPOs pocketed 7.6 billion dollars in fees in 2022 alone, and because those fees are not technically rebates, PBMs can tell employers with a straight face that they pass through 100 percent of rebates while never disclosing the GPO take, booked through entities domiciled in places like Ireland and Switzerland [uyVsmq0w3FM @ 00:01:47]. Bricker flags the same misalignment in hospitals: because the GPO earns a percentage of what the manufacturer sells, it financially wants the manufacturer to sell as much as humanly possible, even if per-unit cost drops [N0B0bUjLrUE @ 00:05:57]. Beslic's member-side trap is self-inflicted: carve-outs are not a free pass, and they are not the leftover percentage outside the commitment, and too many carve-outs equal lost leverage and weaker contracts, so you have to play in one field and cannot have your cake and eat it too [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:03:01]. The compliance version is hanging around 78 percent on an 80 percent commitment without a documented carve-out, which risks both contract penalties and rebate loss; Beslic's target is to sit at 80 to 81 percent and stay fair to the vendor [K2Slgt-y1wo @ 00:21:21].
