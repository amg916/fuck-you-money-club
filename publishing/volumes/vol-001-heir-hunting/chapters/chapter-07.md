## The Practitioner Consensus

The common starting point is not a surname match; it is evidence connecting a person to the money. Eric Richardson starts deceased-owner work by verifying death, then searching for an obituary and matching its relatives against skip-tracing results when several people share a name. Their example of a generic John Smith search is explicitly a reason to cross-reference rather than grab the first obituary. The California investigator describes the same identity problem from a failed recovery: a client could establish their identity but could not establish that they had worked for the employer associated with the funds, and eventually abandoned the claim. Ohio's documentation team draws the same distinction between knowing someone's name and establishing ownership. The team asks for identification plus a reported address or business relationship, explaining that holders sometimes supply an address rather than a Social Security number. For a deceased relative, Ohio still wants evidence that the deceased person owned the funds before moving to the claimant's relationship. Richardson's obituary work, the investigator's employment problem, and Ohio's ownership documents therefore address different parts of the same identification job: the deceased owner, the particular asset, and the person seeking payment. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

California's two heir forms have different instructions, including different handling of property identifiers. For the Declaration Under Probate Code 13101, the Controller's Office directs the claimant to enter the deceased owner's name, place of death, and date of death from the death certificate. Section 3 distinguishes the absence of California estate proceedings and appointed representatives from written consent by the personal representative. Section 5 requires every Property ID being claimed; an attached list is allowed, but a Claim ID is not acceptable there. Section 6 distinguishes a beneficiary or direct relative from someone acting in a listed representative capacity. The claimant signs, prints their name, and dates the declaration; the tutorial says no notary is required. The Table of Heirship, by contrast, asks for one Property ID when multiple properties are involved, followed by the family entries; additional names can go on another table. Emend The Mass Media Group demonstrates the surrounding portal sequence: choose the claimant relationship, enter contact information, answer the finder question, sign electronically, and upload documents. Their demonstration ends with a documentation confirmation and a separate status lookup using the Claim ID. The forms' Property IDs and the portal's tracking identifier serve different tasks. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest change is inside Ohio's own guidance. In the older webinar, the presenters say state unclaimed funds remain available forever and reject the suggestion that they eventually move beyond the owner's reach. In the February 2026 webinar, Susie Wagner instead describes House Bill 96, says everyone has until 2036, and explains a ten-year period from the reported date, labeled the receive date on the claim form, unless a valid claim has been made. Those messages are materially different; the newer presentation supplies the deadline discussion rather than repeating the older forever language. Notarization also changes: the older webinar describes a threshold over $1,000, while the newer presentation says the total must exceed $3,000. Pennsylvania presents another distinct route rather than an Ohio-style requirement: eligible online claims can avoid a paper claim form, and claimants can choose direct deposit. Its presenter still tells claimants to complete a PDF when one appears. The useful comparison is therefore specific: Ohio changed its stated holding period and notary threshold, while Pennsylvania demonstrates a payment and paperwork branch that depends on eligibility and the screens returned for the claim. Neither presentation describes a universal state filing package. [GrE0UUh_3BI @ 00:21:32] [GrE0UUh_3BI @ 00:29:55] [8nmo4S1v3Co @ 00:07:17] [eRjGqaIOHWI @ 00:00:26]

Deceased-owner routing produces a different contrast. Sean Buju of SurplusFundsRiches pushes deed-first investigation instead of immediately chasing an extended family. Their worked example uses Barry and Elizabeth Hurley, joint tenants with survivorship, and compares the death date with the foreclosure date. In Buju's example, a foreclosure before Barry's death leaves heirship work for Barry's portion; a foreclosure after the death lets Elizabeth claim the whole surplus. Buju also makes broad claims about community-property states and directs attention to the administrator when a trust owns the property. Richardson's deceased-claims lesson instead starts with death verification, an obituary, and family questions, then distinguishes full probate, summary administration, and an affidavit of heirship according to the surplus, existing estate documents, and the holder's requirements. Their California mortgage case followed the probate route: the client had no will, trust, or established probate, so the team opened probate and reported a nine-month recovery. These are alternative investigative sequences and different case facts, not two accounts of the same payout. California's Controller tutorial adds another distinct path: the 13101 declaration is used where probate was not required or where the personal representative has provided written consent for the claimant to receive the property. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Church's collection bottleneck was self-created and specific: they initially told clients to send one document and find the others later, then accumulated papers arriving through different channels. They describe roughly twenty documents and the resulting sorting process as chaos. Their fix was the checklist and coordinated notary return, not simply more reminders after the file became incomplete. Richardson describes another firsthand correction from an early Florida case. They prepared a summary administration themselves, submitted it, and received a judge's response advising legal counsel without identifying the errors. Richardson subsequently favors having the attorney handle that work, while still describing situations where holder instructions permit direct filing. Their newer document lesson adds a reported student bottleneck: Christian's $138,000 deal remained stalled for more than a year because a death certificate was missing. That is Richardson's account of a student's case, not Richardson's own recovery. Ohio's documentation team describes the same operational drag from the examiner's side: missing documents require emails, calls, or letters, and that communication extends processing. Across these accounts, the delay has an identifiable source: fragmented collection, an unsuccessful filing attempt, or absent evidence. The reported remedy addresses that missing piece rather than promising that every claim will pay quickly.
