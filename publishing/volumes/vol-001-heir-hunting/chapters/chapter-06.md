## The Practitioner Consensus

Christa Cowen makes the source citation part of the investigation, not decoration added after a tree looks finished. Creating it forces questions about who made the record, why it exists, when it was created, and how much weight its information deserves. A cemetery marker placed sixty years after a death gets different treatment from one placed immediately afterward. Diana Elder puts the same work inside a defined sequence: objective, analysis of existing research, locality research, research plan, logged searches, and written conclusions. The citation is created during the search, while the source is open, rather than reconstructed when a report is due. Rebecca Coford calls the log a diary of the entire project, including successes, failures, expectations, and ideas. A genealogy program or online tree does not replace it because those usually show what was found, not the searches that failed or the same-name people excluded. Together, these practitioners demand more than an attached image: identify the record, explain its use, and leave enough detail to recover the work. Elder then reuses the citation in a database, online tree, shared document, and report rather than writing a different trail from memory for each destination. Cowen likewise connects the citation process to avoiding records that belong to somebody else. [7M3XZfN7814 @ 00:09:24] [DUAbGD8rVzA @ 00:04:25] [PfqiDmLnMIg @ 00:01:34]

Shannon Green's credibility routine starts with a citation, then examines the source and its information separately. Was it an original, a derivative, or an authored work? Who probably supplied the information, and what could that person actually know? Calling something derivative is useful because it points the researcher toward the original, not because the label itself settles the case. Ancestry's vital-record presenter makes the distinction concrete with James Thomas Payne's death certificate: it can be strong evidence for the death while providing a birth date reported long afterward by someone probably absent from the birth. Cowen makes the complementary point that a death certificate supports more than a death event. It may also support name, gender, birth details, parents, spouse, and burial. Restricting its image to one fact limits that usefulness. Green organizes statements chronologically and tracks relatives and associates, especially when several people share a name. The working agreement is to evaluate the information item, its informant, and its connection to other records, rather than treating every statement on a document as equally strong. The record remains a source for several questions, but each answer still needs its own analysis. A polished tree and a familiar surname do not perform that analysis for the researcher. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Elder builds layered citations by answering five questions: who, what, when, where is, and where in. The digital collection has a creator and location; the underlying record or microfilm has its own identity and locating details. Keep each layer together, separate layers with semicolons, and avoid repeating information already supplied adequately elsewhere. A collection title alone does not identify the individual's entry. Add the person, relevant event, and the waypoints, page, image, volume, or certificate detail needed to reach it. Ancestry's vital-record presenter follows a compatible template: responsible entity when applicable, database title, medium, website, URL and access date, identifying information, and the original-source details used for evaluation. Tanner demonstrates the image-finding side in the FamilySearch catalog. Search the place, descend through localities to church records, choose the appropriate record type and book years, then move through image numbers to bracket the date. Image 90 reaches 1852; image 65 reaches July and August 1848; the preceding image supplies the April entry. Tanner checks the birth and christening dates, parents, and locality before selecting the tree person and adding the parents. The attachment includes a descriptive source title and an explanation of the match, followed by reloading the person page to check the link. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Coford does not prescribe one research-log layout for every case. The date-searched style records work in the order performed and fits a straightforward investigation. The record-category style groups searches under headings such as marriage, land, probate, or tax records and becomes a checklist when jurisdictions and record types multiply. The plan-and-log style places findings under individual search ideas and can be rearranged as the investigation changes. Coford explicitly warns that this last format can become too narrow. Elder's citation practice is flexible in a related but separate way: an indexed record may be easy to relocate with a basic website URL plus the collection and identifying information, while another source warrants the complete record URL. The question is how somebody will get back to that entry, not whether every citation has the same URL length. Coford personally favors Word because it is familiar, searchable, easy to print, and easy to reformat, but also permits paper and other familiar software. The contrast is between organizational choices, not competing claims that one style discovers all answers. Every version still records positive and negative searches, citations, questions, and analysis. Elder likewise allows judgment about citation presentation while requiring enough source-specific detail to recover the record and understand what was actually consulted. The sharpest disagreement comes at the end of graph deduplication. Johannes merges the demonstration's duplicate nodes after creating same-as links. Tomas pushes back on treating that final merge as necessary: leave the relationship in place, allow a domain expert to review it, or retain it permanently. Merging can lose information and make the original mistake hard to recognize later. Tomas also describes researchers choosing a high threshold because they preferred missed merges to incorrect merges, whereas Johannes deliberately uses a low threshold in a small constructed example. The NODES presentation offers another representation choice: store resolution as an attribute, create an empty linking node, or create a fused node with consolidated attributes. Selection depends on access and traversal requirements. Its resolution methods also differ: WCC groups connected nodes broadly, while the weighted hierarchical method separates more strongly connected communities. Those are alternatives within the demonstrated system, not interchangeable labels for a single result. Johannes proposes narrowing candidate groups before asking an LLM for further analysis, but says they have not personally worked with that combination. Tomas considers an LLM decision step potentially more automated and more expensive. Neither presentation supplies a universal similarity threshold that turns every related pair into the same person or company. Jennifer distinguishes forensic investigative genetic genealogy from forensic genealogy generally: probate work can fall under the broader field without involving DNA. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Green received hints assigning Mary Taylor the same parents in more than a dozen trees. Checking those claims showed that the proposed father was twelve when Mary was born, the proposed parents married nine years afterward, and their location did not fit Mary's Pennsylvania birth. Popularity had not repaired the unsupported relationship. Green's David Durst investigation went the other way: a widely copied, unsourced biographical sketch contained much that could be verified. Separating its statements, sorting them into verification buckets, and adding citations produced a stronger sketch and identified an omitted child. Elder encountered a different failure: the microfilm had skipped the very page needed for a project. Because the reference included the page number, the county clerk could locate the original book, photograph the page, and send it. Cowen reports discovering previously missed evidence while revisiting older citations created with less care. The firsthand reversals are specific: copied trees can repeat a false family, an unsourced narrative can contain recoverable evidence, a digitized sequence can omit a page, and an old citation can conceal an incomplete reading. None of these cases is solved by merely collecting another image. The researchers return to the statements, identifying details, original records, and reasoning that establish what the material actually supports.
