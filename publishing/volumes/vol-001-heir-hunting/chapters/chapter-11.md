# Chapter 11: Heirs' Property, Tangled Title, and Ownership Checks

## Executive Summary

The practitioners separate inherited ownership, recorded title, and mortgage liability. Their common starting point is the acquisition deed, the complete family tree, and the debts and conditions affecting the property. Richardson searches owner names; the Clerk-Recorder distinguishes deeds from deeds of trust; Reynolds reconstructs families through archives. Gibbs quotes $100–$150 for preliminary professional searches, while Nicoletti usually quotes four to eight weeks for Florida summary administration. Fullmer pursues difficult interests when the equity supports curative work; housing and agricultural practitioners emphasize retention, shared governance, and successor servicing rights. Goldovansky negotiates governmental claims that Fullmer often avoids on a short holding cycle. Their failure stories expose hidden relatives, wetlands, municipal charges, missing originals, and probate orders that still do not deliver an insurable closing.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with what the family owns, not who sounds most entitled to speak for it. Rusty Rumley tells families to establish the actual acreage and last clear-title owner before tracing every potential co-owner; a person called an uncle or cousin may not have the relationship the label implies. Apparent boundaries can also differ from ownership. Rumley's example is deliberately concrete: ten owners of one hundred acres do not each possess a particular ten-acre corner. They share undivided interests in the whole property [whDzDrFNADQ @ 00:14:28]. Mary Henderson and Annette Hayyatt make the same distinction with a three-percent interest in one hundred acres: the interest reaches the entire parcel, rather than creating three separately owned acres. They start with deeds and estate files, including wills that can themselves create shared ownership among children [OODJe43FyEs @ 00:10:14]. Jonathan Reynolds applies that principle to Jarrett, the relative living in, maintaining, and paying taxes on an inherited house. Those acts do not erase siblings, nieces, nephews, or remote cousins. Reynolds describes ownership acts as presumed to benefit all owners. The baseline investigation therefore follows the ownership branches rather than treating occupancy, upkeep, or family recognition as a substitute for the chain of inheritance [59C1meISvlc @ 00:11:08].

The recorded-document search and the family investigation belong together. The San Luis Obispo County Clerk-Recorder demonstrates a name search using last name followed by first name, without a comma, and warns against adding an uncertain middle name. In the results, the relevant deed entry is not interchangeable with a deed of trust; grantor and grantee identify the transferring and receiving parties [EDl18zX1-F0 @ 00:00:52]. Jamel Gibbs then demonstrates continuity in actual transactions: the seller transfers to the trust, and the trustee later transfers to the buyer. Their Peterson Avenue example includes a purchase-money deed of trust, so the ownership transfer and financing document must be read as different parts of the history. Gibbs reports a clean chain in that example, not a shortcut around examining the instruments [TWPIHeWyybg @ 00:10:21]. Reynolds begins Louisiana research with the assessor's tax card and its references to the family's acquisition deed in parish property records. When an address or owner's name is unavailable, the Orleans assessor's map can locate a known neighboring parcel and then the target. That establishes the property record from which the heir tree can grow; it does not replace tracing subsequent transfers or identifying the people who inherited [JPgk0Y7ZzuY @ 00:13:00].

For an inherited mortgaged home, Sarah Bowling Mancini and Lisa Sitkin split intake into borrower status and ownership. The promissory note creates personal repayment liability; the recorded mortgage or deed of trust supplies the security interest. Someone may have signed the security instrument without signing the note, and the note itself ordinarily will not be in deed records. Closing participation and statement names are therefore insufficient substitutes for checking the documents [JsPL1vXcgCI @ 00:03:52]. In their tangled-title training, the presenters explain that a covered successor can stay and pay without assuming personal liability. Confirmation for servicing requires identity, ownership, and an eligible transfer; it is separate from assumption. Confirmed successors receive access to information and servicing procedures, while assumption adds a personal promise to pay. The inherited property still remains exposed to foreclosure if the secured obligation is not paid [ME2xl7cczH8 @ 00:38:36]. Carrie Davis places this work inside ordinary housing counseling rather than waiting for a death or foreclosure emergency. Wealth Watchers starts estate-planning conversations during prepurchase counseling, alongside tax exemptions, and sends intake to Three River Legal Services. Their shared operating premise is continuity: establish ownership, keep mortgage communication functioning, and prepare the next transfer before the household reaches another crisis [VFSnbIIfy-M @ 00:06:38].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Eric Richardson's low-cost opening move is to look for a county property information report or owner-and-encumbrance report, sometimes available through a tax-deed portal or case docket. It can identify the title holder, liens, and book-and-page references. Otherwise, find the county recorder and search the owner immediately before foreclosure by name, rather than assuming the property address will retrieve liens indexed against that person. Richardson quotes paid title searches from sixty dollars up to three hundred to five hundred dollars, and tells recovery operators to investigate lienholder rights and filing periods rather than abandon every encumbered claim [q95klfFm9mE @ 00:00:53]. Gibbs prices a preliminary professional search at approximately one hundred to one hundred fifty dollars, depending on the provider, and uses DIY work as screening before purchase rather than as their final clearance method [TWPIHeWyybg @ 00:03:24]. Alex Goldovansky adds the work that a county-only search can miss: township records, municipal violations, utility attachment, and auction redemption checks. Fullmer prefers outsourcing those searches rather than personally visiting finance, infrastructure, and city attorneys. They recall expedited thirty-year reports arriving in one or two days, compared with title-company waits of one or two weeks. The extra research addresses a different exposure, not merely a longer list of recorded mortgages [lKsfBAioCYU @ 00:16:44].

Build the family tree as an evidence trail, not a collection of matching names. Reynolds uses obituary notices, marriage indexes, court files, annual directories, cemetery records, and historical maps. Their Barlow/Bartow/Barto example shows why spelling variants need systematic searches: military records, a headstone application, a draft card, and death-index material supplied different spellings and family contacts. A familiar name can also recur across generations, so it does not by itself establish identity [JPgk0Y7ZzuY @ 00:32:59]. Nicole's Maryland workflow adds birth and death dates, marriages, divorces, remarriages, children inside and outside marriages, living relatives' contact information, and records of diligent search efforts. Their named tools include the family-tree handout, Family Tree Builder at myheritage.com, and the National Genealogical Society. Identifying an heir and actually locating that person remain separate tasks [TP6n8TBsCGs @ 00:39:15]. Rumley assigns families the research they are uniquely positioned to supply: Bibles recording births and deaths, photographs, and private papers that courthouse searches cannot produce. Title transfers, statutory proceedings, and entity creation belong to the formal resolution work. This division preserves the value of family knowledge without treating an oral family account as the complete ownership calculation. Unknown-heir notice and the eventual structure still depend on the property and family being addressed [whDzDrFNADQ @ 00:12:20].

Al Nicoletti's intake package is concrete: death certificates, deeds, and title commitments. Under contract, obtain the commitment from the title company; use Florida property-appraiser records to retrieve recent deeds and establish ownership and the number of probates. Their family calls ask about all relatives ever, not merely relatives still living or still in contact. One sister initially presented herself as the only sibling before acknowledging thirteen others [onebNbNQSBM @ 00:06:01]. Courtney Fricke and Nicoletti add parallel property diligence: examine mortgages, environmental problems, code enforcement, taxes, and auction exposure while working through the inheritance chain. Their four-waterfall-probate case had cooperative relatives and completed paperwork, yet died when wetlands costs emerged after filing. The execution sequence is therefore title work and supporting documents plus the property's other obstacles, before instructing the proceedings to begin [pQwwvYbUYrU @ 00:02:55]. For a household trying to retain a mortgaged home, Mancini and Sitkin run successor confirmation alongside that ownership work. Their written information request identifies the original borrower and loan, supplies available ownership and death or divorce documents immediately, and asks what remains necessary. The servicer should confirm, identify missing material, or explain rejection; the presenters challenge probate demands when ownership has already passed automatically [JsPL1vXcgCI @ 00:55:38].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Goldovansky and Fullmer disagree most sharply over how much governmental-lien work fits an investor's business. Goldovansky says nonmunicipal claims are negotiable and describes seeking a property release through a ten-thousand-dollar payment against a million-dollar IRS lien. Their approach uses standardized forms, a lower appraisal, and an actual homebuyer disposition; a property release can leave the owner personally liable. Fullmer generally pays governmental claims or avoids federal complications because their target conversion cycle is one hundred twenty to one hundred fifty days. They reserve longer projects for unusually strong value, and their flipping model does not fit every presentation Goldovansky proposes [lKsfBAioCYU @ 00:11:19]. Richardson takes a different recovery angle: the existence of a lien is not the end of a surplus claim. Investigate whether the holder has rights against the claim and whether the relevant filing period has passed. Their Florida tax-sale and mortgage-foreclosure periods are expressly different, and they tell operators to call or email the county [q95klfFm9mE @ 00:03:15]. Ted Thomas likewise separates canceled mortgages from other surviving liens and makes their IRS discussion conditional on county notice. These are different foreclosure, recovery, and holding-period questions, rather than a shared instruction to ignore government claims [xtdWFaJY8Xc @ 00:02:27].

Fullmer's acquisition playbook deliberately enters situations conventional buyers reject. They describe buying estate interests while probate is pending, including a New York eight-unit property valued around eight hundred thousand dollars for two hundred fifty thousand dollars. The package included an estate interest, deed, assignments, and executor-related papers; quiet title was proposed as a fallback, not reported as a completed court victory [onebNbNQSBM @ 00:18:36]. Henderson and Hayyatt start from a different objective: retaining family land and defining authority without necessarily transferring everyone into one owner's hands. Their menu includes tenancy-in-common contracts, representative authority, voluntary division, family-line pooling, LLCs, and trusts. One example keeps thirty relatives' one hundred acres intact for forestry or farming rather than cutting it into smaller parcels [OODJe43FyEs @ 01:01:16]. Matt Price similarly says resolution can preserve joint ownership, illustratively twenty-five percent each, when that meets the family's goals. Better documentation and agreements can support financing, assistance, and transferability without making sole ownership the universal endpoint [DG4Uhckk-fU @ 00:09:58]. The contrast is in the desired exit: Fullmer purchases interests and works toward a transaction, while the preservation practitioners may keep ownership shared and change the family's management and transfer arrangements instead.

Partition is another place where jurisdiction and family goals change the playbook. Reynolds describes Louisiana co-owners' ability to demand partition and explains why a house usually cannot be divided physically. In their January presentation, UPHPA adoption is prospective in Louisiana, not an existing protection to assume. The proposed pillars include notice, appraisal, buyouts, preference for physical division, and open-market sale [59C1meISvlc @ 00:39:46]. Nicole describes Maryland's enacted 2022 Partition of Property Act and stresses that a uniform model has no effect until a state adopts a version. Their explanation includes ownership valuation, non-selling co-owners' buyout opportunities, sentimental and economic considerations, and an open-market sale when division does not work [TP6n8TBsCGs @ 00:14:02]. Rumley's ten-owner example gives the remaining eight a chance to buy the two exiting owners' valued interests, but explicitly leaves tenancy in common in place after that buyout. An LLC, trust, or other entity may address future fragmentation. FSA relending can support buyouts, while a failed buyout and impractical division can still end in sale [whDzDrFNADQ @ 00:17:11]. These approaches offer different procedures and outcomes; they do not turn every partition into retention, erase existing debts, or make continued shared ownership self-managing.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Nicoletti's four-waterfall-probate failure is a sequencing problem, not an unusually hostile family. The inheritance chain was linear; the last estate involved a spouse and seven children; the family returned documents; and the investor explicitly authorized filing. The next day, the investor reported wetlands in a corner of the property and an asserted fifty-thousand-dollar resolution cost. The deal died after the legal work had begun, and Fricke confirms that initiated services still had to be paid [pQwwvYbUYrU @ 00:01:13]. Nicoletti also reports transactional delays that make a quick-probate headline a poor operating budget. Their longest transactional example lasted eight months as successive creditor claims produced repeated interruptions; that deal reportedly still made one hundred thousand dollars. Another eighty-thousand-dollar nonhomestead creditor claim destroyed the economics when the creditor would not accept a reduced distribution [onebNbNQSBM @ 00:34:14]. Henderson and Hayyatt identify another carrying-cost trap: tax-foreclosure attorney fees can equal or exceed the taxes, become immediately payable, and remove payment-plan options. Their intake therefore checks who receives the tax bill and whether it is actually current. A cooperative family and solvable inheritance chain do not cancel environmental costs, creditor exposure, or the expenses accumulating while resolution continues [OODJe43FyEs @ 00:21:06].

Goldovansky says their title business began after investment losses caused by an inadequate online title searcher. Their later warning is specific: county records can miss municipal exposure, including unpermitted work, utility balances, and violations accumulating outside the recorder's system. They describe Arizona trash or mosquito violations at one hundred dollars daily since 2020 and distinguish township-held records from Florida's recorded daily violations [lKsfBAioCYU @ 00:16:44]. Gibbs takes a more conservative acquisition posture: despite nearly twenty years investing, they still use title companies or attorneys, rarely buy with liens, and prefer cure before closing. Seller-finance buyers accepting defective title still face the cleanup when they need an insured resale [TWPIHeWyybg @ 00:22:23]. Thomas adds that a clean-looking parcel number says nothing about a building that burned or a neighborhood that will defeat resale. They insist on inspection or boots on the ground, comparable records, and a maximum bid established beforehand. Their auction screening narrows a large list before expensive research, but does not replace physical investigation. Thomas's point is not that every damaged property is useless: some need paint and cleanup, while others are abused or worthless. The operator must distinguish those conditions before bidding [xtdWFaJY8Xc @ 00:06:56].

The servicing trap is to mistake a paperwork demand for the ownership rule. Mancini and Sitkin describe successors repeatedly submitting death certificates, including an example of ten submissions, while communication remains blocked. Their response is to identify reasonably necessary documents, seek successor specialists or supervisors, and send an error notice describing what was already supplied. They also separate modification and assumption: those processes can proceed together rather than requiring assumption before an application [ME2xl7cczH8 @ 00:48:16]. Their earlier training exposes another false exit: deeding property away does not release note liability. They describe a successful divorce-related release after advocacy as unusual, and warn that adding owners cannot simply be reversed unilaterally. Reverse mortgages require a different analysis from ordinary stay-and-pay [JsPL1vXcgCI @ 01:11:19]. Carrie Davis and the housing panel identify delivery capacity as a practical bottleneck: funding access means little when too few providers can handle the work. They aggregate grants and build standing partner relationships [VFSnbIIfy-M @ 00:44:04]. Price likewise warns that complex family histories may demand sustained trust and experienced help, not a ninety-minute meeting. Resolution includes the time needed to gather people and documents, not only the act of preparing a form [DG4Uhckk-fU @ 00:30:37].
