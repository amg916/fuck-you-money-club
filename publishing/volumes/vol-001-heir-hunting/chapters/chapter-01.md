## Executive Summary

HeirSearch, Leslie Lawson, and Eileen O'Duill describe a business built on identifying the correct family, tracing living relatives, and documenting kinship. PBI adds a two-source relationship check and a second reviewer. The commercial models diverge: Lawson bills hourly, HeirSearch rejects percentage fees, Fraser and Fraser offers four charging options, and John and Char describe a one-third recovery commission. Filmed cases show the work behind those fees: surname variants, missing certificates, door knocks, uncertain assets, and beneficiaries who refuse to sign. Finders reports international property recoveries but also research written off when a will appears. Probate TV's counterweight is independent verification: official-looking references and telephone numbers do not establish that an inheritance letter is genuine.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The common job is to establish the family connection, not merely produce a promising name. HeirSearch starts its consanguinity chart with the decedent, checks for a living spouse and lineal descendants, then works through the parents and their descendants before moving farther back. The chart gives a generational title and a degree of relationship, so the researcher can identify which family unit is being examined. Moving upward is conditional: the previous search level must establish that there are no living heirs. Leslie Lawson describes the working version of that exercise for attorneys: identify parents, siblings, grandparents, and children as appropriate, then bring those lines forward to locate the people who must be notified. Eileen O'Duill describes the corresponding deliverable from Ireland as identifying the nearest next of kin and providing the documentation needed to prove kinship. These practitioners agree on a concrete output: identified relatives connected to the deceased through research and records. Their descriptions also separate the search from a casual family-history project. Lawson's client needs to know where the heirs are now; O'Duill's client needs evidence that establishes the relationship, not simply an interesting ancestral story. The first information often comes from people who knew the deceased, but the work does not end with their recollections. Hull & Hull starts with family and friends, asks them to help build the family tree, and describes Canada 411 and other internet searches before bringing in a researcher. Daryl Williams, speaking through Win-Win Realtors TV about California probate, likewise starts with friends and relatives. Williams adds letters to the deceased person's last known address, birth and marriage records, previous employers, internet searches, and social media to the search avenues. These are practical ways of finding additional children or other missing family members rather than simply repeating an existing list of names. PBI applies a similar insistence on establishing the underlying person before extending the search: its team checks the participant's name, Social Security number, birth date, addresses, and any supplied death date. If that information points to the wrong person, PBI returns to the client for another look at the data. Its relationship labels then distinguish one-source possibilities from relationships supported by two sources. Family contact supplies leads; identity checks and supporting records determine what the researcher can actually report with confidence. The profession also sells work that never resembles a televised race to sign an unknown cousin. Ryan Gregory lists family-tree verification, missing certificates, missing-will searches, missing-asset searches, and international asset transfers alongside beneficiary tracing. Gregory says Finders receives substantial private-client work from solicitors and estate representatives, as well as local-authority referrals and public-domain cases; the government list is only a small part of the mix. Lawson's own business illustrates a specialist route: most clients are attorneys, and the research begins with a request for every will and vital-record document already held. Lawson wants the full file because small differences in wording can open another family trail that the instructing attorney did not recognize. O'Duill puts the same emphasis on the finished work product when describing preparation for the profession: practice with friends and family, gain experience in repositories, write reports, have someone knowledgeable review them, and learn citation. The shared baseline is therefore research accuracy and an explainable record of the work. Gregory's broader service menu does not remove those fundamentals; it places them inside several different estate-administration tasks, from checking an existing tree to obtaining a document from another country. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The filmed James case turns the chart into a sequence of specific record checks. Researchers combine the mother's maiden name, Robinson, with the father's surname to search for siblings. They find a sister, Mary, then establish through a death certificate that Mary died in infancy. The next question is whether either parent remarried and produced half siblings; the team obtains the parents' death certificates as part of that inquiry. On the maternal side, researchers identify the grandparents and use the census to establish the mother's siblings. The 1911 record shows four children, while subsequent research identifies two more. That distinction matters within the actual search: the first family snapshot was not the whole family. The investigation eventually reaches Joyce's four children, including Philip Knight near the Wigan office. PBI describes a separate, more standardized relationship-checking operation. It gathers possible relatives, uses obituaries and marriage or divorce information, and labels a relationship possible when only one source supports it and confirmed when two do. Its review process also includes an initial reviewer and a second-person quality check. Both operations turn individual clues into checked relationships rather than stopping at the first plausible surname match. When the surname stops helping, change the record connection being followed. Shannon Freeman's team faced a large Smith family without reliable birth dates or places, with people changing names along the way. The breakthrough was an address repeated across the records. Instead of looking for more Smiths, the team looked for everyone connected to that property, finding marriages that referred back to the same house. The case ended with nineteen heirs on that side and seven already identified on the paternal side. O'Duill describes another elimination job: a Connecticut decedent left little information beyond a mother's name, and thirty-five women were possible matches. Persistent elimination identified the right mother and led to a half brother and half sister. Lawson's tools address the evidence-handling side of this work. Lawson describes using Snagit by TechSmith to capture online documents and prompt for a citation on the document image. Reusable report and citation templates then keep repeated work consistent. These are distinct practitioner techniques: Freeman changes the search anchor, O'Duill eliminates competing identities, and Lawson records the source while capturing the document. Each addresses a specific part of the research rather than replacing the entire investigation with a single database query. Outreach and recovery have their own sequence. David Wilkerson starts with public and private databases or court records, matches the asset record to a potential owner using identifying information, then contacts that person by mail, telephone, or email. After contact come the documents tying the claimant to the property and the preparation and submission of the claim. John and Char show the field version on the Lydia Matthews case. Before more door knocks, they visit the courthouse to check whether another researcher or an heir has already appeared and whether the money remains there. When a GPS search cannot resolve Milton's address, they ask locally and receive help finding the location. Their opening questions confirm Milton's connection to Anita before they explain the estate opportunity. Their commercial explanation is direct: a one-page agreement, expenses carried by the researchers, and a normal one-third commission paid when the beneficiary gets paid. These are the team's stated terms for its own recovery business. Wilkerson describes a different pricing range for unclaimed-property work, but both presentations place claimant contact between identifying the potential entitlement and completing the recovery paperwork. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The fee disagreement is explicit. HeirSearch quotes in advance from a set schedule based on the type of search and says it never charges a percentage. Lawson works strictly by the hour, explaining that clients should not pay for more research than they need. Fraser and Fraser instead presents beneficiary contingency as the appropriate arrangement when no next of kin is known and no administrator has been appointed. The firm researches first, seeks an agreement with the beneficiaries it locates, and gets paid from their benefit when distribution occurs. If it finds nobody, it receives no research payment. Its separate finder's fee contingency arrangement allows an executor, administrator, or trustee to agree the charge against the entitlement of each heir traced. Fraser says agreeing the fee before research usually produces a lower charge than beneficiary contingency and protects known beneficiaries from paying to find unknown ones. These are different commercial structures, not different names for the same invoice. One sells a specified search under an advance schedule, another sells research time, and the contingency versions depend on locating beneficiaries whose entitlements support payment. The available instructing party is central to Fraser's distinction between those arrangements. HeirSearch [aOXjjN53MT8 @ 00:02:55]; The Genealogy Professional Podcast [R5XtgGL0gkQ @ 00:22:49]; Fraser and Fraser [EPZo6YVIMi4 @ 00:00:35] [EPZo6YVIMi4 @ 00:01:27].

Even within paid research, a budget and a fixed price do different work. Fraser and Fraser's time-spent option begins with an agreed budget, but additional research may require another quote; the client controls budget increases and pays for work performed regardless of results. Its fixed-fee option generally starts higher, prices the agreed research and time frame, and can include a reporting date. Completion of the agreed research triggers payment even if the desired person is not found; failure to complete that research within the agreed arrangement means no fee. Ryan Gregory describes Finders using a budgeted fee basis for suitable professional instructions and invoicing below budget when the work costs less. Gregory also describes speculative commission work funded by Finders, including the possibility of a will appearing or an apparently valuable estate turning out insolvent. In the adjacent unclaimed-property business, Wilkerson gives a ten-to-forty-percent contingency range and says twenty or twenty-five percent is usually comfortable for their own work. That is Wilkerson's pricing position, not the fee schedule of HeirSearch, Fraser, or Finders. The speakers disagree about charging basis and risk allocation, and several offer more than one arrangement. Fraser and Fraser [EPZo6YVIMi4 @ 00:02:53] [EPZo6YVIMi4 @ 00:04:42]; My Lawyer In Spain [ywaYY4cPtAY @ 00:23:42]; Surplus Funds 101 [TTMtgtc31k4 @ 00:04:26].

Entry routes and search scopes differ just as sharply as fees. Ryan Gregory says Finders can recruit someone with no probate background and gives their own progression from joining at eighteen as an example. An interest in family history helps for research roles, while communication, teamwork, accuracy, and self-motivation matter for administrative positions. Lawson describes a different threshold for benefiting from forensic training: a strong intermediate genealogist, possibly without forensic experience, who can then assess whether the specialty fits. That is a contrast between joining a firm and preparing for specialist forensic work, rather than a single universal entrance requirement. PBI's BeneSearch also has a deliberately narrower target than a complete heirship tree: at least one first-degree relative, with the strongest emphasis on a spouse. For that service PBI includes parents, children, spouses, and siblings in its stated first-degree focus and does not center the search on aunts, uncles, neighbors, or friends. O'Duill's nearest-next-of-kin work can instead involve prolonged elimination, as the thirty-five possible mothers in the Connecticut case demonstrate. The operative difference is the assignment: a targeted relative search, a specialist kinship investigation, and an employed research role are not identical products or career commitments. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

A signed beneficiary and an attractive property do not settle the case economics. Danny Curran recounts a Battersea flat where Finders had already traced beneficiaries in several countries before gaining access. A will tucked behind a wardrobe left everything to a cat charity, and the firm's work was written off. In the filmed Hubbuck case, Ryan Gregory and Amy Moyes also searched the property for both assets and a will, openly explaining that a will could undo months of work. The asset search went the other way: bank statements showed £220,000 in a NatWest account and £320,000 in a Nationwide account, and the eventual estate was presented as £900,000 for Nigel and fifteen other heirs. Lawson reports the less spectacular but equally direct payment problem: researchers have sometimes gone unpaid because an estate ran out of money. Sophie Smith supplies a contrasting completed international case, involving two nieces in the UK, a Portuguese property sold for €2 million, and a Spanish holiday property, handled in about ten to eleven months. These accounts show different actual outcomes: a research write-off, unexpected assets, insufficient estate funds, and a completed cross-border property recovery. Boyes Turner [SLqhAWEXEv4 @ 00:17:21]; Ancestry [4F57BlTAWTk @ 00:33:11]; The Genealogy Professional Podcast [R5XtgGL0gkQ @ 00:23:27]; My Lawyer In Spain [ywaYY4cPtAY @ 00:15:35].

A family's confidence can conceal the very branch that changes entitlement. Curran reports repeated instructions framed around cousins being the only heirs, followed by discoveries of closer relatives, including previously unknown half siblings. The risk also appeared inside the Hubbuck investigation: the team worked the wider family while a possible half sibling remained unresolved, knowing that discovery could displace the cousins already contacted. The case also suffered from surname variants and delays obtaining certificates. These were specific operational problems, not abstract warnings about difficult research. Hull & Hull describes another bottleneck after searches have been performed: a judge may still want further work. Ian Hull gives the example of a judge asking for a Yahoo search after Google had already been searched, because the consequences of treating a person as unfindable are substantial. Daryl Williams likewise emphasizes detailed records of every effort so the personal representative can demonstrate the search to the California probate court. Their common pressure point is the unfinished or insufficiently documented step. Curran's missing relative changes who should receive the estate; Hull's further-search request changes whether the work is considered complete; Williams's records make the actual search efforts available for examination. Daniel Curran [AKvEFJHf5K0 @ 00:15:36]; Ancestry [4F57BlTAWTk @ 00:06:59]; Hull & Hull LLP [X-Q-bm1y9no @ 00:02:40]; Win-Win Realtors TV [U6LsR1gr0oA @ 00:04:47].

Contact can fail even after the research finds the right family. John and Char encounter Joy's refusal to sign immediately and request to let family review the agreement. Their later attempt with Jamie leaves John frustrated enough to leave that part of the Rodriguez pursuit for the time being. Ryan Gregory reports a more routine version internationally: people hang up because they think an unexpected inheritance call is a fake sales call, and letters may not arrive or may go to an old address. Probate TV explains why official appearance does not settle the question. Its demonstration uses letters with the same identifying information but dramatically different claimed amounts, and warns that real deceased-person details, case references, and apparently legitimate telephone numbers can be copied or spoofed. Its response is to check independently rather than rely on the telephone number printed in a suspicious letter. Meanwhile, Lawson describes an internal bottleneck that does not require any hostile recipient: getting absorbed in the hunt, losing focus, and giving away time. A time-clock program helped Lawson stay on task and record that work. The reported obstacles span beneficiary suspicion, unsuccessful signing attempts, unreliable contact information, and uncontrolled research time.
