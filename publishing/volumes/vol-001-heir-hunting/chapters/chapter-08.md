## The Practitioner Consensus

Eric Richardson's starting point is one county, understood well enough to explain its sales and claims without improvising. Find the upcoming sale schedule, learn where results appear, and ask the county directly when the website does not supply the list. Richardson names tax sales, sheriff sales, and foreclosures as places to begin, then distinguishes county-hosted results from outsourced auction results. The sale record is also something concrete to show a previous owner when explaining why surplus exists [FNbqvZGEzeE @ 00:00:38]. David Wilkerson takes the same specialization approach to prospecting: choose one niche and one state rather than spreading personal bandwidth across numerous states and different recovery businesses [XHOqG0CY1KA @ 00:13:10]. Wilkerson's Delaware discussion makes market knowledge part of the sales conversation. Knowing the process helps the investigator understand the claimant's account of where money came from and communicate expertise rather than simply announce it [TTfaOSBvLDc @ 00:03:51]. Richardson adds the work beginners often skip: check whether another claim has already been filed and whether liens affect the surplus. For judicial cases, Richardson describes using the foreclosure case number; for nonjudicial cases, contact the trustee. These are specific research tasks, not substitutes for learning the county's actual claim process [FNbqvZGEzeE @ 00:02:48].

The paperwork consensus is equally concrete: learn what the receiving office requires before presenting yourself as the person who can complete the recovery. Richardson tells operators to read the county's claim instructions and claim form, checking whether supporting documents include a deed, driver's license, or utility bill. If instructions are missing, email the county; Richardson explicitly describes the county as a party the business must work with [FNbqvZGEzeE @ 00:01:53]. Wilkerson separates two research jobs in California: establish whether registration is required, then establish how the state wants a valid claim package assembled. California requires registration in that walkthrough, while Wilkerson says some other states do not [_8vyoTSh7Rs @ 00:02:58]. For the claimant agreement, Wilkerson records property descriptions, account identification numbers, individual amounts, and a total when several accounts are involved. The claimant's current address matters because an old address can send the check to the wrong place and delay invoicing [_RTcaTMOwlY @ 00:10:38]. Simplicity does not mean replacing paperwork the state supplies. Wilkerson's instruction is to use state-drafted agreements when offered, reserving the simplified proprietary agreement for states requiring the investigator to provide one. The practical emphasis is an understandable agreement inside the state's stated process, with accurate account and contact information carried through the transaction [_RTcaTMOwlY @ 00:04:43].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Wilkerson's California registration example starts with an ordinary letter prepared in Google Docs. Include the date, personal name, business name and tax identification when registering through a business, address, telephone number, and email. Wilkerson says an individual can work in their own name there, and stresses using an address where incoming mail is accessible. Request acknowledgment, print, sign, date, and send the identification documents described in the walkthrough [_8vyoTSh7Rs @ 00:06:19]. Delaware has a different operating profile: Wilkerson reports a 10% commission cap, a 24-month period before a locator can assist, payment directly to the claimant, and subsequent invoicing by the locator. The walkthrough also describes digital submission of claim packages [TTfaOSBvLDc @ 00:05:14]. For New Mexico, Wilkerson reports a 48-month holding period but also reads an exception involving an attorney's letter. Their attempt to clarify that exception by contacting the department had not resolved whether a power of attorney would work [6yIKsJMj6Gk @ 00:05:30]. New Mexico also means manual searching and aggregation rather than the bulk leads Wilkerson wants for scrubbing, skip tracing, and dialing. The state supplies a disclosure agreement while requiring the investigator to provide a claimant agreement. Those are separate documents in Wilkerson's description, not interchangeable versions of the same form [6yIKsJMj6Gk @ 00:06:41].

Richardson's contact workflow begins by sorting numbers rather than expecting the first skip-trace result to be correct. Dial every listed number, cross out disconnected lines, and text the remaining live numbers, 'Hello, is this John?' Remove wrong numbers, then work the identified contact through a call, voicemail, and text. The voicemail names the caller, company, and property address without discussing money; Richardson also applies the system to relatives appearing in the skip trace [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:03:05]. On the live call, verify the first name, introduce yourself and the company, identify the property, and ask whether the person owned it. Anchor the foreclosure event, then ask whether anyone has contacted them about surplus funds. Richardson's first-call objective is an email or mailing address for proof, not an immediate close [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:06:34]. After sending the email, text that it has arrived and follow up within 10 minutes to walk through it while the conversation is still fresh [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:09:03]. Wilkerson supplies the listening mechanics: lead with questions, let the claimant do most of the speaking, and use prompts such as 'Why?', 'How come?', and 'What else?' Their purpose is to learn the claimant's situation and retain useful details for later conversations, rather than deliver an uninterrupted sales speech [XHOqG0CY1KA @ 00:03:43].

Wilkerson's basic CRM routine is deliberately manual: take handwritten notes during the call, stop the dialer afterward, enter the information, and create a follow-up timer. Their aggressive scheduling preference is to halve the callback interval requested by the lead; a requested week becomes a shorter follow-up interval in their system [ApkuK7OOL9w @ 00:02:37]. For software, Wilkerson names HubSpot and Salesforce as accessible starting options, says they offer free versions, and reports personally using upgraded HubSpot. Conversation logs make it easier to return to a customer's history and maintain a consultative conversation [Gk0D4wVEXmc @ 00:04:05]. The paid email example is G-Suite at roughly $12 monthly, with Wilkerson describing approximately $50 total for four seats in that discussion. The same presentation recommends a quiet work area and basic note-taking supplies, not a complicated office buildout [ES6y4iF-bBM @ 00:05:39]. For signing coverage, Wilkerson uses Las Vegas as the working example: organize lead ZIP codes, divide the metro north-to-south and east-to-west, and recruit notaries within the resulting four quadrants. Travel time affects their quotes, and nearby coverage shortens the gap between a claimant saying yes and signing. If local contacts cannot respond, Wilkerson calls other notaries already in the network [Qn63DtrxFts @ 00:00:33].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Fee presentation is a sharp contrast between Richardson's telephone advice and Wilkerson's written agreement demonstration. Richardson tells callers to discuss the percentage rather than the dollar fee. Their example is 25%, described as covering the legal claim, filing fees, and attorneys; Richardson argues that naming a dollar amount makes the claimant fixate on what the operator receives instead of the recovery [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:12:40]. Wilkerson's agreement demonstration explicitly shows the arithmetic. For five hypothetical accounts totaling $10,000, the example multiplies by 20% and writes $2,000 as the operator's compensation. Wilkerson says to use the state's cap or the amount the claimant agreed to pay, and the demonstration includes a 180-day agreement period [_RTcaTMOwlY @ 00:14:33]. Their Delaware walkthrough reports a 10% cap and treats the ability to obtain bulk leads as more important to market selection than the percentage alone [TTfaOSBvLDc @ 00:05:14]. Their New Mexico walkthrough reports no commission cap, but Wilkerson personally suggests not exceeding 40% or 50%, describing concern about how an excessive amount might be viewed if a dispute reached court. That is Wilkerson's stated business preference, distinct from the reported absence of a cap. The two speakers therefore present different fee examples, different presentation tactics, and different state-specific conditions [6yIKsJMj6Gk @ 00:03:26].

Richardson offers direct, balanced, and mysterious call styles rather than one compulsory opener. The direct version goes straight to business; the balanced version adds one 'How are you doing?' before moving into the property. The mysterious version withholds the amount, describes an open claim, and asks permission to investigate further. Richardson says that version can produce signed agreements faster but also creates more skepticism [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:08:28]. Their standard first-call destination remains permission to send information: obtain an email address or, for someone without email, a mailing address for a physical packet [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:07:39]. Wilkerson's closing discussion advances to an appointment choice after summarizing the conversation and explaining the contract and notary process: ask whether Monday afternoon or Monday night works better. That is a signing-oriented close rather than Richardson's initial proof-delivery objective [XHOqG0CY1KA @ 00:10:50]. Wilkerson also teaches a staged response to complaints: initially ignore a passing complaint, mildly address a repeated one, and fully resolve it when raised a third time. At that point, Wilkerson treats it as an actual objection. The distinction is explicit in their teaching: a casual expression of dislike does not automatically mean the claimant cannot or will not proceed [t4w_wFimZ9A @ 00:05:24].

Wilkerson's software advice contains two operating paths rather than a single spending rule. Their selection criteria are reliability first, ease of use second, and value relative to price third. Clear telephone audio, saved CRM entries, and a platform assistants can learn matter more to Wilkerson than unused features [Gk0D4wVEXmc @ 00:02:07]. Yet the basic manual workflow is not how Wilkerson runs the more developed operation: they describe automation pushing dialer information into the CRM and argue that people willing to invest in time-saving tools get results faster. Their claim that fully manual operators can take 20 times longer to reach a first deal is part of that argument, not a reported comparison table [ApkuK7OOL9w @ 00:03:27]. Richardson draws a different automation boundary: use AI for preparation and follow-up, while retaining human conversations for trust-building with homeowners [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:15:21]. Wilkerson's own scheduling alternative came from working in door-to-door sales while starting asset recovery. Only Saturdays and Sundays were available; after initially expecting poor availability, Wilkerson came to favor weekend calling, especially Sunday. The full-time-job playbook is to fit prospecting around the available schedule and keep making calls, not wait until employment or family obligations disappear [JmuqGo9wYkg @ 00:00:50].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Wilkerson's contract simplification came from losing an actual real-estate opportunity, not merely preferring short documents. An apparently receptive owner had a small house on a large lot in an attractive area. Wilkerson arrived with the eight-page RE21 purchase agreement, watched the owner's demeanor change, and left without the transaction. The following day's conversation centered on frightening fine print and possible adverse outcomes. That experience prompted the search for a simpler agreement [_RTcaTMOwlY @ 00:01:54]. Their CRM insistence has another firsthand origin: sticky notes everywhere, difficulty tracking conversations, and lost deals and money through disorganization [ES6y4iF-bBM @ 00:01:40]. Wilkerson also identifies a personal telephone weakness: a naturally loud, raspy voice became louder with excitement and could be perceived as aggressive even without that intention. Their adjustment is a calm, light tone rather than pushing harder [XHOqG0CY1KA @ 00:06:35]. Richardson describes the corresponding rebuttal trap: when someone suspects a scam, stacking explanations, promises, or a lower fee can make the caller sound less credible. Their response is a short answer and proof, not an increasingly elaborate speech. Richardson particularly warns that long explanations confuse older clients, after which they may hang up and block the number [DMdtmrWAO6c @ 00:11:16].

Wilkerson treats cancellations as a production issue to examine, not automatic proof that the business has failed. Their teaching comparison includes a low-output person with no cancellations, a stronger middle performer with one cancellation, and a high-output person with 12 apparent wins but 10 cancellations. These are explicitly scenarios; Wilkerson criticizes the last pattern because the sales conversation may not explain the process and terms thoroughly enough [foMCyYJ4LgY @ 00:03:13]. For actual management decisions, Wilkerson compares a disappointing week against an eight-week sales average and looks for a specific problem when three or four bad weeks persist, rather than constantly rewriting the script [foMCyYJ4LgY @ 00:08:17]. Hiring brings a related measurement problem. Wilkerson requires a video reading of the script, multiple interviews, workstation pictures, and evidence of a workable internet connection instead of hiring the first applicant [ASO1JUOWTjA @ 00:02:12]. They also warn against firing an assistant after only a week or two without knowing personal or assistant KPIs. Weekly reviews cover calls, conversations, and average call length, while training helps the assistant learn the operator's system. Wilkerson points out that a premature dismissal may occur before leads even receive their second or third contact [ASO1JUOWTjA @ 00:06:17].

Heir Hunters supplies both research reversals and disappointing economics. In Norman Hunt's case, a possible daughter named Pamela initially looked plausible because the area and mother's maiden name matched. Amy questioned the connection, and the team found another Hunt marriage to a Margaret with the same maiden name. Matching those surface details had not established the correct family [rvjn6936-aM @ 00:06:36]. Even reaching the right heirs did not immediately secure payment-producing work. Peter and Mary had heard from several firms, and Tom Smith left the visit without signed agreements because they wanted the weekend to consider the information [rvjn6936-aM @ 00:39:12]. They subsequently retained Finders, with Ryan reporting at least two competing companies. The maternal heirs were identified, but paternal lines remained outstanding; the team's result was not a completed declaration that no other heirs existed [rvjn6936-aM @ 00:41:43]. Mary Jean Gurnie's case produced a different reality check. Fraser and Fraser found 15 heirs on the maternal side, yet the estate was worth £13,000. Gareth Langford called the value disappointing after the amount of work, while still describing finding everyone as a good result. Research success and a large estate value were plainly different outcomes in that case [rvjn6936-aM @ 00:32:51].
