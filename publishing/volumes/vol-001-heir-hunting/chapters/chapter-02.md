# Chapter 02: Tracing Descendants Through Family Trees, Newspapers, and Vital Records

*Beginning and post-1940 descendancy research, family-tree building, digital newspapers, marriage and death records, the Social Security Death Index, and finding relatives of deceased people—with Ancestry, FamilySearch, local record searches, and a Heir Hunters case.*

## Executive Summary

James Tanner and Christa Cowan start with documented relationships, then trace whole families rather than isolated surnames. Joshua Taylor shows why siblings, unmarried relatives, inherited papers, and late-life households can unlock missing generations. Connie Knox and Rachel Ezzell Berry turn marriage searches into locality-specific work; Helen Smith, Amy Cross, and Anne Mitchell extract leads from death records and their alternatives. Fraser and Fraser begin with family knowledge and photographs. David Church starts with existing telephone numbers, relatives, and obituary searches. Their approaches differ on research depth, stopping points, original-record purchases, and contact style. The commercial stakes appear in Finders’ search of a retired postal worker’s flat: bank statements transformed the case, while an undiscovered will or half sibling could have overturned earlier work.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with the family you can actually connect, not the longest pedigree on the screen. Fraser and Fraser begin with a large sheet of paper, a family-tree template, and the researcher in the middle, working outward through parents, grandparents, aunts, and uncles. Their next move is conversation: ask parents and grandparents questions without leading the answers, then place photographs against the tree and inspect the backs for names, dates, and places. James Tanner adds the decisive test before descending from a chosen ancestor: inspect every intervening parent-child relationship for a historically valid source. A name, date, and birthplace already entered into FamilySearch do not supply that connection by themselves. Christa Cowan likewise starts with a solid paper trail, resolved conflicts, and correctly connected people. They converge on a practical beginning: identify the family, inspect the evidence already attached, and establish the relationship before extending it. Fraser and Fraser recommend software that redraws the tree when information changes, but explicitly reject buying the most expensive package simply because it is available. Tanner’s insistence on documentation and Cowan’s treatment of conflicting evidence make those early corrections part of the work, rather than a cleanup exercise after a large tree has already accumulated. Build families, not just a succession of parents. Cowan explains that a grandfather’s death certificate may omit a mother’s maiden name while a sibling’s certificate supplies it. The move is sideways before it is backward: connect siblings, assemble their records, and use the fuller family to resolve the missing relationship. Joshua Taylor takes the same idea beyond vital events. Education, occupation, travel, journals, correspondence, and the people supporting someone near the end of life can supply information that the direct ancestor never recorded. Taylor’s Milo Marsh disappeared from the expected Vermont and New York searches, then appeared with a spouse in Washington County, Rhode Island. Further research identified the household connection as Milo’s younger sister, Wealthy Marsh, whose married name was Clocker. That sibling connection helped Taylor work backward toward the parents. Tanner’s beginning descendancy method also starts with a documented ancestor and searches for additional children through birth records, church records, probate, wills, and land records. The shared mechanic is to follow the family’s records across relationships, rather than demand that one preferred document answer everything. An ancestor’s children, siblings, and in-laws are research subjects with their own papers and households, not merely extra names attached to a finished pedigree. Treat each life event as an opening into additional records. Connie Knox searches both civil and religious marriage material, including licenses, registries, banns, bonds, and ceremony records. The value can extend beyond the couple: Knox found parents and grandparents in Danish parish registers, although translation was needed. Amy Cross makes the same point about death certificates. In the Tenny Alexander example, the certificate identifies the nursing home, usual residence, birth details, occupation, parents, informant, and burial information. Cross separates information supplied by the son from the death details supplied through the attending process; the son did not know the grandmother’s maiden name. Helen Smith then widens the search when no certificate is available: funeral directors, hospitals, inquests, police records, church burials, newspapers, wills, and household papers can all enter the investigation. None of these speakers treats a single index hit as the whole event. Knox wants the original marriage material because an index may expose only brief information. Cross reads the certificate for the next address or institution to investigate. Smith asks what happened around the death and who was involved. Together, those methods move the research from finding a date to finding the people, places, and surviving records associated with it. ## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Tanner’s FamilySearch sequence is concrete: select a verified ancestor, open that person’s tree, change the view to descendancy, and select the number of generations to display. The display allows up to four; an apparently short result can mean nobody has entered the missing descendants. Inspect people with few sources, missing spouses, missing death dates, or unexplored children. Cowan adds an orderly traversal: work through the oldest child’s descendants, then return for the next child, using a Family Tree Maker bookmark or even a note to mark the stopping point. For newspapers, Tanner first identifies the event’s place and period, then finds the titles published there. Search Google and Wikipedia for newspaper titles and repositories, but search the newspaper databases themselves for people. His discovery query combines newspaper, digital, online, and the state name. OCR provides a shortcut through digitized pages; microfilm can require reading each page. Knowing the marriage date lets the search concentrate on the date and the weeks immediately around it. Cowan carries that locality work into newspaper family notices: wedding parties, visiting relatives, birth announcements, and obituaries can expose relationships that are not apparent from a pedigree screen alone. Read a death certificate all the way to its burial and undertaker fields. Cross demonstrates using an undertaker’s address in a city directory when the handwritten name is difficult to decipher. From there, identify the cemetery, contact its office, and ask about records even when no headstone appears online. If no cemetery office remains, Cross suggests contacting city hall; for a closed funeral home, investigate a successor business, historical society, genealogical society, or local library. Smith’s alternatives extend to memorial cards, family Bibles, newspaper notices, professional journals, and military records. Smith also distinguishes an exact death date from a range established by a will’s writing and probate. Anne Mitchell adds a specific Social Security Death Index reading task: record the name, birth and death dates, issuance place and year, and whether the entry says last residence or last benefit. Mitchell describes last benefit as usually paid to a qualifying survivor and last residence as the address the administration knew while paying benefits. Keep those field labels in the notes rather than treating both as the same location. Mitchell also points to ordering the original SS-5 application for a fee through the request instructions, rather than expecting Ancestry to hold that original application. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The stopping point changes with the assignment. Tanner says that, in personal descendancy work, the usual stopping point is when children were born in the 1900s, partly because those people or their children may still be living. Tanner presents that as a personal allocation of research time, not an instruction everyone must adopt. Cowan’s post-1940 work deliberately continues toward living cousins, DNA connections, reunions, and family information. The differences are equally sharp around concentration. Tanner warns against spending decades fixated on one marriage record, person, or family while other research stops. Cowan instead stresses a focused ancestral couple and systematic work through each branch. Those are different controls on wasted effort: Tanner attacks obsession with a stalled question; Cowan attacks drifting wherever hints lead. Taylor supplies another important contrast with simply marking an unmarried, childless person as a finished branch. Olive Kingsley had no descendants, yet the will identified nieces and nephews. William H. Shoop’s institutional file contained a sister’s letter; tracing that sister’s descendants led to handwritten family birth information. For Taylor, a branch can end biologically while remaining productive as a source of evidence about other branches. The target determines whether the next task is another descendant, another record, or another relative’s papers. Original-record purchasing is another fork. Knox urges researchers who find an online entry to locate the original source because the original marriage record may contain information the index omits. Cowan says original records are routinely ordered for immediate ancestral research, but descendant projects expand so quickly that the benefit of each purchase needs consideration. Cross follows a cost-conscious acquisition sequence: Ancestry and FamilySearch first, including library access to Ancestry, then county or state ordering, and then consideration of VitalChek. Cross describes VitalChek as convenient and sometimes quicker, but more expensive, with expedited shipping adding further expense. The reported turnaround comparisons cut both ways: Cross encountered a state wait of six months against a county return within a month, but also saw the relationship reversed. For newspapers, Tanner usually begins with the free Chronicling America collection, while also describing subscription archives, state projects, university collections, and local holdings. Cowan uses Newspapers.com heavily for later family reconstruction. These are alternative access routes, not interchangeable collections. Tanner explicitly says a large page count is irrelevant when the collection lacks the required material, and local projects may hold newspapers absent from the large commercial sites. The purchasing decision therefore follows the record and its additional information, rather than the size or price of a platform alone. Contact style separates the family-history and recovery playbooks. Cowan uses a short relationship question: are you the granddaughter of the named person who married another named person? If so, Cowan offers to share information, sometimes stopping the opening message there. The broader approach is to make the tree findable, respond to contacts, and share without expecting anything back. Church leads with the deceased person’s name, property address, account type, and a callback number repeated in the message. The sample opening is, “This David Church, I’m calling about 123 Main Street,” followed by the account and owner. When someone refuses information, Church reports using a student’s pressure line: “Before you hang up the phone, I want to let you know that there’s money on the table.” Church continues that hanging up does a disservice to the family and claims the approach usually gets information or acceptance of contact details. Church also emails attention-getting subjects containing the name, account, and address, sometimes with an attachment of the message text. Despite those efforts, Church describes email as the weakest response channel, although it has produced a few warm leads. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

A matching name can still attach the wrong family. Tanner describes two people in England with the same name, wives with the same name, and children whose names mostly matched. Location initially failed to separate them because they lived next door to each other. In another demonstration, Mary appeared as Mary V even though the attached census sources said Mary A. Correcting that reading exposed a death-record hint, a married surname, and the family attached to Isaac Cook. The Finders team encountered a different name problem in Victor’s maternal line: Amy Moyes identified four possible spellings of the grandmother’s surname, turning each potential record check into repeated work. On the separate Theodori case, Shannon Freeman faced a grandfather using several different names across records. A birth certificate helped establish the correct family before the tree could be extended with confidence. Cowan adds a relationship trap even when the names are correct: a recent obituary listed surviving children, but only three were biological children, one was a foster child, and four were stepchildren. A named surviving spouse also need not be the parent of every listed child. These are observed errors and ambiguities that changed the search, not merely untidy spelling to smooth over. A missing search result and a missing record are different problems. Berry demonstrates how given-name-first searching can hide an existing Delaware County marriage entry, while a recent marriage may still be awaiting entry. Cowan reports obituary links that stop working after newspapers remove online notices; the index can still identify the newspaper for a direct request or library search. Tanner encountered a cemetery employee who answered questions but volunteered nothing: obtaining the records required repeatedly asking for the next specific record type. In another burial problem, an ancestor’s cemetery date appeared decades later than the known death. City archive material showed an earlier church burial followed by removal to another cemetery when the original site was built over. Smith describes the reverse mismatch between stone and plot: William Busby’s wife was buried without having a name added to the stone, while Lieutenant Cassidy’s Brisbane memorial commemorated someone buried in South Africa. A headstone therefore did not settle either who occupied the plot or where the named person was buried. The practical responses are equally concrete: repeat the search in the system’s required format, follow the index to its publisher, request the separate cemetery records, and distinguish death, burial, removal, and memorial information. Victor’s case shows the economic exposure behind an expanding tree. Ryan Gregory and Amy Moyes worked a privately referred estate while assuming rivals might also have the lead. A second marriage by Victor’s father created a possible half-sibling branch that, in the team’s account, could displace the wider relatives already being pursued. They continued wider-family work while that question remained open; the later search found no additional children. The flat visit carried another reversal risk: finding a will could make months of work on the contacted relatives useless. Instead, the search revealed statements showing 220,000 in one account and 320,000 in another; the programme subsequently reported a £900,000 estate for Nigel and fifteen other heirs. The separate John Theodori case began with a council flat and uncertain assets. The team feared spending substantial resources only to find no distributable money, then discovered about £80,000 and intensified the Smith-side research. A repeated address finally unlocked that difficult family. Cowan likewise makes original-record purchases selective when descendant numbers become large. Research effort, waiting time, and estate value did not move together automatically in these accounts.
