## Executive Summary

Christa Cowen, Mindy Taylor, Alice Childs, and Brian M put original images, informant knowledge, independent corroboration, and written reasoning ahead of matching names or repeated claims. Melissa Finley and Mike O’Neill resolve apparent contradictions through chronology, siblings, geography, and complete probate records. DNA specialists add match trees and descendancy research, not automatic identities: Katherine Moore’s reopened identification received a 228 cM match, while Turi King explains why relatives may share no detectable DNA. Workflow differences matter: Cowen separates candidates before tree entry; Moore uses preliminary trees before documenting relevant lines. James Tanner challenges imported courtroom terminology. The economics remain case-specific: Heir Hunters found 53 heirs without confirming estate value, and Katherine O’Connell’s mineral-rights assignment produced 56 cents per inheritor.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Start with the question, not the matching name. Christa Cowen’s John Wesley Wright demonstration begins with a marriage, a child, and a household separation, then asks what happened to John. Cowen rejects both extremes: throwing away a record because the spelling looks wrong and attaching anything with a familiar name or approximate date. Mindy Taylor likewise starts with a question narrow enough to answer, then researches, correlates, weighs, and writes. Taylor’s death-certificate, obituary, and tombstone example explains why three matching statements can still represent one grandmother’s account rather than independent corroboration. Alice Childs asks when and how each record was created, who supplied each fact, and how the information answers the particular question. In Childs’s census example, the image adds birthplace, children, widowhood, occupations, military service, and a neighboring surname that the index did not deliver. Brian M makes the same practical distinction between weight and quantity: retain an erroneous source for comparison, but seek independently created, event-proximate material. These practitioners examine information item by item rather than granting an entire document blanket authority. Their working sequence is retrieval, contextual comparison, and an explained conclusion—not a vote among matching search results. Separate people before settling the spelling. Cowen’s Daniel Jones investigation finds several men with similar ages, nearby residences, and wives called Elizabeth or Eliza. All were farmers, so occupation supplied no useful separation in that case. Children’s later censuses, death records, obituaries, and family connections eventually distinguished five identities. Melissa Finley explains why spelling alone is a poor separator: clerks recorded sounds, people used nicknames or call names, families reused names, and some individuals changed names after moving. Finley uses one consistent spelling in narrative reports while preserving the recorded spelling in extracts and citations. Mike O’Neill’s Julia Chew case shows why an apparently established father does not finish the job. William Huggins named Julia as a daughter, but Julia’s marriage bond called the bride Minser. O’Neill followed sister Thirza, whose bond also used Minser, rather than treating the discrepancy as an isolated clerical nuisance. The two sisters’ records redirected the investigation toward Rebecca Minser’s estate. Cowen’s candidate comparisons, Finley’s name explanations, and O’Neill’s sibling reconstruction all keep the surrounding family in view. A matching surname can initiate a search; the documented relationships, chronology, and associated records determine whether the proposed connection survives. DNA practitioners also return to documentary reconstruction. Turi King explains that shared DNA does not specify exactly how two people are related; a possible second-cousin connection and a removed relationship require family-tree work to separate. King starts with the question and selects testing around it, distinguishing autosomal matching from particular maternal or paternal lines. Katherine Moore describes forensic investigative genetic genealogy as a component of a broader investigation that generates leads, not a stand-alone identification. Match trees lead back to common ancestors and forward through descendants, after which documentary support and confirmatory investigation matter. Juli Whittaker’s repatriation assignments likewise require eligible DNA-relative charts, narratives, and citations, with adoption capable of interrupting an assumed biological line. Whittaker separately identifies DNA donors and legal next of kin rather than treating those tasks as interchangeable. Joseph Ross places the same emphasis on competence: advanced genealogy, genetic genealogy across population groups, and substantial practical experience precede effective investigative work. Ross describes unknown-parentage cases as using the same tools and techniques as IGG, while warning that mistakes affect real family relationships. Across these approaches, the deliverable remains an explained connection supported by research, with the particular assignment determining which relationship must be established. ## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

James Tanner explicitly objects to importing courtroom terminology into ordinary genealogy. Drawing on trial practice, Tanner argues that evidence rules, burdens of proof, judges, opposing advocates, and binding decisions belong to a setting that a family-tree conclusion does not reproduce. The researcher evaluates veracity, cites the source, and offers a conclusion that others may reject. Taylor, by contrast, teaches separate classifications for sources, information, and evidence, using them to expose assumptions rather than treating a document’s label as its verdict. Finley places conflict resolution within the Genealogical Proof Standard and demonstrates how written conclusions handle unresolved dates. The FamilySearch presenter on indirect and negative evidence adds another methodological warning: premature classification can bias the search, so the researcher should assemble information and reason through it before settling its evidential use. These are distinct positions on terminology and timing, not a dispute over whether records should be examined. Tanner still asks who created the record, who supplied the information, whether that person witnessed the event, and how the item survived. Taylor still qualifies conclusions because additional evidence may change them. Both describe documented, revisable judgments, while disagreeing about the value of particular analytical labels. Tree-building order changes with the assignment. Cowen keeps unresolved Daniel Jones material in electronic folders, OneNote, and spreadsheets, then enters the family tree after separating the people. Cowen attaches reasoning about identity, spouse, children, residence, and why the other candidates do not fit. Moore deliberately starts exploratory genetic work with quick-and-dirty trees to find connections, postponing documentary proof until the relevant lines emerge. Moore then proves the connections used in the report, rather than every preliminary branch. These approaches concern different stages and outputs; neither practitioner presents an exchange with the other. Reporting also varies. Katherine O’Connell describes court-facing findings, affidavits, citations, and a record of every search. In a missing-beneficiary case, approximately 25 unsuccessful steps supported a due-diligence finding, distribution, and a reserved share without declaring the missing person dead. Ross emphasizes direct, concise explanations that law enforcement can use for the next investigative step, distinguishing that report from conventional genealogy writing. The common requirement is intelligible reasoning; the differences are when a tree becomes a documented conclusion and what the recipient needs the report to accomplish. Neither a preliminary genetic tree nor an unsuccessful search log is presented as a substitute for its intended final deliverable. ## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

A household label can manufacture the wrong parent. Finley’s Percy Farnworth example lists Nora and children with ordinary wife, son, and daughter labels, but other records establish that Ovid came from Nora’s earlier marriage. Childs encounters the same trap with Robert and Betty Hedrick, listed as Moody Beck’s children. Fern’s 1924 marriage record supplies the Shepherd maiden name, earlier marriage, divorce, and Hedrick married name; the 1920 household places Fern and Robert with Paul Hedrick. Childs’s Sanders research supplies a second warning: the 1910 census says Alexander and Kate had no children, although Eldon had been born in 1907 and appears with Alexander in 1920. The omission did not establish Eldon’s death. Taylor’s George Taylor baptism problem shows how a second document can repeat rather than resolve an error: the bishop’s transcript duplicated the Sarah entry. Taylor instead compared settlement documents, sibling baptisms, birth spacing, naming, and the missing expected burial before concluding the Sarah entry probably represented George. In all three investigations, the attractive direct answer failed to settle the relationship. The practitioners returned to other records rather than forcing the family to match the first household label or duplicated entry. Absence needs an expectation before it can do evidential work. The FamilySearch indirect-evidence presenter distinguishes an expected person missing from an extant source from an unsuccessful search, a lost record, or an absent DNA match. The Christina Greenfield reconstruction combines disappearance after 1860, Julia’s placement, Franklin’s remarriage affidavit, and continued absence to support a death interval rather than pretending a missing certificate states the date. King likewise explains that even third cousins may share no DNA, so a nonmatch does not automatically sever the documentary relationship. O’Neill uses Doctor’s missing share in Rebecca’s estate to support a different mother, but leaves that mother’s identity unresolved. Contact work introduces another failure point. Whittaker reports unanswered calls, scam fears, and a difficult repatriation search that reached second cousins once removed and produced one callback. Whittaker uses a script and a way to verify the repatriation outreach, and describes contacting a child before approaching a 95-year-old relative. These examples keep the conclusion at the level the work actually supports: a reasoned interval, an unresolved mother, a possible genetic connection, or a contact attempt—not a completed identity or relationship merely because other avenues have failed. A solved tree and a valuable estate are separate outcomes in the reported cases. The Gagneux investigation took 18 months and identified seven paternal heirs in France and 11 maternal heirs; newly discovered assets brought the estate estimate to £35,000. Fraser and Fraser’s Bob Wearing investigation identified 53 heirs without confirming an estate value. Expanding branches required shared staff, and the programme explicitly notes that some completed investigations reveal little or no value. O’Connell’s mineral-rights assignment traced seven siblings’ descendants forward nearly 150 years and reached more than 200 inheritors, yet declining gas value left each with 56 cents. O’Connell’s missing-beneficiary case produced a different useful outcome: documented unsuccessful searches allowed the court to proceed while reserving a share, rather than requiring a located beneficiary. Ross adds the employment-side bottleneck: courses do not ensure a position, qualified people may wait years, volunteer openings are competitive, and some opportunities circulate only through personal networks. Ross’s own entry combined extensive prior work with a cousin’s introduction to a forensic company. The monetary figures describe estates or distributions; the reported research volume and hiring experience do not turn them into investigator earnings.
