## 1. Assign six plays to one five-person pod before bidding  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with one narrow asset lane: ordinary commercial shop equipment with identifiable models, accessible loading and reachable business buyers. The six plays here are the chapter's operational mapping of Candidate #17, not six independent businesses. Full-time operator A owns discovery, bid limits and buyer relationships; full-time operator B owns verification, custody records and the ledger. Three contractors provide scheduled hauling, mechanical inspection and bookkeeping review. Contractor availability is purchased by job, not assumed to equal three additional salaried positions. Use GSA Auctions for applicable federal sales, GovDeals and PublicSurplus for participating public sellers, and GovPlanet only after identifying the actual seller and applicable lot terms. Marketplace branding does not establish federal ownership, transferable title or unrestricted resale. Each morning, operator A imports permitted alerts and records the source URL, seller, auction identifier, closing time, location, asset description and inspection window. Operator B rejects missing ownership evidence or impossible removal logistics before anyone spends inspection money. Keep an immutable copy of the listing and amendments; a changed pickup term can destroy the economics of an otherwise attractive machine. Assign each lot one accountable owner and one next action with a timestamp.

Convert discovery into a maximum all-in commitment, not an emotional hammer-price target. Establish a defensible resale estimate from completed transactions, dealer quotations and named buyer conversations, then subtract selling fees, transport, repair allowance, required contribution and carrying cost. If expected resale is $10,000, selling costs are $600, transport $800, repairs $900 and required contribution $2,000, only $5,700 remains for hammer price, premium and nonrecoverable acquisition taxes combined. At a hypothetical ten percent premium with no acquisition tax, the hammer ceiling is $5,181.81, rounded downward to the permitted increment. Actual premium and tax treatment come from that sale's terms. Do not substitute a resale permit for evidence that the seller accepts it. Score removal feasibility separately from price: a cheap lathe requiring an unavailable rigger is not an executable purchase. Inspection records must distinguish observed operation, seller statements and untested functions. Photograph serial plates, power requirements and missing attachments; ask whether loading assistance is contractual or merely customary. Auction alerts and bid worksheets automate sorting, while operator A retains responsibility for the submitted bid and available cash reservation.

Verification must resolve sale eligibility, restrictions and ownership before a funded award enters the removal queue. For federal property, read the sale-specific terms alongside 41 CFR Part 102-38; municipal sales follow their own authority and contract conditions rather than automatically inheriting federal procedures. A vehicle's SF-97 supports an application for a state title; it is not itself a universally usable state title or permission to operate the vehicle. Confirm the destination state's documents, liens, identification checks and dealer-license thresholds before bidding repeatedly in vehicles. For defense-origin assets, inspect the assigned demilitarization requirements and any end-use certificate conditions. Never infer unrestricted status from photographs or a seller's casual description. Route export-sensitive goods through item-specific classification and destination review; the baseline lane excludes weapons, munitions and unresolved controlled equipment. Operator B records the evidence supporting every check, while the relevant contractor confirms practical removability. Escalation means stopping that lot, not stopping the entire business. The remaining pipeline continues through inspected, ordinary assets whose transfer and resale are documented. This separation protects the five-person pod from spending its week untangling a single restricted or undocumented purchase.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Four tactics that protect recovery value**
> 
> The best bid advantage is often better removal and buyer evidence, not faster clicking.
> - Ask the property custodian for doorway dimensions, forklift capacity and appointment rules before hiring the hauler; retain the written response with the lot.
> - Photograph the serial plate during inspection and again at pickup; mixed municipal storage yards can release a visually similar but different machine.
> - Obtain a parts-only buyer quote before bidding on untested equipment, then use that quote as a documented downside recovery rather than a retail listing.
> - Schedule the inspection contractor and hauler together where practical; one coordinated site visit can expose rigging costs before an irreversible bid.

## 2. Install a serialized ledger and evidence-bound state machine  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Use a local SQLite ledger as the authoritative economic record, with one database on a local disk and one connection per operation. The six modules below concatenate into a runnable standard-library reference implementation. Set the database path explicitly, deploy schema changes before accepting writes, and keep application access behind an authenticated operator service. SQLite WAL improves reader concurrency; BEGIN IMMEDIATE serializes writers before they inspect inventory state. Every mutation has a unique operation key, canonical payload and atomic journal. Reusing the same key and payload returns the original result; reusing it with different content raises an error. A stale transition fails rather than silently overwriting another operator's work. Unique marketplace and source-lot identifiers prevent duplicate intake, while journal lines use integer cents and debit-positive signs. Balanced entries are checked inside the write transaction. The design eliminates check-then-write races within these application functions; it does not claim that a local database can make an external auction payment exactly once. Give only the service account database write access, preserve evidence outside the database by content hash, and test restore procedures before committing meaningful auction capital.

The workflow separates verified, awarded, funded, removed, ready, sold and closed states. Verification captures five explicit checks covering eligibility, ownership, restrictions, removal and buyer economics. Those checks summarize evidence; they do not replace the underlying records. Funding debits acquisition clearing and credits cash, then removal reclassifies that amount into inventory when the illustrative custody policy recognizes the asset. Adapt recognition timing to the actual sale contract with the bookkeeper. The clearing account is not a claim that GSA or another marketplace offers escrow. Where actual third-party escrow exists, maintain its separate confirmation and reconcile the corresponding asset balance. Repairs paid before sale increase inventory cost through an explicit operation; sale books receivables, gross resale revenue and the full recorded cost. Collection reduces receivables only after bank evidence supports the receipt. Closing requires the lot's receivable to equal zero. A full-lot disposal uses the scrap transition and expenses remaining cost, preserving the failed purchase rather than deleting it. Partial write-downs, returns, taxes collected and credit sales with complex adjustments require additional reviewed posting templates before use; they are outside this deliberately bounded whole-lot reference implementation.

External execution remains a controlled handoff. Operator A submits a bid through the authorized marketplace interface; operator B records the award only after receiving a seller-issued result. Before funding, operator B checks the invoice reference against the bank and existing payment records. If an external request times out, inspect its status instead of sending another payment. The funded transition is posted after confirmation, using the confirmation identifier as the operation key. This ordering leaves an observable recovery task if the local process crashes after payment but before recording it. A subsequent bank reconciliation supplies the missing journal without repeating the transfer. Evidence identifiers should reference append-only documents, not mutable desktop filenames. The reconciliation view exposes each lot's cost, proceeds, receivable and workflow state so the bookkeeping contractor can inspect exceptions without editing source transactions. Run the quality checks after every import and before releasing another bidding budget. Back up through SQLite's backup interface, not an uncoordinated copy of an active database file. Test identical retries, conflicting retries, stale transitions and concurrent duplicate collections in a temporary database. Never run that destructive test harness against the operating ledger.

## 3. Walk a 90-day cohort from initial capital to Base-case cash  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

The worked cohort begins with $75,000 unrestricted owner capital, zero inventory, zero receivables and no debt. It assumes a prepared buyer network and executable auction access; it is a production illustration, not a promise that a cold start generates sales immediately. Candidate #17's stated time to first deal remains one to six months. Over the quarter, the pod screens 108 leads, rejects 42 before purchase and acquires 27 lots. Twenty-one lots sell and six remain on hand. Each month's sales cohort means lots accepted by buyers that month, including carryover purchases; it does not imply that every sale originated in the same month's lead pool. Acquisition and preparation cash totals $126,625. This includes hammer prices, premiums, nonrecoverable acquisition taxes, inbound transport and direct refurbishment. All acquired lots clear removal within their purchase month, leaving no acquisition-clearing balance at month end. Selling fees and routine outbound delivery are paid inside the stated operating expense budget. Sales tax collected for a jurisdiction would be a liability, not resale revenue; this example assumes documented business resale transactions with no sales tax collected. Opening capital is funding, never revenue or owner profit.

Month one sells five lots for $35,000 and collects $30,000, leaving $5,000 receivable. Cash acquisition and preparation payments of $45,000 exceed recognized cost of goods sold of $27,178, so inventory ends at $17,822. After $4,000 operating expense, owner profit is $3,822 while operating cash flow is negative $19,000. Month two sells seven lots for $47,500, collects $45,000, pays $40,000 into inventory and recognizes $36,886 cost of goods sold. Inventory reaches $20,936 and receivables reach $7,500; monthly profit is $6,614 but cash increases only $1,000. Month three sells nine lots for $61,250 and collects $63,750, including older invoices. It pays $41,625 for acquisitions and preparation, recognizes $47,561 cost of goods sold and spends $4,000 on operations. Profit is $9,689 and cash increases $18,125. The three cost figures include defect-related overruns of $500, $750 and $1,000 on sold lots. These are already expenses through cost of goods sold, not additional deductions. Prepurchase scrubs consume staff time covered by overhead but create no inventory asset or separate invented cash loss.

At day ninety, gross resale revenue is $143,750, cost of goods sold is $111,625 and operating expense is $12,000. Owner profit therefore equals $20,125, exactly fourteen percent of revenue. Cash ends at $75,125, inventory at $15,000 and receivables at $5,000. The balance sheet reconciles: $95,125 of assets equals $75,000 contributed capital plus $20,125 retained profit. The cash bridge is equally important: $20,125 profit minus $15,000 inventory growth minus $5,000 receivable growth equals $125 operating cash inflow. Four quarters at the same revenue and expense economics yield $575,000 gross revenue, $446,500 cost of goods sold, $48,000 operating expense and $80,500 owner profit. Do not multiply the startup quarter's inventory buildup indefinitely when estimating mature cash generation. A steady portfolio can replenish sold stock while holding working capital approximately constant. This model assumes the two full-time operators are working owners whose compensation comes from residual owner profit; the $4,000 monthly overhead includes contractor work and ordinary business expenses, not two market-rate salaries. Hiring replacement employees requires rebuilding the economics. Owner income tax, financing and distributions are outside this unlevered illustration.
