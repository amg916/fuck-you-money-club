## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who has handled titled surplus treats the paperwork chain as the real asset. The steel comes second. Zero Tax Tags draws the hard line: the title is the deed, registration is renewable permission to drive, and a bill of sale is only a receipt. Ownership takes the signed title plus the bill of sale [uOA3FOVLCGA @ 00:01:35]. The same channel flags that government does not title its own vehicles. The SF-97 therefore has to carry reassignment forms across the full chain of custody, and some states add military-specific rules [uOA3FOVLCGA @ 00:03:13]. Wisdom & Authority Title Solutions backs this up from the manufacturer side. The MCO starts the title trail, and the title carries it across state lines, such as Pennsylvania to West Virginia. When there is no MCO, there is no title record nine times out of ten, and the DMV may have to generate a VIN [-S82T9iXF7Q @ 00:06:31]. Dirt Legal puts dollar figures on the military version of that chain. The buyer signs an end-user agreement barring overseas export, then pays $25 for the bill of sale and $50 for the SF-97. A clean title arrives in up to eight weeks, and the whole GovPlanet process runs up to six months [9EAPaeoXhFs @ 00:01:46].

The second point of agreement is to price every lot as if it is broken, because listings routinely miss defects. Flying Wheels works a public New Hampshire surplus auction on that premise. Everything sells as-is, so preview the day before and bid assuming the item does not run. Know your market, set limits ahead of time, and add 10% to cover the 8% premium [SEESfrbGoLY @ 00:19:19]. FlatBroke Garage reports roughly 80% winners across nine or ten GovDeals vehicles. The advice there is to call the town or village, set an inspection date, and fire the vehicles up in person. Yet the F-150 went uninspected on a minimum bid, and the same operator steers clear of drug and police seizure units entirely [pN1E-KA7qxw @ 00:04:50]. Kevin24Seven learned what trusting listing copy costs. A GovPlanet Humvee advertised with only a weak brake pedal turned out to be described incorrectly [Vl0TRUioSn0 @ 00:01:15]. The basic build budget now carries $2,500 for repairs, because even trucks listed as perfect need work. The TCM and the start box are singled out as the expensive failure points [d2qrhLbKEbI @ 00:16:39]. Nobody in this group bids on the seller's description alone.

The third point of agreement is that the hammer price is the smallest number in the deal. Vantor Insider frames landed cost as a 5-12% buyer fee plus sales tax. Payment goes by wire within 48 hours, and removal is due within 5-7 business days, so the cash and the transport have to be lined up before the bid lands [tBIT1SUqIz8 @ 00:18:14]. Kevin24Seven shows it on a single Humvee. A $12,500 winning bid picked up $800 in shipping from Georgia to St. Louis and roughly $1,500 in registration and taxes. That made $14,800 before a wrench turned [Vl0TRUioSn0 @ 00:01:54]. FlatBroke Garage shows the same math at the cheap end: a $2,600 F-150 took a 12% auction fee of about $300 and landed near $2,900 all-in [pN1E-KA7qxw @ 00:35:03]. AutoHacker claims 15 years and more than 12,000 cars bought for clients, and posts the most brutal ratio. A Hawaii car bought for $1,450 cost about $3,700 and five weeks just to ship [wNIgM_BLGik @ 00:09:33]. Every operator here budgets fees, tax, freight, and title paperwork before setting a ceiling. Any number that skips those lines is a fantasy price, and the overrun lands on the buyer.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether a surplus Humvee needs a paid titling service at all. Kevin24Seven insists he made his GovPlanet truck street legal himself with 'no middleman' and no modifications, and he argues other channels overcomplicate the job: red states are easy, and for blue states his answer is to 'just get a prius' [VvAg_yxxkPo @ 00:03:12]. Dirt Legal runs the opposite playbook, warning that private-party trucks need a theft check because there have been base thefts, and that some states will not title a Humvee at all, which is exactly the gap their paid service fills [9EAPaeoXhFs @ 00:02:19]. The contradiction sits inside Kevin24Seven's own catalog: in his basic-build breakdown he calls Dirt Legal 'the best and easiest' option, discloses that they sponsor him, and books an $800 line item for the service inside a $21,335 budget [d2qrhLbKEbI @ 00:14:13]. Waldo's World adds a third data point from New Hampshire, where registering his truck needed only a bill of sale, while conceding that other states vary [UhFstJrc_44 @ 00:53:21]. The operator read is simple: your state, not the auction house, decides whether the DIY highway-patrol route, a bill-of-sale registration, or a paid out-of-state service is the cheapest path to plates.

Humvee selection splits Kevin24Seven against himself. In his full buying walkthrough he tells bidders to avoid soft-top units because they sell for $5,000 to $10,000 more and the tops are usually shot, notes hardtops can climb to $15,000 to $20,000, and reports paying $12,000 for his [k5WD_GKlQhs @ 00:03:11]. In his basic-build video he flips the filter, hunting sold listings for two-door soft-top and truck-body units because two-doors 'go for far less' [d2qrhLbKEbI @ 00:02:24], and he rates a $9,500 runner with hard steering, minor blow-by, and oil and fuel leaks as a 'really good deal' [d2qrhLbKEbI @ 00:04:30]. Waldo's World played a completely different game: he bought two non-running trucks sight unseen at an online-only government auction, paying $3,600 for an Army M1097 and about $4,500 for a Marine M1123 on a day when running Humvees went for roughly $10,000 [UhFstJrc_44 @ 00:55:41]. That discount came with a long wrench bill, including an in-frame bearing job and a lift-pump and check-valve chase. The trade-off is clear: Kevin24Seven pays for a running platform and budgets repairs on top, while Waldo's World buys dead iron cheap, harvests one truck to revive the other, and estimates another $5,000 just to complete the second unit.

On civilian vehicles the split is inspection discipline and broker use. FlatBroke Garage, with nine or ten GovDeals vehicles and about 80% winners, admits he skipped the scheduled inspection on his F-150 and simply placed a minimum bid, even though buyers can contact the town, set an inspection date, and fire the vehicles up [pN1E-KA7qxw @ 00:04:50]. We Hustle Daily takes the hard line on Copart: inspect in person, because buyers who purchase out of state 'are some Maniacs' [VRmsT2o_6og @ 00:06:29]. Brokers split the field again. Vantor Insider calls the dealer-license requirement a myth for government surplus and warns that salvage brokers charge unlicensed buyers $400 to $1,200, with $800 in broker fees typically buying a branded title [tBIT1SUqIz8 @ 00:05:30]. AutoHacker argues the opposite math on salvage pools, showing a $15,000 bid costs $2,250 in public fees versus $650 through a broker, and $750 versus $525 on a $5,000 bid [wNIgM_BLGik @ 00:08:02]. The workable split: go direct on GovDeals, GSA, and municipal surplus where the public bids openly, and only pay a broker on Copart or IAAI when the fee spread favors the broker and the title is clean enough to register.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive surprise is a listing that lies by omission. Kevin24Seven won his GovPlanet Humvee at $12,500 on a description claiming only a weak brake pedal, which 'turned out to be incorrect' [Vl0TRUioSn0 @ 00:01:15]; his maintenance tally reached $2,977.30, led by a $937 start box and a $552 steering box. In his basic-build budget he books a flat $2,500 repair reserve because 'even trucks listed as perfect' fail at the expensive points, naming the TCM and the start box [d2qrhLbKEbI @ 00:16:39]. Waldo's World hit the same wall on his non-runners: he moved the ignition switch and protective control box from the Marine truck into the Army truck and warned that these boxes commonly don't work [UhFstJrc_44 @ 00:02:02]. His fuel-system chase found sediment in the filter, two bad lift pumps, and a stuck check valve; he called the just-under-$300 milspec valve 'ridiculous,' swapped in a roughly $20 substitute, and cracked it by overtightening [UhFstJrc_44 @ 00:32:14]. The pattern across both builders is that the cheap hammer price is only the entry ticket: start boxes, steering boxes, lift pumps, and valves are the line items that turn a bargain into a bill, so the repair reserve has to be in the bid ceiling before the auction clock starts.

Civilian salvage and surplus lots carry their own traps. FlatBroke Garage's F-150 came with diesel-contaminated fuel, broken 4x4 vacuum lines, a stripped drain plug, and a bad ABS module, and he warns that pressure-washing ruined a coil pack [pN1E-KA7qxw @ 00:24:20]. We Hustle Daily flags Copart storage fees that kick in after three days and escalate in $5 steps, $5, $10, $15, and says they are mandatory or you lose buying access [VRmsT2o_6og @ 00:07:59]. AutoHacker, citing 15 years and more than 12,000 cars bought for clients, documents staged cars: a Lexus that sold for $8,600 on February 23, 2022 later resold for $20,000, so he says treat those as parts cars [wNIgM_BLGik @ 00:02:43]. He also warns that certificate-of-destruction and non-repairable titles cannot be registered at all, which turns a cheap win into a parts-only asset [wNIgM_BLGik @ 00:04:53]. Logistics compound the damage: his Hawaii example cost about $3,700 and five weeks to ship against a $1,450 purchase price. The economics check is to price the title brand, the storage clock, and transport before bidding, because any one of them can erase the spread on a car that looked like a steal on the lot photos.

Paperwork is where surplus buyers get stranded after paying. Kevin24Seven got plates before his title by using the manufacturer number from the SF97, then received a state letter demanding a Troop C inspection and a DOR 551, 'probably because i have a short vin,' and admits he is unsure whether his plates are valid [VvAg_yxxkPo @ 00:04:06]. Zero Tax Tags warns that a bill of sale is only a receipt and 'does not transfer ownership by itself'; ownership takes the signed title plus the bill of sale, and an SF-97 needs reassignment forms covering the full chain of custody [uOA3FOVLCGA @ 00:01:35]. Wisdom & Authority Title Solutions adds that a missing MCO means no title record 'nine times out of 10,' and the DMV may have to generate a VIN [-S82T9iXF7Q @ 00:06:31]. Vantor Insider flags the DLA version of the trap: a scrap bill of sale means no plates, so verify an SF97 option and expect a 30-to-60-day processing window before bidding [tBIT1SUqIz8 @ 00:16:24]. The check is blunt: confirm which document actually ships with the lot, and whether your DMV will accept it, before the hammer drops rather than after the truck is sitting in the driveway.
