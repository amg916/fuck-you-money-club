# Chapter 02: Buying Fleet Cars, Trucks, and Police Vehicles

*State and local vehicle sales, seized-vehicle lanes, GSA online truck buys, on-site F-250 inspections, sub-$5,000 rental-fleet picks, GovDeals tips for new accounts, and splitting a $900 Expedition for profit*

## Executive Summary

Operators buying retired fleet cars, trucks, and police units agree the vehicles come serviced but sold as-is: good tires and oil changes, no records, no test drives, and wrecks rarely repaired. Street mechanics center on GovDeals radius searches, GSA registration through the auction house, OBD scans and door-sticker checks at preview, a $12 to $15 VIN history, and buyer premiums running about 5% on GSA up to 15% at sheriff sales. Reported numbers range from a $900 Expedition landing near $2,000 all-in against a $5,000-plus KBB to an F-250 hammering at $4,150 and closing at $4,669 with fees. Practitioners split on sniping, with Top 5 Autos pushing last-minute bids while Street Rat Garage says extensions kill the tactic, and on whether real bargains exist at all, since East Coast Classics calls them few and far between.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Across lots in Indiana, Texas, Delaware, and Arizona, the operators converge on one baseline: retired fleet units are maintained but unvetted. Street Rat Garage reports that fleet units arrive with good tires and regular oil changes, while wrecks are rarely repaired [qieQr_fEJQ8 @ 00:16:16]. AJ at Handyman Process says GSA is pretty thorough about maintaining its vehicles, but you do not get those records, so bring a mechanic [FZRiWN_dcS4 @ 00:06:55]. East Coast Classics, who has bought a couple dozen cars at surplus sales, confirms service history is limited and warns that genuine deals are few and far between [g9Wt5wG1aGM @ 00:01:08]. The paint is usually gray or white, the drivetrain usually saw scheduled service, and the unknowns sit in damage the agency never fixed. Street Rat Garage adds a reading rule for listings: state employees write up every tiny leak, so a scary condition report should be taken with a grain of salt rather than treated as a death sentence [cqTAqIJLC10 @ 00:15:09]. AJ also calls selection hit or miss, so the consensus play is patience: walk every sale, log what each unit actually brings, and only pounce when a clean fleet truck slips under the crowd.

The second point of agreement is that you buy blind on the drive cycle. East Coast Classics lays out the preview rules: start the car, feel the brakes, engage the transmission, test HVAC, lights, and electrical, and plug in an OBD code reader, but the biggest restriction is you cannot drive the car, and some auctions allow no on-site inspection at all [g9Wt5wG1aGM @ 00:03:20]. Street Rat Garage gets the same terms on GovDeals: call ahead, start it, roll it forward and back, but no test drive and no shop inspection [cqTAqIJLC10 @ 00:08:33]. At the San Antonio dealer auction, AJ finds most if not all units unlocked with keys inside, so the move is to fire it up and hook an OBD reader during the preview day [FZRiWN_dcS4 @ 00:01:50]. Because the price must absorb that risk, Tool Demos argues these buys only make sense if you have mechanical skill and patience; their $900 ex-fire-rescue Expedition was cheap precisely because of an audible engine noise, which turned out to be a scored cylinder-three exhaust cam lobe and a destroyed roller follower [EFXJtp6q_B8 @ 00:01:31]. The operators agree that the discount exists because the government will not let you find the problem first, and the buyer who can diagnose by ear and scanner collects that spread.

Every operator who posted receipts treats the hammer price as a partial number. Top 5 Autos pegs the buyer premium at roughly 5% on GSA Auctions, up to 10% on GovPlanet, and up to 15% at sheriff sales [WFVGJDWST7I @ 00:05:16]. UX Auctions documented a 2006 Southport fire department F-250 with 157,119 miles hammer at $4,150 after 47 bids and land at $4,669 with fees [IUAs4NGQfyo @ 00:05:15]. Junk Solutions Experts LLC won a school-district F-150 at $2,000 and was in for $2,250 after buyer premium and tax, noting that can take a little bit out of your profit [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:13:42]. The crowd is the other cost: Tool Demos warns you can definitely get into bidding wars with people, and that is how the price gets out of control [EFXJtp6q_B8 @ 00:09:31]. Tool Demos landed the Expedition at roughly $2,000 cash in against a KBB of $5,000 to $5,500 after holding the buy under $1,000. The working consensus is to fix a ceiling from comps before the sale opens, price in premium, tax, and transport, and walk away the moment the clock or the room pushes past it.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Sourcing starts with geography. Street Rat Garage runs GovDeals Advanced Search, sets Locations to a radius around a ZIP, 150 miles from 46201 in their case, and then opens a seller's other items to see that agency's full inventory [cqTAqIJLC10 @ 00:04:05]. Junk Solutions Experts LLC uses a 100 or 250-mile radius and says shipping only happens if you arrange it with the seller; as they put it, while Kansas may have a great deal on a welder, they are not going to Kansas for a welder [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:03:39]. New GovDeals accounts are free but start on probation, capped at about three bids at a time, and the watch list counts against the same limit, so watch one unit and bid near the close; probation ends after three completed auctions, meaning bid, win, pick up [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:01:54]. On the federal side, JR finds trucks on autoauctions.gsa.gov, contacts the auction house running the sale, Metro AAA in Phoenix, emails the registration form with a driver's license scan, and receives an access code and bidder badge [14j6GQVGkBk @ 00:01:12]. Payment is due in 24 to 48 hours and pickup within 30 days, so line up funds and transport before you bid.

On the lot, UX Auctions runs a hands-on checklist: never check just one tire, expect drained batteries and check fuses, hunt for keys hidden in a door panel or under a mat, and always check the door sticker so the VIN and paint code match the listing [IUAs4NGQfyo @ 00:01:14]. They flag low brake fluid as a warning sign, check whether coolant was flushed, smell the oil and rub it between your fingers, and note that separately run aftermarket wiring pulls out cleanly because they ran their own wires. Auto Dealer Startup insists on scanning every car even at a dealer-clear sale, rejecting a 2019 unit outright because the OBD port was inoperable: I got to be able to pull that code on it [dzr9aYx98oY @ 00:11:30]. They also read a clear distance of zero as a sign the car sat or the battery died. Street Rat Garage sorts Chargers by sight, where recessed hubcaps mean AWD, and lists the carrier bearing as a common, easy DIY fix while listening for the Hemi tick from long idling [qieQr_fEJQ8 @ 00:02:04]. Before bidding, Top 5 Autos says pay the $12 to $15 fee for a VIN history [WFVGJDWST7I @ 00:04:44].

Pricing discipline comes from outside comps. JR sets a stop price from KBB before the sale and paid $13,000 plus $888 Arizona tax by credit card for an F-150 that carried an open recall, then pulled transport quotes of $780 for 650 miles or $1,400 for 2,200 miles [14j6GQVGkBk @ 00:02:42]. Junk Solutions Experts LLC checks Facebook Marketplace comps before buying anything, which is how they knew a $453 Durham fireproof cabinet would turn at about $775 within a week [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:10:43]. At the live Kesler Schaefer lane in Indianapolis, Street Rat Garage watches the screens: current bid is real money, asking means nobody has bid yet, a green flash marks an online bidder, and deals land either at the beginning when people are scared to bid or at the end when they run out of money [6chXsHCTJA4 @ 00:12:27]. Their own exit math on a 2008 Ram bought for about $1,396 with fees, where the check-engine light was a loose gas cap, is to list at $3,500 and accept $2,700 to $2,800 because Facebook Marketplace buyers negotiate down [cqTAqIJLC10 @ 00:28:32]. Build the bid backward from that realistic sale price, not from the sticker you post.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over when to pull the trigger. Top 5 Autos tells online buyers to snipe in the last 3-5 minutes of a GSA Auctions, GovPlanet, publicsurplus.com, or county sheriff listing [WFVGJDWST7I @ 00:05:35]. Street Rat Garage, watching GovDeals police Chargers close, calls that pointless: most bidders already sit on their hands until the end, and 'sniper bidding' buys nothing because every bid extends the clock, so the last-second ambush just resets the timer and invites a counter [qieQr_fEJQ8 @ 00:33:35]. Junk Solutions Experts LLC lands in the middle for a different reason. On a probation GovDeals account, bids and the watch list draw from the same roughly three-item limit, so they watch a single item and only bid near closing to avoid tying up slots on losers [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:03:58]. In a live lane the timing logic flips again. At the Kesler Schaefer police and seizure sale in Indianapolis, Street Rat Garage says the deals come either at the very beginning, when the room is scared to open, or at the very end, when buyers have run out of money, and a green flash on the screen means an online bidder just jumped in [6chXsHCTJA4 @ 00:12:27]. Late bidding is common ground; the fight is over whether the final seconds are an edge or just noise.

How deep the discount runs depends on who is talking. Top 5 Autos sells the goldmine version: over 25,000 vehicles a year, with seized units, repos, and fleet vehicles retired at 50-80K miles as the best hunting categories, plus case stories of a $55K SUV landing under $18K [WFVGJDWST7I @ 00:02:30]. Those case numbers wobble on a close read, with one Suburban quoted at both $14,500 and 14,000 before selling for $9,000. JR, who bought an F-150 by finding it on autoauctions.gsa.gov and registering with a Phoenix auction house, watched over 200 vehicles cross and saw only one go significantly below value, adding that dealers skip sales tax, so their disadvantage against retail buyers is smaller than it looks [14j6GQVGkBk @ 00:03:23]. Handyman Process, on a monthly San Antonio dealer lane running government whites and grays, clocked trucks at roughly $2-3k under retail, with 15-passenger vans still hammering at $22k-26k in 30-40 second runs against dealers, the public, and online bidders [FZRiWN_dcS4 @ 00:06:26]. East Coast Classics, after buying a couple dozen surplus cars, puts it bluntly: the deals exist, 'and those deals, they're few and far between' [g9Wt5wG1aGM @ 00:01:08]. The buyers who report firsthand numbers see thin spreads on clean late-model trucks; the fat margins sit in rough, cheap, or mechanically flawed units.

Practitioners also split on what to do with the unit once it is home. Tool Demos runs the fix-and-sell play: a $900 ex-fire-rescue and police Expedition, about 4 days of labor, junkyard console, rear hatch, and grille, police wiring stripped, black cladding flame-restored, and roughly $2,000 cash in against a KBB of $5,000-5,500, then sold below KBB to a friend [EFXJtp6q_B8 @ 00:08:56]. Street Rat Garage treats wrecked fleet units as parts inventory. On a frame-bent DNR van with 93K miles that hammered at $825 plus a fee they put at about 7%, the math was engine around $700, transmission around $600, and rear end $300-500 [qieQr_fEJQ8 @ 00:34:53]. Auto Dealer Startup rejects flipping as the main exit for cheap fleet-grade cars and rents them instead: a $2,500-3,500 economy car pulls $350-400 a week, the coach skips Teslas as off-strategy, and a partner bought a 301K-mile Camry for $700 and rents it for $350 a week [dzr9aYx98oY @ 00:18:04]. That fleet only takes Ford, Toyota, or Honda, while Dodge, Subaru, and Nissan Sentra get pushed to the flip pile [dzr9aYx98oY @ 00:12:53]. Same auction stock, three exits: repair and retail, part out, or weekly rental cash flow.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive surprises hide behind inspection limits. East Coast Classics lays out the preview ceiling: you can start the car, feel the brakes, engage the transmission, test HVAC, lights, and electrical, and plug in an OBD reader, 'and the biggest is you cannot drive the car,' with a fixed preview window and no makeup session, so the bid has to compensate for that blindness [g9Wt5wG1aGM @ 00:03:20]. Street Rat Garage reports the same wall on GovDeals: call ahead, start it, roll it forward and back, but no test drive and no shop inspection [cqTAqIJLC10 @ 00:08:33]. Tool Demos shows what slips through. The $900 Expedition was cheap because of an audible engine noise, and a scope capture referenced to cylinder 1 plus an amp-clamp relative compression test exposed a scored cylinder-3 exhaust cam lobe and a destroyed roller follower [EFXJtp6q_B8 @ 00:02:08]. Auto Dealer Startup refuses to bid blind on codes even in a dealer-clear lane, walking from a 2019 with an inoperable OBD port because 'I got to be able to pull that code on it,' and reading a clear distance of zero as a car that sat or had a dead battery [dzr9aYx98oY @ 00:11:30]. East Coast Classics' NJ State Police impound Jaguar, with a seized engine, blown brakes, and a leaking radiator, is the endpoint of skipping that discipline.

The second trap is the gap between hammer price and all-in cost. Junk Solutions Experts LLC won a school-district F-150 at $2,000 and was at $2,250 after buyer's premium and tax, warning 'that can take a little bit out of your profit' [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:13:42]. UX Auctions' 157,119-mile Southport fire department F-250 hammered at $4,150 after 47 bids and landed at $4,669 with fees [IUAs4NGQfyo @ 00:05:15]. Top 5 Autos pegs buyer premium at about 5% on GSA Auctions, up to 10% on GovPlanet, and up to 15% at sheriff auctions, plus $300-900 for transport [WFVGJDWST7I @ 00:08:09]. JR stacks the full bill on a long-distance buy: $13,000 for the F-150, $888 Arizona tax paid by credit card, transport quotes of $780 for 650 miles or $1,400 for 2,200 miles, a gate pass at pickup, and an extra 2% owed to New York because AZ-NY reciprocity applied. Without reciprocity, JR warns of double tax and filing DTF-804, DTF-802, and AU-11, and that truck also carried an open recall [14j6GQVGkBk @ 00:08:40]. On a $1,000-class fleet car, a 15% premium plus a transport bill can swallow most of the spread before a wrench turns, which is why every operator who posted real numbers quoted the all-in figure, not the hammer.

The last cluster is self-inflicted: bidding emotion and pickup logistics. Street Rat Garage watched a buyer drop a one-and-done $5,000 bid on a Carmel Explorer and overpay by about $600 when the next comparable unit went for $4,400, and warns to read the screens, because 'current bid' is real money while 'asking' means nobody has bid yet [6chXsHCTJA4 @ 00:10:04]. The same sale saw a seized Trackhawk hammer at $64,000 on a bill of sale with unknown miles, a number Street Rat Garage figured needed about $70k on resale to clear a profit [6chXsHCTJA4 @ 00:18:29]. Tool Demos' verdict on whether government auctions are worth it hinges on the same trap: 'You can definitely get into bidding wars with people and that's how the price gets out of control' [EFXJtp6q_B8 @ 00:09:31]. Junk Solutions Experts LLC adds the pickup squeeze: weekday-only pickup, a locked account and accruing storage if you no-show, and a seller who sat on marking an item picked up for about 1.5 weeks over Thanksgiving, which froze the speaker's probation bid slots until a call to GovDeals, because 'unless they mark that it's been picked up, Gov deals doesn't know' [El3cLMGi_9s @ 00:09:47]. Win at the right number, then have the weekday trailer run locked before the auction closes.
