## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every board scrapper here works from the same rule: the money is made while stripping and separating, not at the scale. Storage Unit Scrapper pulls batteries and heat sinks before anything else, because boards still carrying batteries cannot be shipped to BoardSort [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:20:35]. Shark Scrapper makes the same point on a pallet of mixed computer-repair boards: 'remember before you sell these you have to remove that battery.' Heat sinks, motors, and brackets also come off to maximize BoardSort value [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:02:10]. Both use the same grading vocabulary. Storage Unit Scrapper calls a board low grade when it is brown on one side and green on the other, and that material sells for about 10 cents a pound [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:00:56]. Boards that are green on both sides, or carry gold plating, move up out of low grade into the peripheral bucket. Shark Scrapper sorts a socketless board that still carries chips as an integrated board, grouped with peripheral high-grade [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:14:07]. The operator takeaway is blunt. An unsorted tote or pallet gets paid at its worst grade. Every battery, bracket, or chunk of aluminum left attached either blocks the shipment outright or drags the whole lot down to the lowest category it touches.

Both scrappers also agree on where bad buys come from: trusting a label. Shark Scrapper sorted a near-pallet from a new Asheville computer-repair client into 19 categories, then warned: 'don't ever buy a full Gaylord box of boards believing that they are what they say they are' [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:20:51]. Buying that load at a flat mixed-board price risked being 'taken to the cleaners.' The standing rule is to never buy a pallet or gaylord blind unless the mixed price already accounts for the risk [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:19:54]. Storage Unit Scrapper buys 4 to 5 storage units a week and adds freight discipline on top. Ribbon wire and low-grade boards go to the local yard because the shipping price gap to BoardSort is not worth it [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:03:14]. In the tote video, the same operator puts BoardSort's 50 to 60 cents a pound in the not-worth-shipping column, while denser, higher-value fractions earn the trip [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:03:38]. The common pattern is a two-lane exit. Dense, high-grade fractions ship to the specialist buyer. Bulky, low-value material sells locally by weight. A seller's description never replaces your own hands-on inspection before money changes hands on a pallet, gaylord, or storage-unit tote of mixed electronics.

On test equipment, the consensus is that brand pedigree and calibration history hold resale value. EEVblog argues that Fluke's premium comes from neither accuracy nor ruggedness. It comes from a lifetime warranty, US manufacture that matters under government and military purchase rules, and production runs reaching back to the 1980s 70-series [ay9wFQAW19Y @ 00:02:37]. The same video gives surplus buyers their core heuristic: 'You can buy a 30-plus-year-old Fluke on eBay and if it works, it's almost certainly in spec' [ay9wFQAW19Y @ 00:10:20]. Keysight's refurbishment line confirms what the factory treats as resale-grade. Units are vacuum cleaned, damaged parts are replaced, and firmware is brought current. Each unit gets a calibration certificate with data and a 101-point quality check, on the same Penang site as new production [TipNUpHGLw0 @ 00:01:03]. ElectroHoard's workshop shows the street-level price split: GW distortion analyzers sell cheap while HP units cost more [2ZRWlImJMzE @ 00:08:04]. Together, the speakers point to one buying filter. Legacy HP, Agilent, Keysight, Fluke, and Tektronix hardware that still powers up and still measures commands a premium. Off-brand gear trades on price alone, even when its readings track the premium brands closely on the bench.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Storage Unit Scrapper's price ladder explains every minute spent at the bench. At the top, RAM pulls about $54 a pound at BoardSort [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:21:03]. IC chips chiseled and snipped off boards bring around $15 a pound there, though collecting about 3 pounds of them took roughly 8 months [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:09:15]. At the bottom, whole laptops sold intact to the local scrapyard bring 15 cents a pound: 'I sell these for 15 cents a pound at the local scrapyard' [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:00:19]. That floor has dropped hard. The same operator recalls selling laptops to the scrapyard for 98 cents a pound, so the whole-unit lane has lost most of its value [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 01:11:32]. The gap between a 15-cent laptop and $54-a-pound RAM is the whole business model. The laptop is just a container. The operator's job is to open it, pull the dense fractions, and send each one to the buyer paying the most for that material. Everything left over goes to the local yard by weight, where it still adds to the ticket: shells, steel, dirty aluminum, spare wire, connector ends, and low-grade boards. The math only works if the high-value fractions are pulled consistently from every unit, not just from occasional standouts, and if each fraction is stored separately until a full shipment or yard run justifies the trip.

The tote workflow runs like a disassembly line. Storage Unit Scrapper starts by removing screws, cutting ribbon wire off for the mixed-wire pile, and pulling copper spools [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:02:26]. ICs on low-grade boards get chiseled off, adding another sellable fraction to a board that would otherwise go at the bottom rate [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:08:54]. In the laptop video, the operator cuts connector ends off as boards come apart instead of batching the job for later. A 15 lb bucket ends up holding about 10 lb of connector ends [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:16:47]. Tape comes off with a heat gun so boards ship clean and are not downgraded on arrival [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:15:37]. Each step targets the same failure. A board that reaches the refiner with foreign material attached gets graded down or rejected. Copper, wire, and connector ends pulled at the bench become their own sellable piles instead of dead weight riding on board pricing. The discipline is to separate at the moment of disassembly, with labeled containers already staged beside the bench. Nothing gets handled twice, and the operator never has to choose between re-sorting a mixed bucket by hand and selling it at the lowest grade inside it. Staging the containers before the first screw comes out is what keeps a 4-to-5-unit weekly pace sustainable.

Shark Scrapper's pallet sort relies on fast physical tests, not part-number lookups. The quarter test sizes sockets: if a US quarter fits inside the socket, it is a large-socket board; if not, it drops to a smaller-socket category [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:04:05]. A bent retention arm marks an i-series board. An i3 21xx is worth about $1 in BoardSort's test-and-buy program, while newer i-series CPUs that test functional bring 'really good money' [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:11:30]. CPUs can stay in place and be weighed with the board for payment, then pulled later, and clients are told they can pull and sell CPUs separately [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:03:26]. On the business side, a sorting fee of about 10% is the going rate. Shark Scrapper waived it for this new client to build goodwill on a source that could keep supplying pallets [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:06:47]. The mechanics favor speed over precision at the first pass. Sockets are sized with a coin, CPU families are spotted by hardware cues, and the slow decisions are deferred until the board is already in the right bin. That ordering lets one person sort a near-pallet of mixed boards without stopping to research individual parts. Any judgment call about pulling CPUs goes against the posted price list, which pays for CPUs left on the board unless they were separated first.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull batteries and heat sinks first
   - Remove every battery and heat sink before any other teardown work.
   - Boards with batteries cannot be sent to BoardSort, so this step comes before sorting or packing [Storage Unit Scrapper — 4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:20:35].
2. Free boards from trays and brackets
   - Lift the bracket levers, reach the screws, and release each board from its metal tray.
   - Heat-sink brackets are aluminum. Boards come out of their trays so only board weight is paid at board rates [Shark Scrapper — 6XObIp5c91U @ 00:17:45].
3. Sort by socket and chip cues
   - Apply the quarter test and route each board into its socket or grade category.

   - Ribbon wire and low-grade boards go local because the shipping price gap to BoardSort is not worth it [Storage Unit Scrapper — 4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:03:14].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is where each grade of board actually goes. Storage Unit Scrapper runs a hybrid: low-grade boards, brown on one side and green on the other, go to the local yard at 10 cents a pound, because Boardsort's 50 to 60 cents a pound is not worth shipping [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:03:38]. In the laptop teardown the same channel keeps ribbon wire and low-grade boards local because the shipping price gap is not worth it, while high-grade peripherals ride to Boardsort at about $4 a pound and RAM at about $54 a pound [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:03:14]. Shark Scrapper plays the opposite game. Back in Florida, AMD boards went to the local yard as high-grade, but selling to Boardsort now means separating every category, and one near-pallet from an Asheville repair shop ended up in 19 bins before a sales slip, a check, battery removal and a freight run to Boardsort [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:09:59]. The local yard rewards speed and zero freight on bulk low-grade; the specialist buyer rewards granular sorting on high-value fractions. Run the price gap against shipping cost per category, not per load, before deciding which truck each tote rides.

Grading authority is the second fault line. For Storage Unit Scrapper, AI grading called some boards high-grade that Boardsort's Fred photo tool, capped at 50 submissions a day, graded low-grade, and the verdict was blunt: you can't believe everything you read on the AI [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:04:45]. Shark Scrapper grades by eye: if a US quarter fits inside the socket it is a large-socket board, a bent arm flags an i-series board, and a board with chips but no socket is integrated and goes with peripheral high-grade [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:04:05]. Stripping depth splits them too. Storage Unit Scrapper chisels ICs off low-grade boards with grinder-sharpened chisels, banking about 3 pounds in 8 months at roughly $15 a pound, yet skips MOSFETs on time grounds even after a viewer pointed out they contain copper [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:09:15]. Shark Scrapper leaves CPUs on the board for weighing, notes that the price list weighs CPUs and RAM on the board unless separated first, and tells operators to balance stripping time against weighing as-is [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:21:54]. Pick one grading authority per buyer and let the buyer's own tool, not a chatbot, set the bin.

On the test-equipment side of surplus, the argument is brand premium versus bench value. EEVblog prices a Fluke at 2 to 3 times a Brymen, calls the Brymen better bang for buck, and argues the Fluke premium buys lifetime warranty, made-in-USA status for government and military purchasing, 21-plus years of production longevity and metrology history, adding that a 30-plus-year-old Fluke off eBay that works is almost certainly in spec [ay9wFQAW19Y @ 00:16:32]. PA's Shop, with 40 years in the field, ran two uncalibrated meters, a Fluke 87V and a FNIRSI 2C53T, and found the cheap unit reading 119.75 on 120 volts AC against the Fluke's 120, reading a 3300 microfarad cap at 3420 microfarads where the Fluke cannot measure capacitance, and called the FNIRSI a good starter unit [5Qaj8MhpVIc @ 00:15:18]. Keysight pitches the middle path: refurbished units processed in Penang alongside new production, with calibration certificates with data, a 101-point quality check and the same standard warranty, sold as like new, for less [TipNUpHGLw0 @ 00:01:03]. ElectroHoard shows the hoarder's arbitrage: GW distortion analyzers are cheap while HP units cost more, and an HP multimeter sits cheap on eBay [2ZRWlImJMzE @ 00:08:04]. Buy the name when traceability matters; buy the clone when troubleshooting is the job.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest trap is buying boards on trust. Shark Scrapper's Asheville near-pallet looked like one lot but held dirties likely from customers after the Helen flooding, boards still bolted to metal plates that must come off before weighing, and enough mixed grades to produce 19 categories [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:01:09]. Paying a flat mixed-board price for that load would have risked getting taken to the cleaners, and the rule that follows is simple: don't ever buy a full gaylord box of boards believing they are what they say they are, unless the mixed price reflects the risk [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:20:51]. Batteries are a hard stop: Storage Unit Scrapper notes boards with batteries cannot be sent, so batteries and heat sinks come off first, and Shark Scrapper repeats that the battery has to go before selling [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:20:35]. Leftover tape is a quieter loss; Storage Unit Scrapper heat-guns it off so boards ship clean and are not downgraded [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 00:15:37]. Shark Scrapper also admits the bin layout for that job did not work, a real bottleneck with more pallets coming from two PC repair clients and a scrapyard. Fix the sort floor before the next pallet lands, not after.

Local yard prices move against the scrapper. Storage Unit Scrapper once sold whole laptops to the scrapyard at 98 cents a pound and now gets 15 cents a pound locally, while laptops listed on HiBid went for $4 to $5 through a business that lists 1,000 items a week there [4TJbVuWoX3A @ 01:11:32]. Labor is the other leak: dirty aluminum brings 18 cents a pound, and the time and effort to clean it to pure aluminum is not worth it, the same time logic that has the channel skipping MOSFETs [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:06:37]. That channel also sits on a copper tote of about 100 pounds as money in the bank, betting EVs and data centers keep copper rising [bAvpAOGu8Kw @ 00:30:33]. On the buying side, Shark Scrapper normally charges a 10% sorting fee but may waive it for a new client to build goodwill, then plans to teach that client to pre-sort, since even a cursory sort earns the client more [6XObIp5c91U @ 00:06:47]. Waiving the fee trades margin for supply, and that supply is fragile: the same client is weighing scrapping against repairing because the real money is in repairing. Price every hour of stripping against the current yard sheet, not last year's.

On the instrument side, the failures come from trusting a number without checking what produced it. Data Center MEP System shows a meter set to 1200/1 on a 1600/1 CT under-reading by 25%, a generic 1,000-volt DC instruction issued for a controller rated 250 volts DC, where the call is to hold the test and raise a TQ, and a 96.0 plus-or-minus 2.0 L/s flow against a 95.0 minimum that drops to 94.0 and does not demonstrate the requirement [SQ3IcEPrZ9g @ 00:18:28]. The same channel's ICT resilience session warns that two network paths can look independent on a logical drawing and still fail together, and that packets resuming at 0.18 seconds does not close the test when the application recovers at 1.4 seconds, the figure that governs acceptance [BqHUjrp6rGo @ 00:22:34]. Bench gear carries its own blind spots: ElectroHoard's TH2685 capacitor analyzer only goes to 200 volts, too low for 450 to 500 volt tube caps [2ZRWlImJMzE @ 00:09:46]. Live Mobile Technology adds that an ethernet cable can look completely fine and still cause network problems, and that a failed cable should not be replaced immediately; look at what LinkIQ is telling you first, then diagnose, fix and retest [2tSL4NkyYWM @ 00:02:33].
