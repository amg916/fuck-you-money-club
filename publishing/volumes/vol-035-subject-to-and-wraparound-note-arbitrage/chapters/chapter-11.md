# Chapter 11: Buying Mortgage Notes: Discounted and Non-Performing Loan Investing

*Where to source notes, buying at deep discounts, non-performing note resolutions and six exit strategies, notes vs. rentals returns, note funds vs. direct buying, first-purchase stories, and 'become the bank' breakdowns*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Note buyers, from boutique shops to a $600-700M NPL desk, agree that non-performing mortgage paper trades cheap because banks refuse partial payments and dump defaults at 25-60% of value. They also report that most defaults trace to a life event and that many of those borrowers can be brought back to paying. The operator consensus is to buy first-position, secured, single-family collateral with deep equity. Hire a licensed servicer. Then exit through the borrower via forbearance, modification and resale rather than foreclosure. Street pricing runs from 96 cents for scratch-and-dent paper down to roughly 40 cents at notice of sale, and PPR prices off the lower of UPB or property value. Reported results include 90% reperformance, an 11.5% average yield and a 20% IRR on a 189-loan trade. Practitioners split on whether NPL foreclose-and-flip suits beginners and whether to buy notes directly or through a fund.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator here agrees that the inventory exists because of a broken bank workflow, not broken borrowers. Lauren Wells of 7e Investments says roughly 90 to 95 percent of the non-performing notes the firm buys trace back to a life event: job loss, a death, a health crisis or COVID [nauUgmh1YZg @ 00:04:00]. 7e's fund walkthrough adds the mechanical trigger. Banks refuse partial payments, so a three-month delinquency snowballs into a twelve-month one. The firm then steps in to renegotiate so the borrower's payments become investor distributions [El1w73FgjM0 @ 00:00:11]. Larry Hoffman explains why the paper hits the street cheap. Banks dump non-performing loans to free lending capacity and satisfy regulators, at 25 to 60 percent of their actual dollar value [R_jwbBWh1LA @ 00:04:43]. FIXnotes adds that charge-off is how most loans reach the secondary market in the first place [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:03:33]. The consensus play follows directly. Buy the debt at a deep discount, step into the lender's shoes and fix the payment relationship the bank was too rigid to fix. The business is the spread between what the seller accepts and what the borrower ultimately pays or the collateral fetches. These operators price from that gap rather than from face value.

The second point of agreement is collateral discipline. Bill Mencarow of Paper Source tells beginners to buy first-position notes only and to stick to single-family homes or farmland. Mencarow also tells them to ask whether they would actually want to own the property, and rules out factories and shopping centers [LuxJXWLFKHI @ 00:13:54]. FIXnotes puts a number on why lien position matters. An unsecured loan is worth roughly 100 times less, about 50 basis points versus 50 cents, so checking county records for secured status and lien position comes before any bid [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:01:23]. Sierra Davis, a data scientist who buys and originates notes, warns that junior liens carry wipe-out risk and pushes secured paper only [UimgP_cJydU @ 00:17:39]. Institutional buyers run the same filter at scale: PPR Capital Management's NPL book is 1-to-4 unit residential and 99 percent single-family detached [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:02:11]. The equity cushion is the other half of the rule. Mencarow calls a down payment under 15 to 20 percent scary and says mobile homes with zero or $1,000 down usually default. The best collateral, per Mencarow, is an owner-occupied single-family home in a stable, diversified economy, backed by an amortized note rather than a balloon. That cushion lets an operator negotiate from strength instead of hoping the market holds.

Third, practitioners agree the money is made through the borrower, not the courthouse. PPR's Taylor Nelson says every exit decision starts with borrower intent. Nelson adds that exiting through the borrower (reperform, season, resell) is more profitable '99% of the time' [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:06:37]. Mencarow has been investing in notes since the 1980s, has never foreclosed and negotiates instead [LuxJXWLFKHI @ 00:16:46]. FIXnotes lays out six exits: full or discounted payoff, modification or structured settlement, cash for keys or deed in lieu, short sale, foreclosure, and an NPL loan sale to a local investor. It ranks foreclosure as the least favorable of the six [qxx8ymWOrGU @ 00:03:34]. Colin Whitenack describes the same posture. Close, get a servicer involved because you cannot service the mortgage yourself, then enter 'problem-solving mode,' where the borrower either reperforms or gets help exiting the property [gxiK62dzE34 @ 00:00:42]. The servicer point is near-universal. PPR outsourced servicing after Dodd-Frank, Davis insists on a licensed servicer, and FIXnotes calls skipping servicing to save $25 a false economy. A reperformed loan can then be held for cash flow or sold to performing-note buyers. That resale is the flip that turns a distressed purchase into a realized gain, with foreclosure kept as the backstop that gives negotiation its teeth.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Pricing is where the operators get specific. Lee Arnold's ladder starts from a $100,000 note at 8 percent over 30 years, about $833 a month, and walks the price down as the paper gets uglier. Arnold quotes 96 cents for scratch-and-dent paper paid past the 15-day grace period, 92 cents for 30-to-60-day slow-pay, and 80 to 84 cents once the borrower stops responding [tszcHb-Spo4 @ 00:02:25]. The price falls to 60 to 65 cents at notice of default or lis pendens and roughly 40 cents at notice of sale. At that stage, a modification that restores the note to $100,000 turns a $40,000 buy into a $60,000 profit [tszcHb-Spo4 @ 00:03:39]. PPR's Nelson prices off the lower of the loan balance or the property value. That means 80 to 90 cents of UPB on equity loans and about 70 cents of property value on upside-down ones [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:13:56]. Wells reports that 7e buys at 40 to 60 percent of value from credit unions, whole-loan traders, liquidating funds and a broker's weekly list [nauUgmh1YZg @ 00:05:54]. Sierra Davis runs the yield math backward. Take a $40,000 UPB note paying $500 a month at 8.5 percent, secured by a $100,000 property. Offering $22,000 targets a 12 to 15 percent yield and nets about $475 a month after servicing.

The diligence and closing sequence is consistent across shops. PPR prices the data tape first and submits a bid. Once awarded, it runs 30 to 60 days of diligence on collateral, the assignment chain, tax and title, and a drive-by BPO, then closes 30 to 45 days after that [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:15:09]. Davis compresses the retail version into an indicative offer followed by 14 to 21 days of diligence on the three Ps: people, property and paperwork. Davis prefers 24 to 48 months of payment seasoning, closes with an assignment and allonge, then boards the loan with a servicer [UimgP_cJydU @ 00:33:40]. Whitenack's checklist is the field version. Order a BPO, review payment history, rates, amounts paid, the last payment and anything unusual, and confirm insurance is in place and taxes are current [gxiK62dzE34 @ 00:00:16]. Sourcing feeds the front of that pipeline. Dan of Fusion Notes names Paperstac, Tradex and Watermark Exchange, plus Facebook buy/sell groups where you post your buy criteria and conferences like Note Expo, DME and IMN. Dan then points to two overlooked channels. Foreclosure attorneys have large investor networks, and loan servicers know which clients are selling portfolios or closing funds [PXDW2SqUfMA @ 00:01:19]. Davis caps exposure at or below 75 percent ITV and prices to the worst-case exit.

Once the loan is boarded, the workout script decides the return. FIXnotes says the two biggest points of loan resolution are building rapport and gathering intel, while playing good cop against an unseen 'loan committee' [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:05:29]. Every call runs on three questions: What happened? Where are you now? What do you want to do? [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:06:48]. In the FIXnotes lifecycle, a loan goes 90 days late, gets a demand letter, then moves to forbearance, modification, reinstatement or foreclosure. FIXnotes favors forbearance because it keeps the foreclosure pending and avoids modification phantom income, and writes its modifications at 9 percent and up. 7e's sequence is an attorney demand letter, then a negotiated plan, then reperformance [El1w73FgjM0 @ 00:07:24]. Wells describes the plan itself: forbearance or a trial plan for six months, then a loan modification. Arrears are wrapped to the end of the loan or paid quarterly, and the reperformed note is then resold for profit [nauUgmh1YZg @ 00:05:15]. PPR's modification toolbox adds deferring principal to a balloon and extending terms to 30 or 40 years. The FIXnotes exit menu supplies the stick: a deed in lieu, cash for keys, or a short sale where waiving the deficiency buys cooperation. 7e frames having multiple exits as the negotiating leverage itself.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Get on the Tape Lists
   - Build inbound deal flow from exchanges, groups, conferences and direct relationships.
   - Get on the lists that send note tapes from small banks, hedge funds and private investors, then layer in Paperstac, Tradex, Watermark Exchange and Facebook buy/sell groups where you post your buy criteria [Colin Whitenack | Real Estate & Investing — gxiK62dzE34 @ 00:00:00].
2. Price the Tape and Bid
   - Bid off the lower of UPB or property value, not off face amount.
   - Pay 80-90 cents of UPB on equity loans and about 70 cents of property value on upside-down loans. Submit the bid before any deep diligence is ordered [PPR Capital Management — DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:13:56].
3. Diligence the Three Ps and Close
   - Verify people, property and paperwork, then close and board with a servicer.
   - Run 14-21 days of diligence on people, property and paperwork, preferring 24-48 months of seasoning. Close with an assignment and allonge, then board the loan with a licensed servicer [Sierra Davis — UimgP_cJydU @ 00:33:40].
4. Work Out, Reperform, Resell
   - Move the borrower through a trial plan into a modification, then sell the reperformed note.
   - Use forbearance or a six-month trial plan, then a loan modification with arrears wrapped to the end or paid quarterly. Then resell the reperformed note for profit [Preferred Trust — nauUgmh1YZg @ 00:05:15].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to run your own tape or hand capital to a fund. Rick Allen and TJ Osterman of Paperstac put DIY returns at 12 to 18 percent, with non-performing loans hitting 40 to 50 percent, but they warn flatly that you will lose some deals, while fund returns sit in the 8 to 12 percent band with risk spread across the book [EdBetMgLDeQ @ 00:06:20]. Their answer is a hybrid: park $100K of $150K in funds and run the remainder through partials and hypothecation, or split $500K into $400K passive and $100K active [EdBetMgLDeQ @ 00:15:35]. 7e Investments sells the passive side directly, quoting a $5K Reg A+ minimum at 7 percent for one year or 8 percent for four years, and an accredited-only Reg D Class E5 bond paying 11 percent monthly on a $500K minimum [El1w73FgjM0 @ 00:03:15]. Kevin Shortle, running a personal book after 30-plus years, reports an 11.5 percent average yield at 86 percent investment-to-balance and under 60 percent investment-to-value [VrGeElvxk5E @ 00:17:02]. The operator read: a solo buyer who executes well matches or beats the top fund tier, but absorbs every blown deal personally instead of across 120-plus pooled assets.

Practitioners split hard on whether non-performing paper is a beginner play. Stealth Wealth pitches buying non-performing notes, foreclosing, and flipping as low risk and says it works especially well for investors who are just starting out [0m8vHNeASRY @ 00:06:24]. Bill Mencarow of Paper Source tells newcomers on Toby Mathis's channel to get a mentor and stick only with first-position notes, and adds that in notes since the 80s Bill has never foreclosed, negotiating every time instead [LuxJXWLFKHI @ 00:13:54]. FIXnotes treats the property as the outcome to avoid: set upset bids low because REOs are the opposite of why they are in the business, and buy under 60 cents so even a 70-cent recovery still pays [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:04:18]. Taylor Nelson at PPR Capital Management ranks exiting through the borrower as more profitable 99 percent of the time, reperforming, seasoning, and reselling rather than taking title [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:06:37]. The FIXnotes six-exit menu still keeps foreclosure live as the least favorable lever, alongside cash for keys, deed in lieu as the stick, and short sales where waiving the deficiency buys cooperation. Net: the foreclose-and-flip camp is a minority voice, and the operators with the biggest books push negotiation and treat the courthouse as leverage, not the business model.

Pricing frameworks diverge too. Lee Arnold anchors to cents on the note balance by delinquency stage: 96 for scratch-and-dent, 92 for slow-pay, 80 to 84 for non-performing with no response, 60 to 65 at notice of default, and about 40 at notice of sale [tszcHb-Spo4 @ 00:02:25]. PPR instead works off the lower of loan balance or property value, paying 80 to 90 cents of UPB on equity loans and about 70 cents of property value on upside-down loans [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:14:14]. Larry Hoffman frames bank dumps at 25 to 60 percent of actual dollar value, while Preferred Trust guest Lauren Wells of 7e buys at 40 to 60 percent [R_jwbBWh1LA @ 00:04:58]. Servicing splits the same way: PPR outsourced to a licensed servicer that executes its loss-mitigation waterfall, while FIXnotes pushes client-managed servicing and calls skipping a servicer to save $25 a false economy [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:09:31]. On exits, Bill Mencarow sells the next nine years of payments and keeps the tail, Tracy Rewey sells a partial that makes the seller whole so everything out in the future is gravy, and Kevin Shortle buys the next 40 of 56 payments. Pick one pricing model and one servicing model before you bid, and run every tape through the same math.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Time is the silent killer of NPL returns. Taylor Nelson of PPR describes a loan underwritten for a 12-month hold that a Chapter 13 bankruptcy filing stretched to 36 months, still closing at roughly a 20 to 25 percent return but tying up capital three times longer than projected [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:20:18]. Sierra Davis flags that foreclosure timelines vary by state, with New York able to take years, and that junior liens carry wipe-out risk [UimgP_cJydU @ 00:15:35]. Kevin Shortle puts Florida foreclosure at about 18 months, warns that a contested contract for deed can be forced into foreclosure by a judge, and notes COVID-era modifications stretched some loans to 60 years before rules changed October 1, with foreclosures expected to rise from here [VrGeElvxk5E @ 00:28:24]. PPR's own 189-loan trade, roughly $23M UPB against $50M of property value bought for about $18M, still saw about 20 percent foreclose, even as it produced about $8M gross profit at a 20 percent IRR over an 18-month weighted hold [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:21:34]. The check: model the worst-case state timeline and a bankruptcy delay into every bid, because even a well-bought 189-loan book carried a one-in-five foreclosure rate and holds that can triple. Plan reserves accordingly.

Overpaying is the most repeated self-inflicted wound. FIXnotes warns that borrowers sitting on 3 to 5 percent legacy rates can simply reinstate, leaving you holding cheap debt, so the bid has to drop for those files, while their own modifications get written at 9 percent and up [lxjMbDIOEGw @ 00:07:28]. PPR confirms the resale side of that trap: low-coupon loans fetch a weaker price when you go to sell the reperformed paper [DEk2gIFHJiM @ 00:12:02]. Kevin Shortle sees the same mistake on note creation, where a seller carries a $90K or $80K note on a $100K lot and Kevin pays at most $70K for it; the fix is structuring a $70K first, a $20K second, and $10K down so the paper is sellable [VrGeElvxk5E @ 00:21:12]. Bill Mencarow adds collateral traps: a down payment under 15 to 20 percent is scary, and mobile homes sold with $0 or $1,000 down usually default, so stick to single-family homes or farmland over factories and shopping centers, and ask whether you would want to own the property [LuxJXWLFKHI @ 00:14:30]. Personal Finance for Everyone lists skipping diligence, overpaying, impatience, and thin cash reserves as the four standard ways a cheap note becomes a costly mistake.

Even the passive route has trapdoors. Rick Allen and TJ Osterman describe note investing as very passive until it is not, then very involved, and admit Reg A+ was exorbitant once you stack an attorney, accountant, controller, fund admin, and two audits, which is why they would pick Reg D in hindsight [EdBetMgLDeQ @ 00:04:08]. Lauren Wells of 7e reports the honest loss line from just under 100 loans: less than $8,000 lost across three loans in a year and a half, with about 5 percent going through full foreclosure via a partner REO firm [nauUgmh1YZg @ 00:09:18]. 7e's own fund walkthrough labels the investments speculative and illiquid and flags rising delinquencies and flattening values as the reason for stricter buying [El1w73FgjM0 @ 00:12:05]. Tracy Rewey, in the business since 1988, tells newcomers to expect that not every note pays and to learn the financial calculator [Il6eOQ_69VM @ 00:23:49]. Fusion Notes adds a sourcing trap: the best deals never really hit the street, so a buyer who fails to close inside a stated buy box never sees the good paper. The economics check is simple: budget for losses, fund overhead, and dead time before you count the yield.
