# Chapter 04: Seller Finance Fundamentals: Making the Seller the Bank

## Executive Summary

Pace Morby, Key & Capital and Greg Schwartz agree that seller finance turns a free-and-clear owner into the bank, negotiated on four terms: price, down payment, interest rate and length. Pace pays the seller's number in exchange for terms: Susan's mobile home closed at $110K, principal-only, $10K down split into two $5K installments, $375/mo, while the best cash offer was $57K. Mario's 30-unit building traded at $3M, $0 down, 4%, 50 years, no balloon, against a $1.9M cash offer. Pace prices assignment fees at $2K per $100/mo net cash flow and targets 30%+ cash-on-cash. Key & Capital counters that a 0% seller note is not clever, models 6-9% rates with 5-7 year balloons, and flags due-on-sale and seasoning traps. Greg Schwartz demands credit and background checks; Pace verifies income only.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator here frames seller finance the same way: the owner stops being a landlord and becomes the bank, and the deal is built on four levers, not one. Pace Morby lists them as purchase price, down payment, interest rate and length of time, first proving it with an iPhone demo where the market offer was $550 and he bought at $800 as $80/mo for 10 months [_M0q3Da-vxU @ 00:26:22]. Greg Schwartz in Bryan-College Station runs the identical checklist, telling operators to start with the down payment at 20, 10 or 5% before touching rate, amortization or monthly payment, then choose between a 3, 5 or 7 year balloon for liquidity or a 5, 7 or 10 year prepayment penalty for steady income [_xN91ltYUa0 @ 00:01:43]. The consensus on seller psychology is equally tight. Pace separates Sub2 sellers, driven by pain such as divorce or no equity, from seller-finance sellers, driven by gain: they want more than the market will bear, often after pulling the house off the market [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:00:00]. The target list follows directly. Roughly 30% of properties are owned free and clear, Sub2 lists filter for 60%+ equity, and seller-finance lists should aim at 100% equity [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:31:09].

The second pillar is that terms beat price. Pace's line is that the value of a property is never the purchase price but what you can do with it, and he taught it to Susan through his F-150 story: a truck Kelley Blue Book valued at $5K sat on Craigslist at $10K for 3 months with no calls until he added 'will take payments,' then sold for $12.5K with $1K down and $350/mo [6FXApQ1y2KE @ 00:10:17]. The same logic drives his claim that creative finance pays the seller more money every time; he says he has never seen a creative transaction that was not a win for the seller, the buyer and any agent involved, while in a cash deal only the buyer wins [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:01:26]. On the masterclass, Pace puts the contrast in numbers: on a $200K ARV, wholesalers pay $110-130K and only about 5% of sellers accept, while creative finance pays 80-100 cents on the dollar [-E2cCPmM_Q0 @ 00:03:49]. His Leilani case shows why distressed owners say yes: a zero-down VA loan, a husband who died 30 days after closing, an 11% commission hit if she listed, so Pace took the $1,800 payment subject-to and now rents it for $2,800 [Teh6C2LGk8g @ 00:05:56].

Protection is the third point of agreement, and every speaker lands on the same paper trail. Pace says the deal structure is how you protect the seller and that the main protection is the buyer's profit; on top of that he hires a servicing company such as Westar, Evergreen or Del Toro at about $100 setup and $17 per payment drafted 5 days early, writes a new insurance policy listing the seller as additional insured, and records both the 1-1.5 page promissory note and the 80-90 page deed of trust [w1wq3uZtNx8 @ 01:06:54]. In his paperwork Q&A he draws the line on public record: the purchase and sale agreement is never recorded, but the deed, deed of trust and note are [IdhS21bvT_o @ 00:29:24]. Greg Schwartz mirrors it for Texas: a promissory note plus a recorded deed of trust, lower closing costs, a local attorney, and payments made direct or through a servicer at $30-40 [_xN91ltYUa0 @ 00:06:17]. Key & Capital adds title insurance and a recorded deed of trust to the checklist and tells sellers to use a licensed originator before carrying paper [dzuLMB2Szxc @ 00:11:14]. Where they agree most is the order: structure first, so the buyer cash flows, then the paperwork locks it.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The call script is codified. Pace records every appointment, then pivots with one line: 'I would be willing to come up to your price if you would give me terms,' followed by the F-150 story and a reframe that he will upgrade the seller from landlord to the bank [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:04:27]. When the conversation turns to buyer risk, he says 'abducted by aliens' instead of 'hit by a bus.' He never sends the first offer. The seller names terms, and when Susan asked for $20K down at 8%, he answered that he was probably not her buyer and stepped back [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:12:32]. The rebuilt structure on her mobile home: $110K, principal-only, $10K down paid as $5K now and $5K after six months, seller-paid closing costs, inherited tenants at about $1,650 rent throwing off roughly $1K/mo net that funds the down payment, and a $375/mo note matching her RV spot cost [6FXApQ1y2KE @ 00:14:14]. The vocabulary matters. Pace says he never writes 'zero percent seller finance' in any offer; every offer says principal-only payments [IdhS21bvT_o @ 01:02:52]. On the masterclass version the term runs 20 years, the best cash offer was $57K, and the house is now worth about $233K, roughly $120K of appreciation in 3 years.

The unit economics are just as specific. Pace's deal test: the terms must beat a bank asking 30-40% down, then the cash-on-cash return has to clear the bar; investors want a 12% minimum, he wants 30%+, because the lower the down payment, the higher the return [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:23:58]. Wholesalers get paid off a cash-flow formula: $2K of assignment fee per $100/mo of net cash flow, or $5K per $100 at 0% interest, which with average cash flow above $500/mo puts the average fee above $10K [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:25:18]. The ceiling is the biggest assignment fee of his life: $210K, paid as the buyer, on 30 Texas units at $3M, no money down, 4%, 50 years, no balloon, about 10x the $21K/mo cash flow, when the wholesaler's cash number was $1.7M [-E2cCPmM_Q0 @ 00:07:45]. Only 20-30% of opportunities need no money; the rest get funded by partners or private lenders sitting in second position at 8-12%, where $10K at 10% costs about $80/mo. Pace calls it financing your financing, and on a Chandler flip the interest sat in escrow. His all-in entry cost, down payment and closing costs together, averages about 8%, against the 30-35% down a bank demands, roughly $80-100K on a $300K house [_M0q3Da-vxU @ 00:43:32].

Term length and balloon mechanics are where Pace protects his downside and the seller's number. His default is a payment based on 30 years with a 10-year balloon, backed by a balloon-extension clause that automatically renews for 5 years if the property doesn't appraise at the purchase price [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:34:02]. The Jillian deal in Atlanta shows the full stack: $1.1M, $0 down, no payments for 6 months, then about $6K/mo at 3% on a 30-year amortization with a 10-year balloon, plus a clause requiring her to buy him a steak dinner; five years later Airbnb was outlawed, he sold at $1.5M and rolled the $400K gain into an RV park [w1wq3uZtNx8 @ 00:35:23]. Key & Capital benchmarks what conventional seller-carry looks like: 5-20% down with a 10% median, 6-9% rates or 3-8% for investors, balloons at 3-10 years and usually 5-7, closing in 7-21 days against 30-45 for a bank, with no prepayment penalty [dzuLMB2Szxc @ 00:07:34]. Greg Schwartz closes the loop on collections: the buyer pays the seller directly or through a servicer at $30-40, and the note ends by refinance, sale, buyout, the balloon coming due, or foreclosure, which he calls easier than you would think in Texas [_xN91ltYUa0 @ 00:07:28].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the free-and-clear target list
   - Pull owners with 100% equity and source seller-finance listings from Landwatch, the MLS and owner-finance aggregator sites.
   - Roughly 30% of properties are free and clear; seller-finance lists target 100% equity versus 60%+ for Sub2, and Landwatch showed 25-30 seller-finance listings in Kalispell alone [Flipping Mastery TV — MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:31:09].
2. Run the pivot call
   - Record the appointment, deliver the terms line, tell the F-150 story, and reframe the seller from landlord to bank.
   - Pace's line is 'I would be willing to come up to your price if you would give me terms,' followed immediately by the F-150 story [Flipping Mastery TV — MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:04:27].
3. Structure the four terms
   - Give the seller price, then win down payment, rate and a long enough term; anchor on 30-year amortization with a 10-year balloon and an extension clause.
   - The seller wins on price while Pace wins on down payment, rate and term, and a 3-year payoff is too short [Pace Morby — 8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:06:31]; the extension auto-renews 5 years if the property doesn't appraise [Flipping Mastery TV — MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:34:02].
4. Paper, record and service the note
   - Record the promissory note and deed of trust, add the seller as additional insured, and route payments through a servicer.
   - Pace uses Westar, Evergreen or Del Toro at ~$100 setup and ~$17 per payment [Pace Morby — w1wq3uZtNx8 @ 01:06:54]; Greg Schwartz closes with a recorded deed of trust and a local attorney [Greg Schwartz | Investing in Bryan-College Station — _xN91ltYUa0 @ 00:06:17].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in seller-finance pricing is the interest rate. Pace Morby writes principal-only paper and refuses to say zero percent out loud: "I don't ever say zero percent seller finance in any of my offers we always say principal only payments" [IdhS21bvT_o @ 01:02:52]. His logic is that a seller charging 4% simply gets a lower price, Pace's illustration being $60K instead of $90K, and that a refinance would wipe out the interest they expected anyway, so 0% locks the seller's number [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:04:37]. To back the tax side, he cites a CPA who has sold more than 40 properties at 0% for 1.25 times retail value, while admitting he is unsure of the IRS guideline he quotes for notes over $150K [IdhS21bvT_o @ 01:01:44]. Key & Capital runs the opposite playbook. Their Dallas duplex math puts the seller at 6% against a 7.32% bank loan, saving the buyer $210 a month, and they quote typical seller rates of 6-9%, or 3-8% for investors. They flag the mid-term AFR at 4.35% and land the counterpunch bluntly: "So, a 0% seller note is not clever" [dzuLMB2Szxc @ 00:11:35]. Same deal shape, two completely different answers to where the seller actually gets paid.

Term length is the second fault line. Pace pushes amortization as long as the seller will tolerate: the Mario deal ran $3M at 4% over 50 years with no balloon, the seller himself proposing 50 after Pace pitched 40, and Pace calls a 3-year payoff too short for the buyer to win [w1wq3uZtNx8 @ 01:55:13] [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:06:31]. His default fallback is a 30-year amortization with a 10-year balloon plus a balloon-extension clause that auto-renews five years if the property won't appraise at the purchase price. Key & Capital treats the balloon as the first of three traps and describes market norms of balloons at 3-10 years, usually 5-7, with the duplex example coming due at five years with roughly $223K still owed [dzuLMB2Szxc @ 00:05:35]. Their defenses overlap with Pace's (an extension clause, a longer balloon, a refinance plan, or a fully amortizing note) but their floor is far lower: never accept a balloon shorter than 12 months. Greg Schwartz frames the choice as a menu rather than a rule: a 3, 5 or 7-year balloon suits buyers who need liquidity or plan a BRRRR refinance, while a 5, 7 or 10-year prepayment penalty suits sellers who want steady income [_xN91ltYUa0 @ 00:01:43].

Risk tolerance on the underlying loan and the paperwork splits the camp again. Pace says the due-on-sale clause has been called 10 times in roughly 40,000 deals, calls it "really easy to overcome," and keeps a deed-back plus a pre-signed lease option as the remedy [EO9EtMTTjm4 @ 01:25:45]. Key & Capital lists due-on-sale under Garn-St Germain as trap number two, alongside Fannie Mae's 12-month seasoning on refinances that pay off a seller note [dzuLMB2Szxc @ 00:08:31]. On recording, Pace records the promissory note and deed of trust on standard seller-finance closes, yet on a building carrying an SBA 504 loan he used a contract for deed with the deed left unrecorded in a safety deposit box so nobody would know ownership had transferred [p0ukMOdzoAc @ 00:11:34]. Key & Capital calls for title insurance and a recorded deed of trust; Greg Schwartz likewise records the deed of trust. Buyer vetting diverges just as hard: on vehicles Pace runs "verification of income" only, no credit check, with bankruptcies and divorces acceptable, while Greg Schwartz sets income, credit and a background check as the minimum and notes full Dodd-Frank underwriting costs over $1,000 [_xN91ltYUa0 @ 00:05:11]. Pace's view is that the buyer's profit, not the paperwork, is the seller's main protection.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Pace's own origin story is the first failure mode on the table. As a renovation contractor for Opendoor, Offerpad and Zillow, he financed a flipper's rehabs, putting up $100K and getting roughly $105K back at sale; over four years the unpaid balances ran into the millions [_M0q3Da-vxU @ 00:01:30]. He then liquidated his rentals to pour another $700K in, and the flipper filed bankruptcy owing about $16M. His lawsuit rule from his seller-finance teaching is blunt: "The number one deterrent for lawsuits is making sure you're not the one that writes the first check" [w1wq3uZtNx8 @ 01:28:46]. Lease options, a structure he says he is obsessed with, burned him once when heirs contested the contract and it cost $7K to sue. Shena's Mesa duplex shows the investor-side version of the trap: bought in 2021 with 30% down, one unit vacant for months, listed by an agent for about four months without a sale, until a Sub2 student offered back the 30% plus payments in exchange for 0% interest [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:13:52]. Pace frames that as the norm rather than the exception, claiming 50% of investors throw the house back on the market within a year [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:14:52].

The seller side carries its own set of traps. Pace names the biggest downfall in seller finance as sellers wanting a very high price, and his workaround is to let the seller state terms first and walk when they are wrong: Susan opened at $20K down and 8%, and Pace answered "probably not your buyer" and stepped back [MeNrueZIYeE @ 00:12:32]. Sellers who try to stay landlords instead face his Arizona eviction clock: late in month one, eviction filed in month two, tenant out by months three or four, repairs in five and six, marketing in seven, a new tenant in month eight, roughly seven months of payments with no rent [8VNf8TfmufA @ 00:09:13]. Key & Capital adds the regulatory and tax drag that sellers routinely miss. Dodd-Frank tiers allow one property a year, up to three with a fully amortizing note, and more than five makes the seller a creditor; §453(i) depreciation recapture is taxed in year one even under installment treatment [dzuLMB2Szxc @ 00:11:14]. On the 1031 path, Pace warns that the 90-day pressure of a strict exchange lets replacement-property sellers exploit the clock, and he prefers a delayed 1031 with cost segregation [w1wq3uZtNx8 @ 00:44:10].

Vehicles and businesses add operational failure points that real estate doesn't. On the Escalade, Grant's balance exceeded market value and a Facebook Marketplace listing at payoff drew zero responses; attorney Sean Sinclair spent about $3,000 of research to find a vehicle cannot be bought subject-to in North Carolina, forcing a $0-down lease option instead [o3cjfzx5Ykk @ 00:11:15]. Turo cash flow cut both ways: Angelo's Turo projection showed about $2,000 a month on a roughly $1,000 payment, but Grant stopped renting after a Denali renter smoked weed in the car and Pace rejected Turo as not worth the risk [o3cjfzx5Ykk @ 00:08:40]. Pace also flags that a stolen-vehicle history can trigger a salvage title or make the car uninsurable, killing Turo use, so Carfax gets pulled first [B5EMtjV6RJM @ 00:18:40]. On businesses, brokers who refuse to submit offers get waited out, and Pace warns that buying a business is not passive until people are hired in place and the operation is automated; the Alaska carpet cleaner ran roughly $6.5K weekly payroll and 15 interviews a day in peak season [p0ukMOdzoAc @ 00:01:24]. And on institutional sellers, his verdict is short: don't waste time sending Opendoor creative offers.
