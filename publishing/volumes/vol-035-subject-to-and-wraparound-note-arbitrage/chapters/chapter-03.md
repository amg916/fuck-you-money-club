# Chapter 03: Wrap Arbitrage: Selling on a Wrap for Spread, Down Payment, and Cash Flow

*Subject-to wraparounds, $1,000/month wrap case studies, low-equity owner-finance wraps, mirror wrap vs. sub-to, wrap vs. lease option vs. contract for deed, underwriting spreadsheets, private-lender recycling, and wrapping buyers banks turn down*

## Executive Summary

Wrap arbitrage practitioners agree the money is in the spread: buy or hold a low-rate underlying loan, then sell to a non-bankable buyer at a higher price and rate. Dom Inglese wraps a 2.75% Tampa sub-to at 7% for $400,000, banking $10,000 at entry on a $50,000 down payment; Grant Kemp turns $20,000 of equity into $142,377 of total collections; Nick Disney carries notes around 10.9% on private money. Operators structure the payment first, sell the monthly number instead of the rate, and prefer principal over interest. They split on servicing, with Horne Real Estate Law always using a servicer while Nick Disney pays lenders directly, and on exit, with Creative Finance Playbook declining to sell on wraps at all and preferring lease options for depreciation.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus — Spread, Non-Bankable Buyers, and Seller Security

The money in this play is the spread, and the practitioners say it plainly. Vena Jones Cox tells her class that most of the money made in long-term real estate investing is made on spread, and she traces wraps back to her father using them in the 1960s [jeHouEguhhE @ 00:32:32]. Grant Kemp of Propelio TV defines a wraparound as any time you finance someone a subordinate lien at a higher price or payment than the underlying lien, which means the operator profits from both a price markup and a rate markup [pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:00:34]. Dom Inglese runs the same logic on his Tampa portfolio: he holds the underlying mortgage at 3%, sells to a non-qualifying buyer at 8% with 10% down, and keeps the difference between 8 and 3% as cash flow [VRUDR8FXNo0 @ 00:05:03]. On a separate Tampa sub-to locked at 2.75% almost three years ago, he wraps the buyer at 7%, frames the rate as in line or even a little lower than bank rates, and notes his low-rate loan amortizes faster so the equity spread keeps widening every month [Lv34MZLHg1I @ 00:04:32]. Three income streams stack in every deal: the down payment, the monthly spread, and the back-end principal.

Every practitioner selling on a wrap is hunting the same buyer: someone who can carry a monthly payment but cannot or will not get through a bank. Dom Inglese calls them penalty box buyers, ITIN holders and people paid in cash who are simply not bankable, and he sells his Seminole Heights block house as-is to that pool [StKrDLiCONM @ 00:04:00]. In his homeownership pitch he widens the net to buyers with cash-heavy income, a recent missed payment, or no way to bring the 40% a bank wants, while still vetting each one for affordability before he sells [VRUDR8FXNo0 @ 00:01:38]. Philip Louden of WrapAcademy targets first-generation Americans, a buyer pool he credits with a trillion dollars in spending power, along with 1099 earners [oGD3QKECaGk @ 00:03:04]. Nick Disney, fifteen years into the business out of San Antonio, sells at or under $200,000, runs credit through an RMLO, and reports scores that often come back NA [DAhcIhHIoI0 @ 00:05:24]. The consensus is blunt: the wrap buyer is defined by bank rejection, not by inability to pay, which is exactly why the operator can charge a rate well above the underlying loan and still close fast. The pool is deep precisely because banks keep saying no.

The practitioners also agree on why the wrap beats a naked subject-to when the operator sits in the middle. Ron LeGrand, with 40 years buying, says he always takes the wrap, that the document type makes no difference even in a mortgage state like Florida, and that the wrap payment matches the underlying so total debt stays $275,000 rather than doubling [-3xVEOBXFzc @ 00:03:38]. Mr No FluFF reduces the difference to remedy: under subject-to the seller can only sue, while a wrap pledges the property through a deed of trust so the seller can get an attorney and foreclose, which he calls very fast and very economic [RPbZyS3NPFI @ 00:00:17]. Caleb Christopher frames the same split as court versus process, warning that neither party wins 100% in court, whereas a right to foreclose lets you bring the hammer down and just follow process [UBngwrCrCxM @ 00:09:54]. Jen and Joe of Creative Finance Playbook add that the recorded note lets the seller foreclose and, together with the HUD statement, proves to future lenders who is actually paying the underlying loan [u3lV-kVx-LU @ 00:12:09]. Mr No FluFF's summary holds across the group: a wrap is a little more complex, but it gives the seller more security.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers — Pricing, Payment Design, and Deal Math

Dom Inglese's Tampa wrap is the cleanest unit-economics template. His mortgage balance was $290,000 and he had $40,000 in, for $330,000 all-in; he priced the wrap at $400,000, took $50,000 down from his buyer Tom, and carried a $350,000 note, which put him positive $10,000 before the first spread payment ever landed [Lv34MZLHg1I @ 00:02:59]. His Johnny's-house example runs the same formula: buy subject-to at $300,000 on a $280,000 balance at 3% with $20,000 of his own cash, resell at the typical 10 to 15% markup for $330,000 with $33,000 down, and wrap at 7 to 8% for $13,000 net at entry plus the rate spread, both loans 30-year fixed [VRUDR8FXNo0 @ 00:03:11]. On the Seminole Heights block house he carries about $250,000 of debt at 3.125% with roughly $40,000 in, for about $290,000 all-in, and targets a buyer rate of 7.5 to 8% to clear about $1,000 a month net, with a rental fallback at $2,300 to $2,500 for $600 to $800 gross [StKrDLiCONM @ 00:10:41]. Nick Disney buys at 75 cents or less, often 70, averaging $120,000 purchases with 85 to 90 cents all-in [DAhcIhHIoI0 @ 00:15:10]. The entry profit is the down payment minus cash in; everything after is spread.

Payment design is where the operators control the spread. Ron LeGrand sets the outgoing wrap payment first, $2,700 in his example and as high as you want it, strips out taxes and insurance such as $400 to reach $2,300, then matches principal and interest from the amortization schedule; on the contract he writes the $450,000 seller carryback in paragraph 2B as $2,700 including taxes and insurance with principal and interest left to be determined [qGlXYUEjyr8 @ 00:00:59]. Ron uses a five-year example term on the schedule. Caleb Christopher runs it through his Creative Underwriter Spreadsheet: a $155,000 underlying at 4.75% over 30 years pays 808, while the same balance at 7.5% pays 1,083 for a $275 spread, and pricing the house at 250 with 30 down adds another 65,000 at 7.5% worth $454, for $729 a month total [UOyoE5Fc7VE @ 00:01:03]. He tells operators not to talk about interest rate at all and to sell the monthly payment instead [UOyoE5Fc7VE @ 00:02:24]. Grant Kemp pushes operators to prefer principal over interest, because $90,000 at 6% over 30 years produces $539.60 a month, the same payment as $64,000 at 9%, and any sale, refinance or note sale pays off principal only [pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:18:50].

Grant Kemp's resale case shows how all three income streams compound. He sells a $100,000 house with 10%, or $10,000, down, carries a $90,000 note at 9.5% over 30 years at $756 a month, and nets $7,000 from the down payment after a $3,000 agent fee; the spread is $245 a month for 22 years, about $65,000, then the full $756 for the last 8 years, about $73,000, for $142,377 total on $20,000 of equity, which he discounts by a third to a half for time value of money [pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:15:27]. Nick Disney layers private money underneath: a $50,000 private loan at 10% interest-only costs about $400, while the buyer's $100,000 at 11% pays about $900, leaving a roughly $500 spread [DAhcIhHIoI0 @ 00:25:42]. Scott Horne's firm reports Andrew Webb wrapping a 3% loan at 5.99% for underwater sellers, against roughly 7% from banks, pushing price to 410 to 415 on a $400,000 house with $25,000 down [00RujvVfDRc @ 00:08:37]. The firm layers balloons at 5, 7 or 10 years or step-ups to 7% in year 3 and 8% in year 5, and notes that note buyers want a higher rate and more equity [00RujvVfDRc @ 00:12:48].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the Low-Rate Underlying
   - Acquire subject-to on the cheapest available debt before marketing the resale.
   - Buy subject-to and sell with a wrap; buying with a wrap and selling subject-to leaves a lot of money on the table [Propelio TV — pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:04:06]
2. Set Price, Down Payment and Rate
   - Mark up 10 to 15% over purchase, take about 10% down, and wrap at 7 to 8%.
   - Johnny's house bought at $300,000 on a 3% loan resold at $330,000 with $33,000 down on a 30-year fixed wrap [Dom Inglese — VRUDR8FXNo0 @ 00:03:56]
3. Underwrite the Payment Against Rent
   - Run the underlying and wrap payments, then test the buyer payment against local rent.
   - Compare a roughly 1,500 buyer payment to local rent and leave room in the down payment for rehab [Caleb Christopher — UOyoE5Fc7VE @ 00:01:49]

   - Close through attorney, note pro, loan processor and servicer, with the buyer paying RMLO, attorney and title fees.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is which side of the wrap you sit on. Grant Kemp at Propelio TV draws a hard line: buy subject-to, sell on a wrap, because buying with a wraparound wastes money and selling subject-to leaves money on the table [pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:04:06]. Dom Inglese runs that sell-side play in Tampa, holding a 2.75% subject-to loan and wrapping a buyer named Tom at 7% on a $400,000 price; Tom's $50,000 down against Dom's $40,000 in put Dom $10,000 ahead before the first spread payment [Lv34MZLHg1I @ 00:03:24]. Jen and Joe at Creative Finance Playbook run the mirror image. They do not sell on wraps; they buy on wraps to keep depreciation and appreciation for themselves, then sell through lease options or rent-to-own rather than carrying paper as the lender [u3lV-kVx-LU @ 00:15:17]. Ron LeGrand also wraps on the buy side: the land trust gives the seller a wrap for exactly what is owed, the wrap payment matches the underlying payment, and total debt stays at the $275k loan balance rather than doubling [-3xVEOBXFzc @ 00:04:37]. That leaves operators choosing between two income models: collect spread and down payment as the lender on the sell side, or keep the ownership benefits and exit through leasing on the buy side.

Payment plumbing splits the room. Scott Horne's firm always routes wraps through a loan servicer, framing it as protection against seller fraud on deals the firm and First Option Title close out of DFW and San Antonio [00RujvVfDRc @ 00:04:43]. Nick Disney, who funds San Antonio deals with interest-only private money, often from self-directed IRAs, pays that private lender directly with no servicer sitting in the middle [DAhcIhHIoI0 @ 00:25:14]. Caleb Christopher takes a third lane: never let the seller act as a pass-through for the buyer's money, and either pay the original lender directly or run funds through a servicing clearing house that sends receipts, so every party can prove the underlying loan is being paid [UBngwrCrCxM @ 00:05:36]. Jen and Joe skip servicing companies on their own wraps, yet flip the rule for the other structure, telling anyone buying subject-to that a servicing company is required [u3lV-kVx-LU @ 00:45:19]. The disagreement comes down to control and proof. Horne treats a third party as standard protection on every single file, Disney keeps a direct line to a lender relationship built on recycled capital, and Caleb's priority is a paper trail that keeps the seller's credit protected while the buyer pays.

Pricing philosophy diverges just as hard. Caleb Christopher tells operators not to talk about interest rate at all; sell the monthly payment, benchmark it against local rent, and leave enough room in the down payment for rehab [UOyoE5Fc7VE @ 00:02:24]. Grant Kemp argues for weighting the note toward principal: $90k at 6% over 30 years produces $539.60 a month, the same payment as $64k at 9%, and because a sale, refinance or note sale only pays off principal, the bigger balance wins at exit [pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:18:50]. Andrew Webb's underwater-seller play, presented by Scott Horne's firm, deliberately prices below the bank, wrapping a 3% loan at 5.99% when banks are quoting roughly 7%, so the buyer gets a cheaper payment on a low-equity house [00RujvVfDRc @ 00:06:52]. Inside Vena Jones Cox's class the hosts disagree with each other: Bill Cook avoids 0% seller notes, while Vena does almost nothing but zero percent seller-held mortgages, offering terms like $100,000 at $1,000 a month for 100 months [jeHouEguhhE @ 01:31:36]. Underneath all four positions sits the same lever: the spread can come from rate, from price, or from principal, and each practitioner picks the one the buyer and seller will accept.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is pricing a wrap the market will not carry. Jen and Joe walked away from a Sarasota deal where the wrap payment would run $5,500 a month at 6.75% against $3,400 rents, and they watch other investors wrap at 8% for $6,000 a month in $3,500 rent areas, deals that simply sit [u3lV-kVx-LU @ 00:18:26]. Their Cape Coral case failed the same check from the expense side: taxes and insurance ate $2,500 of a $4,800 payment, leaving no cash flow despite $450,000 of equity and a 2.99% loan [u3lV-kVx-LU @ 00:20:25]. Dom Inglese keeps a rental fallback on the Seminole Heights house: if the wrap buyer never materializes, the house rents at $2,300 to $2,500 for $600 to $800 in gross cash flow against a roughly $1,700 payment [StKrDLiCONM @ 00:11:20]. Grant Kemp's check runs on the back end: a $100,000 sale with $10,000 down totals $142,377 in payments on $20,000 of equity, but Grant discounts that by a third to a half for time value of money, and the $10,000 down nets only $7,000 after a $3,000 agent fee [pCK7ihJF_-Q @ 00:16:10]. Headline spread totals overstate what an operator actually banks, so every number gets stress-tested against rent, carrying costs and timing before the deal is signed.

Default handling is where naked subject-to bites hardest. Mr No FluFF warns that on a subject-to deal the only way a seller can take the collateral back is by suing, while a wrap's deed of trust lets the seller foreclose in 30 or 60 days or whatever the terms allow [oHV9AiZdwro @ 00:00:43]. Caleb Christopher spells out how those lawsuits end: neither party wins 100% in court, there is generally some sort of compromise, whereas a holder with the right to foreclose can bring the hammer down and just follow process [UBngwrCrCxM @ 00:09:39]. Ron LeGrand flags the damage that lands on the seller under subject-to: the loan stays on the seller's debt ratio and blocks new loans the seller may want later [-3xVEOBXFzc @ 00:05:22]. Scott Horne's Texas timeline shows what a clean foreclosure costs in time: a 20-day cure notice, then posting 21 days before the first Tuesday, about 43 days total with title back in roughly 60 [00RujvVfDRc @ 00:22:59]. The pattern across all four: subject-to saves paperwork up front but leaves the seller with a lawsuit and a damaged debt ratio, while the wrap's recorded note and deed of trust turn a default into a defined, calendar-driven process the note holder controls.

The remaining traps are operational. Jen and Joe list the mistakes that sink first wraps: going DIY, leaning on a transaction coordinator, using any title company that will take the file, and taking the deed outside a land trust [u3lV-kVx-LU @ 00:47:33]. Ross Badri at Make Your Money Multiply, working a $1,249,000 Ventura County example, lays out the risk stack sellers inherit: the original lender can call the loan due when title transfers even if payments stay current, the seller stays liable, California deals carry DRE disclosures and title or escrow hurdles, and the structure is wrong for unsophisticated sellers [upiVxcM_yIM @ 00:01:40]. Scott Horne puts the due-on-sale fear in hard numbers: 13 cases across 10,000 deals closed [00RujvVfDRc @ 00:19:04]. Philip Louden warns that a contract for deed keeps insurance and code problems on the investor's books, can turn into a contested eviction once a buyer has put around $30,000 down, and violates DSCR loans, a conflict Louden ties to the Jackie strategy and its 65-plus deals [oGD3QKECaGk @ 00:08:23]. Every one of these traps sits outside the spread math, so the paperwork, title partner and holding entity have to be chosen before the first payment is ever collected.
