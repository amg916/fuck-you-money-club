# Chapter 02: Buying Subject-To: Pitching Sellers, Agents, and Lenders

*Step-by-step subject-to timeline, live seller and agent phone scripts, in-person appointments, subject-to vs. assumption vs. seller finance, talking to the seller's mortgage company, and buying pre-foreclosures before auction*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners buying subject-to converge on one pitch: the asset being bought is the existing low-rate loan, not the house. Pace Morby, Lex Levinrad and Flipping Mastery TV all frame the seller's alternative as a retail sale that eats roughly 10% in commissions and closing costs, then offer to take over payments, cover arrears or commission, and hand the seller a small check. Street numbers include Morby's 80% equity-deferral rate, Flipping Mastery's 16% entry fee scored against a 25% ceiling, and kris haskins' roughly $50,000 reinstatement on a Virginia Beach preforeclosure. Disagreements center on due-on-sale risk, where Robert Crager reports zero calls in 25 years and Morby reports multiple; on interest, where Ben Jensen never mentions it; and on balloons, which Maximilian Dier hates.

## The Practitioner Consensus: You Are Buying the Loan, Not the House

Every speaker who defines the play draws the same line: subject-to is for encumbered property, seller finance is for free-and-clear property. Pace Morby put it to an Instagram follower who asked about doing a paid-off house subject-to in four words, debt versus no debt, and answered that a free-and-clear asset is seller finance by definition [ajwU62m3ADk @ 00:00:54]. In his shorter breakdown he lists the four terms every creative deal turns on, price, down payment, rate and length, and notes that on a subject-to deal those terms are already fixed by the existing loan, so the buyer is inheriting a structure rather than negotiating one [Kk_nfIhy2LY @ 00:02:27]. Ben Jensen uses the same split from the preforeclosure side: sub-to means taking over the existing mortgage while getting on the deed, seller finance usually means a paid-off house where the buyer pays a down payment and the seller acts as the bank, and a wrap is selling owner-finance on top of an existing mortgage [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:01:17]. Morby's Nevada deal shows the mechanics in plain terms: the $290k Wells Fargo deed of trust stays in the seller's name, Morby logs into the seller's account and makes the payment, and the deed moves through title and escrow [ajwU62m3ADk @ 00:02:09].

The second point of agreement is the pitch: the seller is handing over a mortgage, and the alternative is a retail sale that costs them money. Meeting a seller in person who owed about $350,000, Morby stacked the fees at 3% plus 3% plus about 4%, roughly 10%, which puts a listing at about $35,000 out of pocket [1GjR0GDWoyo @ 00:03:48]. He then told the seller the value was "the mortgage," not the house, offered to make the May and June payments, and set move-out for the end of June. Lex Levinrad runs identical math on his zero-down case: a 6% commission plus 3–4% closing means the seller nets $153,000 against a $170,000 loan, so a retail sale leaves them writing a check to get out [NKTKZd6UIHg @ 00:05:39]. Flipping Mastery TV turns the same logic on listing agents, anchoring with a low cash offer and then pivoting to taking over payments, handing the seller $5,000, and paying the closing costs and commission [rYMNGajRMfo @ 00:01:12]. kris haskins packages it for distressed owners as "our D program": closing happens at the attorney's office and the payments resume, pitched to a seller who fell behind after the birth of their child and whose PCS was expiring [X4XzY55e_n4 @ 00:15:13].

The baseline prerequisites are just as consistent: the loan has to be cheap and the payment has to sit well under rent. Levinrad's intake questions are the filter, whether the seller is current, the balance, term left, rate, payment, and whether that payment includes taxes and insurance [NKTKZd6UIHg @ 00:01:03]. His 2023 thesis names the target: owners who bought in 2021–22 and sit 10–15% underwater on 2.75–3% loans [NKTKZd6UIHg @ 00:01:29]. His longer walkthrough prefers fixed rates, less term remaining and lower rates, using a $150,000 loan at 2.875% with 25 years left, a $1,500 payment against $2,200 rent, for +$700 a month [YY7F8y8K0cU @ 00:02:34]. Flipping Mastery TV scores the rate below 4–5% and wants a spread like $2,000 rent against $1,200 PITI, or $800 a month [rYMNGajRMfo @ 00:04:46]. Morby's collaborated VA deal at 3% cash flowed on day one, with the agent paid $2,000, the seller paid $0, and a private money lender covering costs and furnishings, which is the deal he points to when he says "Equity comes and Equity goes but the cash will always flow Real Estate Investors buy cash flow" [Ou7n95pTZFE @ 00:20:19].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Scripts, Equity Deferral, Escrow and the Servicer

On agent calls the script is built around the commission. Flipping Mastery TV treats an agent's 5% commission objection as the signal that a sub-2 conversation is on the table [rYMNGajRMfo @ 00:00:18], and Morby's go-to line is to ask, on the spot, whether the seller would let him take over payments if he could get the agent's commission paid [Ou7n95pTZFE @ 00:42:25]. On a live call with agent Victoria, whose North Carolina buyer had fallen out, Morby opened by refusing to touch price: "I will never negotiate with you on price" at $340K, close in 24 hours on a warranty deed, commissions paid by him, against a retail net of about $320K and a cash buyer at $260–270K [bKMcgnoYXfQ @ 00:02:27]. The loan sat at 3.75% with roughly $180–200K owed. Maximilian Dier, testing Richard Taylor's seller-finance version on realtors, opens with investor vocabulary, a 1031 exchange, cash flow, a pre-approval letter, asks the agent for rent, then lays the checkmate: a lender demands 20% down and at high rates the payment exceeds rent, while a cash buyer's poor cash-on-cash return means a lowball. He then asks how they expect to sell it and stays silent for 3–10 seconds [GqiH4Kh8eaQ @ 00:03:50].

Sellers who want equity get it deferred, not paid. Morby says 80% of his sellers defer equity 3–4 years; his offer on the North Carolina deal was $20K down with the remainder paid within about 24 months through a refinance, adding, "I would be an idiot to refinance right now" [bKMcgnoYXfQ @ 00:06:58]. His TC writes refinance conditions into the paperwork, such as a 2.5% rate trigger, and he raises objections before the seller does [bKMcgnoYXfQ @ 00:27:23]. Levinrad handles the same ask smaller: if a seller wants $15,000–20,000, offer $5,000 down plus installments [NKTKZd6UIHg @ 00:03:36]. Ben Jensen's version, on his seller-finance-to-wrap deal in Carrollton, is to reverse-engineer the seller's desired monthly payment and price and never mention interest; he took a $150K price off the seller's Zillow number, $5K down, $800 a month at 0%, shortened the amortization to 15 years because 30 years produced only about $400 a month, and set a 7-year balloon, his floor [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:06:18]. On free-and-clear rentals Morby lets the seller build the terms: the 86-year-old Las Vegas seller and spouse set their own down payment and monthly figure that night, wanting payments "close to the rent" [vJ6uGVFs4M4 @ 00:04:13].

Once a seller says yes, the work moves to escrow and the lender. Morby's TC Molly sends the agreement, he opens escrow with a $5K EMD from a gator, runs each deal in its own Slack channel, and only underwrites after escrow opens because he "will go and comp and underwrite a 100 houses to get one under contract" [X94W660WDh8 @ 00:22:00]. On 3932 W Myrtle the gator funded arrears, EMD, the attorney, the TC, two months of buffer payments, and $15K to Morby upfront. kris haskins works the servicer directly: a reinstatement on his Virginia Beach preforeclosure required an authorization to release form, and the $46,000 figure, with about $10,000–11,000 of it principal, put the entry near $50,000 [DhOoZcypJCM @ 00:07:25]. To talk to the seller's mortgage company he gathers the seller's SSN (last four or full), an authorization to release, and a notarized limited POA; PNC verified those documents on file [FybFcuS9T7s @ 00:06:23]. Levinrad says insurance "trips up 90%": keep the existing policy and add the trust as additional insured [YY7F8y8K0cU @ 00:08:36]. Robert Crager's standing rule is to always pay the bank directly and send the seller proof [q1drqFk-FH4 @ 00:10:01].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull the loan facts
   - Run the intake questions before pitching anything.
   - Ask whether the seller is current, the balance, term left, rate, payment, and whether the payment includes taxes and insurance [Lex Levinrad Real Estate Training and Coaching — NKTKZd6UIHg @ 00:01:03]
2. Show the retail-sale math
   - Lay out what listing actually costs the seller.
   - Stack 3% plus 3% plus about 4%, roughly 10%, which ran about $35,000 out of pocket on roughly $350,000 owed, then frame the value as the mortgage [Pace Morby — 1GjR0GDWoyo @ 00:03:48]
3. Make the takeover offer
   - Offer to take over payments with a small seller check and costs covered.
   - Take over payments, give the seller $5,000, pay closing costs and commission, and keep the total entry under 25% [Flipping Mastery TV — rYMNGajRMfo @ 00:01:12]

   - Fund escrow, underwrite, and get servicer access in place.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over interest and balloon structure on seller-financed paper. Ben Jensen reverse-engineers the seller’s target monthly payment and price and will “never mention interest.” His Carrollton deal closed at $150K, $5K down, $800/mo, 0% interest, while a peer of his opens sellers at 2–4% [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:07:57]. When a seller digs in on 4%, Jensen trades rate for price, dropping a 300 number to 250 [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:08:59]. On balloons the camps diverge again. Richard Taylor’s script, as Maximilian Dier runs it, pays 10–15% over asking with a 5–7 year balloon. Dier says he hates balloons, and his own deal was 30 years at 0% with no balloon at all [GqiH4Kh8eaQ @ 00:06:07]. Jensen sits between them with a hard floor of never shorter than 7 years. The pricing formulas also clash. Taylor goes 15% over ask, 10% down, and sets the payment at 40% of rent, a structure Dier says he had never heard anyone use. Dier’s own method is 10% over ask with the price divided by 360, shown on a $380,000 Cleveland example [GqiH4Kh8eaQ @ 00:08:06]. Operators choosing a lane are really choosing which number they let the seller win.

Practitioners do not agree on how real the due-on-sale threat is. Robert Crager says that in 25 years a lender has “never one time” called a loan due on him [q1drqFk-FH4 @ 00:08:16]. Pace Morby says the opposite happened to him “multiple times.” He insists the correct term is the acceleration clause and points students to roughly 5 hours of training on it [Ou7n95pTZFE @ 00:09:12]. Lex Levinrad treats it as a structuring problem. He says the loan can come due in 30 days and lists USDA, federal savings bank, and VA loans as exempt. He also walks the 1982 Garn-St Germain exceptions for divorce, death, and the inter vivos land trust. In that setup the seller is trustee and beneficiary, the buyer is successor trustee, and the seller then resigns, a setup he says courses sell for “$10,000” [YY7F8y8K0cU @ 00:04:41]. The assumption-versus-subject-to debate splits the same way. Crager argues that assuming costs full qualification with tax returns and that banks “simply will say no,” so subject-to wins on speed, rate, and seasoning [q1drqFk-FH4 @ 00:03:16]. Levinrad counters with a Port St. Lucie file where the bank let the buyer assume without points. One camp shrugs off the clause, one camp papers around it, and Morby trains on it as a known event.

The exit is the third fault line. Pace Morby says wholesaling “takes money out of the seller’s pocket.” He pushes students to “skip the line” and buy, citing the Tangs closing 24 deals in 12 months [X94W660WDh8 @ 00:15:30]. Flipping Mastery TV builds a $15,000 wholesale fee straight into its subject-to entry math. The fee sits next to an $8,000 commission, $4,000 for the TC and closing, and $5,000 to the seller, for $32,000 out of $192,000. That is a 16% entry fee, which still scores under its 25% ceiling [rYMNGajRMfo @ 00:02:27]. Lex Levinrad lists rental and assignment-fee wholesale as equal exits. Hold versus wrap is the second exit split. Ben Jensen says a wrap pays you three times but surrenders appreciation and cost segregation. So he holds in appreciating areas like McKinney and wraps older houses under about $300K [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:15:28]. Morby’s hold case runs the other way on paper. His five-year math shows roughly $30K of cash flow, $340K growing to $475K at 7% appreciation, about $40K of loan paydown, depreciation, and a tax-free cash-out refinance [bKMcgnoYXfQ @ 00:35:56]. He even pays full retail, a move he calls “a very stupid thing to do,” when the debt takeover is $0 down. Pick the exit before the pitch, because it decides what you can concede.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Most subject-to deals break after the handshake, not on the call. Pace Morby’s 3932 W Myrtle file looked clean until title came back with two lawsuits, a lien, two wholesaler memorandums, and an illegal quitclaim. Clearing it took 2 months, an attorney, and another $5K from the gator. He says 99% of the title reports he sees show issues [X94W660WDh8 @ 00:27:19]. In his words, once you open escrow “this is where the challenges start” [X94W660WDh8 @ 00:27:35]. That is why he refuses to underwrite before escrow opens: he will comp and underwrite 100 houses to get one under contract. Ben Jensen hit the contract-side version on his subject-to wrap. He self-listed at $350K and drew a full-price, day-one offer with $100K down. He then caught the realtor trying to sneak in terms, so his rule is “read your contracts” [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:21:13]. Lex Levinrad flags insurance as the quiet killer and says it “trips up 90%” of buyers. His fix is to keep the seller’s existing policy in force and add the trust as additional insured [YY7F8y8K0cU @ 00:08:36]. Budget time and gator money for the back end before you promise a seller a move-out date, because the paperwork, not the pitch, is where these deals stall.

Servicing is where cash leaks. kris haskins needed the seller’s SSN, an authorization to release, and a notarized limited power of attorney before PNC would talk to him. Once he was inside, he found the payment had moved from $1,229 to $1,077 because of an escrow re-analysis triggered by an insurance change [FybFcuS9T7s @ 00:13:01]. Worse, a separate $342 principal check landed in unapplied funds instead of reducing the balance. His fix is to send one larger check or to pay principal by phone, which carries no fee; he made a $1,551 principal payment that way [FybFcuS9T7s @ 00:16:43]. On the Virginia Beach pre-foreclosure he shows that the number you see is not the number you pay. Arrears ran about $37,000–38,000, yet the reinstatement came back at $46,000. With principal added, that was roughly $50,000 to get in, and the figures were only good until June 1st [DhOoZcypJCM @ 00:07:25]. Robert Crager’s rule closes the gap on the seller side: you have no control over the loan terms, so always pay the bank directly and send the seller proof [q1drqFk-FH4 @ 00:10:01]. Pace Morby runs the same discipline by logging into the seller’s Wells Fargo account himself. If you are not reading the statement monthly, the lender will rewrite your spread for you.

The final checks are economic. Ben Jensen warns that on a wrap you must vet buyers or face foreclosure and taking the house back. He adds that a single AC failure runs about $6K and that the buyer pool thins out above roughly $300,000 [t96wzMUDN58 @ 00:15:28]. His second deal shows another trap. The Airbnb figure was about $4K against long-term rent near $2,500, but the city made Airbnb difficult, and the wrap he sold instead spreads about $400/mo. Pace Morby opens one in-person video by calling arbitrage a “trash model.” He then works through a seller whose $2,138 payment sits above a $1,800 US average rent. He floats PadSplit at about $125/week, around $750/mo, before landing on a traditional rental [1GjR0GDWoyo @ 00:14:30]. Maximilian Dier’s live test exposed the agent-side trap. The agent said seller financing was possible but only “shortterm,” with a refi out within 12 months, at a time Dier cited 7–8% rates [GqiH4Kh8eaQ @ 00:10:50]. kris haskins adds the honesty check that keeps zero-equity, high-payment wraps alive: “Be honest” and do not pull a switcheroo on the seller [X4XzY55e_n4 @ 00:18:42]. He also reminds operators that real estate is not passive. Run rent against payment before the second call, not after.
