# Chapter 01: Wraparound Mortgage Mechanics: How the Wrap Rides on the Underlying Loan

*Wrap notes, all-inclusive trust deeds (AITD), installment notes wrapping an existing first, wrap vs. subject-to vs. land contract vs. assumable loans, the due-on-sale landmine, and exam-level definitions*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners describe one machine: a new, larger wrap note laid over a live underlying loan, the buyer paying the wrap holder and the holder paying the original lender. The consensus edge is that the spread is earned on the full balance, not just equity: Bill Brunchick, with roughly 1,200 wrap closings, shows 6% on the equity plus a 2% rate gap on an $80,000 first at 4%, and Chris Goff models $126,000 total profit on a $350,000 wrap over a $300,000 loan at 3.5%. Street mechanics include buying on mirror wraps, selling on equity wraps, unrecorded notes with recorded AITD riders, Texas bilingual disclosures, and third-party servicers who pay the underlying lender first. Disagreement centers on acquisition: Pace Morby never buys on a wrap and prefers split-note hybrids, while Brian Gibbons says never sell on a seller-held wrap.

## The Practitioner Consensus: The Wrap Rides on Top of a Live Loan

Every practitioner on the board describes the same machine: a new, larger note laid on top of a loan that stays alive underneath. Kris Haskins frames it with a $100,000 first at 6% wrapped into a $150,000 wrap, where the borrower makes one payment to the wraparound lender and that lender remits the first-mortgage payment to the original lender [kM__5b36grI @ 00:03:01]. Scott Horne of the Owner Finance Network calls the wrap a brand-new note and deed of trust subject to the underlying lien, technically a subordinate lien rather than a second, and says the cleanest way to grasp it is the flow of money: buyer pays seller, seller pays the underlying mortgage company [VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:00:36]. Tim of Tim Talks Real Estate walks the same path, adding that the buyer's single payment is usually priced a bit above the seller's old rate, the seller pockets the difference, and in most cases the bank underneath does not even know the house changed hands [Dklhw3jMfVo @ 00:02:25]. Pace Morby adds a hard definitional line: a wrap requires two notes or more, because without an existing debt there is nothing to wrap together [hTxjhDN2Cyo @ 00:07:11]. Everything downstream depends on that live underlying payment arriving on time.

The second point of agreement is why operators bother: the spread is earned on the entire balance, not only on the equity. Attorney Bill Brunchick, who says they have closed roughly 1,200 wraps through their own escrow company, lays it out on a $100,000 sale over an $80,000 underlying loan at 4%: a separate second at 6% earns 6% only on the $20,000, while an all-inclusive wrap at 6% earns 6% on the $20,000 equity plus the 2% difference on the underlying $80,000 [kM__5b36grI @ 00:10:56]. Maximilian Dier calls this flipping loans and runs a $400,000 FHA loan at 3% resold inside a $600,000 wrap at 8%, collecting a 5% spread on the $400,000 and the full 8% on the $200,000 of equity [49GchpJCUB0 @ 00:04:36]. PrepAgent's exam-prep math lands in the same place: Vivian carries an $80,000 loan at 4%, sells for $120,000 with 10% down, finances $108,000 at 7%, and earns 7% on $28,000 plus 3% on the $80,000 [-8VDqc249XY @ 00:02:29]. Edspira's Suzie example repeats the pattern, a 1.5% spread on a $35,000 loan at 7% while collecting 8.5% on the rest [OXZZS-KeaXc @ 00:05:52]. Same math, four different teachers, one conclusion: the cheap underlying debt is the asset being resold.

The consensus on prerequisites is equally tight, and it centers on the underlying lender that never signed off on the deal. Tim of Tim Talks Real Estate, in the business since 2007, calls the due-on-sale clause the landmine: almost every regular mortgage lets the lender demand the entire balance once title transfers, and a wrap almost always transfers title while that loan stays open [Dklhw3jMfVo @ 00:03:16]. Brian Gibbons of REISkills says wraps almost always violate the due-on-sale clause in the seller's underlying financing, and adds the second baseline risk: a buyer has to worry about the seller collecting the payment and never paying the first [9rVA292DHlY @ 00:01:49]. Scott Horne's answer is a loan servicer who collects from the buyer, pays the underlying mortgage company first, and releases whatever is left to the seller, a setup the industry calls its fraud preventer [VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:07:47]. Chris Goff lands on the same control point, recommending an escrow company coordinate monthly payments because paying the seller and hoping the bank gets paid carries risk [X_UODDIMvFs @ 00:06:51]. Across all four, the baseline is identical: assume the clause exists, plan for buyer default, and never let the underlying payment depend on one person's honesty.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Bill Brunchick's core street play is to buy on a mirror wrap and sell on an equity wrap. When there is little or no equity, the note to the seller mirrors the underlying loan exactly: if the first has 26 years left at 4% with a $1,200 monthly payment, the wrap note carries the same terms and the seller gets a little cash at closing [kM__5b36grI @ 00:12:11]. On the exit, the operator marks up both price and rate: on a $100,000 house over an $80,000 first at 4%, Brunchick sells at $105,000 because owner financing earns a premium, then charges about 6.25%, catching 6.25% on the $25,000 equity and 2.25% on the rest [kM__5b36grI @ 00:13:23]. Scott Horne uses the acquisition mirror wrap to solve a different problem: a seller with 25 years left at 4% on $100,000 who wants out in five years gets a wrap with identical payment and amortization plus a built-in five-year balloon [VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:02:40]. Epic Real Estate builds the wrap from parts, combining a $175,000 first at 6% and a $75,000 seller carryback at 8% into one $1,599 payment, $1,049 to the lender and $550 to the seller [5n5x76_xKLU @ 00:03:06]. Epic notes the blended rate lands closer to the 6% than the 8% because the first carries more balance.

Chris Goff publishes the cleanest full-cycle numbers. The seller owes $300,000 at 3.5%, a $1,347.13 payment with 20 years left; the wrap sells at $350,000 with $10,000 down at 5%, amortized over 30 years for a $1,878.88 payment with a 10-year balloon [X_UODDIMvFs @ 00:04:08]. That produces $531.75 a month, or $63,810 over 120 months, plus a $52,415.31 gap between the two amortized balances, for roughly $126,000 total profit including the down payment [X_UODDIMvFs @ 00:05:32]. Goff's split: the $40,000 above the first earns the full 5%, the $300,000 underneath earns only the 1.5% difference, and there are no repair, turnover, or management costs. Bill Brunchick says equity wraps work best on high-end homes: a $2,400 all-inclusive payment that only rents for $2,500 is a dead rental, but pushing taxes and insurance to the buyer, who pays PITI while the operator pays only P and I, can catch $400 to $600 a month with no landlording [kM__5b36grI @ 00:14:43]. Maximilian Dier prices for buyers the banks rejected, such as cash-business owners and people whose credit was wrecked by divorce, quoting 360-month terms at 8 to 10% with 20% down [49GchpJCUB0 @ 00:06:36]. Dier's rule for every one of those deals: never overpay, because a buyer who refinances early leaves no spread to recover the loss.

The paper stack is specific. Brunchick attaches a wraparound addendum to whatever standard purchase contract the operator already uses, with a choice between mirror wrap and equity wrap, then executes an all-inclusive wraparound promissory note that is never recorded; the recorded security instrument is a standard deed of trust with a wraparound rider, making it an AITD, or a wraparound rider to the mortgage in mortgage states [kM__5b36grI @ 00:17:27]. Brunchick is blunt that notarizing and recording a note means nothing; the recorded rider is what gives the world notice that this is a wraparound and not just a second. In Texas, Scott Horne adds the underlying lien disclosure notice and finance disclosure notice, delivered to the buyer in Spanish and English at bare minimum [VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:06:45]. When buying an existing wrap note on top of a subject-to, Horne wants a power of attorney, an authorization form, the mortgage statement, the seller's Social Security number and date of birth, and the online password [ZLt_A_LBSu0 @ 00:03:16]. Note Unlimited tracks the structure in software: two notes on the same property address, the wrap flagged with the wraparound parameter set to yes, linked under a related notes tab with amortization, cash flow and NPV views [gUg3ObEfMB0 @ 00:02:25].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull a Targeted Seller List
   - Query absentee owners with moderate equity and two-plus years of ownership before spending on mail or skip tracing.
   - Goff sets equity at 20% rather than high-equity only because owners with little equity are more likely to work with you when they cannot afford to sell with commissions [Chris Goff — X_UODDIMvFs @ 00:02:21].
2. Acquire and Lock Control of the Underlying Loan
   - Take the deed subject-to or on a mirror wrap, and collect everything needed to service the underlying debt yourself.
   - Brunchick mirrors the underlying note when there is no equity and adds a little cash at closing [kris haskins — kM__5b36grI @ 00:12:31]; Horne demands the mortgage statement, seller identifiers, and the online password to control the underlying [Scott Horne's Owner Finance Network — ZLt_A_LBSu0 @ 00:03:32].
3. Underwrite the Wrap Buyer
   - Run consumer buyers through a residential mortgage loan originator and review the loan package before approving the sale.
   - Horne checks ability to repay and debt-to-income: a 30% DTI is reasonable, while a buyer at 70% DTI simply cannot afford the house [Scott Horne's Owner Finance Network — VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:06:09].
4. Paper, Record, and Service the Wrap
   - Attach the wrap addendum, sign the unrecorded all-inclusive note, record the deed of trust with the wraparound rider, and set up third-party servicing.
   - Brunchick records the wraparound rider so the AITD gives notice it is a wrap, not just a second [kris haskins — kM__5b36grI @ 00:17:27]; Horne's servicer pays the underlying mortgage company first and the seller last [Scott Horne's Owner Finance Network — VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:07:32].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Pace Morby draws the hardest line on the buy side: 'we never want to buy on a wrap we want to sell on a wrap' [hTxjhDN2Cyo @ 00:05:25]. Their reason is the blended rate. In Morby's car example, a $459 payment at six percent and a $200 equity payment at zero percent become one $659 payment at six percent once wrapped, so the equity slice that could have carried zero percent now bleeds interest. Morby's fix is to separate the notes and acquire on a 'hybrid': subject-to on the existing debt, plus a separate seller-finance note on the equity with its own terms [hTxjhDN2Cyo @ 00:05:40]. Attorney Bill Brunchick, who owns an escrow company that specializes in wraps and lost count of closings after about 1,200, runs the opposite playbook: buy on a mirror wrap when there is little or no equity, then sell on an equity wrap with the price and interest rate marked up [kM__5b36grI @ 00:13:02]. Brian Gibbons at REISkills sits in a third camp, calling buying on a wraparound or AITD a great idea for investors renting out or running lease options, while flatly telling operators never to sell their own property on a seller-held mortgage or wraparound because the seller will have to foreclose if the buyer defaults [9rVA292DHlY @ 00:05:09].

The second split is whose side the structure protects. Bill Brunchick prefers to buy straight subject-to and simply take the deed, but concedes that under a bare sub-to the seller is without recourse if the buyer stops paying; an all-inclusive wrap or AITD hands the seller a lien and a foreclosure path to get the property back [kM__5b36grI @ 00:11:53]. Chris Goff reports that attorneys are more likely to do a wrap than a sub-to because subject-to protects the buyer, not the seller, and a seller shopping for another property can show the wrap income. Yet Goff flips back to subject-to when seller death is the worry, because a sub-to takes the seller out of the picture and nobody needs their signature down the road [X_UODDIMvFs @ 00:07:35]. Ashland Mortgage splits it on payment: subject-to delivers the absolute lowest payment, while the wrap is more attractive to sellers who want their equity paying them every month, and Ashland treats the due-on-sale call as rarely enforced when payments stay on time [IKu9qf2jZBk @ 00:00:45]. Tim Talks Real Estate, in the business since 2007, rejects that comfort outright: treat the risk as real, and never build a plan that only works if the bank never notices [Dklhw3jMfVo @ 00:04:06].

Exit structure splits again by state and by appetite for control. Brunchick buys on a deed subject-to everywhere, then sells on an unrecorded installment land contract and keeps title until the buyer pays off, except in Texas, where the rules on installment land contracts force a wraparound deed of trust on both the buy and the sell [kM__5b36grI @ 00:18:44]. Pace Morby hits the same Texas wall from the other direction, calling executory contracts illegal there and naming Texas the only state where they cannot be done [hTxjhDN2Cyo @ 00:02:40]. On stacking, Ron LeGrand gives a one-line green light: yes, you can do a wrap over a wrap [OuQxeQu2HJA @ 00:01:32]. Scott Horne, whose Dallas lunch group started calling a wrap of a wrap 'sub 3,' refuses to let a downstream buyer simply assume an existing wrap. Horne instead buys the house from the seller for the same down payment the new buyer is paying and simultaneously sells to that buyer on a fresh wrap with the same terms, becoming the direct mortgage holder and running the buyer through loan processing [ZLt_A_LBSu0 @ 00:05:49]. Horne's logic is exit-first: if the assumption simply passes through, there is no telling whose mortgage is being sold in a future note sale or what financial information exists on that borrower.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The landmine every practitioner names is the due-on-sale clause. Tim Talks Real Estate spells out the mechanics: almost every regular mortgage carries the clause, a wrap almost always transfers the property while the original loan is still open, and the lender can demand the whole balance, not the monthly payment but the whole thing, the day they find out [Dklhw3jMfVo @ 00:03:16]. Brian Gibbons calls it the main problem with wraps and adds the buyer-side trap: the seller collects the buyer's payment and then never pays the first mortgage [9rVA292DHlY @ 00:01:49]. Gibbons handles both by putting the property in a land trust and routing the buyer's money to a third-party payee that pays the mortgage first and sends the rest to the seller. Scott Horne runs the same defense through a loan servicer: buyer pays servicer, servicer pays the underlying lender first, seller gets whatever is left. Horne calls it the industry's fraud preventer, because a later default can then only mean the buyer missed, not that the seller pocketed the money [VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:07:47]. Chris Goff wants an escrow company coordinating the monthly payment for the same reason, flagging the risk in just paying the seller and hoping the seller is making the payment [X_UODDIMvFs @ 00:06:51].

The second failure mode is the buyer simply stopping. PrepAgent's Sam-and-Bill example flips from win-win to 'not so fast': if Bill stops paying, Sam cannot pay the existing mortgage, the bank gets angry and it becomes a big issue [uHpaHL4qN84 @ 00:02:45]. Shop Rates calls buyer default the biggest risk point because the seller stays legally responsible for the original mortgage, telling sellers to choose their buyers wisely and buyers to make sure the seller is financially stable [Z_X2-JRfBYs @ 00:01:26]. Ron LeGrand puts the credit cost on the table: selling on a wrap means nobody pays off the underlying debt, so a personally guaranteed loan stays the seller's responsibility for as long as it takes the buyer to cash them out. With guaranteed debt in play, LeGrand says to be more selective on who goes in the house, or even sell at retail price right now and get that debt out of your life [OuQxeQu2HJA @ 00:05:15]. Brian Gibbons adds the closing-table bleed: title transfers and a new mortgage or trust deed gets recorded, attorney fees stack on top, and if the seller is getting no money down they may refuse to bring cash, so the investor can wind up paying all the costs just to get the deal done [9rVA292DHlY @ 00:04:50].

Then come the economics checks. Maximilian Dier says the place people get in trouble is overpaying: buy at $300,000 on a house only worth $270,000, wrap it out at $280,000 telling yourself you will make it up over time, and if the buyer refinances or pays the loan off in full, you are screwed. Dier's rule is to buy under market value with a low interest rate and build in instant equity [49GchpJCUB0 @ 00:09:19]. Bill Brunchick runs the rent-versus-wrap test on high-end homes: a $2,400 all-inclusive payment that only rents for $2,500 is not worth the risk, but pushing taxes and insurance onto a wrap buyer and raising the rate can catch a spread of $400 to $600 a month with no landlording responsibility [kM__5b36grI @ 00:14:43]. PrepAgent sets the floor: to break even, the seller must charge at least as much interest as the existing loan costs, which is why sellers negotiate the highest rate they can so the first mortgage gets paid while the deal still earns a profit [-8VDqc249XY @ 00:01:35]. Scott Horne's buyer screen is the debt-to-income check run through a residential mortgage loan originator: a 30 percent DTI is pretty reasonable, but at 70 percent the bottom line is the buyer cannot afford the house [VDXOvtqzbZk @ 00:06:09].
