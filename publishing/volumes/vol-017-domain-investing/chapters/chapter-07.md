## Executive Summary

Brokers and outbound sellers converge on one rule: name the buyer before you buy or pitch. Zuiker, Conkle and Saw.com's Jeff Gabriel all screen for obvious end users, while Harbin and Rosener push quality over volume, citing 1–5% passive sell-through and $15K yearly renewals per 1,000 names. Street mechanics are precise: Yogi Solanki mines Google pages 1–20, withholds price in the first email, follows up every third day up to three times, and closes $300–700 deals through Dan at about 9% versus GoDaddy's 20%. Alex Verdea imports how-much replies as Dan leads, offers installments and caps discounts at 10%. Disagreements are sharp: Chris Zuiker gives a price when asked while Eisler and Yogi make buyers raise it first, and Chandler Bolt tells buyers never to hire the brokers that GoDaddy's Joe recommends.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every serious seller here starts from the same premise: a domain only moves when you can name the buyer before you list it. Chris Zuiker calls an unidentified audience the number one reason names sit unsold, and dismisses the reflex of pitching Amazon, IBM or Salesforce, firms that already dominate their categories [DBqMibOXGo8 @ 00:00:27]. Zuiker maps every name against four strategies (brand, sub-brand, marketing, defensive) and five buyer types, warning that a defensive buyer pays pennies on the dollar. Brooks Conkle trained a newly hired outbound team on the same filter: is this an upgrade over a weak existing domain, or a defensive buy for a large company? If no use case comes to mind, as with Summit Nest or ryzen shop, outreach will be hard [USC2_VCPYbc @ 00:01:48]. Jeff Gabriel of Saw.com screens broker submissions with an even sharper test, demanding obvious targets where, with the CEO on the phone, the broker could say with conviction that the business needs the name; the minimum bar is a few small businesses willing to pay $1–2K [EA8QZJvkKP4 @ 00:00:18]. Yogi Solanki quantifies it on the buy side, purchasing only names with 100–200 potential businesses visible across Google pages 1–20 [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:49:19].

The second point of agreement is that portfolio size is a trap, not a strategy. Brian Harbin of Grit Brokerage puts passive sell-through at 1–5% and renewals at about $15 a name, so 1,000 domains carry $15K a year in overhead before a single sale; Harbin would rather hold 5–10 names you lose sleep over [uIsWkqUglS4 @ 00:13:26]. Andrew Rosener spent roughly $300K over three years on about 1,800 domains learning that lesson, and now reduces it to one line: it is about quality, not quantity [ksUajnoey7I @ 00:43:53]. Rosener's prescription is to skip 20 junk names and buy one good name for $250–1K instead. Joe from the GoDaddy aftermarket team lands in the same place from the valuation side: start really slow, because buying too many names early is costly, and buy inside an industry you already understand, such as guitars [SNukqlGstqs @ 00:08:22]. Yogi Solanki proved the niche rule in live trading, flipping insurance names sourced through former lead-generation contacts, including a $10 hand-registered Medicare Advantage Plans .net that sold for $300, and tells beginners to use whatever industry experience they already have [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:55:01]. Velocity beats hoarding: Yogi sells at least 80% of each week's buys before buying more.

Third, the closers agree that trust and stamina win deals that pressure loses. Alex Verdea needed about 32 contacts across email, text and phone over several months to close a landscaping buyer, and stayed professional, offering escrow, when the buyer's web designer called Verdea a scammer [5UOGADHB0l0 @ 00:11:27]. Verdea names trust as the hidden objection behind most stalls and fixes it by putting a phone number in the signature; Yogi Solanki does the same from New Delhi with a US Skype number at about $20 a month [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:35:09]. Brooks Conkle's first rule for outbound email is not to be salesy: be friendly, genuine, researched and helpful. Rosener says a broker must expect to hear no a thousand times and needs tenacity, endurance and patience [ksUajnoey7I @ 00:35:54]. Chris Zuiker adds the ceiling on pressure: you cannot strongarm a big sale, the buyer has to want it, and while one sentence may not sell a domain, one sentence can certainly kill one [3C8uYmkV_aE @ 00:34:11]. Verdea's operational version is blunt and repeatable: when prospects resist, do not drop the price, increase activity level, and take full personal responsibility for every buyer who does not close today.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The lead engine is Google, worked far deeper than most sellers bother to go. Yogi Solanki targets advertisers and businesses found across the first 20 pages of Google results, reaching them by email or contact form and skipping LinkedIn and social entirely, because Google is free [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:32:15]. Jason Eisler of dnadvisor runs what Eisler calls a reverse round-robin, restarting the search from page 10 rather than page one, and layers Snov.io and Hunter.io on top as email finders and verifiers [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:53:10]. Brooks Conkle's walkthrough uses pillarbuilders.com: search the phrase in quotes, go past page one, and treat owners of weaker variants such as pillarconstructionco.com and pillarhomes.us as leads, noting the .us owner must be educated that the extension is weaker than .com [USC2_VCPYbc @ 00:03:14]. Conkle collects info@ addresses, uses LinkedIn to find owners or marketing staff, logs everything in Trello, a CRM or a Google Sheet, and rates leads hot, medium or potential. OpenCorporates surfaced 13 pillar builders entities with active or inactive status and doubles as a pre-purchase check [USC2_VCPYbc @ 00:08:19]. DotOutbound packages the same job as software, letting sellers paste up to 200 domains at a time into its Radar step to keep only names with reachable companies.

Message construction is tightly specified. Yogi Solanki never puts a price in the first email, keeps copy short and personalized after a long-essay test drew zero replies, and names the top-producing subject line as simply the domain name plus for sale [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:32:34]. Yogi follows up only with recipients who open, every third day, no more than three times, then lists the name everywhere, re-pitches after about three months, possibly once more at renewal, and finally dumps it to NameLiquidate [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:38:36]. Jason Eisler runs far longer sequences, 12–16 stages, after running a manual six-stage drip for a year and a half, and insists that DKIM, SPF and DMARC only get you inboxed; the message still has to add value [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:30:18]. Yogi warms accounts for 4–8 weeks of conversation with family and friends before pushing 100–500 emails a day, and rotates subject lines because overused ones drift into spam. DotOutbound automates that warm-up over about 15 days, requires an app password rather than the normal login, and verifies addresses on send to protect sender reputation [6cANvHgSKgI @ 00:03:29]. Timing converges on Tuesday through Thursday; Eisler adds Sunday 6–8pm and holidays, where Eisler reports the best response rates.

Closing mechanics run through marketplaces that put the price in writing and chase the money. Alex Verdea's Dan outbound method imports any prospect who replies how much as a Dan lead, tagged negotiation or agreement reached, so Dan's email states the price and Dan support pursues payment; some deals have closed on a single email [5UOGADHB0l0 @ 00:42:44]. When budget is the block, Verdea offers $950 down plus $200–300 a month through Dan installments, concedes 10% only if it will close the deal, never 20%, and adds the tax-deduction pitch [5UOGADHB0l0 @ 00:58:30]. Yogi Solanki started with GoDaddy Buy Now links because buyers trusted them, then moved to Dan once confident, since GoDaddy takes 20% against about 9% on Dan; one email noting the name was publicly listed produced a $350 sale without any reply [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:43:04]. Commission drag elsewhere is steep: Brooks Conkle puts Afternic at 20–30% depending on nameserver and push settings, so a $1,000 sale can cost $300, while broker commissions average 15% and are negotiable [ciS3H3ANjuM @ 00:01:56]. Yogi's sweet spot is $300–700 per name, with the first flip, NewYorkPhotos.com, asked at $1,000, countered at $500 and closed at $600 inside 48 hours.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the Name for Outbound
   - Confirm obvious target companies, real search demand and a $1–2K small-business floor before spending outreach hours.
   - Gabriel passes on names with no clear buyers, no search volume and devoweled spellings, requiring a pitch you could make to a CEO with conviction [SawSells — EA8QZJvkKP4 @ 00:00:18].
2. Build and Rate the Lead List
   - Search the phrase in quotes past page one, capture weak-domain owners, info@ emails and LinkedIn decision-makers, then rate hot, medium or potential.
   - Conkle tracks leads in Trello, a CRM or a Google Sheet and checks OpenCorporates for active entities [Brooks Conkle // Build Income Streams — USC2_VCPYbc @ 00:03:14].
3. Send and Follow Up on Cadence
   - Send a short, priceless first email with the domain name plus for sale as subject, then follow up openers every third day, three times maximum.
   - Yogi then lists everywhere, re-pitches after about three months and liquidates unsold names [kickstartcommerce — gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:38:36].
4. Close Inside Dan
   - Import every how-much reply as a Dan lead, state the price in writing, and offer installments or a 10% discount only to close.
   - Verdea lets Dan support chase payment after agreement is reached, with some deals closing on one email [kickstartcommerce — 5UOGADHB0l0 @ 00:42:44].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is who names the number first. Jason Eisler and Yogi Solanki run a let-them-ask playbook. Yogi says flatly “i never ever do give give them my pricing on my first email,” and Jason lets the prospect raise price because roughly 80% of replies come back as a bare “how much,” warning “don't negotiate against yourself” [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:23:49] [gQJxEyrhqec @ 01:07:33]. Chandler Bolt, working from the buyer's chair, treats it as law: “this is negotiation 101 you never offer first,” and only floats estimator figures when pressured [NheyTdmBlH0 @ 00:05:29]. Chris Zuiker of MediaOptions pushes the other way on outbound. When a prospect asks for a price, give one, keep the conversation open and skip the hard pitch, because the “biggest risk to your company” line repels anyone who is merely interested rather than hungry for the name [dgnnNdyoENg @ 00:02:22]. The reconciling variable is buyer state. Zuiker's frame is interest versus desire: a buyer with desire “is going to keep coming back and maybe make a higher offer,” while an interested buyer walks the moment the seller goes hardline. Withholding price works when the first reply already shows lean-in. Stonewalling a lukewarm prospect kills the thread before a counter ever lands.

Whether to hand the name to a broker splits the room. Chandler Bolt's rule is “do not hire domain broker and this is in almost all cases,” because a percentage fee rewards a higher price. The one exception is a big company whose identity would inflate the ask [NheyTdmBlH0 @ 00:04:08]. Joe from GoDaddy's aftermarket says the opposite: “if you wanna sell your names, the best place to go, is to try and talk to a broker.” Joe recommends brokers on both the buy and sell side for fair-market advice [SNukqlGstqs @ 00:07:25]. Brooks Conkle splits the difference, paying an average 15% commission, which Brooks calls negotiable, and listing CardFanatics.com with Grit Brokerage while demanding monthly status reports [ciS3H3ANjuM @ 00:03:08]. On DomainSherpa, Drew Rosener of MediaOptions questions whether most sellers should be outbounding at all: “99 of the people in this industry uh are not qualified to be doing outbound sales,” and junk outreach dilutes the market for everyone [Yvi8ys0RQH8 @ 00:13:35]. Jebediah Burnett, GoDaddy's first senior premium domain consultant, insists “every quality domain deserves to be outbound brokered.” Burnett still concedes the point and tells sellers to get a professional review before blasting prospects. The fault line is fee versus skill: Bolt fears the percentage incentive, while Rosener fears the amateur.

The third split is volume versus conviction. When Brooks Conkle floated a 500–1,000 domain target, Brian Harbin of Grit Brokerage pushed back. Harbin's advice is to hold 5–10 domains you “lose sleep over,” because less is more. The high-end model is six figures into a one-word .com, held 3–5 years and aimed at 10x, since “you make your money when you buy” [uIsWkqUglS4 @ 00:13:26]. Drew Rosener lands on the same side after burning about $300K on roughly 1,800 names: “it's about quality not quantity” [ksUajnoey7I @ 00:43:53]. Yogi Solanki runs the opposite engine, a cash-flow treadmill where “cash flow is the king.” Yogi prices in a $300–700 sweet spot and sells at least 80% of each week's buys before buying more [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:47:53]. Chris Zuiker frames the same trade as a pricing dial rather than a portfolio choice. Holding out for 100% of value takes months, while pricing at 60% sells faster, and the right bucket price is a realistic three-to-five-year max cut back to move [3C8uYmkV_aE @ 00:34:38]. Harbin's warning applies to both camps when the buyer is a deep-pocketed end user: “you get one shot,” and price-gouging a billionaire earns silence rather than a counter.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is trust, and it shows up even on deals that close. Alex Verdea's landscaping sale took months and about 32 contacts across email, text and phone. Along the way the buyer's web designer called Alex a “scammer,” and the save was offering escrow and staying professional [5UOGADHB0l0 @ 00:11:50]. Alex names trust as the hidden objection and fixes it with a phone number in the signature [5UOGADHB0l0 @ 00:53:46]. Yogi Solanki makes the same move from New Delhi with a US Skype number at about $20 a month [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:35:09]. The self-inflicted version is lying. Alex is blunt: “that's one way to blow a deal is is being dishonest and telling people you have other offers when you actually don't.” The rule extends to never mentioning real offers either and never asking what competitors charge. Chris Zuiker adds the cost of a single careless line: “one sentence may not sell a domain but one sentence can certainly kill a domain” [3C8uYmkV_aE @ 00:35:23]. The economics check is patience. Alex's outbound Dan deal started March 26 and closed more than six months later for a small sum, so a quiet thread is not automatically a dead deal.

The second trap is inventory nobody will ever outbound successfully. Drew Rosener's tuition bill was “three years and and about 300 Grand” for roughly 1,800 domains. Early names like sustainablescallops.com found no buyers because seafood is B2B, low-margin and indifferent to domains [ksUajnoey7I @ 00:21:14]. Brian Harbin puts the carrying cost on the table: about $15 per renewal means “if you got a thousand domains your overhead right there is 15 grand,” against a passive sell-through of 1–5% [uIsWkqUglS4 @ 00:12:07]. Jeff Gabriel of Saw.com shows how a broker rejects weak stock in minutes. Robotwrld had no Ubersuggest volume, only 2018 arXiv results, a “Not exciting” DotDB read, about 10 parked sibling extensions and a $200K ask on Atom. Gabriel's verdict: “I don't see many targets, the name isn't great and the domain is not priced right” [EA8QZJvkKP4 @ 00:06:21]. The minimum bar Gabriel sets is a few small businesses willing to pay $1–2K. Scott Redgate, flipping trend names like searchgptmarketing.com from a $36.40 batch, lands on the same floor: a “crappy domain” is “probably not going to sell” [mRcM1Utk5hI @ 00:06:35]. Rosener's prescription is to skip 20 junk names worth about $1K over five years and buy one good name for $250–1K instead.

The third trap is outreach that burns the sender before it sells anything. Yogi Solanki's warning to volume-blasters is direct: “if you are thinking that you can send thousand emails a day and you can sell it domain um I'm afraid it's not gonna work” [yMsNlST-y9M @ 00:39:01]. On the joint kickstartcommerce panel, Yogi adds three lessons: a long-essay email test pulled zero replies, overused subject lines drift into spam so they must be rotated, and sending relies on warmed, aged accounts. Jason Eisler notes that DKIM, SPF and DMARC get a message inboxed, but it still has to add value [gQJxEyrhqec @ 00:31:53]. The DotOutbound walkthrough runs verification automatically on every send to block invalid addresses, calling it “absolutely crucial for protecting your sender reputation,” and pairs it with a roughly 15-day warm-up [6cANvHgSKgI @ 00:02:31]. Even clean, researched mail can land in silence. Brooks Conkle's Pillar Builders email was short and friendly, linked to buy and lease-to-own options, and ran under the subject “expanding Pillar Constructions footprint,” yet it got no reply [USC2_VCPYbc @ 00:11:24]. The economics check is time, not software. Jason ran a weak hand-reg portfolio through outbound twice in 6–8 month passes and still sold some, and Yogi caps follow-ups at every third day, no more than three times.
