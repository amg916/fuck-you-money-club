## The Practitioner Consensus

Every dispute practitioner on the circuit lands in the same place: the UDRP is the trademark owner's default weapon, and domain investors should plan their holdings around how cheap it is to fire. Doug Isenberg, who filed nine business-email-compromise UDRP complaints in a single two-week stretch, walks through the Debevoise case and concludes the UDRP is the best option in most cases, with six ICANN-approved providers, electronic filing, and no court appearances, even though it cannot grant injunctive relief [GI9Us2WWsAo @ 00:06:31]. Steven Rinehart frames the same procedure as a set of rules every registrant agrees to at registration, cost-effective and finished in 34 to 44 days through the National Arbitration Forum in Minnesota or WIPO in Geneva [BL9pY270FpM @ 00:01:35]. The NDDCamp panel puts the economics bluntly from the investor side: a complainant spends roughly $5,000 on a UDRP instead of paying 50,000 to 100,000 euros to buy the name, which makes filing the cheap play for complainants [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:34:29]. Both regimes date to 1999, yet Isenberg counts tens of thousands of UDRP decisions against a thin ACPA docket, because federal suits are expensive and jurisdiction-bound, so the realistic threat to a portfolio is a fast panel decision, not a courtroom.

The second point of agreement is that trademark clearance happens before money moves, not after a complaint lands. Ruth Carter, a licensed Arizona attorney practicing in Phoenix, has watched clients forced to rebrand because their names sat too close to a competitor's, and warns that an available domain does not mean the trademark is available; the USPTO database is usable and word searches are easy, so run it before building a logo or launching marketing [JxPAjtHrSQo @ 00:01:12]. The NDDCamp panel extends the search stack for investors: a basic Google search, the EU trademark and design view, and the WIPO global brands database, while steering clear of any term tied to one major company [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:33:12]. On the buy side, Drew Rosner describes an end user with $500,000 to $1.5 million to spend who rejected perfect names and burned roughly 28 grand on trademark searches, yet the same DomainSherpa panel holds that smart buyers ignore trademark attorneys when the name is generic [cAguI2-CkwU @ 00:27:59]. Carter adds one more filter for outsourced branding: ask the agency whether it checked the database before pitching a single name, because falling in love with a registered mark is the expensive way to learn.

On the extension map, the consensus is that .com remains the exit and every alternative carries dilution or registry risk. The Unstoppable broker traces the 2014 to 2016 numeric run-up almost entirely to .com, with very little on .net or .org, and states flatly that more extensions make price appreciation more dilutive [FRAPXUkvbv0 @ 00:03:24]. The DomainSherpa crew sees companies built on .ai now hunting the matching .com, arguing that every success raises the .com price and that the only way off the ferris wheel is with a dot com [cAguI2-CkwU @ 01:34:12]. Miller abstained on the new gTLD round, reasoning that a TLD is a startup and most of them fail, while the round itself drew 30% fewer applications even though guests had predicted two to five times more [zCEoH4BHEDI @ 00:52:20]. ccTLDs add a sovereign layer of danger: the NDDCamp panel lists abrupt rule changes at .md, India, and Anguilla for 2027, Montenegro's government pushing to control .me, a broken mandatory identification rollout on .ru, and Afnic deleting a 15,000-domain portfolio after a two-week reply deadline [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:18:37]. The operating takeaway shared across these camps is to treat any non-.com holding as exposed to rules you do not control.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The dispute playbook runs on a three-prong test. Steven Rinehart lays it out: the complainant needs trademark rights plus confusing similarity, bad-faith registration and use, and no legitimate use by the registrant; on the second prong the domain must have been registered after the complainant's rights arose through use, not after a filing date, or the complainant will most likely lose the UDRP and an ACPA case in federal court becomes the easier route [BL9pY270FpM @ 00:04:40]. Doug Isenberg drills into the first prong: US state registrations are usually insufficient because many states grant them automatically on application, and a panel held they lack the presumptions of validity and ownership a federal registration carries [OfgGOACCYO0 @ 00:02:56]. Isenberg has even won with a registration obtained after the domain was registered, though timing can bite at Elements 2 and 3. Unregistered marks require a distinctive identifier backed by five evidence types: duration and nature of use, sales, advertising, public recognition, and consumer surveys [OfgGOACCYO0 @ 00:06:06]. When speed matters more than cost, the Debevoise firm went the other way, filing under the ACPA in the Eastern District of Virginia on December 13, 2021 and getting a preliminary injunction transferring both typo domains three days later [GI9Us2WWsAo @ 00:03:50].

The unit economics of a dispute are concrete. In the kerosoft.co case, a Serbian investor registered the name and listed it for sale, US company Carahsoft filed with Forum for $1,330, the investor never answered, and the panel ruled typosquatting plus passive holding even though the page offered the name for sale [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:04:05]. The panel's lesson for anyone registering descriptive or brandable names is direct: if a complaint arrives, go and defend yourself instead of letting it default [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:04:24]. Counsel for a complainant runs about $5,000 plus a $1,500 filing fee, and the binance.ae story shows the ceiling of the risk: bought for $300,000, offered to Binance at roughly $1.3 to $1.4 million, and lost to Binance through WIPO [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:57:37]. Jurisdiction shapes the court route: in rem .com cases must be filed in the Eastern District of Virginia because Verisign sits in Reston, and the Debevoise registrant hid behind an Iceland privacy service; final judgment took 259 days against roughly 60 for a UDRP [GI9Us2WWsAo @ 00:05:01]. Isenberg also flags the volume driver: business email compromise scams on typo domains, with fake invoices sent from addresses imitating a partner.

For operators eyeing the registry side, Dotup prices the 2026 entry at $227,000 per string, plus a backend registry service provider at $10,000 to $100,000 a year depending on volume and a $25,000 annual ICANN fee [UVaTuGEfJF8 @ 00:04:05]. Dotup's break-even math is about 40,000 registrations at $10 wholesale or 3,500 at $100, with niche TLDs posting 80 to 90% renewal at $50 to $100 wholesale and broad generics needing volume, such as 500,000 domains at $10 for roughly $5 million a year [UVaTuGEfJF8 @ 00:05:43]. Before filing, ICANN's Name Collision Observatory scores a string's collision exposure on a magnitude scale of 1 to 10 using root server data plus Cloudflare and Quad9 resolvers, but the score is not a pass or fail, should not be read as absolute risk, and cannot be compared across sources [ubAwLfPKk6s @ 00:01:48]. Drew Rosner's launch blueprint for .WEB shows the distribution play: airdrop names to matching .com owners with a 30-day claim, then .net owners, then open registration, netting $12 to $15 per domain against a 96% operating margin, with the panel citing .XYZ's 5% retention for comparison [zCEoH4BHEDI @ 01:04:25].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Clear the mark before buying or branding
   - Run USPTO word searches, then the EU trademark and design view, the WIPO global brands database, and Google.
   - An available domain does not guarantee an available trademark, and the USPTO database makes word searches easy [Carter Law Firm — JxPAjtHrSQo @ 00:01:12]; panelists add the EU and WIPO databases and avoid terms tied to one major company [live NDDCamp — PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:33:12].
2. Map the complainant's first-element rights
   - Identify federal registrations, flag state-only registrations, and test common-law claims against the five evidence types.
   - State registrations are usually insufficient, location of rights is irrelevant, and unregistered marks need a distinctive identifier proven by use, sales, advertising, public recognition, and consumer surveys [GigaLaw - Doug Isenberg — OfgGOACCYO0 @ 00:06:06].
3. Compare registration date to rights arising through use
   - Document when the domain was registered versus when the complainant's rights first arose through use.
   - The domain must have been registered after the complainant acquired rights through use, not after the filing date, or the complainant will most likely lose the UDRP [Steven Rinehart — BL9pY270FpM @ 00:04:40].
4. Respond and anticipate the forum
   - File a response to every complaint and plan for both a roughly 60-day UDRP and a slower ACPA path.
   - The Debevoise ACPA suit won an injunction in 3 days but took 259 days to final judgment versus about 60 for a UDRP, and in rem .com cases must be filed in the Eastern District of Virginia [GigaLaw - Doug Isenberg — GI9Us2WWsAo @ 00:05:01].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The .si question splits the room into three camps. James Booth treats it as cheap optionality: companies should grab their name in .si defensively if it costs under $5K, and James personally bought simulation.si and premier.si, names that renew from $12 [4lvZ-LZJ3Sc @ 00:06:32]. Amar Kuba sees the odds stacked against the extension because "AI" is decades-old nomenclature. Amar would pick up a single name like wisdom.si at $1,000 but refuses to load up on a hundred of them [35YUAM-dUn0 @ 00:27:56]. The demand evidence is thin either way: Rob at Snagged reported that one of twenty founder calls was .si-curious, which Josh framed as up from an expected zero. At NDDCamp the moderator went harder, warning that .si "predators" are doing wholesale, investor-to-investor sales rather than end-user deals, and told the room, "please do do due diligence" before jumping in [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:19:58]. Shane on the DomainSherpa panel took the trader's exit instead of the holder's entry: flip .si names now rather than hold them [cAguI2-CkwU @ 01:30:38]. A VC-backed client discussed on that same panel preferred .co over .si outright. The practical split: James buys defensively and cheaply, Amar buys one-offs only, and Shane sells into the hype.

On .ai versus .com, the practitioners who made money on .ai disagree about where it goes next. James Booth reports .ai names selling for roughly 5x the matching .com, with two-word .ai names bought for $200 to $300 selling for $50K to $100K [35YUAM-dUn0 @ 00:21:41]. James also concedes that a one-word .com is worth about 4 to 5x the one-word .ai, and that buyers spending mid-six figures choose the .com. Drew Rosner bought roughly 15 to 20 .ai names straight from the registry and sold fix.ai for about $125K in 2019, then saw no inquiries until 2022 or 2023 [4lvZ-LZJ3Sc @ 00:12:43]. Drew's stated reason for going hard on .ai was following the money once adoption showed up, and Drew still expects "AI" to become redundant in company names. Shane counters that companies built on .ai are now seeking the .com, and every success raises its price, so waiting means bidding against yourself: "only way to get off the ferris wheel is with a dot com" [cAguI2-CkwU @ 01:34:12]. Miller quoted a source saying "AI has fallen off a cliff," and Zach sees .ai sellers cutting prices by more than 50% [cAguI2-CkwU @ 01:40:55]. The split is timing: James prices .ai at a premium today, while Shane, Miller and Zach read the same market as a sell-down.

On the dispute side, the lawyers differ on forum and on what counts as rights. Doug Isenberg uses the Debevoise case to show the ACPA's one real edge: the firm sued in the Eastern District of Virginia on December 13, 2021, and a preliminary injunction transferred the two typo domains three days later, something the UDRP cannot do because it offers no injunctive relief [GI9Us2WWsAo @ 00:03:50]. But the final judgment took 259 days against roughly 60 for a UDRP, in rem .com cases must be filed in EDVA, and Isenberg's bottom line is that the UDRP is the best option in most cases. Steven Rinehart carves out the exception: if the domain was registered before your trademark rights arose through use, you will "most likely" lose the UDRP, and an ACPA case in federal court is easier [BL9pY270FpM @ 00:05:46]. The two also differ on unregistered marks. Rinehart says common-law rights from use, even under a DBA, satisfy the first prong [BL9pY270FpM @ 00:03:48]. Isenberg answers "depends": with no registration number to cite, a complainant needs a distinctive identifier backed by duration of use, sales, advertising, public recognition and consumer surveys, and US state registrations are usually insufficient [OfgGOACCYO0 @ 00:06:06].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive dispute mistakes discussed were investor-side silence and famous-mark ccTLD bets. At NDDCamp, a Serbian investor registered kerosoft.co through dino dot and listed it for sale; US company Carahsoft filed, the investor never responded, and the panel found typosquatting plus "passive holding" even though the page offered the name for sale [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:01:57]. The complainant spent only $1,330 at Forum, a non-WIPO provider, which is the asymmetry NDDCamp speakers kept flagging: a UDRP runs a complainant about $5,000 against 50,000 to 100,000 euros to buy the name, or roughly $5,000 for a lawyer plus a $1,500 filing fee [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:34:29]. The lesson delivered from the stage was blunt: if you register descriptive or brandable names and get a complaint, "go and defend yourself." The bigger loss was binance.ae, bought for $300,000 and offered to Binance for about $1.3 to $1.4 million before Binance won it through WIPO [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:57:37]. Doug Isenberg's warning cuts the other way for complainants: proving trademark rights should not be difficult, but failing to do so is fatal to a UDRP case [OfgGOACCYO0 @ 00:06:47]. Cheap filings and fast losses mean every inbound complaint needs a response.

The second trap is registry risk that has nothing to do with the name's quality. NDDCamp speakers listed registries changing rules abruptly: .md, India, Anguilla changing its rules for 2027, and .si devolving into investor-to-investor flipping [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:18:37]. In .me, Montenegro's government is seeking control of the registry; in .ru, mandatory identification after 1 September is not working. The sharpest reported loss came from Afnic: a two-week reply deadline, and a 15,000-domain portfolio deleted [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:26:31]. On .co, cannabis and drug names risk deletion outright [PdwE4T9wmMo @ 00:29:20]. The .si camp carries its own fine print: Barry Hill warned investors to read the .si terms of service because they include "moral" type clauses, and that warning landed alongside renewals from $12 that make .si names cheap to carry [4lvZ-LZJ3Sc @ 00:19:19]. Pricing risk sits on top of rule risk. The same NDDCamp session put the two-letter .com floor at about $1 million, yet an "I" combination sat unsold at $800,000, and a one-word .fr tops out at five figures. Cheap renewals and loose ccTLD rules do not protect a portfolio when the registry can delete names, demand replies in two weeks, or rewrite eligibility between renewals.

The third set of traps is deal and business-model economics. One DomainSherpa panelist described a 12-month lease-to-own buyer who defaulted after 11 payments and then walked away rather than make one extra payment, and the takeaway was "you have to be able to step back and make a commercial decision" [4lvZ-LZJ3Sc @ 00:30:29]. James Booth also watched Helios fall through after a low-seven-figure offer when the buyer walked. The Unstoppable guest lived through the 2014 to 2016 numeric .com run-up and the 2016 to 2017 crash that took values down 60% or more, and now calls current numeric pricing "highly speculative and probably a bubble," with 10,000 four-number names in every extension [FRAPXUkvbv0 @ 00:05:15]. For would-be registry operators, the DomainSherpa September panel had predicted 2 to 5x more new gTLD applications, but the round came in with 30% fewer; Miller abstained because a TLD is a startup and "most of them fail," and .XYZ retention was cited at 5% [zCEoH4BHEDI @ 00:52:20]. Across all of it, margins are compressing: anyone buying names today should expect a much tighter margin than in the past, driven by market maturity, competition and AI-informed seller pricing [35YUAM-dUn0 @ 01:08:22].

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Checks Before You Register, Buy, or Defend**
