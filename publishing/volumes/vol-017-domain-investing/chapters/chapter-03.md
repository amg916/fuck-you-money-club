## Executive Summary

Expired-domain hunters converge on one clock and one discipline. GoDaddy's panel maps the cycle: a 10-day auction from day 26, a 5-day declining Closeout from day 36, registry release on day 43, and a public drop around day 75 to 80. Filters do the culling. ReviewUP and Scott Redgate strip numbers and hyphens and lock English .com, while Jamie Lewis calls expired lists 99% trash. The street economics are tight: a GoDaddy backorder places a $10 opening bid, credits cost just under $21, SnapNames runs about 79 per domain, and Closeouts bottom at $5. Practitioners split on bidding style. GoDaddy staff push proxy bids, while Affiliate Credo refuses to reveal a maximum. They also split on supply: Drew Rosner credits a registrar's net transfer-ins, not new registrations, for quality drops.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every GoDaddy insider maps the same expiry clock, and operators who skip it bid blind. Joe walks a .com through Expired, Grace, Redemption and a 5-day Pending Delete, with GoDaddy retrying the renewal on day 1, day 5 and day 12 [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:06:01]. By Redemption the registrant has been contacted about nine times and faces the renewal plus an $80 fee. That keeps unclaimed inventory flowing into the auction on day 26 [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:08:10]. Brian Cowan, Joe Styler and broker Wes lay out the buy-side calendar. Day 26 opens a 10-day expired auction. Day 36 rolls names with no winner and no backorder into a 5-day Closeout, where set prices decline daily. Day 43 releases the name to the registry for about 30 days of Redemption, and the public drop lands around day 75 to 80 [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:02:13]. Rob Kerry and Matt Young explain why catchers crowd that drop. An owner who wakes up in Redemption pays about $100 to recover. Roughly 80% of ICANN memberships are used for drop catching, and dropcatch.com owns over 1,200 of them while paying more than $5M a year. Rob openly calls the setup a gray area [N8NOW92hCY4 @ 00:03:31].

The hunters agree that raw lists are noise, so the first job is cutting volume hard. ReviewUP opens GoDaddy Expired Domains on expireddomains.net to a list of usually over 400,000 expiring names each week. The channel then switches on no numbers and no hyphens, English dictionary words of 1 to 3 words, the last 7 days, and .com only, which it calls the gold standard [LqUB5Dr0vrE @ 00:00:32]. Scott Redgate runs the same stack from the Domain Search side. A single keyword like plumbing returns over 191,000 results until English and .com are locked in through the Additional tab [rzHCbB8_QqU @ 00:02:08]. Joe at GoDaddy Domain Academy starts from 484,000 unfiltered auction names. Feeding up to 30 space-separated Afternic keywords into ExpiredDomains.net cuts 480K to 80K, and keeping only .com and .org lands at 60K [n3Z47PI6nj0 @ 00:02:19]. Jamie Lewis frames the end state bluntly: expired lists are 99% trash. Sort an Estibot drop list of about 100,000 names by appraisal descending, not alphabetically, and stay hardcore and skeptical about the appraisals that survive [h0uYdy4XIdY @ 00:01:00]. The shared rule is that filters do the heavy cutting before a human reads a single name.

Consensus also holds that a dropped name arrives with its full history attached. Scott Redgate warns that you inherit the good and the bad. Sorting by backlinks is dangerous because a site may have bought links, been penalized and then been abandoned, so every candidate goes through Ahrefs' free backlink checker before money moves [rzHCbB8_QqU @ 00:04:22]. Rob Kerry lists the red flags he checks in Ahrefs or Moz: keyword anchors like nike max sneakers, foreign links that miss the site's target country, and previously penalized domains. Any rebuild must stay in the same niche, because Google discounts links when the topic changes; red shoes stays red shoes [N8NOW92hCY4 @ 00:13:31]. Joe on the GoDaddy basics hangout applies the same filter from the investor side. Avoid trademarks, typos and squatting, stay inside a niche you know, and favour aged generic names. Pull DomainTools, archive.org and whois history before bidding, since GoDaddy offers no warranty against Google penalties [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:18:39]. The Time's Up panel closes the loop. Bids are binding, and a winner who doesn't pay can see the name awarded to the second-highest bidder. Check prior spam and BBB reviews before the first click [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:48:09].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

GoDaddy's backorder is the cheapest seat at the table, and the Time's Up panel priced every piece of it. A backorder auto-places a $10 opening bid that bundles the bid and the registration. The speaker first said $12, then corrected to $10 [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:04:07]. A credit costs just under $21. A .com renewal runs about $15 and .net and .org cost more, so the panel frames a credit as roughly $25 of value [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:15:23]. Volume tiers of 1-19, 20-50, 50-100 and 100-plus each shave about $2, bottoming near $16-17. In the worked example, a $150 win normally costs $165. With a backorder it costs $140 plus about $17 upfront, or $157 total, and credits are reassignable when a catch fails [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:27:39]. Joe on the basics hangout prices the Closeout as a Dutch auction. Buy-now starts at $11 and drops $1 a day through 10, 9 and 8, then falls to $5 on the final day. The original owner can still renew until day 42, which triggers an auto-refund, though Joe says that is rare. A winner sees the name in their account on day 43 [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:09:23].

Outside GoDaddy, the catchers compete on access rather than price. Rob Kerry uses SnapNames, which shares ownership with NameJet. He pays about 79 per domain, gets a refund or rollover if the catch fails, and goes to auction whenever a name is contested [N8NOW92hCY4 @ 00:12:10]. DomCop walks both interfaces. SnapNames runs search, Back Order, cart and Place Order, and pending orders stay editable until acquisition. NameJet requires an account, offers Last Chance and Drop sections, and opens bidding at a 69 minimum. On both, a lone backorder wins at the minimum and multiple backorders trigger an auction [fVAMuCrwtUw @ 00:01:26]. Wes on the Time's Up panel stacks registrars. A Wild West Domains backorder gets no auction alert and allows only one holder per name, but a WWD catch goes straight to that holder. A GoDaddy catch instead triggers a 3-day private auction among backorder holders. Because the first backorder placed anywhere gets the first bid, Wes places backorders at both [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:31:52]. cssfx flags Dynadot as the overlooked lane. It sits outside NameJet and SnapNames and holds exclusive pending-delete names in a less saturated marketplace. It also offers a 120-hour deletion grace period for about $2 with a refund, and its prepaid credits feed Snatcher drop-catching [zN6FKHG-ORs @ 00:00:29].

Closing mechanics decide who actually walks away with the name. The Time's Up panel pushes proxy bids: a $7,000 proxy facing bids of $10 and then $15 climbs only to $20. A bid in the final 5 minutes adds 5 minutes, but only when the lead changes, as in a $35 to $40 to $45 sequence [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:24:01]. Emailed outbid notices can lag, so the same panel tells bidders to stay logged in at auction end rather than trust the inbox [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:29:43]. Affiliate Credo runs the opposite discipline. With eight years in, a 250-domain PBN behind them, and bids now up to $5,000 for 3-10x returns, they never show a maximum, skip auto-bids, and bid manually in the final minutes [9vU7vtxcYuc @ 00:06:11]. Jamie Lewis monetizes at both ends. The quick grabber buys at $10, lists GoDaddy-to-GoDaddy at $100, and lets drop-list browsers buy it. For real upside, the getsober.org play listed the name at about $1,000 for 48 hours. It then hired a Freelancer dev, made a ChatGPT logo, and built a starter blog. The blog sold on Flippa through a 30-day auction, returning $35,000 on $280 invested [h0uYdy4XIdY @ 00:07:37].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

GoDaddy's aftermarket team and Affiliate Credo run opposite bidding doctrines. On the GoDaddy Hangout, the team pushes proxy bids: a $7,000 proxy sitting against rival bids of $10 and $15 only climbs to $20, and they tell buyers to place backorders as early as possible because GoDaddy hides backorder counts and shows a single bid [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:24:01]. Affiliate Credo, eight years into expired domains and now bidding up to $5,000 per name, refuses to reveal a ceiling at all: no visible maximum, no auto-bids, watch-list the name instead of bidding, and on Ninja drop the backorder in at the last moment [9vU7vtxcYuc @ 00:06:11]. His third tactic is manual bidding in the final minutes, paired with tracking the visible usernames of repeat competitors [9vU7vtxcYuc @ 00:07:09]. GoDaddy's basics session frames that same last-minute crowd as snipers and points to the five-minute auto-extension as the counterweight [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:29:36]. The practical split is about information leakage: the proxy camp trusts the auction engine to defend a hidden ceiling, while the manual camp treats any early bid as a signal that pulls rivals onto the name. Pick one doctrine per auction and execute it cleanly, because blending both halfway leaks your ceiling and still loses the final-minute fight.

Practitioners split hard on what to filter for before a single bid. DomCop's Sophia runs a metrics-first screen on GoDaddy inventory: minimum PageRank 2, Domain Authority 30, Majestic Trust Flow 15, a $200 price cap, offer and counter-offer listings stripped out as a waste of time, and results re-sorted by Trust Flow [r5xkDG4-mvo @ 00:00:42]. ReviewUP skips link metrics and filters for brandability on expireddomains.net: no numbers, no hyphens, English dictionary words of one to three words, last seven days, .com only, then sort by valuation, optionally narrowed to zero-bid names [LqUB5Dr0vrE @ 00:00:50]. GoDaddy Domain Academy's Joe takes a third route, feeding up to 30 space-separated Afternic keywords into ExpiredDomains.net to cut 480,000 names to 80,000, and leaning on starts-with 'the' because it is the top-selling keyword [n3Z47PI6nj0 @ 00:02:19]. Jamie Lewis sorts an Estibot drop list of roughly 100,000 names by appraisal descending but insists on being hardcore and skeptical of every appraisal, since catlitter.com showed $15,000 and was already taken [h0uYdy4XIdY @ 00:05:36]. The SEO buyer, the brandable flipper, and the keyword-data hunter are working from different filters because they are selling to different end buyers, so the screen has to match the exit you already have in mind.

The DomainSherpa panel openly fought over where quality inventory still comes from. JT worried that the new-registration pipeline is drying up, and Drew Rosner rejected the premise: over 80% of his firm's sales are domains registered before 1997 or 1998, new registrations carry no liquid value, and the big GoDaddy auction prices go to names 20 to 35 years old [iiHOxt6I0u0 @ 00:48:14]. Drew's real driver is a registrar's net transfer-ins, which is how GoDaddy went from garbage to the best drops while NetSol, eNom and Moniker bled transfers; Logan Flat's counter is that gems remain but you have to sift harder [iiHOxt6I0u0 @ 00:54:06]. The same table split on .ai. Drew called wholesale .ai pricing absurd and said paying $10,000 to $15,000 at auction while expecting $150,000 to $250,000 resale is smoking crack [iiHOxt6I0u0 @ 00:29:37]. Mark Lavine, on the same panel, had just sold a hand-registered .ai from July 2025 for $23,000, and NamesBrun is holding ai.green, which cost $1,100 to register and renew, at a $99,000 asking price on Atom [tVPy99Y1MsA @ 00:02:54]. Exit posture split too: Drew paid a $20,000 ask on al.org rather than risk a counteroffer, while Logan turned down $50,000 from Drew's side on casino.gg and still asks six figures.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The firsthand losses start with inherited baggage. Scott Redgate warns that sorting expireddomains.net by backlinks is a trap, because a site may have bought links, been penalized, and then been abandoned, so every candidate goes through Ahrefs' free backlink checker before purchase [rzHCbB8_QqU @ 00:04:22]. Rob Kerry's red flags are keyword-stuffed anchors such as 'nike max sneakers' and foreign links that do not match the site's target country, and he adds that Google discounts the links the moment the topic changes, so red shoes has to stay red shoes [N8NOW92hCY4 @ 00:13:31]. GoDaddy's team states there is no warranty against Google penalties on auction names; buyers check sandbox and fail-list status through NamePros or Bodis, and an end user can work a name out of the sandbox [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:41:33]. Trademark exposure is the second silent killer. GoDaddy tells buyers to avoid trademarks, typos and squatting outright, and Rookie Domainer's Herb sorts the bargain bin by GoDaddy valuation and skips trademark names on the way down the list [Bi-_Gko1m0g @ 00:01:38]. Run both screens on every name before the bid, because GoDaddy's Hangout team is clear that a placed bid is binding and the due diligence, including prior spam and BBB reviews, has to come first.

The auction plumbing burns operators who trust notifications. GoDaddy's Hangout team says email outbid notices can lag, so you stay logged in at auction end, and the five-minute extension only fires when the lead actually changes, as in their $35 to $40 to $45 example [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:29:43]. Venue choice is the next trap: a GoDaddy backorder on a name expiring at another registrar only reaches the public drop, where there are hundreds and hundreds of tries, the Ticketmaster problem, so you need a spot in the pre-release queue at whichever registrar holds the name [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:14:40]. Wild West Domains backorders also send no auction alert, and GoDaddy warns against placing a drop backorder less than two hours out. Closeout buyers face a reversal risk: the original owner can renew until day 42, which auto-refunds the winner, though GoDaddy calls it rare [0f-o9p0h6u8 @ 00:10:03]. Rookie Domainer's Herb reports the same thing in practice, with none of his seven or eight bargain-bin buys renewed out from under him [Bi-_Gko1m0g @ 00:01:07]. Failed catches are recoverable because GoDaddy credits are reassignable and SnapNames refunds or rolls over the fee, and a winner who fails to pay sees the name handed to the second-highest bidder.

The economics checks are where appraisals collapse. Jamie Lewis treats appraisal numbers as hostile until proven: Maxwell.net showed $2,000 and expose.info $1,000, but .info yields about 1% of what the same .com would, and the high-scoring $12,000 .co only rated that way because it used to be a website with past traffic [h0uYdy4XIdY @ 00:05:14]. NamesBrun hit the same wall live, flagging aimed.co as proof that valuations are not always true while watching blackstrawberry.com climb to 29 bids [tVPy99Y1MsA @ 00:29:28]. Holding costs and stubborn pricing compound the error on the exit side. Logan Flat bought podconnect.com for $200 in 2020, priced it higher for five years with no buyer, and only sold after cutting it to $4,995, while Drew Rosner warns most owners reject the best offer they will get that decade [iiHOxt6I0u0 @ 00:33:03]. On the acquisition side, run the backorder math before you bid: GoDaddy's worked example shows a $150 win costing $165 normally, or $140 plus about $17 upfront for $157 total with a backorder credit, and credits fall about $2 per volume tier toward $16 to $17 [OM_5VESPepY @ 00:27:39]. Redemption is the expensive lane, at the renewal plus an $80 fee on GoDaddy and about $100 per Rob Kerry on Ecommerce FM.
