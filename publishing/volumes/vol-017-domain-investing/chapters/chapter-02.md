## 1. Find the budget owner and sell a measurable business decision  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with the buyer's operating event, not an appraisal certificate. A founder approaching launch buys a brandable name because changing packaging, email addresses, and investor materials later creates avoidable work. A marketing director considering an exact-match name needs a credible acquisition hypothesis, not a promise of search rankings. An established merchant considering an upgrade wants fewer mistyped visits and a cleaner spoken address; their chief executive may sponsor the purchase while a controller controls the wire. Identify all three roles: economic beneficiary, contract signatory, and funding operator. Ask which entity will own the registration, which entity will pay, whether procurement requires a purchase order, and whether the registrar account already exists. A sponsored proposal without a funding owner is still a lead. For a $10,000 upgrade, a buyer estimating $2,000 monthly incremental contribution has a five-month simple payback before migration costs, but the assumptions belong to the buyer and require testing. Compare that estimate with keeping the existing name, buying another available name, or postponing the launch. These alternatives explain price resistance more reliably than the seller's historical acquisition cost or an automated valuation.

The chapter's eight mapped plays describe distinct purchasing motives; they are not eight additive revenue forecasts. Brandable launches sell naming fit, exact-match acquisitions sell descriptive relevance, expired-authority purchases sell an asset requiring historical diligence, and drop-catch resale sells scarce availability after deletion. Outbound upgrades address an existing business's naming friction. Portfolio bundles reduce fragmented negotiations, while installment sales exchange immediate ownership for scheduled access and collection risk. The eighth purchasing pathway, newly_registered_domain_trigger_arbitrage, uses observable launch defects to open a diagnostic conversation. None authorizes manufacturing statutory pressure. Under 15 U.S.C. 1125(d), bad-faith intent to profit from another party's protected mark can create serious exposure; 15 U.S.C. 1117(d) provides elected statutory damages of $1,000 to $100,000 per domain for covered violations. A trademark owner may pursue recovery rather than buy the name. Screen marks before acquisition, document independent selection reasons, and stop outreach when the proposed pitch depends on exploiting confusion. A threatened dispute is a reason to preserve records and obtain case-specific review, not a reason to increase the asking price. Search USPTO records, relevant commercial use, and the name's historical content before committing capital.

Build the NRD pathway from permitted data, not assumptions about private registrants. Download available ICANN CZDS zone files under their access terms and compare daily snapshots; an added delegation is a candidate signal, not conclusive proof of a new registration. CZDS does not cover every top-level domain. For .us, use registry or registrar RDAP responses and any expressly permitted feed; do not assume a universal unredacted bulk service exists. Cross-match domain tokens with Secretary of State LLC records to identify a plausible business mailing address, then verify the match before contact. An LLC name match does not establish registrant identity. Run low-rate DNS and HTTP observations across 72 hours, recording timestamps, response codes, sitemap discovery, SPF, and DMARC. An inaccessible sitemap.xml is not proof that no sitemap exists, and public search results cannot prove zero indexed Google pages. Request owner-authorized Search Console evidence where indexing matters. Send a clearly private technical diagnostic with specific fixes and a separate optional domain offer. Missing SPF or DMARC does not force a purchase. If data access or identity confidence fails, fall back to permitted CZDS snapshots and verified LLC addresses, not aggressive WHOIS scraping.

## 2. Choose the settlement structure before releasing registrar control  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Separate payment transport from transaction protection. A wire moves money between bank accounts; escrow defines when a third party may release money; marketplace checkout combines payment collection with contractual transfer procedures. These are different layers, and a transaction may use several simultaneously. For a direct $15,000 sale, the parties might sign a domain purchase agreement, fund an Escrow.com transaction by wire, transfer the registration, and release proceeds after the agreed inspection event. Verify current provider eligibility, supported jurisdictions, identity checks, fees, and dispute handling before promising that sequence. Screenshots of bank instructions or pending transfers are not funding evidence. Use the provider's authenticated dashboard and independently sourced support channels. Confirm bank-detail changes through a previously verified contact rather than replying to the message containing the change. A seller should know whether the provider requires cleared funds, merely received funds, or an additional review before authorizing transfer. Wire payments reduce some collection risks but remain exposed to fraud investigations and attempted recalls. Do not describe any rail as absolutely irreversible. When the buyer cannot pass onboarding, pause the closing rather than redirecting money through an unrelated personal account.

Define delivery as control of the specific registration, not merely a working website. A registrar push changes accounts within one registrar; an inter-registrar transfer changes the sponsoring registrar and may face policy restrictions, authentication requirements, or locks. ICANN transfer rules and provider practices can restrict recent registrations, recent transfers, or changes of registrant; check the actual domain before committing to a closing date. Never disable protective controls simply because a stranger requests a faster deal. Identify the receiving account, exact domain spelling, expiration date, DNS continuity requirements, and evidence acceptable to the settlement provider. Public RDAP data may be redacted or delayed, so combine registrar confirmation with buyer acknowledgement rather than relying on one public lookup. Deliver authorization codes only through the agreed secure channel after the provider authorizes that step. Keep the domain, associated website, email mailboxes, content copyrights, and historical customer data separate in the agreement; selling a domain does not automatically transfer those assets. For a bundle, specify whether each successful transfer releases its allocated proceeds or whether all transfers must complete. Ambiguous delivery language turns minor registrar delays into disputes over the entire purchase price.

Use installment structures only when the collection economics survive default. Twelve $500 payments create $6,000 of scheduled consideration, not $6,000 of cash on signing. If only three payments arrive, collections are $1,500 before fees, renewals, enforcement expense, and any recovery work. A domain lease or lease-to-own arrangement needs explicit custody, DNS permissions, prohibited uses, renewal responsibility, cure periods, and the event that finally transfers ownership. Do not give uncontrolled registrar access while assuming the name remains recoverable. Retainers serve a different purpose: a buyer may prepay a broker for defined search or negotiation work, but that service receipt is not automatically the owned-domain sale revenue modeled here. Factoring is also different; a factor purchases an eligible receivable, often with reserves and recourse, and cannot make an unsigned prospect into collectible revenue. Statutory disbursement is an exceptional legal process, not a marketing label for escrow. Where money has actually been deposited with a federal court, 28 U.S.C. 2042 governs withdrawal by court order. A normal domain purchase does not qualify merely because the parties mention a dispute. Match the structure to the asset, counterparty, and enforceable release conditions.

**Eight Payment and Settlement Structures: Funding Evidence, Release Conditions, and Exposure**

| Rail or contractual settlement structure | Authority and evidence required before proceeding | Release mechanics and allocation of charges | Residual risk and practical operating response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bank wire directly between verified business account holders | Authorized payer matches the agreement and bank confirms receipt | Transfer only under agreed conditions after independent funding verification | Misdirected instructions and fraud remain possible; verify changes independently |
| Third-party escrow funded through an accepted payment method | Provider completes identity checks and expressly authorizes asset delivery | Written inspection conditions determine release and allocate escrow charges | Disputed delivery can freeze proceeds; preserve registrar evidence carefully |
| ACH collection through a provider supporting this transaction | Confirm authorization, account validation, settlement timing, and return exposure | Delay ownership delivery according to provider rules and contract | Returns and unauthorized-payment claims require reserves and careful screening |
| Service retainer for separately contracted domain acquisition assistance | Buyer signs a scope specifying earned fees and cancellation | Recognize service obligations separately from owned-domain purchase consideration | Unclear scope invites refunds; itemize work and separate asset deposits |
| Statutory disbursement following an applicable court-directed deposit process | Actual court order and authorized disbursing officer control payment | Release follows the governing order rather than commercial preference | Ordinary sales lack this mechanism; use commercial escrow instead |
| Marketplace checkout through a provider such as Sedo | Confirm listing ownership, payout identity, and supported transfer workflow | Platform deducts contracted charges and releases under current terms | Off-platform diversion can breach terms and remove settlement protections |
| Factoring of an accepted and eligible business receivable | Factor verifies debtor credit, assignment restrictions, and underlying delivery | Advance less reserves and fees is financing, not additional sales | Recourse can return losses to seller; reject unsuitable receivables |
| Milestone holds within a documented multi-domain escrow transaction | Each domain has allocated value and objectively verifiable delivery | Provider releases allocated amounts only after specified evidence arrives | Vague milestones prolong holds; define failed-transfer treatment before funding |

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Bind the parties and the asset
   - Execute terms naming buyer, seller, exact domain, price, charges, and delivery method.
   - Confirm signatory authority and receiving registrar account; identify any separately excluded website assets.
2. Verify funding through the provider
   - Wait for authenticated confirmation that the agreed delivery step is authorized.
   - Reject payment screenshots as evidence and independently verify any changed beneficiary instructions.
3. Deliver with a timestamped control record
   - Complete the agreed registrar push or transfer using a secure channel.
   - Retain account confirmation, registrar notices, buyer acknowledgement, and any documented DNS continuity checks.
4. Release and reconcile the actual proceeds
   - Match the settlement statement to the purchase agreement and bank receipt.
   - Record gross sale proceeds, separately classified charges, inventory cost, and any continuing holdback.

## 3. Screen counterparties and protect fees without coercive tactics  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Counterparty screening should answer whether the buyer exists, can commit the named entity, and can fund the chosen structure. Check the relevant Secretary of State record, business website, business email domain, and independently verified telephone contact. Entity registration establishes a filing, not solvency or authority. For a material corporate purchase, request confirmation that the signatory can bind the entity and that accounts payable accepts the supplier documentation. For an installment buyer, obtain proportionate, permissioned business credit evidence, trade references, or a larger initial payment; do not collect unrelated personal records simply because a form permits it. A buyer refusing basic identity checks while demanding immediate transfer is unsuitable for unsecured terms. Screen relevant parties against applicable sanctions restrictions using current official resources, and route potential matches through the provider's process rather than assuming a similar name proves a prohibition. Keep only the records needed for the transaction and restrict access. If a buyer insists that an unrelated entity will fund the purchase, document the relationship and obtain provider acceptance before payment. Unexplained third-party funding creates refund, ownership, and fraud questions that a persuasive sales story does not resolve.

Protect fees through a written mandate and an auditable introduction trail, not through hiding important facts from either party. For brokered work, identify the client, covered domains, commission base, earning event, payment date, expenses, and any narrowly defined post-termination introduction period. A buyer-facing acquisition retainer should specify whether it is credited against a success fee and which deliverables earn it. For owned inventory, marketplace commissions are selling costs, not a separate service revenue stream. Record inquiry timestamps, introduction emails, offer versions, and the buyer's acknowledged entity name. If a lead arrived through Afternic or Sedo, examine the current listing and referral terms before proposing another checkout route. Moving a transaction outside the platform solely to avoid an owed commission can create a contractual dispute and remove useful settlement support. Use the provider's permitted workflow or negotiate a documented release. Avoid dual compensation that neither principal understands. Where the escrow provider supports commission instructions, have the parties authorize the allocation before funding; a private broker invoice does not automatically bind the provider. If the fee cannot be secured, choose whether to accept that credit exposure before doing the work.

Build an evidence packet for the name as carefully as the packet for the buyer. Preserve acquisition invoices, registrar history available to the seller, renewal receipts, historical website captures, and the rationale for selecting the name. Search USPTO Trademark Search, ordinary commercial uses, and relevant dispute decisions; a clean exact-word search does not eliminate confusing similarity or common-law claims. For expired-authority inventory, inspect Wayback Machine captures and backlink samples using tools such as Ahrefs or Majestic, but distinguish historical observations from transferable performance. Do not sell scraped personal data or imply that old customer relationships accompany the registration. Under the UDRP, a complainant must establish its policy elements, including confusing similarity, absence of rights or legitimate interests, and bad-faith registration and use; outcomes depend on facts, not a seller's preferred disclaimer. Commercial email outreach also requires attention to 15 U.S.C. 7704, including truthful routing and subject information, a valid postal address, and a functioning opt-out mechanism where applicable. Postal diagnostic mailers should identify the actual sender and avoid resembling government invoices, registrar renewal notices, or mandatory assessments. Accuracy protects both response quality and the eventual closing record.

**Counterparty and Asset Forensics Before Extending Credit or Transferring Control**

| Screening question that changes the decision | Evidence collected from a dependable source | Failure pattern requiring a different structure | Concrete operator response before further exposure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Does the purchasing entity exist as represented? | Current registry filing plus independently verified business contact details | Entity spelling differs across contract, invoice, and payment request | Resolve discrepancies and obtain one consistent contracting identity |
| Can this individual bind the purchasing entity? | Signed authority confirmation and procurement contact for material purchases | Enthusiastic employee cannot identify the controller or authorized signatory | Keep discussion preliminary until an authorized purchaser joins |
| Can the buyer support the proposed payment schedule? | Permissioned business credit information and verified relevant trade references | Buyer requests long unsecured terms without credible operating evidence | Require funded escrow or reduce exposure through controlled custody |
| Does the funding account match the documented transaction? | Provider identity verification and accepted explanation for related-party payment | Unrelated payer seeks immediate transfer followed by refund changes | Pause delivery and resolve funding through the provider |
| Does the seller control a defensible transferable asset? | Registrar control evidence, acquisition records, and documented trademark review | Dispute notice or registrar lock contradicts promised immediate delivery | Resolve restrictions before accepting a fixed closing deadline |
| Are introduction fees and platform obligations documented clearly? | Executed mandate, platform terms, and timestamped introduction correspondence | Buyer requests side payment that bypasses an earned commission | Use authorized settlement instructions or decline the proposed diversion |

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four checks before funds or domain control move**
> 
> Proceed only when identity, asset status, payment authority, and release evidence agree.
> - Verify buyer entity, signatory authority, funding relationship, and provider acceptance before treating the offer as executable.
> - Confirm registrar control, transfer eligibility, expiration runway, and absence of unresolved restrictions before promising delivery.
> - Execute price, fee allocation, inspection criteria, cancellation treatment, and installment custody terms before accepting funds.
> - Obtain authenticated funding authorization and define registrar evidence before sharing transfer credentials or pushing the domain.

## 4. Reconcile proceeds, carrying costs, and settlement exceptions  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Conservative revenue is $20,000 with a 15% margin and $3,000 annual owner net profit; Base revenue is $160,000 with a 27.5% margin and $44,000 profit; Scaled revenue is $300,000 with a 40% margin and $120,000 profit. Teams are respectively 1 FT plus 1 C, 1 FT plus 1 C, and 2 FT plus 2 C. One illustrative sales mix is four $5,000 closings, sixteen $10,000 closings, and twenty $15,000 closings. These mixes explain arithmetic, not predicted sell-through. Total annual costs must therefore equal $17,000, $116,000, and $180,000. A reconciled illustrative allocation assigns acquisition cost, renewals, transaction charges, and other operating costs respectively as $8,000/$3,000/$2,000/$4,000 Conservative; $55,000/$12,000/$16,000/$33,000 Base; and $75,000/$15,000/$30,000/$60,000 Scaled. The transaction-charge allowance is a modeling input, not a quoted provider rate. Other operating costs include the scenario's staffing and administrative burden; actual compensation choices must fit that envelope. Buyer merchant sales, website turnover, and uncollected offers never enlarge domain sale proceeds.

Keep the settlement ledger separate from the acquisition pipeline. For a $10,000 completed sale with a seller-paid $1,000 selling charge, the bank may receive $9,000 while the owned-domain sale record shows $10,000 gross proceeds and $1,000 expense under the applicable accounting treatment. Acquisition and renewal costs still reduce the modeled margin; do not call the payout profit. A buyer deposit held subject to completion is not automatically earned revenue, and an installment contract's face value is not current cash. Set recognition rules with the accountant for actual contract terms and distinguish inventory purchases from the cost assigned to sold domains. The illustrative annual allocations represent costs charged to the modeled year, not permission to expense every unsold acquisition indiscriminately. Reconcile each closing to the agreement, provider statement, bank entry, domain delivery record, and any holdback schedule. Renewals continue while negotiations stall: 500 names at an assumed $12 annual renewal consume $6,000 before premium renewals or other costs. That would exceed the Conservative renewal allocation by $3,000, requiring a smaller portfolio or a revised operating plan rather than quietly preserving the published profit.

Manage exceptions with prewritten branches instead of improvising under buyer pressure. If funds arrive but a transfer lock prevents delivery, use the agreed extension or refund process; do not substitute a different domain without written agreement. If the buyer disputes delivery, preserve provider messages and registrar records while following the contractual resolution process. If a payer requests a refund to another account, refer the request to the original collection provider rather than creating a second outbound payment. For installment defaults, apply the stated notice and cure terms, document any permitted DNS changes, and keep renewal protection active while custody is unresolved. Review aging inventory monthly using comparable-sale monitoring, inquiry routing, and renewal tracking; automation should expose carrying cost, not manufacture demand. The stated startup capital range is $8,000 to $60,000, monthly operating expense range $500 to $5,000, and first-deal window three to twenty-four months. Those ranges are planning inputs rather than a second set of annual profit equations. Maintain a cash calendar that survives delayed closings, reserved proceeds, and renewals. Reject a deal when its collection risk consumes the contribution, even if accepting it would make the revenue dashboard look stronger.
