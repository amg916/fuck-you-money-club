# Chapter 01: Buying Groups: Mechanics, Platforms & Order Survival

*BFMR walkthroughs, group-purchasing and multi-tiered B2B pricing, dealer credit limits at checkout, avoiding retailer order cancellations, and seller's permits vs. resale certificates*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Every practitioner agrees on the core trade: buying groups exist because retailers cap units per household, so members buy capped items on their own cards, ship to a group warehouse, get reimbursed by ACH or check, and keep the rewards. Profit on the spread usually runs 2-5 percent and rarely 10, so the real prize is points, minimum spend and status on near-break-even volume. BFMR is the consensus beginner pick for fast, simple payouts. Order survival is its own discipline: $500-$1,000 checkouts, split orders, PayPal to mask billing, jigged addresses, warm-up purchases and multiple household accounts. The split comes on risk tolerance and ethics: Eunice drifts toward gift-card groups with fixed payout dates, Mitch distrusts BFMR's secrecy, and the Frequent Miler hosts argue over concert tickets and scarce items after watching The Plastic Merchant vanish owing members.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Buying groups exist to beat per-household caps. Mac on Lets Get To The Points frames it with the Apple Watch: retailers hold a person to about three, so the group recruits members who buy on their own cards, ship to its warehouse, submit tracking and collect reimbursement while keeping every point the card throws off [mEjNm8zKoQs @ 00:05:01]. Pack Your Bag With Points runs the same logic on a $600 item marked down to $400 that resells for $500 to $600 but is capped at one or two per buyer, which is why groups recruit members for free instead of charging them [bUKjFvG2KfM @ 00:00:54]. Czech It Out spells out the cash-flow angle that hooks newcomers: BFMR selects the sale item, the member buys and ships it, and an ACH transfer goes out as soon as the warehouse receives the box, so the play runs on card float rather than cash up front [HNO_8rnhPeE @ 00:00:54]. Under the street language sits plain aggregation. Marketing Business Network describes three cafes each needing 1,000 cups turning into one 3,000-cup requirement with negotiating weight, and stresses that the product itself has not changed; only the buyer's size has [_lyxCWYwwJA @ 00:00:22]. The group is the large buyer; members are its distributed checkout lanes.

The consensus on economics is blunt: the spread is thin and the points are the product. Frequent Miler's hosts put typical profit at 2 to 5 percent, rarely 10, with mint coin deals as the bigger outlier, and peg roughly $250,000 a year of spend at 4x as about one million points [nMlQUH3s8qY @ 00:38:29]. The same episode notes a $2,500 gift card deal taken at a 1 percent loss that helped clear minimum spend, with $10,000 to $15,000 requirements becoming easy. Pack Your Bag With Points says commissions run $10 to $20 on an $800 iPhone and calls the real benefit points earned on net-even spend [bUKjFvG2KfM @ 00:02:36]. Mac on Lets Get To The Points lists why members bother at all: minimum spend, elite status and the $15,000-spend free-night certificates on Hyatt and Hilton cards, and argues that even taking 3 percent under retail still nets 22 percent against a Chase Sapphire Preferred 100,000-point, $5,000 offer worth at least $1,250 [mEjNm8zKoQs @ 00:37:09]. On where to begin, the room converges on one name: Mac recommends BFMR to beginners, and Eunice tells newcomers on Geobreeze Travel to start there because it auto-credits, often pays the same night and never runs past about a week and a half [zay5oOKCEls @ 00:08:43].

The workflow is the same across every group. Frequent Miler lays it out as alert by email, text, Telegram or Discord, read the instructions carefully, opt in fast because spots fill, ship or drop-ship, then get paid by ACH, e-check or PayPal anywhere from one day to three weeks, with some groups adding 0.25 to 0.5 percent if the member waits [nMlQUH3s8qY @ 00:42:36]. Serena on Lets Get To The Points gives the identical sequence: sign up, find deals, verify stock and price, reserve, order, enter tracking, get reimbursed [mEjNm8zKoQs @ 00:12:07]. Pack Your Bag With Points adds hard checks: match the exact color, size, SKU and price, and skip the buy if the price moved from $395 to $415 [bUKjFvG2KfM @ 00:04:03]. Two baseline disciplines show up everywhere. First, records: Eunice keeps a spreadsheet of group, merchant, price, expected reimbursement, tracking number and check-in value, and uses it to open price disputes, while Mac warns that payout prices can change and belong in a spreadsheet. Second, spread the exposure: Eunice monitors seven groups, dropped one for non-payment, and treats diversification as risk control even when commissions shrink to a penny [zay5oOKCEls @ 00:13:00]. As Frequent Miler puts it, nobody should expect this to run smooth as butter.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The cleanest unit economics come from Czech It Out's first month on BFMR. An Apple Pencil paid out $96.50 against $99 retail, capped at three units, for $297 of spend and about $10 profit riding on an Amazon card that pays 5 percent back, 6 percent with delayed shipping and sometimes 7 percent [HNO_8rnhPeE @ 00:02:00]. Scale changes the picture: twelve MacBooks, three per color across four colors, bought at 4 percent below retail against 6 percent back netted about 2 percent, roughly $250. Month one logged $111,000 of orders, about $70,000 of which went through after a card decline; the tracker showed $30,000 paid, $24,800 pending and $14,000 action-needed, with 154 products paid and no weekend payouts. Earnings ran $300 in June and $1,800 in July on that $70,000, and close to $3,000 had the cancellations gone through [HNO_8rnhPeE @ 00:07:57]. Pack Your Bag With Points walks the platform side: subscribe to a deal such as a Lenovo with a $5 commission, confirm stock and ship date at the retailer, ship to the posted address, upload tracking and expect ACH usually the next day, with no buyer number required [aJ7DjHaD-XU @ 00:03:57]. The same channel flags a bad payout: an Apple Watch paying $399 while Amazon sells it for $410 to $415 [aJ7DjHaD-XU @ 00:04:13].

Order survival is where the real craft lives, and John Muscarello's diagnosis is that cancellations are mostly fraud flags: new customer, billing mismatch, welcome-offer abuse with catch-all emails that end in bans, sale-limit abuse and oversized orders like a $2,000 Target cart, so orders stay at $500 to $1,000 [ThO9ard-k8E @ 00:03:24]. The fixes are specific: pay through PayPal, Apple Pay or Shop Pay to mask billing, match the card's billing address to the prep center, warm up a new account with about five units under $500 two or three times, and when an order dies, call the store, where about 90 percent say it was suspected fraud. In a second breakdown Muscarello splits into separate checkouts of ten units, runs 10 to 15 back-to-back Walmart orders, keeps Target to two or three back-to-back before spacing out, notes full-price items rarely get flagged while loss-leaders do, and refuses to resubmit failed orders because pending charges take 5 to 7 days to clear [rpJHRiGfebQ @ 00:03:39]. Christopher Grant puts hard ceilings on it: about seven Target orders a day, tested up to ten consecutive, and Ulta at six orders then a 24-hour wait with a quantity limit of ten [GFmuZ9Z3Drg @ 00:01:57].

Groups also teach the workarounds that keep warehouse shipments alive. Pack Your Bag With Points notes Walmart and Best Buy block the main warehouse address, so members use backup addresses and vary names, and still logged about 12 cancellations against 10 to 12 successes [aJ7DjHaD-XU @ 00:02:27]. Mac on Lets Get To The Points teaches address jigging: prefix the line with ABC, add punctuation or invent an apartment or suite number, then multiply volume through P2, P3 and P4 household accounts [mEjNm8zKoQs @ 00:22:25]. Eunice on Geobreeze Travel splits three $1,500 laptops into separate orders or spreads them across groups, and runs separate accounts because Target, Costco and Amazon bans cap scale [zay5oOKCEls @ 00:21:51]. Stacking fattens the thin spread. The Lets Get To The Points crew layers Amex and Chase offers with Rakuten and TopCashback, burns Business Platinum's $200 Dell credit every six months alongside $1,000 back on $5,000 of Dell spend, and runs volume on a Business Gold at 4x, timed to Black Friday and Prime Day. Pack Your Bag With Points buys Amazon gift cards on Amex Gold or Chase Freedom Flex at 4 to 5x on groceries plus Kroger fuel points, or at gas stations for 3 to 10x Marriott points plus Speedway points [bUKjFvG2KfM @ 00:05:43].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pick the platform and set hard limits
   - Open a BFMR account first, set a personal loss threshold and cap float at your bank balance.
   - Eunice sets a loss threshold anywhere from $100 to $5,000 and starts beginners on BFMR; host Julie warns against floating more than your bank balance, recalling an attendee who said they lost $60,000 [Geobreeze Travel — zay5oOKCEls @ 00:07:56].
2. Reserve, then verify at the retailer
   - Reserve the deal, then confirm stock, ship date, color, SKU and price before buying.
   - Pack Your Bag With Points checks stock and ship date at the retailer first, about 11 days on a Lenovo deal carrying a $5 commission, before buying [Pack Your Bag With Points — aJ7DjHaD-XU @ 00:00:39].
3. Place orders built to survive
   - Split into $500 to $1,000 checkouts, pay through PayPal, and rotate jigged addresses and household accounts.

4. Ship, track and reconcile every payout
   - Drop-ship to the posted warehouse, upload tracking, log each line in a spreadsheet and dispute short payouts.
   - Check-in takes 1 to 5 days and payment by check or direct deposit takes 1 to 7 days after the tracking number is logged in the group's portal [Pack Your Bag With Points — bUKjFvG2KfM @ 00:04:50].
