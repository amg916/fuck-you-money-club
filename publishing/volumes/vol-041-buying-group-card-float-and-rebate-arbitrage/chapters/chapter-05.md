# Chapter 05: Amex Financial Reviews & Account Shutdowns

*Common review triggers, high-velocity spend survival stories, handling a review honestly, income verification, blacklisting risk, and Amex's crackdown on rewards gamers*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Amex financial reviews freeze every personal and business card plus Membership Rewards until you document income, usually inside a 14-day window. CardRight, Colin Matthew, RJ Financial, AskSebby and Cal Barton converge on the same triggers: sudden spend velocity, returned payments, repeated spending power checks, limit cycling, cash-like purchases and stated income you cannot prove. Cal Barton's case reviews put high-velocity spending behind 65% of 20 data points and half of 40 cases, while two-thirds of reviewed cases asked for two months of bank statements or two pay stubs. The response playbook is unanimous: call the same day, send exactly what is asked, and submit even if your numbers are off. Disagreement centers on whether a clean, high-income profile protects you, with Cal Barton documenting decades-long members shut down by system flags, and on whether a tax transcript is mandatory or negotiable.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

An Amex financial review is a hard freeze, not a polite question. CardRight defines it as Amex temporarily suspending the account and asking to review the tax return [XsemDHBgzWE @ 00:00:23]. Colin Matthew draws the line operators need first: an income verification is small, happens at application or on a credit limit increase request, and your existing cards keep working, while a financial review suspends charging privileges across every personal and business card, locks Membership Rewards, and puts a deadline on you. His reframe is that Amex is both issuer and network, a closed loop that sees merchant, amount and pattern on every swipe, while carrying uncapped exposure on no-preset-spending-limit charge cards, so it freezes first and asks questions second [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:02:11]. RJ Financial separates this from the rewards abuse team that hunts manufactured spending: financial review is a risk-management tool built to catch people who were extended credit on stated income they cannot pay back [vfXgcEGNVuM @ 00:00:33]. AskSebby frames it as trust but verify, a middle ground between secured and unsecured lending, aimed squarely at the synthetic-identity pattern of building good history, maxing out a big line and running [Er1tcSbzC2w @ 00:01:37]. Every trigger the operators list makes sense once you see Amex protecting that unsecured exposure.

The trigger list is remarkably stable across channels. Spend velocity tops it: Cal Barton found high-velocity or irregular spending behind 65% of 20 data points [0pnnp262mLg @ 00:12:07], then half of 40 cases in a larger 2023 to 2026 sample, with no magic dollar amount because Amex compares the charge against your previous spending, account age, stated income and payment history [3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:10:17]. Returned payments come next: CardRight says a payment bounced for insufficient funds can instantly trigger a review, and Simon Katz watched a single returned payment after switching bank accounts flag every other account on file, Centurion relationship included [1AvpfkuaT_Q @ 00:01:14]. Hammering the check spending power tool shows up everywhere; CardRight cites more than three checks a day, and RJ Financial lists it beside cycling the credit limit, limits of 35K across all cards, a 25,000 limit on a single card and large changes to stated income [vfXgcEGNVuM @ 00:01:41]. The operator read is identical across all of them: none of these are nefarious alone, but the bank gets no context, so a wedding, a contractor payment or a curious late-night click all look like a borrower preparing to max out and vanish. Spend like the profile Amex underwrote, or tell them first.

On the response, the consensus is blunt: submit. CardRight recommends sending the tax return whether or not you think your income is sufficient, because skipping it means the accounts definitely get shut down while sending at least gives you a chance, and pay stubs or bank statements have been accepted in place of a return [Nevp4Iee4iw @ 00:01:12]. AskSebby runs it as a decision tree: no documents is a guaranteed shutdown, documents with a discrepancy might mean cut limits or nothing at all, so refusing is opting for the worst outcome, and if the risk is shutdown and blacklisting you might as well shoot your shot [w8CgH2R003g @ 00:08:11]. Colin Matthew adds that you can decline to sign the 4506-C and Amex cannot compel you, but declining generally means closed accounts and a flagged file, and his team has watched people who overstated income submit anyway and keep every card and limit [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:12:38]. Cal Barton's shutdown research lands in the same place: respond quickly, send exactly what was asked, make sure nothing is missing, because delays are one of the fastest ways a review turns into a shutdown [sVToTQaS_Dw @ 00:08:51]. The 14-day clock is the shared deadline every one of these operators works against.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The freeze announces itself four ways, per Colin Matthew: an unexplained decline, a red banner saying charging privileges are suspended, an email giving roughly 14 days to respond, or a call from a number you do not recognize; call back on the number printed on the back of the card, not the voicemail number, because the clock starts when they send the notice [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:03:15]. RJ Financial read the actual letter aloud: Amex places a restriction, requests financial documents, and warns that failure within 14 days can mean cancellation, and calling in routes you to an assigned specialist rather than regular customer service [vfXgcEGNVuM @ 00:04:13]. On what actually gets requested, Cal Barton's 20-case sample showed only 25% were asked for two years of tax returns while 66% were asked for two months of bank statements or two pay stubs, with verification running one to three weeks depending on Amex and the IRS [0pnnp262mLg @ 00:03:01]. AskSebby flags the submission detail people blow: upload through the specific link in the email and include the cover sheet, or nothing gets processed, then expect a call with the decision followed by an explanatory email the same day [w8CgH2R003g @ 00:12:07]. Membership Rewards stay frozen for the duration, so transfers have to be planned before the review starts.

Colin Matthew's playbook doubles as the call script: phone the same day and ask for the financial review department, be exceptionally pleasant because humans process these files, send exactly what was asked with nothing extra and nothing missing, and have a clean, true, specific story ready, such as a business opened in March or a contractor paid for a renovation, because vague answers extend the review. Then follow up every other day or every few days to confirm receipt, and once it clears ask for a goodwill credit on an $800 or $900 annual fee for the weeks you were locked out, or a prorated refund if they close you [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:11:42]. AskSebby warns that the review team is taking notes on the call, so anything you say that conflicts with verifiable documents will hurt you later [w8CgH2R003g @ 00:05:55]. Cal Barton adds a counterintuitive trap from a flooring remodel case: the Amex representative, not the customer, ran spending power checks at 15,000, 12,000, 10,000 and 5,000, advised prepaying the card, and the $12,000 retry was still declined and triggered suspension, so ask exactly what a rep is doing before they start testing amounts on your account [3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:06:32].

Prevention is mostly velocity management. Colin Matthew's curve: someone spending 3,000 a month who needs to reach 15K goes to 5K next month, 10K the month after, then 15K, uses the spend authorization process before a big charge rather than after a decline, pays early from one verified bank account, and keeps two months of statements, the latest pay stub and two years of returns in a ready folder [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:13:41]. Simon Katz relayed a Centurion team email saying Amex averages six months of spending and anything above that average by a certain percent raises a flag; the workaround is pre-warning Amex and splitting large charges across two cards, after a Rolex nearly cost a suspension [1AvpfkuaT_Q @ 00:03:02]. For money to family, Cal Barton points to Amex Send and Split instead of PayPal, Square Cash or Venmo, after a $7,500 PayPal send to a mother froze every personal and business account until two months of bank statements were provided [0pnnp262mLg @ 00:10:15]. Joe Barreto's rule closes the loop: pay large balances from your own bank account, never someone else's, and keep business spend on business cards and personal spend on personal cards so the payment trail matches the cardholder [xgsxM7jSJ8U @ 00:03:26].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Confirm It Is a True Financial Review
   - Check whether Amex lets you withdraw the request without touching your account.
   - If Amex offers to let you withdraw a credit limit increase or large purchase request, you are not in a financial review; a true review locks the account and gives no option to back out [Cal Barton — 0pnnp262mLg @ 00:01:18].
2. Call First and Lock Down the Document List
   - Call before submitting anything and note exactly which documents and deadline they give you.
   - Figure out which documents they need before uploading, because there is no point submitting A, B, C and D if they only want B; then note the roughly 14-day timeline they state [AskSebby — w8CgH2R003g @ 00:09:08].
3. Submit the Least Invasive Satisfying Documents
   - Upload exactly what was asked through the emailed link, with the cover sheet attached.
   - Send the least invasive item that satisfies them, commonly two recent bank statements from the account that pays the Amex bill or recent pay stubs, and only escalate to a tax authorization if specifically required [Colin Matthew — xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:10:50].
4. De-Risk Points and Backup Credit Lines
   - Move points you plan to use and line up non-Amex cards while the review runs.
   - If there is even a one percent chance of shutdown, do not snooze; transfer points to partners you actually want, such as Emirates or Singapore, and diversify to other issuers [AskSebby — w8CgH2R003g @ 00:09:48].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Small Moves That Separate a Five-Day Review From a Shutdown**
> 
> Operators who survive reviews cleanly control the payment rails, the points balance and the spending power tool before Amex ever looks at them.
> - Missed the Amex call? Dial the number on the back of your card, not the one in the voicemail, which protects against scams and spoofing while the clock keeps running [Colin Matthew — xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:03:29].
> - When switching payment banks, verify routing number, account number and available balance, confirm the payment cleared at both banks, and keep the old account open until every autopay has moved [Cal Barton — 3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:10:02].
> - Do not let a big Membership Rewards stash sit idle; one cardholder losing all nine cards only kept value because 580,000 points were moved out in time [Cal Barton — sVToTQaS_Dw @ 00:04:50].
> - Reserve check spending power for purchases that exceed your average spending over the past six months, and use it sparingly rather than multiple times a day [Cal Barton — 0pnnp262mLg @ 00:08:50].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over what to hand Amex once the freeze lands. CardRight runs a hard line: whether or not your income looks sufficient, send the tax return, because skipping it means your accounts will definitely get shut down, while sending it at least keeps you in the game; if no return exists, upload a pay stub or bank statement, which Amex has accepted in many cases [Nevp4Iee4iw @ 00:01:12]. Colin Matthew plays it the opposite way: send the least invasive thing that satisfies them, because plenty of reviews close on two bank statements or two pay stubs, and only escalate to tax authorization when it is specifically required [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:11:04]. AskSebby's read of hundreds of data points tilts toward CardRight: roughly 99% of the time the 4506-T is mandatory, while bank statements, brokerage records and payroll requests are uncommon edge cases for retirees and new arrivals without a prior-year US return [Er1tcSbzC2w @ 00:08:29]. Cal Barton's 20-case tally lands closer to Colin: only 25% were asked for two years of returns, while 66% were asked for two months of bank statements or two pay stubs [0pnnp262mLg @ 00:03:01]. Operator takeaway: call first, learn exactly which document is on the table, then decide.

Practitioners also split on how much paper to push across the table. Trip Astute's Ernest tells reviewees to be proactive: if there is additional information Amex should consider, document it and hand it to the assigned specialist [hdNIogj_le0 @ 00:03:59]. Colin Matthew runs the opposite rule, insisting on exactly what was requested, nothing more, nothing less, with no extra pile hoping it helps and no shortfall hoping they forget [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:12:07]. The Check Spending Power tool is a second fault line. CardRight passes along the community claim that more than three checks in a day can trigger a review [XsemDHBgzWE @ 00:00:59], and Colin widens the window to several checks inside one week. Cal Barton's 40-case audit cuts the panic down to size: only four people, about 10% of the sample, had touched the tool before their review, and in the flooring case the customer says the Amex representative ran the $15,000, $12,000, $10,000 and $5,000 tests, then advised prepaying the card, and the $12,000 retry still triggered the suspension [3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:06:32]. Cal's lesson is that a frontline rep's suggestion is not automatically safe; ask exactly what they are doing before multiple amounts get tested on your account.

The bigger argument is about who Amex is actually hunting. Joe Barreto frames the shutdown waves as targeted at behavior: international spend while cycling limits, paying from someone else's bank account, manufactured spend with quick cash-outs, self-referrals and returned purchases kept for statement credits, while regular travel, dining and everyday cardholders face much lower closure risk [xgsxM7jSJ8U @ 00:11:02]. Points Wire reports Amex executives promising a crackdown on gamers squeezing every Resy and hotel credit, with Sam reading it as tighter credit terms rather than immediate trouble for normal-looking usage [0EsrkpAxvQQ @ 00:00:53]. Cal Barton rejects the idea that only gamers get caught: the shutdown stories are long-time Platinum members with 780 to 850 scores, steady six-figure W-2 income and autopay, swept up by an automated, pattern-based system catching perfectly normal people in the crossfire [sVToTQaS_Dw @ 00:02:50]. IV(4) Prosperity bridges the two camps with an alignment thesis: Amex tracks income consistency, spend discipline and lifestyle fit, so the hack game that looks good for the first 90 days gets a cold shower once the incongruency shows, and nobody teaches how to stay nine years [L5GeiqJtqEc @ 00:07:08]. Same tactics, very different reads on the risk.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Returned payments are the trap that hits even clean files hardest. Simon Katz had their Centurion account suspended after switching bank accounts caused a returned payment; once that one flag went up, every other Amex account got flagged too, and they had to pay down a bill that was not even late to get the card back [1AvpfkuaT_Q @ 00:01:14]. Cal Barton logs a cardholder whose Capital One account was frozen for suspicious activity; the payment bounced, they repaid immediately and it cleared, yet a week later both the Platinum and Blue Cash cards were canceled and the review call dropped mid-explanation [0pnnp262mLg @ 00:10:55]. In Cal's 40-case set, one cardholder switching from Bank of America to Chase fumbled the routing details, then hit an uncleared transfer, and the review still opened about 15 days after the first failure even though the account was never late; another bounced a payment from an already-closed account and was under review within hours, though two bank statements showing $15,000 to $30,000 in monthly deposits lifted the freeze in under 24 hours [3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:09:33]. Cal's fix: verify routing and account numbers, confirm clearance at both banks, and keep the old account open until every autopay has moved.

Sudden velocity is the most expensive mistake, and it does not care about tenure. Colin Matthew cites a 25-year cardmember who ran about $3,000 a month for five months, then charged $27,000 and over $70,000 on consecutive days to pay an HVAC contractor and landed straight in review [xZwOSYlOyVc @ 00:04:08]. Cal Barton documents the welcome-bonus version: a business cardholder chasing 200,000 points on $15,000 of spend had legitimately cleared $13,800 when the card froze with five days left, and Amex flatly refused to extend the window [sVToTQaS_Dw @ 00:04:14]. Another Cal case had a five-year customer with an 800 score, a $50,000 Amex savings account and $500,000 reported income put a $46,000 watch on a card and pay it the next day, then walk out with $4,500 limits on each charge card and a Delta limit cut from $33,000 to $4,200 [3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:11:02]. AskSebby admits doing it to themselves: $100,000 of secondary-market resale purchases in six days, paid down as they went, which edged into cycling risk; the Palazzo deposit in Vegas was the final flag, and pay stubs plus bank statements cleared it in five days [w8CgH2R003g @ 00:14:10].

The economics check is the points balance. RJ Financial warns that when Amex shuts accounts down they take all your points, with no 30-day grace window to move out [vfXgcEGNVuM @ 00:08:30]. Cal Barton's shutdown files put numbers on it: one cardholder saved 580,000 membership rewards points only by moving them out before the remaining five cards and a business checking account were closed, while a 16-year customer with $150,000 income and 800-plus scores lost every card with 400,000 points still sitting in the account [sVToTQaS_Dw @ 00:05:06]. AskSebby's de-risk move during review is to transfer out rather than cash out: 300,000 points is about $3,300 through Schwab, but through Virgin Red it can be two round-trip business or first tickets from the US to Japan [w8CgH2R003g @ 00:11:09]. Repeat exposure compounds the risk: Cal found 25% of 40 cases involved a repeated or reopened review, and 48% of survivors reported lower limits, fixed spending limits, closed cards or other restrictions [3dq0zRBwn8E @ 00:12:06]. Cal also warns two reviews in a short window send shutdown odds skyrocketing, which is the cue to cut Amex spend, transfer points you plan to use and line up backup cards.
