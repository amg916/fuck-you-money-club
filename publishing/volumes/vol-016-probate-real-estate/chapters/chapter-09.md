## Executive Summary

Probate mail operators converge on three rules: hit the lead first, stay in front of it for five to six touches or longer, and open with help rather than condolences. The numbers they cite are specific. PRINTgenie's beta logged 8.9% first-touch response against a 0.7-2% industry norm. Hand-addressed greeting-card envelopes pull about 2% versus under 1% for #10 envelopes and postcards. A full first-class drip runs about $5 per recipient. One Dallas-Fort Worth operator pays $2,200 a month for 1,100 records and mines the out-of-state heirs. The fights are just as concrete: postcards as dead weight versus acceptable later touches, scrubbing non-real-estate leads versus calling every one, one optional follow-up letter versus two years of letters, and outbound pre-probate mail versus inbound-only Facebook audiences.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who runs probate mail treats the first piece as a throwaway and the sequence as the product. Open Letter Marketing tells new mailers to budget a first campaign that lasts about six months, to send at least five to six touches, and to expect the first deal around the third or fourth touch, comparing the early months to pushing a ball uphill [MLoqvKtXJ20 @ 00:04:29]. Andy of PRINTgenie, who has mailed since 1999 and sent almost a billion pieces, calls first-touch response dismal, says it kicks in at touches three and four, and says that by month five things are getting real; Bruce adds that any new list gets a six-month campaign at minimum [IRtAt7-sQHc @ 01:15:08]. Rick Ginn applies the same rule to phone work: it takes five to six touches before you even address price, and while most wholesalers try to run people over in probate, the winning move is the opposite [cyMAMIUsFfg @ 00:09:27]. The All The Leads mastermind pushes the horizon furthest. A probate list stays good for two years, converts at least 2% in year one, and converts better at six to twelve months because competition has dropped to near zero by then [6iVpjQj9wjc @ 00:21:58]. The shared math is simple: the money is in touches three through six and beyond, not in the first drop.

Speed is the second point of agreement. Flip With Rick puts it bluntly: whoever gets to the lead first has the greatest competitive advantage, end of story, which is why that operator pulls county files weekly or twice weekly [ZS8hjtChnG8 @ 00:05:59]. Rick Ginn calls speed probably the most important factor when marketing to a probate and targets seller conversations 30 to 90 days after filing [cyMAMIUsFfg @ 00:08:52]. Matt and Andy at PRINTgenie make the same argument for automation. Manually worked pre-foreclosure lists are crowded, with one card landing beside roughly 50 others, while an event-triggered monitor makes you first to the draw [FfQ1DIK5kHE @ 00:10:48]. Speed only pays when the list is qualified, though. Open Letter Marketing names the most common failure as downloading a list and mailing everyone instead of the most qualified leads, and steers beginners toward absentee owners, equity owners, driving-for-dollars, and trust-owned homes [MLoqvKtXJ20 @ 00:01:04]. That operator also draws a hard line between marketing and conversion. The mail machine only brings leads in; converting them and spotting real deals is a separate sales skill that new investors usually lack. The budget therefore has to cover both the postage and the time it takes to learn the follow-up call, or the fast, clean list still dies on the phone.

Tone is the third point of agreement. Andy at PRINTgenie never opens with 'I'm sorry you're going through' and positions every piece as an authority and a helping hand rather than a spammy salesperson: 'It's always like I am here for you to help and assist you in a situation' [IRtAt7-sQHc @ 00:08:04]. Nasser, the Charlotte investor interviewed on Realestate Doru, warns that leading with the deceased could be highly offensive and that everyone grieves differently. In that view the real work is patient follow-up with heirs who carry taxes, upkeep, and nuisance letters on a vacant house [kVqHwTzx-bo @ 00:01:35]. Bruce on the All The Leads mastermind mocks the generic turnkey letter that lands like 'sorry your wife died, do you want to sell' and replaces it with a concierge service backed by a bold, specific guarantee, such as a 24-day sale or the sale is commission-free [nNLFW4SvPGo @ 00:04:43]. Micah Nichols of US Lead List keeps the opener even softer. The first letter does not even say the sender wants to buy the property, and the conversation that follows runs on active listening, not a pitch [C0exaWWfFMk @ 00:08:58]. Across all four operators, the heir hears service first and hears an offer only after trust has been built over several touches.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The list work starts at the courthouse or with a vendor. Rick Ginn requests the probate docket, focuses on recent open cases, and records the case number, deceased, attorney, and personal representative. The next step is cross-referencing the deceased on the property appraiser site and skip tracing the PR [cyMAMIUsFfg @ 00:04:10]. Flip With Rick does the physical pull, needed seven visits to win over the clerks in a first county, and sizes an average county at 35 to 60 probates, often fewer than 25 usable leads a month, with a ceiling around 110 [ZS8hjtChnG8 @ 00:05:09]. Chad Corbett rejects the courthouse grind outright. Only about 15% of markets put data online, and the recommendation is to find a vendor and stay on dollar-productive activities [ElclT4cyYws @ 00:03:09]. David on Probate Mastery buys 1,100 records a month across 13 counties for $2,200 in Dallas-Fort Worth. A custom Excel sheet flags roughly half of the records as sharing the decedent's address and a quarter as local. David then sorts zip codes ascending and pulls the 30 to 40 out-of-state representatives for aggressive mail and calls, because most of the closed deals come from heirs who live out of state [pgW-G0oZoa4 @ 00:02:23]. The absentee radius gets tightened from 100 miles to 50 or 30, since the commute alone becomes a nuisance.

The physical piece is engineered to get opened. The All The Leads mastermind reports that greeting-card envelopes, hand-addressed in blue ink with a live first-class stamp, pull about 2% response, while #10 business envelopes and postcards come in under 1%. The goal is mail that looks messy, like a wedding or birthday card from family [6iVpjQj9wjc @ 00:02:48]. Flip With Rick prints at home on 8.5 by 11 paper in black ink at 14-point, signs in blue felt-tip pen, and prices the letter at about 75 cents, call it a buck with ink [ZS8hjtChnG8 @ 00:17:13]. The Probate Mastery letter playbook goes heavier: a cover letter plus three to four services pages, separate checklist and brochure pages, and a CMA only for hot leads, all inside a substantial FedEx-style envelope, because the odds that anyone else sends this much information are small [7NYfu3V_uSQ @ 00:02:38]. For outsourced print, Andy prices a 9x6 first-class postcard at $0.84, $0.79 at 500-plus, $0.78 on Pro, and $0.76 for minis. Competitors demand 200-piece minimums at $0.89 to $0.98. Plans run $49, $97, or $197 a month with a 14-day trial, and Bruce notes that do-it-yourself letters were costing over $1 each [IRtAt7-sQHc @ 00:51:27].

Contact data and tracking close the loop. Phone Sniper's heir-research button runs a genealogy report, the find-heirs step skip traces relatives and associates, and one click pushes phones and emails onto the contact. In the demo, six heirs received the same soft opener, which never names the deceased, with sends scheduled one hour apart [HH0UX5IwhHM @ 00:03:06]. PRINTgenie's monitor charges nothing until an event fires, then skip traces for about 10 cents including relatives' numbers and starts each person at touch one [FfQ1DIK5kHE @ 00:25:53]. To know which piece worked, the All The Leads mastermind assigns a unique CallFire number to every letter at about $1.99 a month plus 5 cents a minute. Operators then hand over a cell number on the first call to stop per-minute fees [6iVpjQj9wjc @ 00:05:12]. BatchTV launches from My List rather than Campaigns so list stacking works. In the demo a 40,000-plus database was filtered down to 785 records sitting on three or more lists. Those records were tagged 'Direct Mail' before launch, duplicate mailing addresses were removed, and the drip ran day 1, day 14, then every two weeks [xF9PxjJDUX0 @ 00:06:55]. Every tool in the stack serves one goal: reach every heir fast and know which touch made the phone ring.

   - Space the drops, autofund with a cap, and prune respondents.

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to filter the probate list at all. Chad Corbett of All The Leads says flatly that you are messing up if you are not calling every lead, and rejects the common scrub of running the decedent through the GIS or tax site and tossing anything without real estate as junk [D2NGOVJ2s6U @ 00:00:31]. The proof is a broker named Jay who forgot to scrub one month around fall 2018, let the system mail and call everyone, and banked a $12,000 commission off an inbound call [D2NGOVJ2s6U @ 00:02:10]. Open Letter Marketing teaches the opposite discipline: the most common failure is downloading a list and mailing everybody instead of the most qualified leads [MLoqvKtXJ20 @ 00:01:04]. David at Probate Mastery splits the difference with a custom Excel sheet that sorts representatives by zip code, pulls the roughly 30 to 40 out-of-state names from 1,100 monthly records, and mails and calls only those aggressively, because most closed deals come from people living out of state [pgW-G0oZoa4 @ 00:01:26]. Corbett pairs that with a benchmark of 5 to 7 closings a month in markets that call all 30 to 35 monthly leads, while David's New Jersey caller simply lacked the bandwidth to chase 600 leads a month.

Pre-probate outreach splits practitioners on ethics and volume. The Probate Mastery host says outright, 'I do not believe in direct marketing, in outbound direct mail and cold calling these lists,' warning that outbound to death records or obituaries can reach minors or the wrong person during the median 68 days before a probate filing [LaCR15esK0k @ 00:33:40]. The replacement play is inbound: skip-trace heirs into a Facebook custom audience and run ads, while conceding that some practitioners treat it as a pure numbers game and just want the phone. Micah Nichols of US Lead List runs the opposite machine, pushing 3,000 to 4,000 letters a month, outsourcing cold calling because it is scalable, and pulling 60% of last year's net profit from deceased owners holding additional properties [C0exaWWfFMk @ 00:15:27]. Rick Ginn sides with speed, calling it probably the most important factor when marketing to a probate and targeting contact 30 to 90 days after filing, and Rick Ginn never mentions probate on the cold call, opening with 'drove by' instead [cyMAMIUsFfg @ 00:08:52]. Flip With Rick backs the same urgency, saying whoever gets to the lead first has the greatest competitive advantage, end of story [ZS8hjtChnG8 @ 00:05:59].

Mail format is the third fault line. The All The Leads mastermind reports that greeting-card envelopes, hand-addressed in blue ink with a live first-class stamp, pull about 2% while #10 business envelopes and postcards land under 1%, because the card looks like it came from family [6iVpjQj9wjc @ 00:02:48]. Flip With Rick goes further and says postcards do not work in probate because they read as informal and nobody takes time to read them [ZS8hjtChnG8 @ 00:13:35]. Bruce, on the same mastermind, pushes back: one or two postcards are fine as later touches if the USP is bold, and some subscribers send three or four letters before switching to postcards as returns diminish [6iVpjQj9wjc @ 00:08:04]. The Probate Mastery letter playbook goes the other direction, shipping a cover letter plus three or four services pages in a substantial FedEx-style envelope, on the logic that a nice envelope has a psychological effect [7NYfu3V_uSQ @ 00:02:58]. Follow-up horizons split too: the mastermind keeps lists alive for two years and says conversion rises at six to twelve months because competition is near zero, while Flip With Rick runs a cold call, a letter, maybe a second letter, then drops the lead into a catch-all.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is trusting the data. The Probate Mastery host puts the best skip-trace accuracy at roughly 60% and warns there is a good chance at least 40% of your data is flawed, and pegs predictive-analytics privacy suits since around 2011 at high eight figures [LaCR15esK0k @ 00:37:19]. Open Letter Marketing names the most common rookie failure as downloading a list and mailing everyone instead of the most qualified leads, burning budget on mail that goes unnoticed [MLoqvKtXJ20 @ 00:01:04]. Andy of Print Genie adds a blind spot: standard mail is slower than first class's 7-to-10-day arrival and never sends undeliverable pieces back to you [IRtAt7-sQHc @ 00:27:17]. On the billing side, Bruce deletes respondents from the list as soon as they surface to stop further charges, pulling 4 people after the first drop hit. BatchTV flags the self-inflicted version inside the template editor: any merge field marked signature inserts your own info, so a careless setup greets every recipient by your name, and skipping the Direct Mail tag and the duplicate-address removal means mailing the same people over and over by accident; the BatchTV trainer calls that tag a game changer and a time saver [xF9PxjJDUX0 @ 00:03:47].

The second trap is judging mail too early. Andy of Print Genie says dropping one postcard is not really going to cut it, and the PRINTgenie beta's 8.9% first-touch response was framed against an industry standard of 0.7 to 2% [FfQ1DIK5kHE @ 00:16:42]. On a typical probate list Bruce expects 0.1% on mailers one and two, about 1% on mailer three, and 2 to 4% across the whole campaign, and Andy calls first-touch response dismal until touches three and four kick in [IRtAt7-sQHc @ 01:13:50]. Flip With Rick says most wholesalers quit probates between 30 and 90 days, yet that playbook expects only one to three calls a month at $30,000 to $50,000 per deal and asks for a one-year commitment [ZS8hjtChnG8 @ 00:28:35]. Open Letter Marketing budgets a first campaign of about six months and expects the first deal after the third or fourth touch [MLoqvKtXJ20 @ 00:02:11]. The unit math to hold against those curves: the Print Genie math runs about $5 per recipient over roughly 112 days first class, or about $1,000 for 217 people across five pieces billed per drop like a layaway, against Flip With Rick's home-printed letters at roughly a buck apiece.

The third trap is blowback from operations, attorneys and the asset itself. On the All The Leads mastermind, an agent's pen-mailed piece hit a postal glitch that charged recipients $3 apiece, and the agent paid every complainant $20 [nNLFW4SvPGo @ 00:30:41]. The Probate Mastery host warns that some attorneys bill the estate for unsolicited mail, with one charging $250 just for opening a letter, and estimates about 80% of attorneys are revenue-first, so sometimes it is just the wrong attorney [LaCR15esK0k @ 00:06:27]. Micah Nichols took a $110,000 hit after a sump-pump flood and a ruptured toilet on a property whose term insurance had lapsed four days earlier, and has bought under one rule since: always make money when you buy [C0exaWWfFMk @ 00:20:16]. Chad Corbett, speaking with Bill Gross, warns that the management side of this business will eat you alive without strong operations, and cites David Pennell cutting a 15-to-16-person team to run the business on 15 to 20 hours a week [ElclT4cyYws @ 00:03:58]. The same interview says a six-figure earner has no business being a court researcher and should find a vendor, noting only about 15% of markets put data online.
