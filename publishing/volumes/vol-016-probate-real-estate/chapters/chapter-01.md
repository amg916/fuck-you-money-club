# Chapter 01: Probate From Filing to Letters: The Legal Roadmap Behind Every Estate Property

*Probate defined, petitions and first court steps, letters testamentary and letters of administration, Independent Administration of Estates Act (IAEA) authority, state walkthroughs (California, Texas, Tennessee, Virginia, North Carolina), and how long probate takes*

## Executive Summary

Probate practitioners, including realtors, estate attorneys, and probate software operators, converge on one hard rule: nothing moves on an estate property until the court issues letters. A will names the executor, but only the court appoints. Banks, title companies, and insurers stonewall death certificates and wills alike. The street sequence is consistent: file in the decedent's county of domicile with the original will, certified death certificate, petition and fee; notify heirs and publish; clear the hearing; pull certified letters; grab an EIN and open an estate account. Cited numbers range from two-to-six-week letters in straightforward cases to a nine-month floor and a year-to-year-and-a-half average in California. Practitioners split on independent administration leverage, DIY filing versus hiring counsel, and how strictly to wall off the decedent's own accounts.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus: No Letters, No Deal

The first rule every operator on this roadmap repeats: a dead owner's house cannot be sold, and the mortgage company will not deal with family, until a court hands someone letters. Wanda Rochelle, a probate realtor serving Georgia, Florida and Alabama, warns that families routinely run estate affairs with no authority at all, assuming a death certificate is enough, when only letters of administration or testamentary letters unlock creditors, utility companies, the mortgage company and the right to sell the home [Ghymi1mvrRQ @ 00:00:46]. The Executor's Guide sharpens the legal point: the will names the executor, but the court appoints, and until that appointment banks go quiet in week one because they are waiting on the same order the family is [HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:00:33]. Pace Morby's probate guest defines probate as the judicial process to change title out of the deceased's name, and says nobody can carry a will into a title company and close; title wants proof of court appointment [7E_4DS5bEAI @ 00:00:22]. Paul Rabelais explains the institutional logic behind the stonewall: a bank that releases funds to the wrong person pays twice, so a will held up at the counter, photocopy or original, does not clear the bar [CnMSGOG8ey0 @ 00:03:57]. Every downstream move on an estate property hangs on that court document.

Where and how to file is equally settled. The Executor's Guide says probate opens in the court that handles estates in the county of the decedent's legal domicile at death, whether that bench is called probate court, surrogate's court, orphans' court or a probate division; out-of-state real estate may later need a narrower ancillary probate, a bridge to cross when you reach it [HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:01:05]. Scott Hans of Tennessee Probate Realty Group and Kim Ward of Horizon Real Estate in San Diego both hammer the same venue rule: file in the county where the deceased was living at death [_VnXEkxAEO0 @ 00:01:54] [Lt0e4PdSMmo @ 00:03:03]. The opening packet is near-identical across states. SwiftProbate lists the original will, not a photocopy, plus a certified death certificate, then a petition in the county of residence asking the court to admit the will and appoint the executor, listing beneficiaries, heirs and estate assets [lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:01:42]. Practitioners also agree on the naming split that institutions key on: letters testamentary when a valid will names an executor, letters of administration when there is no will, the will is invalid, or the named executor cannot serve, with both carrying the same practical authority. The label changes; the power behind it does not.

For property buyers and listing agents, the consensus turns practical fast. Tiffany Weber, a Mooresville, North Carolina real estate attorney, says the biggest estate issue at closings in Weber's office is significant delay from sellers who did not know the required steps, and that generally all heirs and their spouses must sign to convey clear title, so every heir's status matters: living, over 18, competent, married [ASuTQp-hvNU @ 00:02:07]. Weber also flags families who never opened an estate because granddad supposedly had no assets, then go under contract on the very house that is the asset. Pace Morby's probate guest describes heirs who sit on an inherited house for 20 years on the strength of a will, only for the title search to show a deed in a dead person's name when they finally sell [7E_4DS5bEAI @ 00:01:36]. RLS Law Corporation attorney Renee Speakerman is blunt that a property cannot be listed until letters issue, calling the letters a driver's license to operate the decedent's estate [lNaVAVnUZTE @ 00:13:25]. Gary DeGrazia, a 20-year California probate real estate specialist, frames the letters as proof of authority to open estate accounts, pay bills and, most importantly, sell the estate property [lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:06:48].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The filing packet is where operators win or lose weeks. The Executor's Guide spells out the core stack: original will, certified death certificate, the court's petition form and a filing fee that runs from well under a hundred dollars for small estates to several hundred or more for large ones [HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:01:42]. In Virginia, Dwight Morris of NOVA Real Estate Solutions names the exact form: the executor files circuit court form CC1650, the probate information form, in the county where the decedent lived, and a would-be administrator files the same form when no will exists [JG6VKzYq_uA @ 00:03:52]. In California, Gary DeGrazia directs families to petition form DE111, downloadable from the county superior court, filed with DE147, the duties and liabilities acknowledgment, the original will and a filing fee of usually a few hundred dollars [lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:04:44]. DeGrazia front-loads prep with an asset list covering the estate home, accounts, vehicles and business interests plus contact information for every heir, because one missing relative's address can delay the hearing by months. The same playbook screens for shortcuts first: under $208,850 of personal property may qualify for a small estate affidavit, and a primary residence valued at $750,000 or less may qualify for a simplified transfer starting in April.

Notice and hearing mechanics carry hard numbers. DeGrazia's California sequence: after filing, take the hearing date, complete DE121, the Notice of Petition to Administer Estate, have a non-party mail it to every required relative and heir with filed proof, and publish the notice three times in a qualified newspaper of general circulation where the deceased lived, because notice is jurisdictional [lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:06:25]. SwiftProbate maps the generic five-step path: original will and certified death certificate, petition, notice to beneficiaries and heirs plus often a published newspaper notice, then a hearing where the judge checks for objections and executor fitness, then issued letters; uncontested hearings are usually brief and some states waive them for simple estates [lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:02:04]. Once letters issue, SwiftProbate recommends at least five to ten certified copies because some institutions keep the copy, and warns some institutions reject letters older than sixty to ninety days [lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:02:45]. Paul Rabelais describes the Louisiana version: the executor signs an oath to faithfully perform duties, the judge signs an order confirming the executor, and the clerk issues several certified copies with a raised seal [CnMSGOG8ey0 @ 00:07:55]. Rabelais then sends the client to the bank with letters, an IRS tax ID, the death certificate and a driver's license.

After letters, the clock runs on statutory windows. DeGrazia sets a 120-day post-letters task list: open an estate bank account, pull an EIN from the IRS, file inventory and appraisal on DE160 and DE161 with a probate referee charging 0.1% of total asset values or a $75 minimum, and send DE157 notice to creditors [lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:07:14]. Morris adds Virginia's strict four-month deadline to file the CC1670 inventory, backed by professional appraisals for real estate and date-of-death bank statements [JG6VKzYq_uA @ 00:07:47]. Speed levers come from independent administration. Andrew Meinzer of Meinzer Law Firm explains that limited IAEA authority still forces prior court approval to sell, exchange, option or borrow against estate real property, while other actions usually run on a mailed Notice of Proposed Action giving at least 15 days, filed with the court; parties who do not object in time cannot object later, and IAEA must be specifically requested, usually in the Petition for Probate [k-XM0z4Yjzs @ 00:01:50]. Speakerman says most of Speakerman's own cases run on full authority, letting administrators sell real estate without court confirmation, while limited-authority sales become courtroom overbid auctions with the judge acting as auctioneer [lNaVAVnUZTE @ 00:08:12].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Assemble the opening packet
   - Gather the certified death certificate, original will, full asset list and contact information for every heir and beneficiary.
   - DeGrazia's prep list covers the estate home, accounts, vehicles and business interests, warning one missing relative's address can delay the hearing by months [California Probate Real Estate Made Simple — lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:03:24]; the clerk confirms the current county filing fee [The Executor's Guide — HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:01:42].
2. File in the county of domicile and request independent authority
   - File the petition in the estate court of the county where the decedent legally lived and specifically request full IAEA or independent authority.
   - Venue is the county of legal domicile at death [The Executor's Guide — HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:01:05]; IAEA authority is not automatic and is normally requested through the Petition for Probate [Meinzer Legal Briefs — k-XM0z4Yjzs @ 00:03:04].
3. Run notice and clear the hearing
   - Have a non-party mail notice to all required heirs, publish as required, file proof, and appear or clear the hearing.
   - California requires DE121 mailed by a non-party plus three newspaper publications [California Probate Real Estate Made Simple — lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:06:25]; the judge checks objections and executor fitness, and uncontested hearings are usually brief [SwiftProbate — lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:02:25].
4. Pull certified letters, EIN and the estate account
   - Order a stack of certified letters, obtain the estate tax ID from the IRS, and open a bank account in the estate's name.
   - Order at least five to ten certified copies [SwiftProbate — lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:02:45]; apply online for the estate tax ID [American Estate Planning Series — CnMSGOG8ey0 @ 00:10:02]; open the account in the estate's name and never mingle estate money with personal money [Ronald D. Payne II - Estates Attorney — 5JWPb5QsWx4 @ 00:02:22].

>

> - List every heir and relative in the California petition and notice, even ones disinherited by the will; DeGrazia calls the omission the most common avoidable mistake and says the judge will continue the hearing [California Probate Real Estate Made Simple — lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:05:06].
> - Request full IAEA authority at filing; with it the representative can manage and sell real property with far fewer court hearings, without it the estate goes back to court for approval every time it needs to act [California Probate Real Estate Made Simple — lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:04:14].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over who runs the file. The Executor's Guide treats probate as paperwork and waiting, tells executors the clerk answers fee questions all day, and closes with 'you can do this, one step, in order, at a time,' while handing disputes, businesses, and multi-state property to a real attorney [HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:07:57]. Pace Morby lands on the opposite side based on what he has watched at the courthouse: clients who self-perform the paperwork end up in 'this big old bloody nightmare,' and most people hire a probate attorney to take them through it [7E_4DS5bEAI @ 00:02:47]. Dwight Morris backs the attorney route with his own money. Settling his mother's Illinois estate, he hired a probate attorney who handled the filings, the deadlines, and the communication with every party; it cost him some money and he never regretted it for one moment, framing the fee as an investment against penalties, court battles, and personal liability [JG6VKzYq_uA @ 00:01:27]. Wanda Rochelle, working Georgia, Florida, and Alabama, tells families to hire an attorney to assist with the probate process and to 'trust the experts' [Ghymi1mvrRQ @ 00:01:49]. The operator read: the DIY lane holds on clean, uncontested, single-county estates, and every speaker who touches real estate pushes anything messier to counsel.

Clock estimates diverge sharply, and that gap is the whole underwriting question for anyone buying or listing an estate house. SwiftProbate puts letters at two to six weeks after filing in straightforward cases, stretching to several months in busier or contested courts [lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:03:49]. Paul Rabelais, running a live Louisiana succession, says his office files the pleadings and then waits about a month or so because the judge's office works under no timeline, and sometimes his staff has to call chambers and plead for the signatures [Hi5epLariAQ @ 00:07:03]. Renee Speakerman of RLS Law Corporation says even a short California path takes a couple of months just to reach letters, with first hearings set two to four months out depending on county. She calls herself very suspicious of any attorney promising a six-month probate, has never quoted shorter than nine months, and puts her average at a year to a year and a half before anything goes to beneficiaries [lNaVAVnUZTE @ 00:02:48]. Ron Payne puts North Carolina at six to 18 months, currently running on the longer side, and longer still for intestate files where every heir must be notified first [5JWPb5QsWx4 @ 00:05:17]. The operator move: underwrite holding costs to the slow end of the county where the deceased lived, never to the best-case quote.

Practitioners also split on how much court supervision an estate should accept. Andrew Meinzer lays out two tiers of California IAEA authority: full authority lets the executor act without prior court approval except for fees, accountings, distribution, discharge, and self-dealing, while limited authority adds court approval for selling, exchanging, optioning, or borrowing against estate real property [k-XM0z4Yjzs @ 00:01:50]. Gary DeGrazia tells families to request IAEA authority right in the petition, because full authority lets them manage and even sell real property with far fewer court hearings, and without it they are back in court for approval every time they need to act [lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:04:14]. Amanda Rocha tempers the pitch: IAEA is not a shorter way through probate per se, it is an informal process with less paperwork and less waiting for a judge, and an objecting heir or a surprise creditor still slows it down [vslnvJCGR5c @ 00:00:39]. Paul Rabelais shows the Louisiana version when the will is silent on independence: his office drafts agreements for every out-of-state niece and nephew to sign so the executor becomes independent and can pay estate bills without a judge approving each one [Hi5epLariAQ @ 00:04:47]. For any acquisition, the authority tier printed on the letters decides whether the sale runs through a courtroom.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common street-level failure is showing up without letters. Wanda Rochelle warns that families assume a death certificate will suffice and start handling estate affairs with no authority, when only letters let them deal with creditors, utility companies, and the mortgage company, or sell the home [Ghymi1mvrRQ @ 00:00:46]. Paul Rabelais says he has fielded the same call hundreds of times: an heir walks into the bank mama used for 40 years carrying the will and a fresh death certificate, the mortgage is past due, and the bank refuses both the money and any information about the accounts [CnMSGOG8ey0 @ 00:01:00]. His explanation is pure economics: if a bank releases funds to the wrong person, the bank pays twice, so no one at the counter will act on a will held up as proof, photocopy or original. Pace Morby's segment makes the same point at the title desk: a will says where the house goes and who has authority, but you cannot take it to a title company and sell, because title wants to see that the court actually appointed you [7E_4DS5bEAI @ 00:00:22]. In the gap before appointment, The Executor's Guide draws the line: secure the home and keep every receipt, but distribution before appointment is where executors get into trouble [HPpVk5fp1No @ 00:04:35].

Real estate deals blow up on title, not on paperwork volume. Tiffany Weber says her office routinely hears that granddad had nothing so no estate was opened, then the family tries to sell the house, which is itself an asset, and discovers it may lack legal authority; she adds that generally every heir and every heir's spouse must sign at closing to convey clear title [ASuTQp-hvNU @ 00:02:25]. She warns that sellers who go under contract first risk breach of contract when they cannot deliver clear title or close on time, and estimates that maybe one in 50 estate closings is truly simple [ASuTQp-hvNU @ 00:03:35]. Pace Morby's segment describes the slow-motion version: heirs say they got the house from their parents, change nothing, and 20 years later the title company's deed search shows a dead owner of record and the deal stops cold [7E_4DS5bEAI @ 00:01:36]. Gary DeGrazia flags the most avoidable delay at the front end: leave a single heir or relative off the petition, even a disinherited one, and the judge continues the hearing, because the court is very strict and notice is jurisdictional [lrpGu0nZK1E @ 00:05:31]. He adds that the petitioner cannot mail the DE121 notice personally; a non-party must mail it and proof must be filed.

The economics checks run on clocks and liability. Dwight Morris says Virginia pays insolvent-estate debts in a strict order, funeral expenses first, then administration costs, certain taxes, last-illness medical bills, and finally unsecured debts like credit cards, and paying out of order can leave the personal representative personally liable; he also calls the four-month CC1670 inventory deadline strict, with delays risking court penalties [JG6VKzYq_uA @ 00:10:59]. Renee Speakerman reports a potential buyer can sit three months just waiting for a hearing to approve a limited-authority sale, and she watched an Orange County final petition filed in January draw a hearing date in August [lNaVAVnUZTE @ 00:09:12]. On the property itself, she lists tenants who must be noticed or sold around, a beneficiary living in the house who won't move, and decedents who skipped tax returns for years and must be reconstructed before the estate can wrap [lNaVAVnUZTE @ 00:13:48]. SwiftProbate adds a quiet deal-killer: some states make letters expire outright, and even where they do not, some institutions refuse letters more than sixty to ninety days old, so updated letters must be pulled from the court clerk [lAC-1ovhHAo @ 00:03:29]. Order five to ten certified copies at issuance.
