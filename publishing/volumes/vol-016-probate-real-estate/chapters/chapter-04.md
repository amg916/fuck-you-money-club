# Chapter 04: Before and Beyond the Will: Estate Planning, Medicaid Recovery, Reverse Mortgages, and Heirs' Property

*Estate planning basics and myths, attorney client-acquisition and firm-building, wealth protection for families and business owners, Medicaid estate recovery and Lady Bird/TOD deeds, reverse mortgage eligibility and what happens at death, and the heirs' property trap*

## Executive Summary

Probate operators working Medicaid-encumbered, reverse-mortgaged, and fractured-title houses get a consistent playbook from these practitioners. The consensus: states recover long-term care costs from the estates of recipients 55 and older after death, the paid-off house is the target, and a surviving spouse, a minor or disabled child, a two-year caregiver child, or an undue hardship waiver can stop or delay the claim. Street mechanics include demanding an itemized claim statement before pricing a house, filing hardship waivers inside 30-to-60-day windows, building 24-month caregiver proof files, Lady Bird and transfer on death deeds, and the HECM four-checkpoint screen. Disagreements center on deed strategy: Lady Bird deeds versus irrevocable trusts, DIY deeds versus attorney-drafted ones, and whether title changes even escape recovery in broader-estate states like Virginia.

## The Practitioner Consensus: The Paid-Off House Is the Target, and Paper Decides Who Keeps It

Every Medicaid practitioner lands on the same mechanic: the state does not take the house while the owner is alive, it comes for the house after death. Sam Brooks of Community Legal Services of Philadelphia says the most common caller question is whether they must sign the house over to get care, and the answer is always no; recovery happens only after the recipient passes and DPW then requests the probate inventory, where the home is usually the only asset left [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:02:27]. Marc Cormier, a Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices agent who sells probate homes across Maryland, Virginia, and DC, frames the trigger the same way: anyone who received long-term care Medicaid at 55 or older faces a mandatory recovery attempt, and a paid-off house is pure equity, often the only real asset mom had [t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:01:48]. Walter Hayes names the machine MERP under OBRA 1993 and prices it: long-term care runs six or seven thousand dollars a month, so a two-year stay pushes the claim past $100,000 [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:02:52]. Carol Benning reports a viewer family hit with a demand over $100,000 and told they had two months to settle or face a public auction [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:00:38]. For a probate buyer, that claim sits ahead of every heir at settlement.

The exemption map is consistent across speakers. A surviving spouse, a child under 21, and a blind or disabled child stop or delay recovery, and a caregiver child who lived in the home at least two years and kept the parent out of a facility can keep the house [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:13:25]. Brooks adds Pennsylvania specifics: recovery against a minor's home is postponed until that child turns 21, a disabled adult child postpones it until that child's death, and DPW permanently waives recovery for a resident who gave two years of care and owns no other home [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:06:44]. Hayes draws the line operators miss: spousal protection is deferred, not erased, and in many states the pause ends the day the second spouse dies, when the state can file a claim or place a lien [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:04:14]. The equally firm consensus is that the do-it-yourself deed to the kids is the worst move. Cormier gives two reasons: a gift within five years of applying can trigger a penalty, and the kids lose the stepped-up basis, which can mean a big capital gains bill on resale [t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:04:34]. Brooks warns that removing or adding a name on the deed likely affects eligibility for Medicaid-funded care.

Outside Medicaid, the estate planners agree on two hard truths. A will does not avoid probate: Kendra O'Toole of Legacy Legal Planning says a home in one name still goes through probate even with a perfect will, and only titling, beneficiary designations, joint ownership, or a trust keep it out [WJr4n3V_2aY @ 00:02:12]. Alina Nuñez of Nuñez Estate Law and Title repeats it flatly and calls the will-is-enough belief one of the big misconceptions [7cYFXxbOdbc @ 00:08:35]. The second truth is that untitled family land rots into heirs property. The Accidental Executor describes the pattern: a parent dies without probate, heirs take as tenants in common, and after two or three generations there can be 30 or 40 owners, every one of whom must sign to sell, mortgage, or sometimes even insure [JsLnTFCaOUQ @ 00:01:12]. Greg McIntyre adds that heirs who never draft a new deed hit title problems when they try to sell, and disputes over taxes and maintenance can push the property into foreclosure [T42HfbxscSQ @ 00:00:52]. McIntyre ties it to North Carolina intestate succession, where the state picks the heirs and relatives who do not get along can devalue the parcel. The Accidental Executor is blunter: banks won't lend on it and buyers won't touch it.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Claim Math, Proof Files, Deeds, and Exit Paths

Cormier runs a five-step drill whenever a recovery letter lands on an inherited house: do not ignore it because probate and the waiver both run on deadlines, do not pay before demanding an itemized statement of what Medicaid actually paid, check for a surviving spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child, ask about the hardship waiver with the deadline in writing, and know the claim amount before pricing the house or accepting an offer, since the claim is paid from the sale [t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:03:41]. In the Linda file, the itemized statement matched, no exemption fit, and the state was paid at settlement before the siblings split the rest. Hayes compresses the hardship play into one rule: if the heir lives in the home and it is their sole asset, or their income is below the state threshold, recovery can be waived outright, and on a roughly $220,000 paid-off home that one written request inside the window decides the whole house [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:13:38]. Benning supplies the transfer penalty math: uncompensated value divided by the state's average monthly nursing home cost equals months of ineligibility [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:20:58]. Brooks notes transfers to a spouse, a minor child, or a disabled adult child carry no penalty [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:09:49].

The caregiver-child exemption is won or lost on paper. Hayes says no caseworker mails the proof form; the family builds it by hand: the move-in date, a dated log of meals, medications, and appointment driving, and a doctor's letter stating the care is why the parent stayed out of a facility, laid out month by month for 24 straight months [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:07:38]. Benning tells families to confirm the child lived there the full two years before any transfer and to keep medical notes or witness statements proving the care prevented institutionalization [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:11:10]. Brooks reports DPW wants the caregiver to show they own no other home and to produce a letter from the decedent's doctor, as in the John Smith example where a niece living there five years gets a permanent waiver by proving both [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:08:21]. For pre-death planning, the Medicare Family attorney pushes Lady Bird deeds, available only in Florida, Michigan, Texas, Vermont, and West Virginia, or transfer on death deeds elsewhere: the owner keeps 100 percent control until death, the house skips probate, and the beneficiary's creditors and ex-spouse cannot reach it; the speaker recommends paying an attorney a couple hundred dollars rather than drafting it yourself [hvl2NRPSVTE @ 00:04:10].

On reverse mortgages, Tim Oddo runs the heir math plainly: parents stay on title, the home passes to the estate, and on a $600,000 house with $300,000 owed the heir sells, pays off the lien, and walks with about $300,000, or refinances the balance into the heir's own name and moves in [R4eJOKRQISs @ 00:00:53]. David Todd, the Retirement Loan Guy, screens HECM eligibility through four checkpoints: borrower 62 or older living there as primary residence with enough equity to clear any mortgage, an eligible home type including post-June 1976 manufactured homes on a permanent foundation and FHA-approved condos, title in the owner's name or a reviewed living trust but not an LLC, and an FHA appraisal for safe, sound, secure condition, with repairs often funded through a repair set-aside [T3KZz5kBYZ4 @ 00:04:09]. For fractured heirs title, The Accidental Executor lays out the fix: map the family tree from the last person on the deed with death certificates, open probate or record affidavits of heirship for each death, then have everyone sign deeds consolidating shares into one owner, a family trust, or an LLC, with quiet title as the expensive fallback when heirs are missing or refuse [JsLnTFCaOUQ @ 00:03:00].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull the deed and the claim
   - Confirm vesting at the county recorder and demand an itemized Medicaid statement.
   - A deed still in a decedent's name with no probate or affidavit of heirship recorded signals heirs property [The Accidental Executor — JsLnTFCaOUQ @ 00:02:26]. Do not pay any recovery claim until an itemized statement of what Medicaid actually paid is checked [MarcCormier — t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:03:41].
2. Screen exemptions and file inside the window
   - Call the state estate recovery unit and request the undue hardship waiver application and exact deadline by name.
   - Hayes's three moves: request the hardship waiver and its day count, confirm any spousal deferral and lien status in writing, and assemble proof before it is needed [นพดล เกิดยิ้ม — X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:15:15].
3. Build the caregiver proof file
   - Document move-in date, a dated care log, and doctor letters covering 24 straight months.
   - A wall calendar of two years of crossed-off days plus a doctor's letter is what keeps the house [นพดล เกิดยิ้ม — X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:07:56]; DPW also wants proof the caregiver owns no other home [Community Legal Services of Philadelphia — 306n65tYnxQ @ 00:08:21].
4. Price net of the claim and pick the exit
   - Set list price or offer only after the claim amount is known, then choose sale or refinance.
   - The claim is paid from the sale, so it changes what the family walks away with [MarcCormier — t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:04:01]; on a reverse mortgage the heir either sells and pays off the lien or refinances into their own name [Reverse Mortgages by Tim Oddo — R4eJOKRQISs @ 00:01:09].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Paper Beats Panic**
> 
> The practitioners repeat one edge: the family or operator who gets terms, vesting, and proof in writing before the deadline keeps the equity.
> - Request the spousal deferral and the current lien status on paper with a date, never over the phone; a promise nobody wrote down is a promise nobody has to keep [นพดล เกิดยิ้ม — X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:04:28].
> - Read the deed vesting first: tenancy by the entireties or joint tenancy with right of survivorship keeps the home out of the probate estate, and if the recipient's name is not on the deed at all, DPW has no claim [Community Legal Services of Philadelphia — 306n65tYnxQ @ 00:05:16].
> - Long-term care partnership protection is capped at what was actually paid into an eligible policy, so keep a record of every premium dollar and confirm the state participates [Carol Benning — ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:16:43].
> - List every fractional land interest down to 0.25 percent in the will or trust and name the specific heir who takes it, so the executor or trustee can find and clear it [The Family Land Lawyer — Y515EhC17RU @ 00:02:54].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The deed fight splits into two camps. The Medicare Family attorney sells the Lady Bird and transfer-on-death deed as the cheap bypass. The owner keeps 100 percent control until death, the house jumps probate and lands with the named heir, and the heir's creditors and ex-spouse cannot touch it in the meantime. Lady Bird deeds run only in Florida, Michigan, Texas, Vermont and West Virginia, and the presenter admits a homeowner can draft, notarize and record a deed solo while still pushing a couple hundred dollars for an attorney [hvl2NRPSVTE @ 00:04:10]. Community Legal Services of Philadelphia takes the opposite stance on solo moves. Tenancy-by-the-entireties or joint-tenancy-with-right-of-survivorship language keeps the house out of the probate estate, but taking a name off or adding a name before talking to a lawyer can blow Medicaid eligibility and trigger a penalty for transferring below fair market value [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:09:29]. Mark Cormier warns that retitling is not a universal shield, because states may reach joint ownership, life estates and living trusts, and Virginia's broader estate definition can reach past probate [t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:02:47]. Carol Benning adds that a joint deed can expose the home to creditors or read as a gift inside the 60-month look-back [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:05:14].

Practitioners also split on how permanent the family protections really are. Carol Benning frames the spousal rule as a hard wall: Medicaid cannot recover from the home while a surviving spouse lives there, so the equity is protected from recovery for now [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:03:17]. Walter Hayes calls that same protection deferred, not erased. In a lot of states the pause ends the day the second spouse dies, the claim can still be filed, and a lien can still land on the house. His counter-move is a written request for the spousal deferral plus a written answer on whether a lien already exists [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:04:14]. On children, Hayes says a blind or disabled child keeps the door open with no clock. Community Legal Services of Philadelphia instead describes Pennsylvania as postponing recovery until a minor turns 21 and until the death of a disabled adult child [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:06:44]. The caregiver lane differs by speaker too. Hayes and Benning describe a son or daughter with two years under the roof. The Philadelphia attorneys describe a hardship waiver for anyone, including a niece named Brenda, who lived in the home, gave two years of care during waiver services, owns no other home, and gets a letter from the doctor [306n65tYnxQ @ 00:08:21].

For the attorneys building the practices that feed this pipeline, the client-acquisition playbooks pull in different directions. Laura Cowan ranks workshops, seminars and webinars as the big one because the attorney controls that channel, with no dependence on other people sending business or on luck. Referral partners and a weekly BNI chapter rank second and third; BNI produced a lot of early business after the 2016 launch, plus a Super Lawyers nomination from the group's business law attorney [qquf8lhULDs @ 00:02:37]. Darcel Lobo leans on a reciprocal referral hub of CPAs, financial planners, insurance agents and real estate agents. Lobo states plainly, 'I give far more referrals than I receive,' and credits that giving with 17 years of a ringing phone [wQcPBrtGgYY @ 00:04:38]. Alina Nunez bets on volume video content to stay top of mind, plus sponsoring the Walk to End Alzheimer's. Eric Rosen dismisses card-shoving networking events and credits multi-million dollar cases to a young lawyers board seat [7cYFXxbOdbc @ 00:04:46]. Lobo also splits from software-heavy shops. Beginners doing simple plans can run Word templates with the customizable text only on the first and last pages, saving Wealth Counsel-type software for complex tax, special needs and Medicaid work [wQcPBrtGgYY @ 00:12:08].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most repeated firsthand failure is the claim letter that lands after the funeral. Mark Cormier's client Linda inherited a mom's house that had been paid off in 2009. After three years of nursing-home Medicaid, the state's letter showed up two months after the funeral. The itemized statement matched, no spouse, minor or disabled child existed, the hardship waiver did not fit, and the state was paid at settlement before Linda and a brother split a smaller remainder than hoped [t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:05:50]. Carol Benning reports a viewer's family hit with a demand of over $100,000 and told they had two months to settle or risk losing the property at public auction [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:00:38]. Walter Hayes supplies the clock math. Long-term care runs six or seven thousand dollars a month, so a two-year stay climbs well past $100,000. Many states then give only 30 to 60 days after the notice to request the undue hardship waiver, and after that window silence counts as consent [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:12:35]. In the Ruth example, the widow qualified, read the notice as a decision already made, did nothing, and lost a paid-off home Hayes values at about $220,000 over a form nobody mailed [X-vkv-PR3pY @ 00:13:38].

The do-it-yourself planning moves fail in predictable ways. Mark Cormier calls deeding the house to the kids the worst move, for two reasons. A gift within five years of applying for Medicaid can trigger a penalty, and the kids lose the stepped-up tax basis they get by inheriting, which can mean a big capital gains bill when they sell [t0-A8dVrr1o @ 00:04:16]. Carol Benning's Susan story shows the caregiver version of the same mistake. David moved in and managed medications and meals for over two years, but if Susan signs the deed over the moment Medicaid looks necessary, the state can read it as a gift and David can lose the home he spent years protecting [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:10:31]. Benning's Elina paid long-term care premiums for years and assumed the house was safe. The policy had to meet partnership criteria, and protection was capped strictly at what was actually paid in, leaving any excess care cost recoverable from the estate [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:16:24]. George's home appreciated above the state equity cap over 40 years, and after death the state sold it, leaving the children with nothing. Benning's penalty formula divides the uncompensated transfer value by the state's average monthly nursing-home cost [ZUxMEt0kKoU @ 00:20:58].

Heirs' property breaks families through years of silence on title. The Accidental Executor lays out the mechanics: no will or an unprobated will, kids paying the taxes for 20 or 30 years, then a sale or loan attempt reveals 30 or 40 fractional owners. Every co-owner has to sign, banks will not lend, buyers will not touch it, and investors buy a distant heir's 2% share for a few hundred dollars and file a partition action to force an auction of land worth hundreds of thousands [JsLnTFCaOUQ @ 00:01:50]. Veronica McLendon reports two near-misses from her own clients. A father's will listed the house, other properties and bank accounts but omitted the fractional family land, so the family probating the will never understood that interest existed. Another client nearly walked out of an estate planning meeting without mentioning family land because it was not 100% theirs [Y515EhC17RU @ 00:01:04]. McLendon also traced a 25-acre 'less and except' parcel that got sucked into a larger tract over successive resales [Y515EhC17RU @ 00:05:06]. Greg McIntyre adds the carrying-cost failure. When intestate heirs cannot agree who pays property taxes and maintenance, the property devalues, can go into foreclosure and may never pass to the heirs, while buyers balk without clear title [T42HfbxscSQ @ 00:00:52].
