# Chapter 02: Court Confirmation and Overbids: Selling Probate Property Under Limited vs. Full Authority

*Full-authority sale pitfalls, sales without court confirmation, the court confirmation process, overbid rules and deposits, heir objections, probate auctions, and live courtroom overbid results from Los Angeles and Las Vegas*

## Executive Summary

California probate practitioners agree that limited authority sales, roughly 5 to 10 percent of estates, must survive a court confirmation hearing that doubles as a live auction. Full authority sales close like regular listings unless an heir objects. The opening overbid is the accepted price plus 10 percent of the first $10,000 and 5 percent of the balance, so a $500,000 contract opens at $525,500. Bidders need cashier's checks for 10 percent and lose every contingency once the judge signs. Bill Gross tracked 1 in 6 Los Angeles confirmations getting overbid at a $47,500 median. The Brendan Mohr team counters that properly marketed homes rarely draw bidders. Gross also teaches three moves: 3 percent deposits raised to 10 percent at contingency waiver, escrow-payable checks, and an uncut overbid commission for the overbidder's agent.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner draws the same line. A limited authority estate cannot sell real property without a judge confirming the deal, while full authority estates sell much like a regular listing. Bill Heyman of the Brendan Mohr team puts full authority at 90 to 95 percent of probates. He says full authority escrows commonly run 18 to 30 days, against a 30-day average for a regular sale [G-_4Dz5R2z8 @ 00:01:33]. Bill Gross frames limited authority as the 5 to 10 percent of cases where the court refuses to let the estate sell without approval. That usually happens because the executor is out of state or cannot get a bond, and Los Angeles is especially tight on it [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:08:21]. The full authority label offers no certainty, though. Gross says agents wrongly type 'not required' into the MLS. If any beneficiary objects, the court has to clear that objection, often by granting a hearing continuance [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:02:42]. He also notes that the California Association of Realtors form offers only 'is required' or 'may not be,' because nobody can know at listing time. Heyman confirms that an heir objection, an objection from another interested party, or rarely the court on its own motion can push a full authority sale back into a confirmation hearing [Gz6Ih7tLzdQ @ 00:02:35].

The second point of agreement is that the confirmation hearing is a live auction with a statutory opening bid. Daryl Williams of Win-Win Realtors TV states the minimum first overbid: the accepted offer plus 10 percent of the first $10,000 plus 5 percent of the balance. He adds that the court will not let anyone bid without cashier's checks in hand, and personal checks are not accepted [nenCgIcDQpw @ 00:01:59]. Dan Collins gives the same formula from Sacramento. He adds that the presiding judge sets further bidding increments at the hearing [WinbHNSQLbg @ 00:01:21]. Lori Aronsohn reads the shorthand off page 2 of the California Association of Realtors probate purchase agreement: the accepted price plus 5 percent plus $500 [Ivp-A8gsOnI @ 00:00:51]. Williams also says both the buyer and the buyer's agent must be present to bid. A winning overbidder takes the property on the same terms and conditions as the original buyer. The Frank Lopez Broker Group adds that the original buyer must be in the room bidding. If that buyer skips the hearing, a single bid at the minimum overbid takes the house, and the money already spent on appraisal, inspections and lender approval is gone [6YQBrw5g_-c @ 00:02:42].

Every speaker agrees the confirmation order ends all buyer protection. Heyman explains that once the judge signs the order confirming sale, contingencies vanish whether or not they were released in writing. A buyer who discovers termites afterward is back in court, likely losing the deposit and possibly more, because the probate judge can order damages [Gz6Ih7tLzdQ @ 00:16:11]. Dan Collins says the court allows an overbidder a reasonable period to secure a loan. Failing to close under the notice of sale, though, will likely forfeit the 10 percent deposit as the damages the estate's attorney seeks [WinbHNSQLbg @ 00:02:20]. The second baseline is that the estate keeps selling after acceptance. Bill Gross says the petition must state best efforts to market, and the estate is required to keep marketing through the hearing. That includes inspection access at the same level offered before the petition [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:06:04]. Heyman explains why overbidders usually stay home anyway. A property marketed properly at fair market value rarely draws someone willing to pay roughly 5 percent more. So with an original buyer who came in at market, the job is reassurance, and that buyer can still bid higher in court if a rival appears [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:03:07].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The arithmetic is where unprepared bidders stumble. Brendan Mohr works through a $500,000 accepted contract. Ten percent of the first $10,000 is $1,000, and 5 percent of the remaining $490,000 is $24,500, so the judge calls the first bid at $525,500. Mohr simplifies this to contract price plus 5 percent plus $500 [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:06:30]. Lori Aronsohn runs a Sherman Oaks example: an $850,000 accepted offer plus $42,500 plus $500 sets a first overbid of $893,000 [Ivp-A8gsOnI @ 00:01:09]. After the opening bid, Heyman says judges set increments of $500, $1,000, $1,500 or $2,000 depending on property value. On $1,000 steps, a second bidder would come in at $526,500, and the original buyer can keep bidding until nobody goes higher [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:08:46]. Las Vegas runs a different format. Mario Joyner reports that the commissioner opens live bidding at $1,000 or $5,000 above the original price. His February 10 calendar presented 11 properties ranging from $170,000 to $750,000. Of the ten that remained, eight were confirmed without overbids and two were overbid, by about $15,000 and $19,000. The first stayed with a determined original buyer, and the second went to the overbidder [Eysvzbieyro @ 00:01:06].

Bill Gross teaches the money mechanics most aggressively. He says listing agents wrongly demand 10 percent earnest money in the MLS. In his approach, the buyer opens with a normal 3 percent deposit and contingency period, then raises it to 10 percent when waiving contingencies before the petition is filed. He has won accepted offers by teaching listing agents exactly that [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:23:15]. At the hearing an overbidder needs a cashier's check for 10 percent of the bid, so a $525,500 opening bid means a $52,500 check. Gross tells clients to make it payable to escrow when the rules allow. A check made out to the estate lands in the estate's bank account, and the buyer cannot claw it back without court. Escrow funds, by contrast, need both sides to agree before release [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:18:44]. His little-known angle for buyer's agents is the overbid commission. The entire commission on the overbid increase goes to the overbidder's side instead of being split with the listing agent. In his example that is an extra $2,500 on a $100,000 run-up, a differential he says he has collected four or five times in 12 months [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:13:49]. Mohr adds that bidders planning to go past the opening number should carry additional cashier's checks for those higher bids [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:09:39].

Closing speed hinges on paperwork, not the gavel. Gross measured that after a petition is filed, the estate waits at least 30 days, about 33 on average, then roughly five more weeks to close, for more than 10 weeks total. An overbidder must be ready to close within 10 days of delivery of the court order. Attorneys often wrongly assume an order submitted before the hearing can be used, but in Los Angeles it has to be submitted at or after the hearing [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:09:38]. In Los Angeles the attorney prepares the order, and it frequently gets rejected and resubmitted two or three times, which can take 30 days. In San Bernardino, the court prints the form, the judge signs it, and you walk out the door with it. Gross tells investors to plan on closing about 30 days after the court date. He warns that the deal is not assignable except by selling an LLC [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:17:58]. Aronsohn says the order takes about a week to be scanned into the court system, and escrow then takes another 10 to 21 days [Ivp-A8gsOnI @ 00:01:49]. On full authority sales, the Santa Clara specialist orders a preliminary title report as soon as letters issue. A lien caught late can turn a two-week fix into an escrow delay of 60 days or more [-UQpV25bgAM @ 00:02:50].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Clear title before the sign goes up
   - Order a preliminary title report the moment letters issue and clear any recorded liens with the probate attorney and title officer.
   - Old property taxes, utility liens and decades-old contractor claims block escrow even under full authority. Catching them before an offer keeps a two-week fix from becoming a 60-day delay [California Probate Real Estate Made Simple — -UQpV25bgAM @ 00:02:50]
2. Structure the deposit and lock the contract early
   - Accept a normal 3 percent deposit with a short contingency period, then raise it to 10 percent when contingencies are waived and before the petition is filed.
   - Gross says 10 percent is not needed until the buyer waives contingencies. Mohr sees inspection windows of 10 to 21 days that run from acceptance, not from the hearing, so removals must happen fast [Bill Gross, the LA Probate Expert — 98dpuziGiYY @ 00:23:34]
3. Keep marketing through the hearing
   - Continue showings, post inspection reports and appraisals to the MLS or email them on request, and share photos with prospective overbidders.
   - The estate is required to keep marketing through the hearing at the same inspection access it offered before the petition. Hiding information after filing is the shortcut Gross sees agents take to double end deals [Bill Gross, the LA Probate Expert — ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:06:17]
4. Run hearing day and chase the certified order
   - Have the buyer, seller and both agents present with pre-computed cashier's checks, then request the certified order confirming sale immediately and deliver it to title.
   - Title cannot close without a court-certified copy of the order. Request it the same day, because pickup or mailing can take a week or two [Sell a House in Probate California | Brendan Mohr — GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:16:17]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over how often anyone actually shows up to overbid. Brendan Mohr and Bill Heyman tell buyers not to sweat it: a properly marketed probate house goes into court at fair market value, nobody shopping for a home suddenly wants to pay 5% plus $500 more, and so overbidders rarely appear [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:02:46] [8b2qxYk3wDs @ 00:02:41]. Bill Gross attacks that comfort with a spreadsheet. After sitting through just under a thousand LA court sales and verifying 275 against MLS closings, Gross found 43 closed above the confirmed price, roughly one in six, with a median overbid of $47,500, or 6.7% over the accepted offer [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:03:52]. Gross turns that into roughly $8,000 of expected value per estate just for following procedure, and reports that houses over $1 million draw retail overbidders with a median jump of $322,000, while low-end properties pull flippers. Mario Joyner's Las Vegas calendar lands closer to Gross than to Mohr: of ten properties heard on February 10th, two were overbid, one by about $15,000 that stayed with the original buyer after an extended live auction, and one by $19,000 that went to the overbidder [Eysvzbieyro @ 00:01:24]. Gross's working rule is blunt: every court confirmation case should be prepared for an overbid, even when nobody expects one.

Practitioners teach the opening overbid two different ways, and they fight over who the cashier's check should name. The Sanborn Team quotes the statute as written, accepted offer plus 10% of the first $10,000 plus 5% of the balance, and runs a $175,000 accepted offer up to a $184,200 opening overbid [-1OrRi_sTzA @ 00:01:19]. Lori Aronsohn reads the California Association of Realtors probate purchase agreement shorthand instead, accepted price plus 5% plus $500, which puts an $850,000 Sherman Oaks deal at $893,000 before the judge sets further increments [Ivp-A8gsOnI @ 00:01:09]. The payee fight is sharper. In the data video, Gross frames a 10% cashier's check made out to the estate as seller leverage: the money hits the estate account, the attorney holds it without escrow approval, and the buyer may have to go to court to get it back; Gross also caught an attorney who never collected the check at all [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:08:25]. Yet in a training session, Gross tells buyer clients the opposite, that a check made to escrow is better because both sides must agree to release funds, and admits more than half the time the attorney hands the check back and has the buyer wire escrow anyway [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:19:01]. The payee you push depends on which side you represent.

Contingencies and loans split the room just as hard. Daryl Williams of Win-Win Realtors TV tells overbidders that probate sales usually carry no contingencies, so the loan has to be secured before anyone bids in court [nenCgIcDQpw @ 00:04:18]. Mohr calls the blanket no-contingency claim flatly untrue: the estate can grant normal inspection, appraisal and loan contingencies, which simply have to be removed by the hearing, and the confirmation order wipes out anything left unremoved [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:14:23]. Dan Collins goes further for overbidders, saying the court allows a reasonable period of time to secure a loan, with the 10% deposit as the price of failing to close under the notice of sale [WinbHNSQLbg @ 00:02:03]. The authority label itself is contested. Gross says agents who write that court confirmation is not required on a full authority listing are simply wrong, because any beneficiary objection drags the sale back to court for a hearing extension and written objections; the California Association of Realtors form only offers two choices, required or may not be required, because nobody can know at listing time [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:02:42]. Operators listing full authority property should market it as may-not-be-required, never as a guaranteed fast close.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures are clerical, and they hit both authority tracks. California Probate Real Estate Made Simple watches full authority executors in Santa Clara County rush to list before pulling title, then discover old property taxes, unpaid utility liens or a decades-old contractor claim after accepting an offer; a lien that could have cleared in two weeks now stalls escrow 60 days or more, and nervous buyers demand price reductions or walk [-UQpV25bgAM @ 00:02:26]. On the limited authority side, Gross reports that about half of LA confirmation hearings get continued, delayed or denied because the petitioner or attorney did the paperwork wrong [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:01:18]. Even confirmed sales stall at the certified order. Mohr and Heyman carried a five-month loan-backed deal with a rate lock that can cost thousands to extend, closed only because Heyman ordered the certified copy the day of the hearing, and then got a message from the attorney's office announcing it had the order five weeks after the hearing, three weeks after escrow had already closed [Gz6Ih7tLzdQ @ 00:19:16]. Gross adds that LA makes the attorney prepare the order, which often gets rejected and resubmitted two or three times, so Gross tells investors to plan on closing about 30 days after the court date [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:17:58].

The second failure cluster is treating the hearing as a formality. Frank Lopez Broker Group describes original buyers who spent 30 days on inspections, appraisal and loan approval, were told by their own agent the hearing was just procedural, skipped court, and lost the house to a single bid at the minimum overbid price [6YQBrw5g_-c @ 00:02:05]. The same team warns that the industry sells the property well the first time but never markets or documents the second, auction-style round; a year later an heir hears about overbids, finds no marketing record, and calls an attorney, which is exactly where administrators and executors open themselves to liability [6YQBrw5g_-c @ 00:04:02]. Gross names the motive behind much of this: listing agents chasing a double-ended deal allow inspections before the petition, then shut access and sit on reports and photos once the hearing is set, conduct Gross says could invalidate the sale or force a continuance if the probate attorney knew [ih5FQvZZVck @ 00:06:17]. Gross also watches double-enders sell cheap to investors, only for the court appraisal to come in so high that the contract price falls below the 90% floor [98dpuziGiYY @ 00:25:53]. The counter every one of them teaches is the same: keep marketing through the hearing and get the original buyer into the courtroom ready to bid.

Buyers and heirs who misread the stakes pay real money. Heyman has seen courts order a defaulting winner not only to forfeit the 10% deposit but to pay damages above it because the estate was harmed, and Collins says the estate's attorney will likely seek damages, with forfeiture of the deposit the most likely award [GS1quJmfAA4 @ 00:13:25] [WinbHNSQLbg @ 00:02:20]. Sellers burn money on sloppy sequencing: Mohr and Heyman warn that once the petition and newspaper notice are filed, granting even a $10,000 closing-cost credit forces the estate to repeat the filing and publication, and a buyer who cancels after those fees are paid strands over a thousand dollars of estate money [Gz6Ih7tLzdQ @ 00:13:33]. Heir objections are the costliest trap of all. In an Oakland estate, an heir living in the house wanted it and would not withdraw an objection; the great first offer evaporated, the team exited, and many months later another broker sold through court confirmation for $60,000 to $80,000 less, with holding costs, taxes and legal fees pushing the estate's damage close to six figures [P4n8gqfBg3M @ 00:05:05]. Mohr's playbook is to talk to the objecting heir first, because a written, filed withdrawal lets the sale proceed as if the objection never happened.
