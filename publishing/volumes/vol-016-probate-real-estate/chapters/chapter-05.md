## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who walks into a decedent's house opens with the same move: lock it down before a single item leaves. Lisa Kwasnik, a Sacramento realtor, changes the locks, alarm codes and garage remotes first. Next comes mail forwarding, where the post office wants proof of authority. Then the documents go into a safe deposit box [rnWBVJVgbTw @ 00:01:00]. Kim Ward in San Diego runs the same opener. Secure the home and change the locks immediately. Rehome pets, with shelters as the absolute last resort. Keep paying the upkeep services so the property doesn't slide [zSsn_jeexks @ 00:01:49]. The second non-negotiable is authority. Suzanne Rosha is blunt for California: being a beneficiary does not authorize anyone to remove belongings, and neither does having the whole family agree. That power sits with the personal representative appointed by the Superior Court probate division, and only after letters issue [Bggg_tJ_y2g @ 00:01:41]. JunkRemovalWeb writes the same rule into the hauler's sales process. Confirm who has authority first, and nobody loads a truck until that person signs. Executors work with siblings watching, and some counties demand a full inventory within 90 days [L8fzGwXd_kk @ 00:05:35]. Every play that follows rests on this baseline of a secured house and signed authority, whether that play is an estate sale, a buyout or a haul-off.

The loudest consensus is restraint. The family's urge to purge is the most expensive mistake on the table. Illinois Probate Solutions opens with 'don't throw anything away.' Incomplete sets and broken antiques still carry value, because 'one person's trash would be another person's treasure.' Their second rule is to sell nothing ahead of the estate sale. Garage-sale shoppers and antique collectors cherry-pick at a discount and strip out the advertised valuables that pull the crowd [dHEXFlygm_8 @ 00:00:21]. Jody Whitehurst, a broker in Apex, NC, lists what actually sells: vintage furniture, costume jewelry, tools and records. Jody boils the rule down to 'When in doubt, just leave it' [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:03:54]. Houston probate realtor Cesar Espinoza extends the rule to clothing and books. Call the estate sale company before donating anything, and try consignment. Those same items may also lift a buyout offer, so dumping them early leaves money in the bin [l68yR0_4B4g @ 00:04:30]. Suzanne Rosha states the downside in one line: 'Once items are gone, they're gone.' She tells executors that anything of unknown worth should be treated as valuable, and that verbal promises to hand pieces off should not be honored [Bggg_tJ_y2g @ 00:07:05]. The operating posture is to freeze the contents, then let a professional walk through before anyone fills a bag.

The third shared conviction is that the liquidation trade is unregulated, so whoever gets hired has to be interrogated. Cesar Espinoza counts about 14,000 estate liquidators. He warns that 'most estate liquidators have no formal training at all,' even though a professional association with a code of ethics exists [l68yR0_4B4g @ 00:01:42]. Sam Richardson went from family caretaker to owner of It's Hall Good in Northern Virginia. Their checklist for the cleanout side asks five things:
- Are you properly insured?
- Are employees background-checked?
- Will you hand over a certificate of insurance?
- How many years have you been in business?
- Where do the items actually end up?
They also tell executors to ask a referring attorney 'have you actually personally worked with them,' and to check whether a referral fee is changing hands [aHu-G7VTUnc @ 00:08:45]. The Consumers' Checkbook questions relayed by 23ABC cover the estate-sale side:
- How and where will you advertise?
- When do I get paid?
- What happens to leftover items?
- Do you secure valuables?
[uvfLAJ4V3PM @ 00:01:09]. Jody Whitehurst adds pricing method, fee structure and the fate of unsold items to the same interview list [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:04:09]. The questions cost nothing, while the wrong hire can cost the estate its jewelry, its cash and its paper trail. Every practitioner here treats vetting as part of the job itself.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the haul-off side, Josh of JM Junk Removers runs estate cleanouts on a load-based model built for speed. The crew is never fewer than three. One person drives the dump trailer to the landfill while two stay behind pulling items and filling ten trash cans. The cans get dumped onto tarps laid in a circle, roughly 150-200 lb per tarp. Each loaded tarp is lifted into the trailer and yanked out from underneath, so the crew never hand-stacks loose debris [0dYqJW59Zxg @ 00:05:16]. Load one always takes the big items, furniture and mattresses, so later trips are pure volume. Pricing is load price plus a labor allocation. Every full load gets 4 man-hours, which works out to 2 hours for two people or 1 hour 20 minutes for three. Two loads get 8 man-hours, and a half load gets 2 [0dYqJW59Zxg @ 00:06:56]. Josh says the labor line 'saved our bacon,' adding $400-500 on estate jobs that ran long [0dYqJW59Zxg @ 00:07:55]. A fast crew rarely triggers it at all. JunkRemovalWeb tells haulers to publish three price ranges in plain text, using franchise benchmarks:
- $275-$800 per room
- $1,000-$2,500 for a 3BR
- $2,500-$6,000+ for large homes
- $40-$100 extra per stair, per Angie
[L8fzGwXd_kk @ 00:09:53]. A published number filters tire-kickers before the walkthrough and anchors the quote.

For the sale itself, Sam and Spencer of Smile Abroad ran a DIY weekend that cleared more than $10,000. Their case for skipping a company is pure margin. Doing it themselves kept an extra 30-50% that would otherwise have gone to a company, and companies also take weeks or months to get on the calendar [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:01:37]. The marketing stack used estatesales.net as the anchor listing, backed by Facebook Marketplace, local Facebook groups and Craigslist. Roughly 50 people were already lined up at 7 a.m. on opening morning, proof that the listing stack pulls buyers without a company's mailing list [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:07:06]. Their in-sale pricing lever was a bundle promotion: 'buy five items, get 20% off,' capped at $40. It was built to move small goods off the tables in volume instead of haggling item by item at the checkout table [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:10:50]. Jody Whitehurst lays out what hiring a company costs instead: a base minimum plus 25-50% or more of proceeds. The percentage drops on high-value sales and climbs on hoarder estates, where labor eats the margin [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:08:32]. Every heir weighs the same trade: the commission line on one side, and on the other their own weekend, their own crowd control and their own floor discipline across two long sale days.

The back office runs on photographs and inventory software. EstateSail founder Ryan Root says liquidators inventory 500-700 items per house, so the platform ships with these setup tools:

- AI listings that take up to 6 photos per item and generate title, price, description and dimensions
[bx6BgPRa2JY @ 00:01:51]. Pricing pulls from a Price IQ database of more than 250,000 items, plus eBay-sold and Google Lens lookups. Root tells operators to take those lookups with a 'grain of salt' [bx6BgPRa2JY @ 00:04:12]. Preservation runs on the same discipline. Brian of Property Preservation Insiders has worked in preservation since 2012. His crews shoot 60+ photos per grass cut and at least 150-200 on a winterization, because national clients pay against the photo record [4pX2VabEor4 @ 01:19:01]. JunkRemovalWeb turns that discipline into a sales weapon with an executor packet:
- A written estimate
- A certificate of insurance on request
- A room-by-room photo log
- A list of what was removed, donated or kept
- Charity receipts
- An invoice addressed to the estate
[L8fzGwXd_kk @ 00:06:32]. Across all three trades, the photo record serves as both inventory and defense. It drives the pricing, it satisfies the executor, and it protects the crew when a family member or client asks where something went.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock Down and Confirm the Signer
   - Re-key the house and get written sign-off from whoever holds legal authority before anything is loaded.
   - Change the locks, alarm codes and garage remotes, then forward the mail with proof of authority [Selling Inherited Property - The Prepared Heir — rnWBVJVgbTw @ 00:01:00]. Confirm who has authority, and don't load a truck until that person signs [JunkRemovalWeb — L8fzGwXd_kk @ 00:05:35].
2. Photo-Inventory and Paper Sweep
   - Photograph or video every space, then hunt down documents and hidden valuables in a single thorough pass.
   - Photograph or video the garages, sheds and closets as a preliminary inventory [Local Probate Help — Bggg_tJ_y2g @ 00:03:10]. Search books, coat pockets, high shelves, tins and shoeboxes, because 'You only get one pass' [Cesar Espinoza - Houston Probate Realtor — l68yR0_4B4g @ 00:02:32].
3. Run Family Claims
   - Let relatives claim items with stickers, splitting the visits by family dynamics.
   - Use the sticky-note plan to mark claimed pieces [Horizon Real Estate & Probate and Trust Help — zSsn_jeexks @ 00:06:54]. Bring feuding relatives in on separate days so claims don't turn into fights [Selling Inherited Property - The Prepared Heir — rnWBVJVgbTw @ 00:03:55].
4. Route the Remainder
   - Match the leftover volume to an estate liquidator, a buyout company or a load-based haul-off.
   - Send large volume to an estate liquidator and small volume to a buyout [Horizon Real Estate & Probate and Trust Help — zSsn_jeexks @ 00:07:25]. Expect a buyout to pay 'pennies on the dollar' [Cesar Espinoza - Houston Probate Realtor — l68yR0_4B4g @ 00:06:08]. Haul big items on load one [JM Junk Removers — 0dYqJW59Zxg @ 00:04:39].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to run the sale yourself or hand it to a company. Sam & Spencer of Smile Abroad ran a DIY weekend that cleared more than $10,000. They argue that skipping a company keeps an extra 30 to 50 percent of the gross [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:01:37]. Their case against hiring comes down to speed and split. The companies they contacted needed weeks or months to schedule and wanted roughly 50 percent of proceeds [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:01:59]. Jody Whitehurst, a broker in Apex, North Carolina, lists doing it all yourself as a core mistake [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:10:58]. Jody frames the company fee as a base minimum plus 25 to 50 percent or more: lower on high-value estates, higher on hoarder houses [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:08:32]. JunkRemovalWeb pegs the estate sale company take at 35 to 40 percent of proceeds and positions the hauler as the cheaper follow-on crew [L8fzGwXd_kk @ 00:02:46]. The operator read is straightforward. DIY wins when the family has time, helpers, and a fast move-out deadline, as Smile Abroad did on $400 in expenses [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:15:03]. A company wins when distance, volume, or family friction make a self-run weekend unrealistic. Jody even warns families against attending their own sale [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:10:24].

The second fork is the exit route for whatever the family does not keep. Kim Ward of Horizon Real Estate in San Diego sorts by volume: a large houseful goes to an estate liquidator, and a small one goes to a buyout company [zSsn_jeexks @ 00:07:25]. Kim's file on Ruth's estate shows why buyouts matter when the calendar is tight. The estate sale company declined the job with an eight-week wait, and the buyout cleared the house in two to three weeks [zSsn_jeexks @ 00:07:58] [zSsn_jeexks @ 00:08:13]. Houston probate realtor Cesar Espinoza uses buyouts too but prices them bluntly. The offer is pennies on the dollar, sometimes with a small charge on top, in exchange for a vacant, sale-ready house [l68yR0_4B4g @ 00:06:08]. Suzanne Rosha of Local Probate Help questions whether the house needs emptying at all, and the answer is not necessarily. A property can sell as-is with belongings left inside, as an as-is-plus with selective improvements, or through a traditional sale [Bggg_tJ_y2g @ 00:08:15] [Bggg_tJ_y2g @ 00:08:53]. Jody Whitehurst pulls the other way and lists putting the house on the market before the sale as a mistake to avoid [eQ9rTXDlJQM @ 00:10:43]. Match the route to the clock, the volume, and what the buyer pool will accept.

Practitioners also split on how much the family should touch before professionals arrive. Kim Ward tells families to walk every room and do a rough sort first, pulling high-value items aside for appraisal [zSsn_jeexks @ 00:05:48]. Smile Abroad went further. They emptied one room as a staging zone and sorted the house top-down into sell, keep, or discard piles [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:03:54]. Sam Richardson of It's Hall Good, interviewed on Executorium, tells clients they do not have to separate donation, junk removal, and recycling in most cases, because the crew handles that sort [aHu-G7VTUnc @ 00:12:27]. Illinois Probate Solutions is blunter. Boxing things up or moving them to storage is often a very bad idea: it doubles the work to pack, move, and unpack, and 99 percent of storage facilities bar sales anyway [dHEXFlygm_8 @ 00:02:24]. Pre-boxed rooms also force the sale company to unbox and restage everything before doors open [dHEXFlygm_8 @ 00:02:40]. The payoff for leaving the house intact shows up on sale day. Smile Abroad drew about 50 people lined up at 7 a.m. for an open in-home sale [q0NbvHAFXsA @ 00:07:24]. Sort for family claims and valuables, then stop. Let the people pricing and hauling do the rest.
