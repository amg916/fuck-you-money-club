## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who builds a real operation starts with the legal shell. Geoff Cole puts it bluntly: form the LLC, pull the free EIN right behind it, and treat both as the base everything else stacks on, because no LLC means no enrollment and no payment [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:02:13]. Nemiton lists the same federal layer, covering entity, EIN, NPI, city or county license, insurance, and driver and vehicle files, and says most of it can be knocked out in a week, but stresses that an LLC and an EIN are never permission to transport Medicaid passengers [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:01:26]. On the courier side, The Couvent Collective, speaking as a licensed insurance agent with an entire career in insurance, argues that running under your own Social Security number leaves you personally liable, with current and future personal assets open to liens and seizure, while an LLC limits a judgment to what the company itself holds [tOXRoqW_-CM @ 00:00:58]. Alton Arell reports the market is forcing the issue anyway: right after COVID, couriers signed on under their personal names in a wild-west setup, but platforms are now refusing to work with individuals, and Alton heard that Dropoff tells new applicants they must have an LLC to get set up [9fB-wPdETAk @ 00:05:53]. The shared position is that the entity is the cheapest asset shield and credibility layer an operator can buy on day one.

The second point of agreement: you do not get paid until the payer side has you on file, so trips run before approval are trips run for free. Geoff Cole calls running trips before Medicaid enrollment is confirmed the number one beginner mistake, says the winners set up compliance first and the van second, and compares NEMT to a vending machine contract that pays nothing until you are approved to operate [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:00:31]. Nemiton reframes enrollment as three stacked layers that do not talk to each other: federal basics, the state Medicaid provider packet, and the state transportation authority, which is a completely different agency [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:00:53]. Motivational Charles confirms the broker side enforces this in practice; in North Carolina, most brokers they contacted require providers to be linked to the NC Tracks provider portal to get paid, and the portal will not take an application until an NPI is in hand [pFLQ24hu-Ac @ 00:00:35]. Jasmine and Monica of MJ Business Academy, who run an NEMT company in California, add that even when brokers bill the state or health plan on your behalf, the broker credentialing phase still demands your NPI, so the number is required whether or not you ever bill the state directly [OH1QaUPvJ8k @ 00:03:48]. Approval, not the van, is the real starting line.

Third, commercial coverage is mandatory once a vehicle is earning. Brian Jungeberg of Risk Strategies explains why the personal-policy gamble now fails: every personal auto policy carries a business-use exclusion, and insurers now pull cell phone data and dispatch-system records, or subpoena them on large claims, to prove the driver was working and walk away from the loss [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:13:54]. Brian's verdict on the solo driver who loses that bet is that they are left uninsured, paying for their own defense, and possibly losing everything they own. Geoff Cole states the same rule for passenger work: personal auto insurance does not cover paid NEMT trips, and no trip should run before the right coverage is in place [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:06:49]. Corey of Giganomic Era adds a structural point for couriers deciding whether to title a vehicle to the LLC: insurers care far less about the entity than about risk, asking who drives, how often, what is delivered, and where, so the insurance conversation has to happen before operating [lkdaLTtSbok @ 00:02:34]. Professional Insurance Strategies frames the NEMT version, saying commercial auto and general liability should be essential for any fleet and that NEMT insurance is required by most Medicare and Medicaid contracts [KXI_Sh1G-BQ @ 00:02:36].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The paperwork stack runs as a tight, parallel sequence. Geoff Cole says to file for the NPI the same day the LLC is formed, through the NPPES portal, free, with a two-to-five-business-day turnaround; most NEMT companies register a Type 2 organizational NPI to the LLC rather than a Type 1 individual number, and select the van-transport taxonomy code they give as 34390000X [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:04:22]. RouteGenie clocks the online NPPES application at about 20 minutes, with the number arriving within 10 days, and stresses accuracy because errors surface later as delays and claim rejections [3Z74zrMSRtA @ 00:03:09]. Nemiton's state Medicaid packet checklist covers the signed provider agreement, which they say to read because it is a contract and not a formality, an ACH form for direct deposit, TIN and EIN verification, copies of every business, driver, and vehicle license, proof of insurance meeting state minimums, and NPI confirmation [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:02:48]. Nemt University's walkthrough of the New York form shows how deep the disclosure goes: business name, NPI, EIN, and for each owner the name, title, date of birth, ownership percentage, and Social Security number, plus questions on whether anyone has been terminated, denied enrollment, suspended, or convicted of a crime [FB8bZXWmo1k @ 00:06:35]. The same walkthrough notes the state form itself is free.

Insurance is where the unit economics bite hardest. Geoff Cole's for-hire NEMT spec is commercial auto liability with a for-hire passenger endorsement at a $1 million CSL minimum, general liability at $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate, workers compensation for any W-2 employees, and hired and non-owned auto coverage [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:06:30]. Nemiton budgets per-vehicle premiums at roughly $5,000 to $10,000 a year in most markets and up to $15,000 in the biggest metros, on top of a typical $1 million commercial auto liability floor [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:02:09]. Brian Jungeberg's first structural fix is to keep defense costs outside the policy limit: some hired and non-owned carriers fold defense inside the limit, so a $1 million policy that burns $500,000 defending a claim leaves only $500,000 of actual protection [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:23:58]. Brian's second fix is errors and omissions. Labs routinely demand $1 million in cargo coverage, but cargo pays nothing when a lost biopsy triggers a mental anguish or emotional distress suit; professional liability covers that exposure, runs a couple thousand dollars for a startup, and doubles as a sales tool when the courier walks the client through why they bought it unasked [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:27:08].

The last mechanic is surviving the gap between applying and getting paid. Geoff Cole calls the 60-to-120-day Medicaid enrollment window the dead zone and lists three ways to keep revenue moving inside it: subcontract under an already-enrolled NEMT provider, serve private-pay clients including dialysis patients and skilled nursing facilities, and chase broker contracts that do not yet require Medicaid enrollment [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:05:01]. Nemiton warns that even after approval Medicaid pays on a 30-to-90-day cycle, which is why they say to hold 3 to 6 months of operating expenses in reserve before the first trip [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:05:57]. Jasmine and Monica of MJ Business Academy push billing at least weekly, ideally once or twice a week, because states and brokers set a submission window, roughly 30 to 60 days by their recollection, and late claims either reimburse less than the full cost of the trip or cannot be billed at all [OH1QaUPvJ8k @ 00:06:00]. They admit falling behind on billing has happened to them before. Motivational Charles flags the state-fee asymmetry that hits transportation specifically: North Carolina charged $631 for the transportation enrollment while their wife's therapy application cost only $100, roughly $500 more for transportation than therapy and peer support [pFLQ24hu-Ac @ 00:01:52].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Week one: file the federal stack in parallel
   - Form the LLC, apply for the EIN, apply for the NPI, and open the state Medicaid provider enrollment the same week.
   - Geoff Cole says all four happen simultaneously, not one at a time [Geoff Cole — dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:10:19].
2. Weeks one through four: bind insurance and build files
   - Bind commercial coverage, secure licenses, build driver files, then submit one name-matched Medicaid packet while running the state transportation registration in parallel.
   - Nemiton's sequence puts the transportation authority filing in parallel because it runs on its own slower clock [Nemiton — qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:05:38]; Geoff Cole adds that first-year insurance down payments run 20 to 35 percent of the annual premium [Geoff Cole — dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:06:49].
3. Months one and two: vehicle, brokers, private pay
   - Get the vehicle, register with every NEMT broker in the state, and take private-pay trips while Medicaid enrollment processes.
   - Geoff Cole's month-one-and-two playbook keeps revenue moving during the enrollment wait [Geoff Cole — dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:10:36].
4. Month two onward: bill weekly and document everything
   - Bill at least weekly, log every ride and on-time pickup, and after 90 days of documented history start answering VA sources sought notices.
   - MJ Business Academy warns late billing loses money against state and broker deadlines [MJ Business Academy — OH1QaUPvJ8k @ 00:05:41]; Geoff Cole treats 90 days of documented trips as past performance for federal bids [Geoff Cole — dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:11:12].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The entity fight splits cleanly along job type. Alton Arell runs app-network courier routes and says flatly that an LLC is optional: they filled out Dropoff and Lab Logistics applications with their own name, social, and address and still cleared well into six figures [9fB-wPdETAk @ 00:00:51]. The Couvent Collective, a licensed insurance agent who has spent an entire career in insurance and risk, calls that exact advice social media misinformation, arguing that a sole proprietor running under their social is personally liable, with personal assets current and future exposed to liens and seizure because the contract sits between the client company and the individual [tOXRoqW_-CM @ 00:00:58]. On the NEMT side nobody argues. Geoff Cole treats the LLC and EIN as the first domino of Medicaid work, summed up as no LLC, no enrollment, no payment, with the EIN free and done online in minutes [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:02:13]. Even Alton concedes the personal-name lane is closing: a network they believe was Dropoff now requires an LLC to onboard, filing 1099s under a personal name cost them write-offs on vehicle, gas, and upkeep, and their government contract and joint-venture work required an entity [9fB-wPdETAk @ 00:06:04]. The street read: a solo courier can launch under a social, but every practitioner who scaled past app routes ended up incorporated.

Insurance is where the sharpest philosophical split shows up. Corey at Giganomic Era argues that not everyone needs a massive policy on day one, that a part-time pharmacy run in an SUV prices nothing like full-time Sprinter freight, and that new couriers trigger crazy quotes by telling agents they do medical transportation, which signals passengers, wheelchairs, and Medicaid rides [wJcNFJjkNwA @ 00:01:51]. Corey's playbook is to stay local, hold a small radius, start in a sedan or SUV, and spell out the exact cargo and radius before asking for numbers. Ken and Brian Jungeberg on Medical Courier Connection take the hard line: skimping is a bad idea, coverage belongs in place from the very beginning, and commercial insurance gets priced into every bid as overhead next to coolers, gloves, and scanners [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:37:15]. They also break from what the big labs demand. The labs Ken serviced wrote 1 million dollars in cargo coverage into contracts, but Brian says cargo pays zero when a ruined biopsy triggers a mental-anguish suit; professional liability, or errors and omissions, covers that, and Brian pitches buying it unasked, at a couple thousand dollars, and walking clients through it as a sales tool [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:27:08]. The Couvent Collective lands with Brian, naming E&O as the coverage nobody tells new couriers they need [tOXRoqW_-CM @ 00:01:57].

The payer lane is contested too. RouteGenie frames the NPI as mandatory for anyone billing Medicaid, Medicare, or insurance, comparing NEMT without one to driving without a license and eventually getting stopped cold [3Z74zrMSRtA @ 00:00:19]. Jasmine and Monica of MJ Business Academy counter that they know NEMT operators in the industry for multiple years with no NPI at all, surviving on private-pay clients, though they still advise pulling one because it solidifies the business as legitimate and opens broker and health-plan doors [OH1QaUPvJ8k @ 00:10:56]. Registration type splits the camps again: Geoff Cole steers most NEMT companies to a Type 2 NPI registered to the LLC rather than a Type 1 for individuals, filed the same day the LLC forms [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:04:01], while RouteGenie says many fleets carry both, a Type 2 for the company plus Type 1 numbers for drivers who provide direct patient care. On where the money sits, Willina of WM NEMT Startup Coach flags Medicaid's sometimes less-than-ideal reimbursement and points to Medicare Advantage plans for possibly better rates, while warning those plans are picky about which providers they choose [8iqgyqMjxnk @ 00:02:09]. Geoff Cole goes further, calling Medicaid the floor and five-year VA set-aside contracts posted on sam.gov the ceiling.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure in this chapter is a missed registration layer. Nemiton tells of an NEMT owner who got the federal paperwork perfect, bought a van, hired a driver, and still lost 45,000 dollars in revenue because California requires CPUC registration on top of Medicaid enrollment; operating without it drew a cease and desist after three months on the road [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:01:10]. Nemiton puts the penalty for skipping that second door at shutdown, fines of 5,000 to 25,000 dollars, and contract cancellation, and warns that packets bounce when the insurance certificate name does not exactly match the LLC filing, restarting a queue measured in weeks and months [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:03:27]. Geoff Cole names the number one beginner mistake as running trips before Medicaid enrollment is confirmed and never getting paid for any of them, inside a 60 to 120 day dead zone, and flags Colorado's moratorium on new enrollments over fraud concerns [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:00:31]. Motivational Charles adds a cost shock in North Carolina: the NC Tracks provider portal application ran 631 dollars, roughly 500 more than the therapy application their wife filed, and the brokers they contacted will not pay without that account [pFLQ24hu-Ac @ 00:01:52].

Cash runs out after approval, not before it. Nemiton warns that Medicaid pays on a 30 to 90 day cycle and says to hold 3 to 6 months of operating expenses in reserve before the first trip, with per-vehicle premiums running roughly 5,000 to 10,000 dollars a year and up to 15,000 in the biggest metros [qijT2a0o9VE @ 00:05:57]. Geoff Cole adds that first-year operators face insurance down payments of 20 to 35 percent of the annual premium when policies bind [dLgmDQavyEU @ 00:06:49]. Jasmine and Monica of MJ Business Academy admit falling behind on billing has happened to them; a backlog breeds errors, states and brokers enforce submission windows they recall as roughly 30 to 60 days, and a missed deadline means reduced reimbursement or no payment at all [OH1QaUPvJ8k @ 00:06:00]. They also kill the assumption that a Medicaid partnership delivers riders: the state may put a provider on a vendor list, but it will not send clients, and the dental-plan calls that started arriving this year were mostly low-value trips. RouteGenie reports that wrong billing codes and thin trip documentation lead straight to payment delays, audits, and legal issues [pVsERYNlif4 @ 00:03:53].

On the courier side, the losses come from uninsured exposure. Brian Jungeberg explains that every personal auto policy carries a business-use exclusion, and that carriers such as Progressive and GEICO now request or subpoena cell phone data and dispatch system records after a large accident to prove the driver was working, then walk away from the claim, leaving a solo operator paying for their own defense [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:14:11]. Ken recounts an independent contractor driving for them years ago in the Midwest who went through a four-way stop while distracted and took a life; the contractor carried only a small commercial policy, so the claimants came after Ken's company [0eV-OzFHmX8 @ 00:17:26]. Corey at Giganomic Era sees the same pattern on the contract side: after six months building a hospital or lab relationship, an operator discovers their coverage misses the client's minimums and ends up scrambling for last-minute policy changes, all to have saved 50 to 100 dollars on the cheapest quote [wJcNFJjkNwA @ 00:07:04]. Professional Insurance Strategies flags another blind spot for NEMT owners running 1099 drivers or independent contractors: workers comp gets neglected, yet most states require it [KXI_Sh1G-BQ @ 00:02:00].
