## Executive Summary

Practitioners running fleets from Atlanta to Chicago converge on one rule: lock in insurance, credentials and local demand before buying a van, then fill existing seats before adding new ones. Brokers such as Modivcare, MTM and Verida supply trips from day one at low rates and slow pay, so the operators who last stack dialysis standing orders, facility contracts and private-pay passes on top. The street numbers are blunt: Medicaid pays in 30-60 days, insurance runs $3,500-$7,500 per vehicle per year depending on density, and a $1,100 monthly pass sold to 10 clients brings about $10K a month. They split on the on-ramp: JT Automations says one or two clean cars plus broker approval is enough to start, Joe Padilla pushes private pay first because Medi-Cal approval takes 5-7 months, and Er Fukuda refuses broker software and rate cuts.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The loudest agreement across the roster is sequencing: the van comes last, not first. Shantasia_RN, who runs 8 vehicles, opens by warning that buying a vehicle before researching insurance, contracts and demand can burn thousands with zero trips to show for it [hS8ke40DMr4 @ 00:00:01]. ThatNEMTGirl, operating out of Atlanta since 2019, watched a mentee buy a van before laying any foundation and put it plainly: a van is a bill [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:08:24]. The WM NEMT Startup Coach host goes further, telling owners not to even research vehicles until the business plan, systems and credit are in place [TmcbGA0zH6Y @ 00:16:14]. Research means hard local data. Dante at Nemt University has new owners map every competitor inside a roughly 10-mile radius, logging the services they run and their base rates [sj-P7btgpOQ @ 00:01:20]. Shantasia adds that profitable density matters, because a wide service area turns into empty mileage, and ThatNEMTGirl warns that a broker accepting providers in your state does not mean it accepts them in your area, citing Savannah at roughly 4.5 hours from Atlanta. The baseline prerequisite is a validated pocket of demand you can actually reach without burning fuel on deadhead legs.

The second point of near-total agreement is that personal insurance is a trap. Shantasia_RN is direct that a personal policy will not cover paid passengers and that even an expired certificate can block trips [hS8ke40DMr4 @ 00:03:17]. JT Automations spells out the shortcut some newcomers try: claim personal use to shave the premium, and the result is a denied claim and a dropped policy [dHKhNi-IC-0 @ 00:11:17]. Joe Padilla of MediRoutes learned a subtler version firsthand, starting on a commercial Progressive policy that still did not cover door-to-door service [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:17:49]. On limits, ThatNEMTGirl reports $1M is the most common broker requirement, with some brokers accepting $300K or $500K, and that commercial auto plus general liability is the floor [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:26:30]. Broker readiness also covers optics: brokers check for a website and a professional business phone line. The credential stack repeats speaker to speaker: LLC, EIN, NPI, Medicaid or broker enrollment, background checks and CPR, with drug screening, motor vehicle records and defensive driving layered on for drivers. No medical degree is needed, but CPR, vitals and defibrillator training earn respect from brokers. Approval, as Shantasia and Padilla both stress, is a license to compete for trips, not a promise of volume.

The third consensus is that cash flow, not trip demand, is what kills new owners. NEMT Launch Blueprint frames it bluntly: January trips may not be paid until March, so hold a 60-90 day reserve, because that gap is what kills new businesses [4_YiCCE47fY @ 00:04:54]. Nemiton pegs Medicaid payment at 30-60 days and tells owners to keep 90 days of cash on hand. Shantasia_RN warns that money in the business account is not automatically owner income and tells operators to reconcile billed versus paid trips before treating any balance as spendable [fWyD2LyLaKE @ 00:06:22]. The same speaker's sharpest line covers concentration risk: never confuse having access to trips with owning those trips. Joe Padilla puts dollar figures on that warning, describing a client running 3,000-4,000 trips a day who had about $1M frozen when a broker went bankrupt, and recounts selling a truck for $15K to make payroll [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:22:49]. Before any major move, Shantasia_RN runs a four-question check that includes what happens if revenue arrives late [fWyD2LyLaKE @ 00:08:17]. That is why the operators who last stack brokers, facility contracts and private pay together, keep lines of credit open, and run the business on reconciled receivables rather than gross bookings.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The credential stack is cheap in dollars and expensive in calendar time. Dante at Nemt University prices the Virginia path line by line: an LLC at scc.virginia.gov for $100, a free EIN from irs.gov, a free NPI from cms.gov that takes 7-10 days, in-person CPR through the Red Cross, a fire department or the YMCA for $75-100, and a business checking account opened with roughly a $100 deposit [sj-P7btgpOQ @ 00:04:50]. Virginia also requires CTAA PASS training, about two days at roughly $750 or $650 for members, plus $50-75 background checks with state police fingerprints and a 19-point inspection costing about $20 [sj-P7btgpOQ @ 00:09:28]. Nemiton widens the range nationally: an LLC runs $50-500, a city or county business license $100-400, and a state NEMT license $1,000-3,000 [w246jND2Ahw @ 00:02:30]. The time sink is approval. Nemiton puts Medicaid approval at 30-90 days, while Joe Padilla says California Medi-Cal takes 5-7 months, which is why Padilla's students start with nursing homes, medical groups and regional centers [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:08:01]. JT Automations notes you contract with brokers such as Modivcare and MTM rather than with Medicaid directly in states like Georgia.

Vehicle math is where the operators get the most specific. Joe Padilla sells rear-entry Grand Caravans for $30K-37K against roughly $80K new, with video calls, Carfax reports and an inspection at a shop the buyer chooses, and recommends a 4-year unlimited-mile warranty at about $4,000, roughly $70 a month, with manual ramps over power ramps [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:27:46]. John Meli of Model One Commercial Vehicles gives the ADA checklist: no carpet, commercial-grade flooring, a door opening of at least 56 inches and a ramp at least 30 inches wide, plus an invoice naming the conversion manufacturer [TmcbGA0zH6Y @ 00:11:58]. Meli adds that an $80K minivan or a powered ramp carries a 90-95% chance of not being ADA-compliant, and that new businesses should expect at least 10% down and a personal guarantee. ThatNEMTGirl favors a rear-entry manual ramp because it fits two wheelchairs for multi-loading, and paid $1,900 a month to insure two vehicles [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:21:00]. Er Fukuda of Rainbow Road Transport standardized seven vans on new hybrid Toyota Siennas after used units brought repairs lasting 2-3+ weeks, and spends $3,000-4,000 a month fueling the fleet [r-1ofTEk2SU @ 00:10:20].

On the revenue side, the practitioners describe a stacked trip engine. NEMT Launch Blueprint says brokers like Modivcare, MTM and Verida handle about 70% of Medicaid trips and supply volume from day one at low rates, while a dialysis standing order means 6 trips a week, about 156 a year [4_YiCCE47fY @ 00:02:58]. Nemiton gives published rate anchors: Louisiana at $16 base plus $1.50-2.25 a mile, Arizona at 56 cents ambulatory and 91 cents wheelchair, California at $2-3.50 per loaded mile, and private pay at $50-150 a trip [w246jND2Ahw @ 00:12:45]. ThatNEMTGirl reports private pay runs 3-4 times broker rates because the broker is a middleman, and sells monthly passes in 4, 8 or 12-ride tiers with 10 miles included, a card on file and overage billed at month end [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:43:14]. That model, in their math, turns a $1,100 pass sold to 10 clients into about $10K a month. The same operator contrasts 10 short trips at a $40 base, or $400, against 2-3 trips earning $500-700. Er Fukuda set Rainbow Road's rates by calling competitors as a pretend rider and averaging the quotes [r-1ofTEk2SU @ 00:34:53], and the owners now pay themselves at a 30-40% margin.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Days 1-5: Map Demand and Competitors
   - Plan the service model and research the local market before spending a dollar on a vehicle.
   - Run the planning block of the 30-day launch plan [NEMT Launch Blueprint — 4_YiCCE47fY @ 00:05:44], then log every competitor's services and base rates within roughly 10 miles [Nemt University — sj-P7btgpOQ @ 00:01:20].
2. Days 6-15: Entity, IDs, Insurance, Then Vehicle
   - Form the LLC, pull the EIN and NPI, bind commercial coverage, and only then buy the vehicle.
   - File the NPI early because it is free but takes 10-15 business days [Nemiton — w246jND2Ahw @ 00:02:52]; bind insurance before the vehicle because a personal policy will not cover paid passengers [Shantasia_RN — hS8ke40DMr4 @ 00:03:17].
3. Days 16-25: Brokers, Driver Files, Facilities
   - Apply to every broker in parallel and start facility visits the same week.
   - Apply to all brokers at once and show proof of network capacity [Nemiton — w246jND2Ahw @ 00:08:52]; have the MotiveCare application answers ready: NPI, number of drivers, hours and vehicles [Nemt University — sj-P7btgpOQ @ 00:10:57].
4. Days 26-30: Practice Run, Launch, Reconcile
   - Run a test day, go live, and reconcile billed versus paid trips from the first week.
   - Close the 30-day plan with a practice run and launch [NEMT Launch Blueprint — 4_YiCCE47fY @ 00:05:44]; move off manual billing before passing 20 trips a month [Nemiton — w246jND2Ahw @ 00:15:03] and reconcile billed vs. paid trips before touching the balance [Shantasia_RN — fWyD2LyLaKE @ 00:06:22].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Joe Padilla of MediRoutes pushes rear-entry Grand Caravans at $30K to $37K against roughly $80K new. ThatNEMTGirl splits the difference. Used vans are fine with due diligence, but ambulatory is a weak long-term lane because brokers and families route those riders to Uber and Lyft, so move quickly into wheelchair service [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:24:39]. The real fork is whether you buy cheap capacity now or pay for uptime up front.

Sequencing the first trips is the second open fight. Nemiton tells operators to apply to every broker at once, since Medicaid approval alone takes 30 to 90 days. Nemiton models a sample revenue split of 40/35/25 across brokers and says to show proof of network capacity in the application [w246jND2Ahw @ 00:08:52]. NEMT Launch Blueprint runs a strict trip-engine order. Brokers come first because they hand over trips from day one even at low rates. The play there is nursing homes, medical groups and regional centers first, and nobody should expect 200 broker trips or $1,000 per vehicle in year one [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:08:01]. Er Fukuda goes furthest, building private pay 100% boots on the ground, refusing broker-mandated software and refusing to cut rates. The Rainbow Road owners now pay themselves at a 30 to 40% margin while peers run 10 to 20% [r-1ofTEk2SU @ 01:01:19]. Pick the order that matches your state's approval clock, not someone else's highlight reel.

The deepest split is temperament: is NEMT a cash machine you abandon the moment it underperforms, or a long-haul community operation? United Medical Transportation Providers Group says 'screw NMT,' mocks the 'Yahoos' who make the industry sound wonderful, calls downstate New York the worst market, and frames the end goal as a passive 'honeypot.' NEMT Launch Blueprint flatly rejects the passive framing. Its warning is that this is not passive income and that owners fail when they quit after a single late broker payment [4_YiCCE47fY @ 00:06:38]. The Seabreeze owners featured by Modivcare came in from the clinical side after watching wheelchair and power-chair patients miss specialist referrals. They describe a 'wraparound Circle' of serving their own community and employing single parents [GbR_7Zaa5Oo @ 00:01:16]. ThatNEMTGirl lands in the pragmatic middle. Never put all your eggs in a broker's basket, but take broker rates when they fill schedule gaps, and quote out-of-area broker trips above the contracted rate but below private pay [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:34:24].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Cash timing, not demand, is the trap that kills most first-year operators. Nemiton warns that Medicaid pays in 30 to 60 days, that billing can drag 45 to 90 days after the trip, and that manual billing becomes unmanageable past 20 trips a month. The floor, per Nemiton, is 90 days of operating cash on hand [w246jND2Ahw @ 00:06:12]. NEMT Launch Blueprint gives the calendar version: trips run in January may not pay until March, and that gap is what kills new businesses, so budget for the reserve before launch [4_YiCCE47fY @ 00:04:54]. Joe Padilla lived the worst case. A $15K receivable went unpaid. Nursing homes pay in about 30 days while contracts take 40 to 60. A client running 3,000 to 4,000 trips a day had roughly $1M frozen when a broker went bankrupt, and Padilla sold a truck for $15K just to make payroll. Padilla's fix is standing lines of credit [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:23:38]. Shantasia_RN names the mental error behind all of this: treating the bank balance as available money. Money in the business account is not automatically owner income. Reconcile billed trips against paid trips every cycle, and before any big move ask what happens if revenue arrives late [fWyD2LyLaKE @ 00:07:29].

Insurance is where bad paperwork becomes bankruptcy. Nemiton cites a Florida operator carrying $1M in coverage who faced a $1.4M accident and went under. Nemiton pegs per-vehicle premiums from about $3,500 rural to $7,500 in a major metro [w246jND2Ahw @ 00:04:38]. JT Automations warns that claiming personal use to shave premiums backfires: claims get denied and the carrier drops you. JT's budget is $3,000 to $10,000 a year for commercial auto, general liability of at least $1M, recommended professional liability, and workers' comp [dHKhNi-IC-0 @ 00:11:17]. Joe Padilla started on a commercial Progressive policy that turned out not to cover door-to-door service [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:17:49]. Er Fukuda had the EIN and NPI ready but watched insurance delay the launch, then had one broker push a taxi policy onto the company. Fukuda's lesson is to read every contract and shop around. Rainbow Road's stack runs general liability, commercial auto, sexual abuse and molestation, umbrella, and cyber, with cyber at $1,300 a year against other quotes near $1,500 [r-1ofTEk2SU @ 00:19:04]. Every one of these losses traces back to buying a policy without matching it to the actual service being run.

The third trap is assuming a signature, a van or a driver equals revenue. Joe Padilla's Rancho Mirage account had an administrator sign and then send zero trips for three weeks. Winning the calls was 'your responsibility,' so Padilla showed up with donuts and a mariachi band and now coaches students to visit across shifts, including Saturdays [g6k_yncRR8o @ 00:09:50]. ThatNEMTGirl bought a low-roof 15-passenger Sprinter that could not be converted. To keep it earning, they ran casino trips to North Carolina and Alabama, and it took nine months to fund a real wheelchair van [5gdeSbLFdzk @ 00:18:49]. The WM NEMT Startup Coach host lost a MotiveCare contract outright. As a felon, the host could not drive, and the hired driver ruined the brakes within two weeks. That is why guest John Meli insists the owner must be able to drive and secure passengers personally [TmcbGA0zH6Y @ 00:39:45]. Shantasia_RN sums up the pattern: never confuse having access to trips with owning those trips. Recruit drivers before you need them and keep backup coverage in place rather than hiring out of desperation [fWyD2LyLaKE @ 00:04:52]. Each of these owners paid in weeks or months of dead capacity.
