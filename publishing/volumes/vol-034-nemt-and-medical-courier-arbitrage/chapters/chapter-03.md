## 1. Price collectible service units before buying route volume  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Define the economic unit as a completed, documented, contract-payable trip leg or specimen route, not a referral, passenger mile, scheduled appointment, or specimen count. RouteGenie or MediRoutes supplies dispatch evidence; the payer contract determines whether loaded mileage, waiting time, wheelchair assistance, after-hours service, and canceled appointments produce additional billable units. Link each unit to an authorization, service date, subcontractor settlement, invoice or claim identifier, acceptance timestamp, and final cash receipt. A $50 illustrative service unit would require 5,000, 18,500, or 32,000 annual completed units to reach the three gross-billing cases; these are arithmetic equivalents, not asserted market reimbursement rates. At the canonical 55% contractor settlement ratio, that unit pays $27.50 to fulfillment and leaves $22.50 before dispatch overhead and losses. A scheduled return leg that never occurs contributes zero unless the contract expressly pays a cancellation charge. Specimen routes need separate pricing for validated packaging, temperature management, and custody handoffs. Keep passenger transportation and laboratory delivery cohorts separate until their realized collection and settlement histories justify aggregation. Otherwise, a profitable recurring laboratory lane can conceal a dialysis trip cohort losing money through deadhead mileage and unpaid waiting.

Use nine mapped plays as distinct pricing and collection cohorts: standing-order Medicaid NEMT, workers' compensation transportation, hospital discharge trips, wheelchair service, dialysis return coordination, STAT specimen delivery, scheduled laboratory routes, overflow dispatch coverage, and government_rfp_subcontractor_brokerage. The first eight names describe this chapter's operating applications rather than additional supplied registry identifiers. Each needs its own rate card, denial reasons, driver availability, and cash-cycle assumptions. The government play searches SAM.gov, state procurement portals, and municipal bid boards for low-bid-density courier opportunities, then pairs proposal automation with vetted local fulfillment. A $1,000 awarded service block costing $750 to subcontract leaves a 25% management spread before internal expenses, within the specified 15–30% range. That is not the portfolio's 45% retained share. If government work is 10% of billings at 25% retention, the other 90% must retain approximately 47.2222% to preserve the canonical blended 45%. Reject a bid whose required coverage, insurance, or payment terms destroys that blend. For covered federal small-business service set-asides, assess 13 CFR 125.6 subcontracting limits before pricing. A 75% payment to a non-similarly-situated subcontractor can defeat the model; use unrestricted commercial or municipal opportunities, or qualifying similarly situated fulfillment where the governing rules permit.

Separate gross customer billings from recognized operator revenue at the contract level. The scorecard models a 45% retained dispatch contribution after 55% owner-operator settlements: $112,500 Conservative, $416,250 Base, and $720,000 Scaled. Under ASC 606, principal-versus-agent presentation depends on control of the specified service, not the word brokerage on an invoice. For the supplied net-presentation model, show retained revenue as the operator's economic revenue basis and maintain gross billings as a separate throughput measure. A principal conclusion changes presentation, not the underlying cash waterfall. Never count Medicaid program spending, laboratory test reimbursement, workers' compensation claim value, or a patient's medical bill as dispatch revenue. Document whether fuel, tolls, parking, redelivery, and specimen supplies are included in contractor pay or separately reimbursed; otherwise the assumed 55% settlement ratio is fiction. Contractually reimbursed pass-through charges must be reconciled consistently rather than selectively excluded to improve margins. Review realized retention weekly by payer and lane using completed units, not submitted invoices alone. A payer promising $60 but repeatedly paying $48 changes the realized economics even when the dispatch dashboard continues reporting the original scheduled rate.

## 2. Reconcile every deduction to the canonical owner-profit cases  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Build the annual waterfall with contractor delivery labor deducted first, then assign every remaining dollar to an explicit operating category. Formula v2.0 fixes gross billings, retained share, and final owner profit; the expense allocations below are an illustrative reconciliation, not additional scorecard facts or vendor quotations. Conservative starts with $250,000, pays $137,500 to fulfillment, retains $112,500, and deducts $82,500 to produce $30,000. Base starts with $925,000, pays $508,750, retains $416,250, and deducts $249,750 to produce $166,500. Scaled starts with $1,600,000, pays $880,000, retains $720,000, and deducts $336,000 to produce $384,000. The table decomposes those deductions into reversal allowances, dispatch staffing, software, insurance and training, acquisition, and administration. On each $1,000 of gross billings, all cases initially retain $450; remaining deductions are $330, $270, and $210, leaving $120, $180, and $240. Net margins on retained revenue are therefore 26.67%, 40.0%, and 53.33%, respectively. Do not advertise the retained margin as the gross-billing margin. The two ratios answer different questions: how efficiently the operator monetizes its retained share, and how much owner profit each customer billing dollar generates.

Model scrub and reversal allowances at 2% of gross billings in Conservative, 1.5% in Base, and 1% in Scaled: $5,000, $13,875, and $16,000. These are expected economic losses, not permission to submit unsupported claims. An internal scrub failure that is corrected before submission delays cash but does not automatically create a permanent loss. A rejected electronic transaction, a payer denial, an uncollectible invoice, and a post-payment recoupment require different reason codes and recovery owners. Where the payer accepts 837P transactions, use acknowledgment and remittance data to distinguish transmission acceptance from adjudication; broker portals may instead require proprietary trip files. Scrub authorization dates, member eligibility evidence, required mileage, vehicle class, signatures, and GPS consistency before releasing the billing batch. Record refunds against the allowance when already provided for, avoiding a second expense for the same loss. Track realized recoveries by service month so current collections cannot conceal deteriorating older cohorts. Certain identified Medicare or Medicaid overpayments trigger reporting and repayment duties under 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7k(d). Assign an owner to the applicable deadline and payer process; a reserve entry alone does not resolve an identified overpayment or justify retaining the cash.

Acquisition spending is $7,500, $27,750, and $40,000, corresponding to five, fifteen, and twenty newly activated payer or facility accounts at $1,500, $1,850, and $2,000 CAC. Count a customer only after its first collectible service cohort; proposal submissions and unsigned facility introductions are not acquisitions. If a Base account contributes $10,000 of annual billings, its 45% retained share less the 1.5% expected-loss allowance contributes $4,350 before fixed overhead, implying roughly 5.1 months of contribution-based CAC recovery under even monthly volume. Collection delay extends cash recovery beyond that operating payback. Dispatch allocations of $42,000, $120,000, and $174,000 require explicit staffing schedules, payroll burdens, and founder coverage assumptions. Conservative's allocation is particularly tight for two full-time people; it requires owner coverage or compensation assumptions validated against actual duties and wage requirements. Team labels remain 2 FT + 2 C, 2 FT + 2 C, and 3 FT + 3 C; they are not evidence that every route can be delivered by those named people. Contractor settlements include purchased fulfillment capacity. If replacement staffing costs more, lower the underwriting result instead of concealing the shortfall as owner profit. Owner distributions are not an additional operating deduction in this illustrated bridge.

## 3. Fund the service-to-cash gap rather than the invoice label  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Measure collection days from completed service to cleared customer funds, not merely from an accepted invoice to its contractual due date. A Net-30 broker that receives a weekly batch, rejects supporting documents, and pays by delayed remittance can create a substantially longer service-to-cash cycle. For the illustrated financing model, use effective collection ages of 30, 45, and 45 days and a weighted seven-day interval from service completion to driver payment. The resulting driver-funding gaps are 23, 38, and 38 days. Seven days describes an agreed settlement cadence, not a unilateral right to withhold money until the payer approves a claim. Track operational verification holds separately from contractual payment due dates and resolve disputes under the signed agreement. A weekly payment run can produce a different weighted payment age depending on the cutoff and service distribution; calculate it from actual settlements before borrowing. Use a rolling thirteen-week cash forecast with separate lines for already-earned driver pay, current receivables, expected new service, recurring overhead, and known recoupments. Include unbilled completed services because their labor obligation already exists even when the billing team has not yet created an invoice.

Calculate the contractor bridge as annual fulfillment settlements divided by 365, multiplied by the positive difference between effective collection days and weighted driver-payment days. This produces approximately $8,664 Conservative, $52,966 Base, and $91,616 Scaled. Add cash overhead during the collection interval: annual deductions excluding the noncash expected-loss allowance are $77,500, $235,875, and $320,000. Dividing each by 365 and multiplying by its collection days adds approximately $6,370, $29,080, and $39,452. Finally, earmark one month of modeled expected losses: $417, $1,156, and $1,333. Using unrounded intermediate values gives total illustrated liquidity requirements of $15,451, $83,202, and $132,402, rounded to whole dollars. The earmark is a treasury buffer, not a second income-statement deduction or a complete tail-risk estimate. This simplified bridge assumes even service volume, timely eventual payment of collectible balances, and no accumulated opening arrears. It is a financing floor for the stated assumptions, not a substitute for invoice-level forecasting. Seasonal discharges, concentrated payer exposure, payroll dates, and simultaneous large recoupments can create a much higher weekly peak. Replace smooth daily averages with actual dated cash movements before committing capacity to a new hospital or broker.

The $15,000–$65,000 startup-capital range does not automatically fund Base or Scaled receivables. Base's illustrated $83,202 operating bridge already exceeds the upper startup figure before deposits, equipment, or additional contingency. Secure customer prepayment where commercially available, a documented revolving facility, or slower account activation rather than financing growth by overdue contractor settlements. A $65,000 unrestricted cash balance leaves an $18,202 Base gap under these assumptions. Any interest, factoring discount, reserve holdback, or lender fee must appear in the expense model; the canonical profit bridge has no separate financing charge. An added $6,000 annual financing cost would reduce Base owner profit to $160,500 unless an identified expense decreases by the same amount. The stated $3,500–$18,000 monthly operating-expense range is likewise not a ceiling on all deductions at every scale. This allocation produces monthly cash overhead excluding driver settlements and expected losses of approximately $6,458, $19,656, and $26,667. Base and Scaled exceed that range and require explicit reconciliation in the operating budget. Before pledging receivables, inspect assignment restrictions, payer offset rights, and notice mechanics; UCC 9-406 and applicable healthcare payment rules can affect collection arrangements, and federal contract claims require separate assignment analysis.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Close the completed-service ledger
   - Match every completed trip or route to authorization, custody evidence, billable units, and earned contractor pay.
   - Separate unbilled work, submitted receivables, accepted receivables, disputed balances, and cleared funds without moving the service date.
2. Rebuild dated cash expectations
   - Forecast each payer's receipts using observed service-to-cash timing and each contractor's contractual payment calendar.
   - Include aged claims, holiday settlement shifts, upcoming payroll, insurance installments, known refunds, and committed new service volume.
3. Compare weekly troughs with available funding
   - Subtract restricted balances and lender holdbacks before comparing unrestricted cash plus usable credit with forecast obligations.
   - Run the baseline and fifteen-day collection-delay case; identify the first shortfall week rather than relying on month-end averages.
4. Release only funded service expansion
   - Add a new standing order or laboratory route only after its settlement bridge and backup coverage are funded.
   - If funding is insufficient, negotiate prepayment, arrange documented financing, or defer incremental volume while honoring existing payment commitments.

## 4. Stress retention, collection timing, and operational liability together  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Stress the scorecard by changing one driver at a time before combining shocks. A five-percentage-point increase in contractor settlements, from 55% to 60% of gross billings, reduces annual profit by $12,500, $46,250, and $80,000, leaving $17,500, $120,250, and $304,000. An additional two percentage points of unrecovered reversals reduces profit by $5,000, $18,500, and $32,000. Together, those shocks leave $12,500, $101,750, and $272,000 before any financing or incident costs. A fifteen-day collection delay does not itself reduce accrual profit when the balance remains collectible, but increases the illustrated funding bridge by approximately $8,836, $30,601, and $49,315, holding settlement timing constant. These additions include driver obligations and modeled cash overhead, not a second expected-loss charge. If volume falls 20% while contractor costs and expected losses vary with billings and other costs remain fixed, profit becomes $8,500, $86,025, and $243,200. Conservative therefore has little room for simultaneous reimbursement pressure and lost route density. Reprice or stop incremental loss-making commitments before aggregate volume growth disguises the deterioration. Maintain contractual continuity for existing patients and specimen handoffs while changing future capacity decisions.

Treat credentialing, custody, and worker classification as cost drivers with explicit evidence requirements. A contractor agreement alone does not establish independent-contractor status; actual control, economic dependence, and applicable federal and state tests matter. California Labor Code 2775 illustrates why a dispatch business cannot simply assume that a 1099 form preserves its labor model; assess applicable exceptions and the actual working arrangement. If reclassification creates payroll taxes, overtime, insurance, or scheduling obligations, run those costs through the settlement and staffing lines. Maintain HIPAA business associate arrangements when the service and access to protected health information require them under 45 CFR 164.502(e) and 164.504(e); do not treat a narrowly applicable courier conduit analysis as blanket permission for dispatch software to retain patient data. For employees with occupational exposure, 29 CFR 1910.1030 requires an exposure-control program and associated protections. Specimen classification determines transport requirements: qualifying Category B infectious substances follow 49 CFR 173.199, including applicable UN3373 packaging and marking. Other materials may have different requirements. CLIA laboratory procedures under 42 CFR 493.1242 inform client specifications for specimen transport and integrity; a courier should obtain those specifications rather than invent universal temperature or transit limits.

Set operating thresholds from measured economics instead of copying a competitor's acceptance rate. For each payer, publish realized retention, expected unrecovered loss, median and adverse-case collection age, cash required per new service block, and contribution after incremental staffing. A $10,000 Base-priced monthly service block retains $4,500 before the $150 expected-loss allowance, leaving $4,350 before added overhead. If the block requires a dedicated $4,000 monthly dispatcher and $600 of incremental tooling and insurance, it loses $250 monthly before financing despite matching the headline retention ratio. Decline or reprice that expansion rather than allocating the new expenses away. Conversely, an existing dense route may absorb additional authorized stops without another shift, provided custody, capacity, and pickup commitments remain intact. Review incident costs separately: a missed STAT pickup may create replacement transport, service credits, investigation time, and contract termination exposure beyond a routine claim reversal. Use priced backup capacity and written escalation rules, not optimistic zero-incident assumptions. Release owner distributions only after earned contractor pay, current overhead, known refunds, and the forecast cash trough are funded. Profit establishes earning power; reconciled unrestricted cash establishes what can safely leave the operating account.
