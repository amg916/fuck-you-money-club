## Executive Summary

Solar lead operators agree the pipeline lives on exclusive, pre-qualified lead flow and that buying shared leads or cold calling is a margin leak: David Sugrue pegs traditional acquisition at $1,000 to $2,000 per customer, and Phaibion Royal quotes a 1% call-to-appointment rate. Street mechanics split by market. Residential operators run Meta lead forms: Nazir Yakubu warms pages with video for five to seven days, holds cost per lead under $20, uses AI agents for instant callbacks and retargets booked prospects to reach up to 60% show rates, while Leadbase reports $10 to $20 leads at $25 a day. Commercial operators lean on cold email and LinkedIn, or on TNW-style paid appointments at about £400 each. Disagreements center on build-versus-buy, cold calling's viability and how much qualification a commercial lead needs before a site visit.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator selling or supplying solar leads starts from the same premise: the pipeline lives or dies on lead volume, and buying it the old way is brutal. Anthony J. Conklin opens his six-step commercial process with lead generation because 'everything starts with the lead,' and more leads means more projects [fwRL9lFmciI @ 00:00:22]. David Sugrue puts a price on the old way: a solar company may spend $200 to generate one lead, need five to ten leads per sale, and land at a $1,000 to $2,000 customer acquisition cost, while third-party vendors resell the same lead to many home improvement companies, including your direct competitors [vmJD-cz6rdc @ 00:00:36]. Phaibion Royal frames cold calling as the worst version of that equation, quoting a 1% call-to-appointment rate, roughly one appointment for every 100 dials, against the ten appointments most commercial solar companies say it takes to close one sale [rhgUISy29oc @ 00:01:39]. Leadbase aims squarely at the installer already paying an agency $2,000 to $2,500 a month, or buying leads at $75 to $100 apiece, and argues the same flow can be run in-house for $10 to $20 per opt-in [mb1j52sTZX0 @ 00:00:17]. Whoever controls cost per lead and exclusivity controls margin.

The second point of agreement is that a raw inquiry is worth little; buyers pay for filtering. Ivan Pashov of Solar Boss treats exclusivity as essential because exclusive leads mean clients do not have to be first on the phone or the cheapest bid, so the job stops being 'a race to the bottom' [E_z3wa2X4Pw @ 00:03:57]. Ash at Ingeni Renewables, a commercial installer specialising in petrol station forecourts, contrasts TNW's pre-found, interest-checked leads with earlier providers that simply pinged domestic-heavy inquiries over and forced the Ingeni team to do all the filtering legwork, and has already won a 36 kW job with a purchase order in [cC9kyrNz7RM @ 00:02:52]. Perry Hawks at Get Zero, a self-described lead-gen skeptic, cherry-picks leads from a shared WhatsApp drop, buying only 40 to 50% of what is offered and still taking 18 leads by 17 July [kzJnJVEn_E4 @ 00:04:28]. Sean Sprague at Treepublic, ten years in solar across residential and C&I, describes the industry trap as a choice between a very high acquisition cost or a cheap one full of low-quality leads, and credits TNW with landing low cost and high quality together [3vw0ExOznb8 @ 00:03:07]. Everyone wants qualification finished before a rep's calendar is touched, not after a wasted drive.

Commercial operators also agree that the sale is slow and the first touch must be fast. Conklin is blunt that commercial solar is a financial sell to a business owner buying a revenue-producing asset, never a one-call close, and that the rep keeps control through micro-commitments such as a letter of intent, letter of engagement or notice to proceed before the signed agreement, deposit and financing [fwRL9lFmciI @ 00:04:08]. James Vincent confirms the drag from the field: the strongest TNW-sourced project in the James Vincent Enterprises pipeline, worth roughly 50-60K, is sitting in DNO, a stage that can take six weeks or three months if things get complicated [oZ_jg6mmZEE @ 00:02:33]. Destiny Marketing Solutions says that for 300 kW to 1 MW-plus ventures, cold email and LinkedIn are the most effective ways to set meetings with decision makers [TY6iVOecM0Y @ 00:00:00], and Conklin insists the first commercial meeting must be with the decision maker, not someone gathering data. On speed, Nazir Yakubu runs AI agents that call a new lead within seconds during business hours, qualify it and book it, because no human setter can reach leads that fast [FGU8xAz-R1U @ 00:06:12]. The consensus baseline is a qualified decision maker, contacted immediately, then walked patiently through a multi-meeting close.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On paid social, the operators publishing hard numbers converge on Meta lead generation with tight, testable mechanics. Nazir Yakubu spent $28,000 in October on one solar campaign with cost per lead never above $20, booking 25 to 30 appointments a day, six days a week [FGU8xAz-R1U @ 00:00:00]. The sequence is content, ads, AI, appointments: warm a new or old Facebook and Instagram page for five to seven days with posts that are about 70% video, including short homeowner interviews filmed during installs, before any ad goes live, because warm pages lower cost per lead and lift lead quality. Those install videos then run as cheap retargeting to anyone who just booked, costing up to about $20 depending on campaign size, which Yakubu credits for an appointment show rate of up to 60% [FGU8xAz-R1U @ 00:07:22]. Leadbase launches state-targeted Facebook lead-form campaigns at $25 a day, keeps '$0 down' out of the copy because it gets rejected into a special ad category, and reports $10 to $20 opt-ins carrying address, state and zip [mb1j52sTZX0 @ 00:04:31]. Ritesh Kumar of Solar Scaling Systems adds the sizing rule: target a 3 million-plus audience, ideally an entire state or multiple states, because a handful of zip codes produces ridiculously high costs [UkUTbb_M4eY @ 00:03:13].

For commercial outbound, Duncan at Photonic Growth lays out the full cold email stack and claims it can book meetings 10 to 100 times cheaper than paid advertising [Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:00:50]. Lists come from an Upwork list builder fed with conference vendor lists and rewarded with a bonus for quality over quantity, or from software: Closely, the favourite, with LinkedIn-based data, up to 500 exports at once and a free tier of 50 credits a month; AnyMailFinder for pay-as-you-go credits; and Apollo, which typically caps bulk exports at 50 [Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:05:06]. Target industries carry a high fixed electricity load: commercial bakeries, heavy refrigeration, server farms and manufacturing. Sending runs from a separate domain on Google Workspace's roughly $6 a month starter plan, capped at 50 emails a day despite the official limit of around 200, through lemlist with its warm-up switched on for at least a week [Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:26:30]. The sequence is three emails: subject line 'quick question', a personal icebreaker, the monthly electric cost as pain, government incentives as hook and a request for a 15-minute call, with follow-ups three to four weekdays apart in the same thread. Phaibion Royal sells a similar four to six email sequence aimed at 100 decision makers a month [rhgUISy29oc @ 00:03:13].

Where operators buy rather than build, street pricing is specific. James Vincent paid TNW about £400 a lead for commercial appointments pushed through a WhatsApp group, buying only a handful while using the volume to learn which leads were big enough to service [oZ_jg6mmZEE @ 00:01:39]. Perry Hawks gives TNW's setters free access to the Get Zero calendar; a setter books the slot, re-confirms with the customer the afternoon before, and after the attended meeting the design, quote and relationship belong to Get Zero with no further fee [kzJnJVEn_E4 @ 00:03:20]. On the residential side, one Solar Boss client reports leads at around $25 or less, under $700 in spend turned into over $50K in commission, and five sales in the first seven days, while another client, Jonah Cooper of American Energy Group, runs 14 reps across multiple states on 50 to 60 new leads a day and record weeks of 15 to 20 deals [kjVg-6GP5hc @ 00:02:36]. The cheapest pipe is still referrals: David Sugrue's playbook pays a roofer, electrician or other trade channel partner $200 to $500 for each referred prospect who buys a system, pays customers the same range per referred sale, and later cuts partner fees by steering roofing and electrical work back to them [vmJD-cz6rdc @ 00:02:08].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build A Decision-Maker List
   - Pull CFOs and operations directors at companies with high fixed electricity loads.
   - Target commercial bakeries, refrigeration-heavy firms, server farms and manufacturers; use an Upwork list builder fed conference vendor lists, or Closely exports in batches of 100 for personalised sends, verifying emails before export [Photonic Growth — Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:03:57].
2. Stand Up Protected Sending Infrastructure
   - Buy a separate domain, connect Google Workspace and lemlist, and warm the inbox.
   - Run lemlist warm-up for at least a week on a new account, cap sends at 50 a day, load a three-email 'quick question' sequence spaced three to four weekdays apart, and watch bounces since lemlist shuts campaigns above 20% [Photonic Growth — Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:32:00].
3. Warm The Meta Page, Then Launch State Ads
   - Post five to seven days of mostly video content before switching on lead-form ads.

4. Hit Every Lead Fast And Lock The Appointment
   - Contact new leads within seconds, qualify, book, confirm, and secure the bill.
   - Use AI agents or setters to call within seconds during business hours and reschedule no-shows [Nazir Yakubu — FGU8xAz-R1U @ 00:06:12]; on commercial, phone before emailing and get a copy of the electric bill as the first sign of commitment [The Big Solar Energy Show with Anthony J. ## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over which channel actually fills a commercial calendar. Destiny Marketing Solutions says that for 300 kilowatt to 1 megawatt plus ventures, cold email and LinkedIn are the most effective ways to set meetings with prospects [TY6iVOecM0Y @ 00:00:00]. Photonic Growth pushes harder, claiming cold email done correctly can be 10 times or even 100 times cheaper than paid advertising to book meetings, because the operator controls the list, tracks every number and can forecast the future [Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:00:50]. Phaibion Royal attacks the phone directly: only 1% of cold calls convert into an appointment, so 100 dials a day yields one meeting, and with most commercial companies needing 10 appointments per sale the math collapses [rhgUISy29oc @ 00:01:39]. The replacement Royal sells is a four-to-six email sequence hitting 100 decision makers a month with a dedicated appointment setter. Anthony J. Conklin flips the rule once a lead exists: make a phone call first, never an email, leave a voicemail promising a text and email follow-up, because sending email first just delays the process [fwRL9lFmciI @ 00:00:32]. Read together, email and LinkedIn crack cold decision makers while the phone owns speed once interest is on record.

Paying outsiders for leads splits the room, and every camp brings its own math. David Sugrue warns that third-party vendors sell the same lead to many home improvement companies, even your direct competitors, and that generating a single lead can cost two hundred dollars; at five to ten leads per sale that is a one thousand to two thousand dollar customer acquisition cost [vmJD-cz6rdc @ 00:00:36]. Leadbase argues for cutting the middleman entirely, pitching a $249-a-month do-it-yourself platform to installers already paying an agency $2,000 to $2,500 a month or $75 to $100 per bought lead [mb1j52sTZX0 @ 00:00:17]. Ivan Pashov of Solar Boss makes exclusivity the dealbreaker, arguing exclusive leads mean clients do not have to win a race to the bottom on price or be first on the call [E_z3wa2X4Pw @ 00:03:57]. TNW Marketing, through Roman Love's client interviews, pitches paying only for attended appointments; James Vincent, who paid about £400 per lead, says that model focuses the buyer on exactly which leads are worth paying for, and that paying upfront sharpens the focus even more [oZ_jg6mmZEE @ 00:04:04]. The trade-off is plain: the do-it-yourself route moves media buying and follow-up onto the installer, while the performance route moves qualification risk onto the vendor.

The third fault line runs through who works the lead and what hook pulls it in. Nazir Yakubu argues call centers no longer make sense: AI agents dial every new lead within seconds during business hours, qualify it, chase the energy bill, confirm and reschedule, a stack Yakubu ties to a 60% show rate on cost per lead held under $20 [FGU8xAz-R1U @ 00:06:12]. TNW keeps humans in the loop instead: Perry Hawks of Get Zero describes reps who post leads in a WhatsApp group, book into an open calendar, and reconfirm with the customer the afternoon before a Tuesday appointment [kzJnJVEn_E4 @ 00:03:20]. On the hook, Ritesh Kumar of Solar Scaling Systems says free installation, free consultation and zero down are not offers at all, only add-ons, and builds the whole pitch around cutting the electricity bill [UkUTbb_M4eY @ 00:04:29]. Audience width splits too: Kumar wants at least a three-million-plus audience, ideally an entire state, while Destiny Marketing Solutions steers commercial advertisers toward a specific niche so creative, copy and offer resonate [TY6iVOecM0Y @ 00:00:40]. Kumar also says video and image creative work exactly the same, while Yakubu loads roughly 70% video into page warm-up posts before any ad runs.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest commercial failure is the wasted drive. James Vincent says you never know how good a lead is until you see the property, talk to the person and read the bill, and lists the data gaps that cost trips: whether another installer already has the deal and a DNO submission is filed, rough system size, and single versus three phase supply [oZ_jg6mmZEE @ 00:05:39]. A farm with yurts, a pool and a field for ground mount looked great until transmission and groundworks made it complex, and leads drifting to the South Wiltshire coast blew past the drive limit set for Bristol, Wales and Bath. Perry Hawks protects time by buying only 40 to 50% of leads offered, 18 taken by mid-July from at least 36 shown [kzJnJVEn_E4 @ 00:04:28]. Ash at Ingeni Renewables says earlier vendors that merely pinged inquiries forced heavy filtering because the flow skewed domestic [cC9kyrNz7RM @ 00:02:52]. Patience is the other tax: Hawks has a 300 kilowatt peak lead not closing until Q1 2027, James puts DNO at six weeks to three months, and Anthony J. Conklin says commercial never closes in one call, so lock micro commitments like a letter of intent and notice to proceed [fwRL9lFmciI @ 00:04:08].

Outbound breaks on deliverability long before copy matters, and slower channels hide their own drag. Photonic Growth warns cold email can dent a sending account's reputation, pushing mail into spam or the updates folder, so buy a separate domain rather than sending from the main company address [Sm0uGTgJbJE @ 00:24:35]. Google workspace officially allows about 200 a day, but the advice is a 50-a-day ceiling, a warm-up running at least a week on a fresh inbox, and verified addresses, since lemlist shuts a campaign once bounces climb past roughly 20%. Tooling has friction too: Apollo typically exports only 50 contacts at a time, and exported company names can arrive in lowercase or bracketed legal forms that look absurd in a merge field unless cleaned with a PROPER formula. Calendar booking links also underperform when you are the one reaching out; ask for a time or offer a specific window instead. Destiny Marketing Solutions flags the slow-burn channels: podcasts need at least a few months of new shows, a well-spoken host and someone managing interviewee bookings before they gain traction, and YouTube retargeting is generally not a direct lead generator [TY6iVOecM0Y @ 00:01:58].

On the paid side, the traps are platform rejection, thin budgets and slow vendors. Leadbase says Facebook loves to reject solar ads and that $0 down copy gets flagged into a special ad category [mb1j52sTZX0 @ 00:04:31]. Ritesh Kumar kills two budget mistakes: a $100 cost per lead on a $1,000 budget never builds enough volume, and squeezing ads into a handful of zip codes produces ridiculously high costs [UkUTbb_M4eY @ 00:01:41]. Kumar adds that the offer makes or breaks the system, and more than 70% of effort belongs on the creative rather than the copy. Nazir Yakubu says the old playbook of a fresh page, a few posts and an instant publish still produces leads, but cost per appointment runs five times higher than it should [FGU8xAz-R1U @ 00:01:21]. On vendors, a Solar Boss client recounts agencies saying wait 60 days with nothing happening, against Ivan Pashov's rule that if leads do not arrive on day one the campaign is not working, though that client still watched some sales fail credit [kjVg-6GP5hc @ 00:01:01]. Another Solar Boss client spending six figures a month across providers warns buyers to back the spend with real collaboration instead of tossing money at ads.
