# Chapter 07: From M&A Opportunity to Transaction

*Buy-side strategy and sourcing, private-equity deal finding, formal sale processes, transaction stages, buyer tactics, adviser selection, Lehman-formula commissions, success fees, and earn-outs*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from POTOMAC M&A, BW Forsyth, Exit On Your Terms, and a run of bankers converge on one law: competition sets the price, and acquisitions are a tactic that must increase cash flow or cut risk. The working funnel is consistent: roughly 50 buyers contacted, 20-50 NDAs, about 10 IOIs, 5 approved bidders, 3 final bids, and one winner over four to eight months. The process runs on absolute-price IOIs, pooled diligence, buyer-only deadlines, and LOI terms that lock escrow, caps, and baskets before exclusivity kills leverage. Fee mechanics matter as much as rates: broker waterfalls run 10/8/6%, and two identical 2% quotes can differ by EUR 60K. The sharpest split is sourcing. PE operators Ryan Gable and Steve Balaban walk away from banker-shopped deal flow for proprietary, relationship-driven targets, while sell-side bankers build their entire edge on formal auctions.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Across every sell-side seat, the loudest consensus is that competition sets the price, not a spreadsheet. The POTOMAC M&A banker closes a sell-side masterclass with a blunt order: if you ignore everything else, bring competition into the mix [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 01:20:07]. A business has no price tag; its value gets set at the bargaining table, and competition plus optionality is the single most important leverage factor in that room. Money Sutra frames the advisor job in the same terms: sell-side advisors create competition, while buy-side advisors evaluate, finance, and negotiate [aBB5nV2vr2Q @ 00:02:08]. Even when only one bidder shows up, the POTOMAC banker insists you run the process as if five or ten buyers were in the room, with identical bid instructions and iterative stages. Sophisticated buyers figure out the real field from how you act, and the banker reports extremely successful one-buyer auctions run exactly that way [f7d2hP31YB8 @ 00:06:09]. The operator takeaway is that the process itself is the negotiator. The same banker's rule is to let the process negotiate for you and to review proposals in parallel rather than serially, because reviewing them one at a time is very difficult and hands each buyer a read on your options [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 00:35:20]. Seller fear of a thin field is treated as the leak, not the field itself.

The second consensus point is that an acquisition is a tactic, never the strategy itself. POTOMAC's buy-side masterclass anchors every CEO decision to one test: does it increase cash flow or decrease risk [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:04:45]. The cautionary case is Jennifer, who ran a $10M-revenue finance SaaS company and bought a banker-pitched competitor. The target was 70% of the size, 15 years older, slower-growing, cheaper, and stuck on antiquated tech. Customers refused to migrate off mission-critical software, synergies dried up, and the result was two separate businesses under one umbrella, with a comp clash between an equity-holding team and one holding none. The banker's diagnosis was a choice problem, not failed integration [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:13:54]. The former Bain manager behind Hacking the Case Interview drills the same discipline: know the reason for the deal before anything else. If you are leaning no, weigh alternative targets and opportunity cost, because the money spent on the acquisition could be spent on something else [J1GpLqugFeM @ 00:06:01]. The mnainstitute.com course makes strategy step one of five, ahead of target identification, diligence, negotiation and closing, and integration. It warns that a weak strategy breeds financial losses, cultural clashes, and operational inefficiency [ZJvGhvYmDLE @ 00:01:05]. POTOMAC's baseline stands: organic growth always beats acquisitive growth.

Buy-side and PE practitioners converge on hunting over fishing. POTOMAC's buy-side lesson is to be a hunter, not a fisher. Buy-side brokers blast form emails to 10,000 people, which is not very effective but highly efficient. The real edge is a proprietary pipeline built over lunches and diner breakfasts, delegated to a team member, aimed at roughly 50 targets for optionality, and measured in years [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:26:00]. Steve Balaban, who runs a PE firm, says banker-pitched opportunities are not good deals but just deals, because the same 'perfect deal' gets called in to Bill, Jeff, and Susan. Good deals come from proprietary flow obtained mostly through connections [iTj-KQOCZhY @ 00:00:26]. Ryan Gable of BW Forsyth lived the proof. The first 18 months to two years of ACG events and banker books produced zero deals, and in 200-buyer auctions the firm was usually not the highest payer. So the team went 'inch wide and a mile deep' and landed platforms in April 2011, November 2011, and April 2012 [YxW3Kc2feRY @ 00:29:03]. Balaban's Chobani story is the street version: a $750M raise was already in talks with TPG before the market knew, because a guy knew a guy who knew a guy [iTj-KQOCZhY @ 00:02:49].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The funnel math is remarkably consistent from seat to seat. POTOMAC distributes to 30-40 PE firms plus 5-10 strategics, targets 30-50 NDAs, and calls 10 IOIs off 50 NDAs 'a really good day' [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 00:39:45]. Financeable Training runs a near-identical ladder over a four-to-eight-month process. Contact 50 buyers; 20 sign NDAs and receive the CIM; 10 proceed. The client then approves 5 bidders for a second round with VDR access and management presentations, 3 submit final bids, and one is chosen [LFdUFuhB9jo @ 00:03:04]. Document weight varies by seat. POTOMAC builds a 30-40 page CIM and an NDA that includes non-solicitation of employees and customers. Pietro Conte builds a 50-75 slide CIM with roughly 10 slides of investment highlights on top of a five-year model. Investment Banking Insights describes a 60-80 page PowerPoint CIP covering positioning, competitive strengths, and weaknesses paired with mitigants [zFuxJIjPV2c @ 00:04:54]. Pietro Conte has seen processes range from 1-2 buyers to 300. Each buyer is capped at 10-20 questions plus a management call before the LOI, which keeps the seller's team from drowning [1PBrwvp_6us @ 00:07:37]. POTOMAC gives bidders two weeks to a month to land IOIs and steers clear of July and the holidays.

Bid instructions are where leverage gets manufactured. POTOMAC demands an absolute number in the IOI, '$35 million' rather than a 30-35 range, plus structure, intent, and financing, on a 1-3 page non-binding document [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 00:34:01]. Bidders must revise upward before they earn management meetings, because there is no free ride. Diligence questions get pooled, so five bidders times ten questions equals 50. The full answer package goes to every bidder, with irrelevant items like a DUI stripped out. Deadlines bind only the buyer, and every bidder gets the same one. Repeated slippage destroys credibility, because people do not like to make decisions under time pressure [f7d2hP31YB8 @ 00:14:31]. The same banker rates a sealed best-and-final under new process rules as the most sophisticated buyer move: simultaneous opening, exact dollar amounts, and no further shopping, in a deal priced at $50M [f7d2hP31YB8 @ 00:24:55]. At the LOI, competition dies through exclusivity, a no-shop, and a notice duty. Escrow, R&W insurance, caps, and baskets must be locked in the LOI, or the buyer comes back demanding a $2 million escrow. Concessions should shrink, running $2M, $500K, $1.5M, $750K, and a 60-day exclusivity can be left to expire [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 01:05:02].

Advisor economics hinge on the fee base, not the headline rate. Michael Wildeveld of Exit On Your Terms quotes broker success fees of 10-12% of the first $1M on a declining waterfall. The firm's own ladder runs 10%, 8%, and 6% across the first three millions. M&A advisors charge $5-15K up front plus a monthly work fee that is often credited back, while mid-market and bank retainers run $150-250K or more [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:05:08]. Simple Explain traces this back to the classic Lehman ladder of 5-4-3-2-1%, which has evolved over time and gets customized per deal [vTGW5-gVWNY @ 00:01:11]. Saverio Canepa shows two proposals at the same 2% producing a EUR 60K gap on a deal with EUR 10M enterprise value and EUR 8M equity value. The first fee is EUR 160K on equity, with the EUR 20K retainer credited. The second is EUR 220K on enterprise value, with the retainer not credited. So the first negotiation is about what 'success' means [kIcxjlHfa74 @ 00:01:46]. On the structure side, Gable reports earn-outs now appear in 50% or more of BW Forsyth deals. They are pegged to gross profit because revenue is too simple and EBITDA too controversial. A $10M purchase carries a $2M, 20% seller rollover with a five-year commitment [YxW3Kc2feRY @ 00:46:11].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Set the Valuation Band
   - Anchor a realistic value and an aspirational value before any buyer contact.
   - Review comps as averages with outliers, then set the band: a realistic $50M against an aspirational $80M. In one deal a PE firm paid $75M against an expected $50M [POTOMAC M&A — 8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 00:24:01]
2. Build the Three Marketing Documents
   - Prepare the anonymous teaser, the CIM, and the management presentation, and map the buyer universe.
   - The teaser runs 1-2 pages with no name. The CIM follows the NDA, and the management presentation is built from the CIM, alongside a buyer-universe map, strategy, and timeline [Financeable Training — LFdUFuhB9jo @ 00:01:56]
3. Run the IOI Round on Hard Instructions
   - Require absolute-price IOIs with structure, intent, and financing, and review them in parallel.
   - Demand '$35 million,' not 30-35, on a 1-3 page non-binding IOI. Allow two weeks to a month and avoid July and the holidays [POTOMAC M&A — 8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 00:34:01]
4. Lock the Economics Before Exclusivity
   - Negotiate escrow, R&W insurance, caps, and baskets into the LOI before granting a no-shop.
   - Leverage shifts to the buyer the moment the LOI is signed. A buyer's one-page LOI with long exclusivity is built to capture that shift, so settle the holdback and indemnity escrow terms first [POTOMAC M&A — RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:46:13]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Deal sourcing is the sharpest split. The POTOMAC M&A buy-side banker tells acquirers to be hunters, not fishers. Buy-side brokers who blast form emails to 10,000 people are 'not very effective, but it's highly efficient.' The banker also admits the firm turns away small family buyers because 'I'm going to make them overpay' [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:27:01]. Steve Balaban goes further: bankers and brokers pitch the same 'perfect deal' to Bill, Jeff and Susan, so 'these are not good deals these are just deals.' The fix is to stop taking those calls and to work lawyers, accountants and every executive in one or a few industries [iTj-KQOCZhY @ 00:01:44]. Ryan Gable tested the intermediated route, and it failed. ACG events and banker books produced no deals for roughly the first 18 months to two years, and in 200-buyer auctions BW Forsyth was 'usually not the one paying the most.' The team pivoted to 'inch wide and a mile deep,' with all 16 people sourcing [YxW3Kc2feRY @ 00:27:26]. Michael Wildeveld argues the other side for smaller sellers: the advisor's value is the marketplace itself. That means a database of 40,000 individual buyers and 4,000 family offices, plus top placement on 30 sites, because below roughly $3-5M EBITDA most PE groups will not come looking [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:09:21].

How much competition to manufacture is the second fault line. In the sell-side masterclass, the POTOMAC M&A banker ranks competition and optionality as the most important leverage factor. The masterclass closes with a single non-negotiable: bring competition into the mix, because negotiating with one buyer means no price discovery [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 01:20:07]. In the follow-up Q&A, the same banker refines the rule. A lone buyer damages leverage mainly through seller psychology, so run that process 'just as you would if you had five or 10 different buyers,' with identical bid instructions and iterative stages. Sophisticated buyers 'figure it out by the way that you act,' and that format has produced 'extremely successful' one-buyer auctions [f7d2hP31YB8 @ 00:06:09]. Wildeveld draws the line by size instead of psychology. Under $2-3M EBITDA, deals are negotiated with individuals, regional players or small family offices rather than auctioned, and mismatching the advisor tier is 'shooting yourself in the foot' [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:07:58]. Financeable Training models the full institutional funnel: 50 buyers contacted, 20 NDAs, 10 proceeding, 5 approved, 3 final bids and one winner over four to eight months. Certainty of funding, time to close and reputation are weighed alongside price [LFdUFuhB9jo @ 00:03:04].

Deal structure and capital stack are where buyer playbooks diverge hardest. Ryan Gable says BW Forsyth now puts earn-outs in 50% or more of deals, where a decade ago the firm used seller financing more and earn-outs less. Those earn-outs are pegged to gross profit, because revenue is 'too simple' and EBITDA 'too controversial' [YxW3Kc2feRY @ 00:46:11]. Saverio Canepa refuses to crown any metric, saying 'no metric is always better,' and shows why the choice moves money. With a €4M maximum earnout, beating a €10M revenue target with €11M pays the capped €4M. Delivering €1.6M against a €2M EBITDA target pays €3.2M, an €800K swing on the same business [ZlseQf6dS1g @ 00:01:44]. Pietro Conte stresses the retention angle, citing an LOI that paid 60% at close and 40% as an earnout after two years to keep management incentivized [1PBrwvp_6us @ 00:09:18]. On debt, Gable deliberately runs 2-3x leverage where 4-6x is available. The firm never owns 100%, targets 60-80% stakes with seller rollover, and models returns all-equity: 'I'll take 18% returns all day and sleep well at night than pushing for 25% returns and bust covenants' [YxW3Kc2feRY @ 01:06:02].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest buy-side failure is a choice problem dressed up as an integration problem. The POTOMAC M&A banker walks through the case of Jennifer, who ran a $10M-revenue finance SaaS company and bought a competitor that a banker pitched. The target was 70% of the size, 15 years older, slower-growing and on antiquated tech. Customers refused to migrate because switching mission-critical software is expensive, and synergies dried up. Jennifer's team held equity while the target's team had none, and the two collided, leaving 'two separate businesses under one umbrella' [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:11:25]. The same banker flags the quiet margin killer: paying $15M where the business stands at $10M means 'you've literally just paid the seller for the value creation' [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:41:11]. Money Sutra puts the arithmetic bluntly. Deal value is standalone value plus credible synergies, minus fees, financing and integration costs, and 'the buyer should not justify a deal using synergies alone' [aBB5nV2vr2Q @ 00:06:21]. Hacking the Case Interview adds the check Jennifer skipped. If leaning no, look at alternative targets and opportunity cost, because 'the money spent on making the acquisition could be spent on something else' [J1GpLqugFeM @ 00:06:01].

On the sell side, the traps cluster around the LOI. The POTOMAC M&A banker warns that the LOI ends competition through exclusivity, a no-shop clause and a notice duty. Escrow, R&W insurance, caps and baskets therefore must be locked inside it; leave them open and expect a buyer to come back demanding a '$2 million' escrow once rivals are gone [8jBQ3jZnNvY @ 01:05:02]. The buy-side session confirms the flip from the other chair: the buyer's LOI is one page with long exclusivity, and leverage shifts to the buyer the moment it is signed [RLr8PdCZ6U0 @ 00:46:13]. The biggest mid-market deal killer is not price but seller emotion, and the remedy taught is emotional reappraisal. Repeated deadline slippage destroys credibility too, because 'people do not like to make decisions under time pressure' [f7d2hP31YB8 @ 00:16:52]. Pietro Conte adds the speed tax: 'If the process ever is slowed up by the investment bank, time just does weird things to deal.' That is why diligence answers go back by 9:00 a.m., so management keeps running the business. It is also why overselling backfires, as when drawers were pitched as 'highly engineered linear movement solutions' [1PBrwvp_6us @ 00:14:16].

The economics checks start with the adviser's bill. Saverio Canepa runs two proposals at the same 2% on a €10M enterprise value with €2M net debt. Taking 2% of the €8M equity value, with a credited €20K retainer, costs €160K. Taking 2% of enterprise value, with an uncredited retainer, costs €220K: a €60K gap where 'the percentage is identical.' Canepa's checklist then asks which event triggers the fee, how deferred or reinvested consideration enters the base, what stays due if the deal dies, and how long the tail runs [kIcxjlHfa74 @ 00:01:46]. Michael Wildeveld warns that some M&A advisors sign undersized clients just to collect $5-15K upfront fees. Mid-market and bank retainers can run $150-250K or more, and Goldman Sachs-type banks are not worth the fees on small deals [Dx_mEuS7ipk @ 00:04:37]. Simple Explain flags the incentive cost of percentage ladders like the 5-4-3-2-1% Lehman formula: short-term focus, conflicts of interest and excessive risk-taking [vTGW5-gVWNY @ 00:01:28]. On earnouts, Canepa warns that staying with the business does not guarantee control. A payment guarantee 'cannot ensure that performance targets are achieved,' so the conditional amount must be valued separately from cash received today [ZlseQf6dS1g @ 00:02:43].
