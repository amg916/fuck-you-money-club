## Executive Summary

Operators selling lead generation and services into finance converge on recurring, risk-shared revenue: $200-a-month maintenance plans over one-off builds, hybrid deals pairing a small retainer with per-lead bonuses, and 80 percent-plus yearly retention before any buyer pays a real multiple. Rafael Hernandez cuts the retainer from roughly $5K to $2K, resells unqualified leads on a fifty/fifty split, and hands the client's half back as lead credits. Daniel Fazio takes performance pay when clients allow it, while Max pushes flat fees. On the regulatory side, practitioners map hard trip-wires: more than 25 retail recipients in 30 days, Form D within 15 days of close, and Reg BI's ban on sales contests and quotas.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus - Recurring Revenue, Protected Relationships, and Trust-Gated Capital

Across the agency operators, the commercial consensus is blunt: stop selling one-off builds and stop eating all the risk alone. Darrel Wilson tells web designers they are not in the website business but the monthly maintenance plan business, because a client paying $200 every single month for a year beats a one-time build fee that only covers rent [Yu60z9o8jdQ @ 00:01:57]. Rafael Hernandez pushes the same recurring logic into lead generation with a hybrid deal: the client funds the ad spend, the operator runs ads from their own brand and ad account, and the client pays a small retainer plus a bonus for every lead, call, or appointment delivered [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:00:00]. Daniel Fazio agrees performance pay belongs in the stack when it can be won, telling a student running an $18K-per-month e-commerce email agency with eight clients that if a client will pay performance, take it, and if not, close them on retainer alone [bQCztTB4VbQ @ 00:02:36]. Alex Hormozi frames the endgame the same way from the investor seat: whichever vehicle you pick, recurring retention is what creates enterprise value, and licensing or software needs 80 percent or higher yearly retention before anyone pays a real multiple [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:10:50]. The shared logic: cash flows every month, risk gets split, and the upside rides on delivered results.

The second point of agreement is that the relationship itself is the asset, and operators guard it. The Pryderi Podcast host explains that people pay thousands, sometimes millions, of dollars for lead lists and client lists, and that a non-circumvention agreement stops anyone you introduce from going directly around you to that contact without paying your broker fee, exposing the violator to damages [rnUvOWiFpDQ @ 00:00:29]. That relationship value may have been built over months if not years, which is why introductions to family offices, billionaires, and celebrities need contract language behind them. The principal of Perkins Fund Marketing, a firm that has raised $8 billion over 30 years exclusively for alternative managers, reaches the same conclusion from the capital side: money does not move just because returns are good, it moves because trust is built, risk is understood, and access is curated [N8Z0FPf2yLw @ 00:02:22]. With roughly 34,000 funds now pounding on allocator doors, the placement agent's modern job is screening and diligence, not volume [N8Z0FPf2yLw @ 00:01:00]. Rafael Hernandez applies the same discipline to agency deals, insisting that ad spend, account ownership, pixel data, payout triggers, and credit expiration all get written into the contract [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:05:42].

When the lead flow touches securities, the regulatory instructors and filing operators agree on hard thresholds. Financial Regulation Courses lays out that any FINRA member communication reaching more than 25 retail investors in a 30-calendar-day window becomes a retail communication, so a blast to 30 distinct retail prospects in a month lands in the heaviest category, requiring registered-principal sign-off before first use, with the rule reaching social media, websites, and text messages [CmOezPFI5ew @ 00:00:43]. The same channel stresses that under Regulation Best Interest, effective June 30, 2020, firms cannot run sales contests, sales quotas, bonuses, or non-cash compensation tied to selling specific securities within a limited window [Af7w5a4iS8c @ 00:03:07]. On the private-deal side, the All Things SPVs instructor files Form D and blue sky notices within 15 days of close, and treats the close date, when subscriptions are countersigned, as the date of first sale [F1OsgcF_bhg @ 00:09:25]. Laura Anthony of Legal & Compliance adds that reporting companies face 8-K triggers on changes in control, officer departures, and shell status, with a Super 8-K due within 4 days carrying Form 10 information and audited financial statements [T89w7sq33_M @ 00:03:06]. The common thread: volume and timing thresholds decide which rulebook an operator is playing under.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Rafael Hernandez, who credits Lead Zolo for the model, runs the hybrid offer from a single lead brand and funnel so the pixel data stays with the operator, and every new client feeds more data into that pixel to sharpen lead quality across the whole book. The pricing lever is the retainer cut: where a full retainer might run $5K a month, the hybrid charges something like $2K a month and makes the real money on each lead, call, appointment, or sale [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:05:05]. The extra profit center is out-of-area or unqualified leads, such as a plumber's leads landing outside the service radius. Those get resold to other buyers and split fifty/fifty, so on a $1,000 lead the client gets $500 and the operator gets $500 [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:03:37]. The client's half is issued as lead credits that can only be spent buying more leads, calls, and appointments from the operator, so the cash circles back home and each client feeds the next buyer in the network [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:04:09]. Daniel Fazio adds the pricing reality check: on the sales call, open the Klaviyo account, see the ad spend and growth, then make the number up on the spot and negotiate back and forth [bQCztTB4VbQ @ 00:06:40].

Darrel Wilson sources web-design prospects through lootleads.ai, a subscription that costs about 100 bucks a month and surfaces organizations posting requests for proposals with the contact name, phone, email, and the original RFP download; the example radio station had about eight days left before its proposal deadline [Yu60z9o8jdQ @ 00:00:00]. Pricing then runs off tiered packages, starter, niche, standard build, premium, and enterprise, with the add-ons underneath called the gold mine [Yu60z9o8jdQ @ 00:02:37]. Alex Hormozi brings the unit-economics math for packaging a service into a scalable vehicle. Open company-owned locations when a new site costs less than about six months of its first-year profit; one portfolio business moved from licensing to owned locations and grew from half a million to $12 million a year in 14 months [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:07:49]. Franchising usually costs about $750,000 to set up, the roughly $50,000 upfront franchise fee mostly gets eaten by franchise-broker lead costs, and the model needs 100-plus locations, a mark 90 to 95 percent of franchises never pass [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:08:19]. Business Credit America sells a $5,000 UCC-1 filing package, pitching that the first lender to file usually holds priority and that the filing can build a company's business credit rating [Qi_L4TMGG1A @ 00:00:51].

For operators raising money through an SPV, the All Things SPVs instructor walks the exact Form D path: log in through login.gov on the specific EDGAR Form D site, enter the CIK, confirm the SPV name, and only proceed once the page shows the right red, green, teal, and American flag layout, because the instructor has gotten lost in EDGAR many times [F1OsgcF_bhg @ 00:02:26]. Inside the form, the instructor picks pooled investment fund, selects decline to disclose on offering size, claims 506(b) and Investment Company Act 3(c)(1), and typically enters a $1,000 minimum investment. Sales compensation and finder-fee pages get skipped because SPVs rarely pay people to hunt investors, and offered always equals sold with zero remaining. Any org fee, such as $10,000 pulled for admin software or taxes, goes into use of proceeds [F1OsgcF_bhg @ 00:13:56]. After submitting, download the PDF and capture the accession number the system issues. Anyone running broker-dealer marketing faces filing clocks: Financial Regulation Courses says first-year FINRA members file broadly disseminated retail pieces at least 10 business days before use, and established firms file investment company pieces within 10 business days after first use [CmOezPFI5ew @ 00:01:56]. Options retail communications may need filing at least 10 calendar days before use, with pre-approval by a Series 4 registered options principal [HPkIpR-sbx4 @ 00:00:15].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split on the commercial side is how to get paid. Daniel Fazio's rule is blunt: if you can get a client to pay you performance, you should, and if you can't, you don't, and every client ends up on a different deal construction because an agency is not a SaaS company [bQCztTB4VbQ @ 00:02:36]. Fazio reports that Max is strongly against performance share and argues for flat fees, and that Connor stopped doing performance altogether, then explains why: performance is normally coupled with an abnormally lower retainer, so operators drop it to stop servicing clients who barely produce [bQCztTB4VbQ @ 00:04:19]. Rafael Hernandez pushes the opposite direction with the hybrid model credited to Lead Zolo: the client funds ad spend, you run ads from your own brand and ad account, and you charge a small retainer that only covers team and software while a per-lead, per-call, per-appointment, or per-sale bonus carries the real upside [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:02:25]. Hernandez's pitch framing is a two-thousand-dollar monthly retainer instead of a five-thousand-dollar flat one, so the client only pays more when results land [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:05:05]. Fazio's counterweight is that the clients you most want performance from are exactly the ones who refuse to give it.

On how a service business should scale, Alex Hormozi argues there are only four vehicles and that the build-out math picks for you: if a new location costs less than roughly six months of its first-year profit, own it as a private chain; if it costs a million dollars to open, franchise and push the capital risk onto investors [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:05:34]. Hormozi acted on that by pulling a portfolio company out of licensing because licensees were making so much money on cheap locations, taking it from a half-million-dollar business to twelve million a year in fourteen months [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:07:49]. Yet licensing was also the right call for Gym Launch, the first business Hormozi built, because it costs almost nothing and throws cash from day one, at the price of low exit multiples. Rafael Hernandez scales a different way, turning one client into a network by reselling out-of-area or unqualified leads to other buyers, so each client feeds the next [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:04:46]. Darrel Wilson's playbook is simpler still: stop chasing build fees and sell a monthly maintenance plan, because a client paying two hundred dollars every month for a year beats a one-time project [Yu60z9o8jdQ @ 00:01:57].

On the capital side, the split is over who brings investors in and how much paper trail follows. The Perkins Fund Marketing speaker, thirty years in and eight billion dollars raised for alternative managers, argues AI can background a manager but cannot decide whether you trust them, so placement agents still win on face-to-face meetings and curated access [N8Z0FPf2yLw @ 00:03:28]. The All Things SPVs operator runs the opposite lean model: SPVs typically have nobody running around looking for investors, so the sales-compensation and finder-fee pages get skipped, offering size gets 'decline to disclose' whenever the option exists, and the fund type is marked 'other' even though peers pick venture capital fund [F1OsgcF_bhg @ 00:11:29]. Ticket sizes show the gap: the SPV operator often sets the minimum as low as one thousand dollars, while the Perkins speaker says the minimum for most of these alternative managers is a million. On the broker-dealer side, the standards themselves diverge: under Rule 2111 a product only needs to be suitable, built on nine customer-profile elements and three obligations [nST9vhaTkek @ 00:00:42], while Reg BI forces cost consideration, so a broker cannot push a higher-commission product when an equally suitable lower-cost fund exists, and it is transaction-specific rather than the continuous fiduciary duty advisers carry [Af7w5a4iS8c @ 00:03:35].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is pricing against the wrong traffic. Fazio warns that what Max commands from content and referrals is not what a cold-email operator can command, and that if you pitch a real estate agent a retainer they will flatly refuse, so operators who copy a referral-fed peer's terms stall on cold calls [bQCztTB4VbQ @ 00:05:17]. Fazio's fix is to read the client's account on the call, quote a number, and negotiate back and forth until it closes. The hybrid model carries its own operational drag: Rafael Hernandez admits there are a lot more moving parts to track and that it only works with the right systems and software [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:05:23]. Hernandez's hard rule is to write everything into the contract: who funds ad spend, who owns the ads, funnel, pixel and data, what exactly counts as a paid result, how the fifty-fifty resale split works, what lead credits buy, at what price, and when they expire [XPcFTfp7i8E @ 00:05:42]. The Pryderi Podcast host flags the leak at the other end: if an introduced party goes directly around you, the middleman loses value built over months or years unless a non-circumvention agreement makes that a contractual violation subject to damages [rnUvOWiFpDQ @ 00:01:35].

On the capital side, the most expensive failure the Perkins speaker reports is speed without a handshake. One of the fastest investors they ever landed sent a ten-million-dollar check without ever meeting the manager, sat in the fund for seven months, hit one bad month, and called for the money back, because the investor never knew or trusted the person running it [N8Z0FPf2yLw @ 00:08:33]. The bottleneck feeding that problem is volume: roughly 34,000 funds are now pounding on allocators, who have stopped answering the phone, and Perkins screens out pitches like a trader who grew a personal account from two hundred thousand to eight hundred thousand, because the firm wants managers with a hundred million dollars or more in the bank [N8Z0FPf2yLw @ 00:04:30]. The SPV side fails on paperwork. The All Things SPVs operator says they have gotten lost in the EDGAR system many times and landed on sites that do not look like the real one, so the only safe screen is the one with the red, green, teal and American flag [F1OsgcF_bhg @ 00:02:26]. The clock is the other trap: Form D and blue sky filings are due within 15 days of the close, and the operator dates first sale to the day subscriptions are countersigned, not the day money first lands [F1OsgcF_bhg @ 00:09:25].

The economics checks come down to retention and build cost. Hormozi's most expensive firsthand mistake was outsourcing software to a third-party team without a technical co-founder, calling the warning a three-million-dollar gift after two to three million dollars of development [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:04:43]. Even when software works, it only earns a software exit with 80 percent or higher yearly retention; below that, buyers treat it as a normal service with a tech element. Franchising carries a parallel trap: roughly 750,000 dollars just to set up, an upfront fee around 50,000 that typically only covers the cost of acquiring the franchisee, profits that take one to two years, and 90 to 95 percent of franchises never clearing the 100 locations needed to make real money [O1As2zxy0es @ 00:09:55]. Ryan Floyd of Storm Ventures puts numbers on the recurring-revenue side: SMB gross churn of 20 percent or more, mid-market around 15 percent, and enterprise at 10 percent or less, with anything above 10 percent in enterprise meaning the acquisition spend has gone up in flames [su9KuGnbAW8 @ 00:07:29]. On the regulated side, any communication reaching more than 25 retail investors in 30 days becomes a retail communication requiring principal pre-approval [CmOezPFI5ew @ 00:01:28].
