## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who prices land for data centers starts with electrons, not acreage. AECOM's panel defines powered land as “as close as possible to plugging in and generating revenue.” They sharpen that to sites with “a realistic and deliverable path” toward energization [DmHfrc4zfn0 @ 00:07:19]. Adam Wei's primary trinity is power, water, and land/access. His blunt caveat is that everything else can be fixed with enough time and money. Spencer Smith and Jared Parker of Saunders Real Estate say the same thing in broker language. Value comes from how much power you can generate or draw to the site, so the order is “power first and then land.” That breaks the per-acre and per-unit pricing most landowners know [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:05:11]. Backing into end-user math, they argue raw land is still undervalued. LandApp by LandGate's qualification checklist lines up behind them. It ranks substations, transmission lines, fiber, and a favorable political climate for approvals ahead of everything else. Ten acres is the floor and 40-plus acres is ideal for enterprise and hyperscale users [UqoyFfPDAmA @ 00:01:42]. The Industrial Real Estate Show puts a number on the spread. Farmland worth a couple grand an acre can become million-dollar-an-acre land if it qualifies for a data center [jak1MCZd_UQ @ 00:23:06]. That repricing gap is the opportunity every speaker is chasing.

On commercial flat roofs, the consensus is that what you see on the surface is not the claim; what sits underneath is. Mark of Jansen/Adjusters International says TPO behaves like rubber. Hail leaves an indentation rather than a tear, and the real damage is cracked ISO board below it. That is why his team cores every file an adjuster doubts. In his words, “it's a little bit of a fight but we win it every time” [oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:04:29]. Sean Smith of Sky Guard, speaking on Digital Roofing Innovations, runs the identical playbook from the inspector side. Hail on TPO, EPDM, and PVC leaves a windshield-style circle crack, and the insurer wants the membrane cut open so it can see the back split [GciwM0T3IPE @ 00:03:28]. Merlin Law Group attacks the carriers' favorite deflection, the cosmetic label. Their argument is that impacts reach the underlayment and shorten life expectancy. The insurer only wins that fight when the policy actually excludes cosmetic damage, which is increasingly common in hail regions [hb-cKsFxfwg @ 00:00:43]. Chad Michael pushes the same system logic in Boulder. Wet decking plus the one-roof-system limit force a tear-off to the deck. Solid nailable sheathing and an IECC R-value upgrade then ride along with the claim [d6mF320R8u0 @ 00:02:21]. Jansen's closing rule sums up the camp: no part of the roof works alone.

The lien lane runs on public notice, and every UCC speaker agrees a filing is a signal to read before money moves. Applecation Lab lays out a three-step chain. The borrower signs a security agreement with a full collateral description. A UCC-1 is filed with the recorder of deeds. The filing then surfaces on the title report. As a result, removable fixtures like chandeliers or commercial refrigerators can be pulled on default, so the instruction is to check the title report for fixture liens before you buy [Qa47WMCz55k @ 00:04:07]. BizNick went through the records and is emphatic that the paper alone does nothing. “A UCC-1 financing statement does not create a security interest” unless there is a signed agreement, value given, and the debtor's rights in the collateral [22cx7DJjhTY @ 00:16:09]. PK Patel of Midwest Corporate Credit gives the operator risk list. Filings block new financing, filings from the past five years show on business credit, and default triggers repossession, so check your file and demand releases [QzlAvX38RS4 @ 00:02:31]. Credit Suite confirms the friction costs real money. Filings deter second- and third-tier lenders and undercut stacking strategies, and they are often never released. In one case, a defunct lender's filing took months to clear before an SBA loan could close [TvwdUsUy-Cs @ 00:02:52].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Saunders Real Estate walks through the exact value ladder on a 100-acre parcel at $70,000 an acre. Rezoning, a PSP, a full site plan, and SWFWMD permits each step the price up. The landowner should structure the deal to get paid at milestones rather than waiting on one close [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:30:05]. Their diligence budget is concrete: $30,000 to $40,000 on diligence or energy consulting, sometimes running up to a quarter million. The single document that moves a site “to the top of the list” is a utility megawatt letter [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:34:59]. They also coach patience on price. The first offer is probably not the best, and land is only about 6% of the overall development deal, so moving from $100k to $150k an acre is a couple tenths of a percent of the project. LandApp by LandGate adds the clock. If zoning is wrong, rezoning can take three to six months. Purchase is more common than lease because buyers want control over operations, zoning, and expansion [UqoyFfPDAmA @ 00:02:04]. AECOM's Adam Wei runs the screen at scale. He scores megawatts per acre uniformly across 50-to-1,000-acre filters using sources like Open Infrastructure Map. Because local data is weaker, a human reviews every result [DmHfrc4zfn0 @ 00:20:40].

The roof crews run a repeatable field routine. Jansen/Adjusters International starts on soft metals (caps, collars, vents, and AC fins) to gauge hail size and quantity before touching the membrane. Golf ball or baseball hits “should pop out,” while pea-size takes real hunting [oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:01:09]. Their documentation system is the 10x10 test square. Circle every hit inside the square. Highlight marks that are not hail, such as mechanical damage, to buy credibility. Separate heat blisters by their very clean circle. Mark the roof hours or a day before the adjuster arrives. About 20 hits in a single square got one roof bought [oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:25:17]. Digital Roofing Innovations contrasts the benchmarks. On slope, the bar is about three slopes with 10 or more hits per square, but flat roofs carry no set threshold. Carriers typically replace the whole roof when cuts show hits on every flat section [GciwM0T3IPE @ 00:02:20]. Tyler Melton of Proclaim Roofing turns that into a service model. On 20-year-old modified bitumen with damage every three feet, a prior roofer had called it no hail damage, yet the file ran to roughly a $1M loss. His crew fixes and maintains leaks for free while the insurer takes months, then bills the carrier [m37LDnkUURI @ 00:01:35].

The money math on the claim side is unforgiving. Policy & Property's breakdown of the State Farm memo shows the replace-to-repair ratio sliding from 3.2:1 to about 2:1. Their homeowner example: on a $300,000 home, a 2% deductible is $6,000. A $2,500 repair therefore pays nothing, a $15,000 replacement pays $9,000, and actual cash value on a 12-of-25-year roof drops the check to $1,800 [V-xAfnhz1BY @ 00:04:40]. Chad Michael's counter is to stack code-driven scope. That means tear-off to the deck, solid nailable sheathing, IECC R-value, the lightning rod system, and damaged fasteners, with overhead and profit justified by trade complexity [d6mF320R8u0 @ 00:04:39]. On liens, BizNick lays out five legitimate moves. Run a UCC search on yourself. Read your security agreements, where “all assets” is the most aggressive form. File only when you lend against real collateral. Search sellers before an acquisition. Build an entity with clean credit [22cx7DJjhTY @ 00:22:54]. The Business Guy adds payoff discipline: make the lender terminate the filing after payoff. He also describes an aggressive asset-protection structure. A UCC-1 payable to the client's international LLC is sold to an international lender, and the proceeds park in an international asset protection trust. The filing is later terminated so the amounts cancel each other out [TmmMYBOJ_3c @ 00:02:58].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Screen the Parcel for Power Before Acreage
   - Confirm transmission voltage, acreage, and zoning before marketing any parcel as data-center land.
   - Require a 230 kV line at minimum, with 500 kV better, and treat gas as the alternative source [Saunders Real Estate — j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:11:32]. Check for at least 10 acres, ideally 40-plus, and budget three to six months if rezoning is needed [LandApp by LandGate — UqoyFfPDAmA @ 00:02:04].
2. Pull UCC and Title Lien History
   - Search the owner and the property for UCC-1 filings and fixture liens before buying or lending.
   - Check the title report for fixture liens before purchase [Applecation Lab — Qa47WMCz55k @ 00:04:07]. Run UCC searches on yourself and on sellers, and read whether security agreements claim all assets [BizNick — 22cx7DJjhTY @ 00:22:54].
3. Map Hail Damage with Soft Metals and Test Squares
   - Size the hail on soft metals, then circle hits in 10x10 squares before the adjuster arrives.
   - Start on caps, collars, vents, and AC fins. Circle every hit in a 10x10, flag non-hail marks for credibility, and mark the roof hours or a day early [Jansen/Adjusters International — oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:25:17].
4. Prove It Under the Membrane
   - Cut or core one spot per section with the manufacturer rep present to expose split backing and cracked ISO.
   - Carriers cut each section rather than peel-testing; a split back and busted underlayment gets that section replaced [Digital Roofing Innovations — GciwM0T3IPE @ 00:04:14]. Bring the manufacturer out and use a licensed roofer for a patch they will warranty [Jansen/Adjusters International — oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:08:16].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether data-center land is a windfall or a trap. Saunders Real Estate's panel argues the value is “power first and then land,” that end-user math shows land is undervalued, and that buyers are already offering 5 to 7 times farmland value [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:26:44]. They add that land is only 6% of the overall development deal, so pushing from $100,000 to $150,000 an acre barely moves the project budget [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:36:22]. The Industrial Real Estate Show host runs the opposite math: qualifying a site takes roughly five years and only about 1% of land is conducive, so the million-dollar-an-acre dream applies to a sliver of owners [jak1MCZd_UQ @ 00:24:12]. Matt Smith of RealNex pushes back on the host's obsolescence thesis, arguing that shrinking servers will simply be packed into the same buildings [jak1MCZd_UQ @ 00:13:06]. Even inside the bull camp, Jared Parker hedges by doubting micro data centers will spread, flagging obsolescence, and warning against jumping “on the bandwagon too hard.” The working playbook that falls out of this fight is to chase the power-first upside on sites with a real energization path while pricing in the low hit rate before committing capital or marketing dollars.

Water and power sourcing split the room just as hard. AECOM's Adam Wei names power, water, and land or access as the “primary trinity” and calls water cooling the most cost-efficient option, with air cooling as the fallback, while the panel insists all of it “can be fixed with enough time and money” [DmHfrc4zfn0 @ 00:12:18]. LandApp by LandGate likewise lists water as a cooling input consumed in significant amounts daily. Saunders Real Estate's Jared Parker flatly disagrees that water is the constraint, pointing out that a 100-acre farm or a golf course uses more [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:12:34]. On power, the Saunders panel sets the landowner screen at a 230 kV line minimum with 500 kV better, treats gas as the alternative, and says adjacent solar is likely not enough [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:11:32]. AECOM's Barry Baker runs a phased playbook instead: gas first, the grid three to five years later, with pipeline suppliers demanding 20 to 30 year contracts, and the panel's verdict is that gas solves today's timing problem but not tomorrow's energy strategy [DmHfrc4zfn0 @ 00:30:59]. Steven Buset of Siemens reports gas sits very low on the UK agenda and gives only a tentative yes on whether Europe falls behind without it.

The lien and roof-claim crowds split on paperwork and proof. The Business Guy of Asset Protection Planners calls a security agreement recommended but not required, notes some filers drop the debtor's e-signature into the optional file reference field, and pitches a UCC-1 payable to a client's international LLC, sold to an international lender and later terminated so the amounts “cancel each other out” [TmmMYBOJ_3c @ 00:02:25]. BizNick says a financing statement without a security agreement, value given, and described collateral is “a nullity,” and points to Winston Shrout's 13-count felony conviction and 10-year sentence as the endpoint of the secured-party-creditor play [22cx7DJjhTY @ 00:07:57]. On flat-roof hail, Jansen/Adjusters International builds proof with 10x10 test squares, and about 20 circled hits in one square got a roof bought [oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:27:18]. Digital Roofing Innovations counters that flat roofs have no set hits-per-square threshold at all; the carrier has the membrane cut open once per section and replaces any section where the back is split and the underlayment is busted [GciwM0T3IPE @ 00:03:57]. Even within Jansen's panel, Mark rejects adjusters pushing 200 patches while Luis accepts 100 patches on 20,000 square feet added at 10 per year, so the patch count conceded becomes a negotiating line.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest data-center trap is building or buying an asset nobody else can use. The Industrial Real Estate Show host prices industrial construction at $200 a square foot against $800 for a data center, notes the buildings lack loading doors and are single-use, and puts a retrofit at roughly $1,000 a square foot, about five times the industrial baseline [jak1MCZd_UQ @ 00:11:30]. Richmond, Virginia has re-use demand; remote sites do not, and decommissioning and remediation costs can turn the asset into a liability, which is why the host says there is zero way to invest in a rural remote data center [jak1MCZd_UQ @ 00:26:14]. Speculation is the second trap: Steven Buset of Siemens contrasts roughly 10 billion in planning against about $1 billion actually spent, and grid allocation is shifting from queue position to project readiness, while the UK regulator has floated an upfront per-megawatt fee to curb speculative applications [DmHfrc4zfn0 @ 00:15:29]. The third trap is people: Saunders Real Estate reports the state shut down Okeechobee, Palm Beach was delayed about three times, and Virginia was shut down, with 6 to 12 month moratoriums now appearing [j0rQ27ZfIcU @ 00:15:49]. AECOM's Barry Baker adds that local, then state, support is eroding.

On the lien side, the traps are stale filings and fake paper. Credit Suite warns that filings sit on business credit reports, scare off second- and third-tier lenders, and gut the strategy of stacking multiple funding approvals even though scores are not hurt. Filings are often not released, and in Credit Suite's own case a defunct lender's filing took months to clear for an SBA loan [TvwdUsUy-Cs @ 00:03:17]. Midwest Corporate Credit's PK Patel lists the same blockage, warning that a filing can force refinancing or financial engineering, that filings from the past five years show on business credit, and that default means repossession [QzlAvX38RS4 @ 00:02:31]. BizNick catalogs the downside of the secured-party-creditor play: an IRS $5,000 frivolous-position penalty, up to 10 years under 18 U.S.C. 1521 for false liens against federal officers, and no court recognizing the straw man concept [22cx7DJjhTY @ 00:12:36]. Buyers get burned from the other direction: Applecation Lab shows removable fixtures such as chandeliers or commercial refrigerators can be confiscated on default, so the title report has to be checked for fixture liens before buying [Qa47WMCz55k @ 00:04:07]. The Business Guy adds the borrower-side fix: ask the lender to terminate the filing after payoff.

Roof-claim money leaks through missed damage, voided warranties, and deductible math. Proclaim Roofing's Tyler Melton walked 20-year-old modified bitumen roofs where a prior roofer had reported no hail damage, found hits every three feet, and sized the loss at about $1 million [m37LDnkUURI @ 00:00:54]. Jansen/Adjusters International reports a manufacturer opposed coring done without its rep present, so the panel always brings the rep out and uses a licensed roofer for a bonafide patch the manufacturer will warranty; Mark also found that a granule-replacement repair cost about as much as another ply and violates the warranty [oAK4wMoYcMA @ 00:08:16]. Digital Roofing Innovations flags false positives: bird droppings on TPO and PVC mimic hail the way blisters do on shingles, and Jansen's crew warns that drone thermal over rock ballast reads false because the rock holds moisture [GciwM0T3IPE @ 00:04:58]. Policy & Property runs the homeowner deductible math: on a $300,000 home a 2% deductible is $6,000, so a $2,500 repair pays nothing, a $15,000 replacement pays $9,000, and actual cash value on a 12-of-25-year roof pays $1,800 after $7,200 depreciation [V-xAfnhz1BY @ 00:04:18]. The State Farm memo shows replace-to-repair ratios falling from 3.2:1 to about 2:1 after full replacements began requiring manager approval in January 2021.
