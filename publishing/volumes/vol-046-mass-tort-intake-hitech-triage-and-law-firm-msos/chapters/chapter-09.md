## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on this beat starts from the same premise: mass tort is a volume business, and the medical record is the raw material. Bernadette Catalana, defense-side national coordinating counsel at MGM, puts the math bluntly: a typical lawyer carries 20 to 40 cases, while a mass tort lawyer may carry about 4,000, and organization is the only counter to that load, because missing a deadline or disclosure day is the risk [n2icO1gMkN8 @ 00:02:07]. Cara Wall watched the HRT litigation run to roughly 14,000 to 15,000 plaintiffs and reports current MDLs reaching hundreds of thousands [1O2RKMH06LU @ 00:06:30]. Inside those inventories, Supio CEO Jerry Joe estimates that 70 to 80 percent of all personal injury data is medical, which is why the platform ties that medical data into a knowledge graph [Yq68SC86WTQ @ 00:09:49]. Pattern Data founder Matt Francis frames the same reality as an infrastructure problem: mass tort requires structured data at scale, and the firms still stitching intake, outsourced review, CRMs and spreadsheets together by hand are paying for every data point and skipping re-evaluation because of it [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:23:43]. The shared baseline is simple: control the records pipeline, structure what comes out of it, and the rest of the docket becomes manageable.

The second point of consensus is that HITECH is a retrieval weapon, not administrative paperwork. The New York Injury & Malpractice Law Firm tells every client to request records in digital form, because the 2009 law hands the patient the choice of format and caps the federal fee for digital copies at $6.50 regardless of page volume, while New York paper copies at up to 75 cents a page turn a 3,000 to 5,000 page file into a bill in the thousands [uJITvDxd4jA @ 00:00:39]. Bernard Nomberg in Birmingham reaches the same number from the preemption angle: federal law overrides state per-page schedules for electronic records, leaving $6.50 or a reasonable cost-based fee covering the labor to make the CD plus postage [_9ZPWIgPW14 @ 00:01:14]. The lawofmedicine breakdown of the HHS OCR rule confirms the access right sits on a reasonable fee for copy labor, and that business associates are now directly bound by HIPAA [n3wWlYBSpfg @ 00:02:00]. The New York firm's second video puts the spread in real dollars: about $6.50 for a CD holding 2,000 pages, versus roughly $1,500 at 75 cents a page and months of waiting [JdDeicrkI-o @ 00:00:23]. Nomberg adds that scanned paper and stored records from non-electronic providers still carry a flat fee with no per-page charge, so paper billing is the trap to refuse up front.

The third agreement cuts across vendors, founders and trial lawyers: AI accelerates the record work, but a human signs off. CERIS operations VP James Contes argues that even a 5% error rate applied to billions of dollars in claims at payers like United or HCA is gigantic money, so trained staff double-check OCR output with multiple QC and back-end QA passes, since OCR that misses the H&P triggers a second request [Coka2u38__k @ 00:17:43]. Matt Francis, whose entire company is AI-first, still says human expertise is critical and routes low-confidence calls such as pathology to experts while medical and smoking history come back near 100% confident [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:19:24]. Tyler Schneider at Tor Herman Law runs Supio on trial work, yet warns to fact-check everything because blurred records and handwriting can fail OCR, and the firm's science lead Chad still reads every page [Yq68SC86WTQ @ 00:20:26]. Cara Wall uses AI for the first cut of medical chronologies and then puts humans on review, and treats any tool like a paralegal or associate [1O2RKMH06LU @ 00:19:43]. The baseline prerequisite all four share is a human validation layer between machine extraction and anything that reaches a matrix, a special master, or a jury.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The retrieval playbook from The New York Injury & Malpractice Law Firm runs on speed, not patience. Portals are fast but incomplete, and the statutory 30-day deadline is almost always ignored, so the firm calls the records custodian directly, cites ongoing medical care, and asks for email delivery or sends a courier at a set time, chasing a 24-hour turnaround with follow-up calls [uJITvDxd4jA @ 00:01:48]. When a provider stonewalls with a $5,000 quote, the move is to narrow the request to the operative or imaging report, roughly 10 to 20 pages instead of 2,000 to 3,000, and if that fails, subpoena the records, since ignoring a subpoena means contempt; the firm has sued just to get records [uJITvDxd4jA @ 00:03:40]. The firm's no-expense routine adds a three-page cover letter citing HITECH that refuses to pay for paper, then a walk-in at Albany Medical Center with a HIPAA power of attorney to pick up a CD the next day, often at no charge, and a $10 Starbucks gift card, then scans the file into case-management software and burns CDs for experts [JdDeicrkI-o @ 00:02:35]. Bernard Nomberg flags one carve-out: X-ray films can carry a charge, so get a quote first because a third-party vendor may be involved [_9ZPWIgPW14 @ 00:03:36].

On the review side, Matt Francis builds one lens per litigation at Pattern Data and runs three products off it: same-day screening on usage, exposure timelines, diagnosis and evidentiary weight; development work on treatment patterns, severity, risk factors and gaps; and settlement packaging with eligibility, points or matrix valuation, and proof packets for a neutral or claims administrator [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:06:16]. Valuation turns on dosage, duration, latency and cancer-subtype dividing lines, and Francis reliably sees 7 to 10x better throughput on case review, which matters when settlements land with 60, 90 or 120-day windows for thousands of clients [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:13:03]. At Tor Herman Law, a NICU child's file ran 43,000 pages, where even a nurse summary at 10% would still be 4,300 pages; Supio filters by doctor or system and pulls weights and PT notes in real time, and across 86 depositions at seven hours each, or 602 hours, it surfaces contradictions from a witness deposed seven times, plus rebuttals to comparative-fault and preemption defenses [Yq68SC86WTQ @ 00:27:29]. Cara Wall's firm runs an over-20,000-case voluntary census by scraping medical records, then has humans spot-check, and answers show-cause attacks with percentages for proof of use and product ID [1O2RKMH06LU @ 00:18:04].

Downstream, the numbers come from administrators. Archer's Gary Goldberg says the term sheet or MOU stage takes several months before the MSA is final, a special master then needs a few additional months to build the matrix, and lien resolution has to clear Medicare, 50 state Medicaid agencies, health plans and military plans, with holdback payments arriving about 6 to 9 months post-allocation. Goldberg's benchmark is that 90% of funds are generally cleared and distributed within 10 to 14 months, with VA liens as the long poles [8CBO3CgHLvA @ 00:22:02]. Michelle Prince reports LitPros has worked thousands of Boy Scouts bankruptcy cases for about a year, cutting liens by 99% on average, around $72,000, after firms opted out of the trustee's vendor, and promises a 24-hour response on every email and call [73xfuxsKsEM @ 00:15:34]. On disbursement, Paige Richardson prices Milestone's Pathway at $200 per claimant, with Zelle, PayPal, ACH, paper check, or a custom prepaid Mastercard so unbanked claimants avoid check-casher fees [Y7FbfNp9_X4 @ 00:38:20]. Michael McCready, whose clients mostly complain about not hearing from the lawyer, sends seven to eight texts over 30 days after the release is mailed to the adjuster, at day 7, 10, 12, 14 and 21 [Y7FbfNp9_X4 @ 00:05:14].

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Record-access operators split on who pulls the trigger on a HITECH request and through which door. Bernard Nomberg of The Nomberg Law Firm teaches that the request has to be written by the patient, not the attorney, with the patient directing delivery to the lawyer, and that electronic copies carry no per-page fee, only $6.50 or a reasonable cost-based fee covering the labor to make the CD plus postage [_9ZPWIgPW14 @ 00:04:16]. The New York Injury & Malpractice Law Firm runs the opposite play: after a DC federal lower court barred lawyer requests, lawyers could again request digital records as of April 17, 2020, so the firm sends a three-page cover letter citing HITECH, refuses to pay for paper, and walks into Albany Medical Center with a HIPAA power of attorney to collect a CD the next day [JdDeicrkI-o @ 00:01:56]. The same firm writes off patient portals as fast but incomplete and 'not reliable at all,' preferring to call the records custodian, cite ongoing medical care, and push for a 24-hour turnaround [uJITvDxd4jA @ 00:01:15]. Jeannie Hennem of ChartFast, speaking on the CERIS Health panel, is betting the other way, forecasting Amazon-style e-commerce shopping for records riding on interoperability [Coka2u38__k @ 00:15:16].

The sharpest split among operators is how far to trust machine review. Matt Francis of Pattern Data claims 7 to 10x better throughput on case review and says medical and smoking history come back near 100% confident, with confidence grading routing only edge cases such as pathology to human experts [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:13:03]. James Contes of CERIS Health counters that a 5% error rate on billions at United and HCA scale is 'gigantic dollars,' and that OCR missing the H&P triggers a second request, so trained staff double-check through multiple QC and back-end QA steps [Coka2u38__k @ 00:17:43]. Tyler Schneider of Tor Herman Law sits in the middle: Supio filters a 43,000-page NICU record by doctor or system in real time, yet the firm's science lead Chad still reads every page, and the LawNext line is blunt: 'you still have to be a lawyer' [Yq68SC86WTQ @ 00:31:22]. Cara Wall lets AI produce only the first cut of medical chronologies before human review, runs an over-20,000-case voluntary census by scraping records with human spot-checks, and treats Lexis AI strictly as a 'hunting tool' after a Stanford study showed a lower hallucination rate than Westlaw [1O2RKMH06LU @ 00:19:43].

On the back end, the timeline fight is stark. Gary Goldberg of Archer says 90% of funds are generally cleared and distributed within 10 to 14 months, with holdbacks landing about 6 to 9 months post-allocation and programs able to run first-in-first-out [8CBO3CgHLvA @ 00:22:02]. Paige Richardson of Milestone frames the claimant's reality as 5-plus years to settle and another 2 to distribute, and prices Pathway at $200 per claimant, 'well below the market standard,' with Zelle, PayPal, ACH, paper check or a prepaid Mastercard for unbanked claimants [Y7FbfNp9_X4 @ 00:20:00]. Lien playbooks diverge too: Michelle Prince of LitPros says raising liens at intake is a turn-off, yet pushes collecting health insurance information early and accurately, and pitches single-event lien work only to high-volume, tech-forward firms that integrate [73xfuxsKsEM @ 00:19:34]. On the vendor market, Francis calls the last 12 to 18 months of demand-letter and individual-tort entrants a 'bubble,' while host Gordon answers that competition is good [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:24:25]. Francis insists Pattern is not document review, a general LLM summary or a chronology tool but cross-document inference, and predicts the platform integrating intake through claim forms wins.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Record pulls bleed money and time at the request desk. The New York Injury & Malpractice Law Firm says the 30-day deadline is 'almost always' ignored and calls Syax and MRO charging 75 cents a page for digital records a 'total scam' that survives because nobody reports it; when stonewalled with a $5,000 quote, the fix is narrowing to the operative or imaging report, roughly 10 to 20 pages instead of 2,000 to 3,000 [uJITvDxd4jA @ 00:02:49]. The firm's own math: 2,000 pages in 24 hours on a CD, versus 75 cents a page, roughly $1,500, and months of waiting [JdDeicrkI-o @ 00:03:17]. On the vendor side, Jeannie Hennem of ChartFast reports a request sent to the wrong person sitting for weeks, 8 points of entry found in one case study, and SFTP deliveries corrupted by the vendor's own tech nearly triggering a takeback; lost requests mean lost revenue [Coka2u38__k @ 00:07:22]. Hennem also puts commingled patients from scanning errors in at least 2% of every project file, patched with NPIs plus OCR and actionable feedback to hospitals, while James Contes adds that systems switch ROI vendors unannounced and some centralize intake for 10 hospitals [Coka2u38__k @ 00:25:57].

The penalty history is the cautionary tale for any MSO or firm holding PHI in bulk. Dr. Peter Polack and Mike Meikle (Hawkthorne Group) walk through Cignet Health of Prince George's County, Maryland, which denied 41 patients their records between September 2008 and October 2009, stonewalled HHS, then delivered a truck full of thousands of records and ate a $4.3 million civil monetary penalty [CuDhCaHyZnI @ 00:01:51]. Two days later Mass General paid $1 million after an employee left documents on the subway containing PHI for 192 HIV/AIDS patients, never recovered, plus a corrective action plan and policy retooling that added cost. Their fix is process, not technology: train staff, encrypt storage, run asset management, know the laptop count, and enable remote wipe and tracking. lawofmedicine adds that HITECH binds business associates directly to HIPAA, so BA agreements need HIPAA and breach-notification language plus safeguards [n3wWlYBSpfg @ 00:03:04]. Michelle Prince of LitPros carries that into the AI era, warning that open AI tools leak legal and health data [73xfuxsKsEM @ 00:21:01], and Cara Wall's line is flat: don't upload client documents or sensitive things because they are not secure [1O2RKMH06LU @ 00:26:20].

Inventory and payout traps hit later and harder. Cara Wall warns that lawyers who bring in cases and don't work them hand the defense its meritless-case narrative, that PFS forms went from roughly a 15-page exercise to 60 to 80 pages, and that the Taxotere-style defense pulls social media for every plaintiff through show-cause and deficiency processes; dual representation means losing clients or sharing fees unless the firm keeps up client touches [1O2RKMH06LU @ 00:07:05]. Gary Goldberg of Archer cites a $100,000 lien sitting against a $30,000 settlement, probate and bankruptcy-trustee claims stacking delay, and VA liens as the long poles, noting it's not like going to one central source [8CBO3CgHLvA @ 00:19:06]. The Jon and Marc hosts add that bad reviews from waiting mass tort claimants hurt local PI SEO across a 3-to-10-year relationship, and Paige Richardson of Milestone reports Facebook claimant groups forming mini classes that generate bar complaints, while nine of ten callers just need information [Y7FbfNp9_X4 @ 00:13:51]. Matt Francis notes the old labor-only model made every data point a cost, so firms skipped re-evaluation even as criteria shifted mid-litigation [_HuCGlH6Wyw @ 00:04:40]. Bernadette Catalana pegs a mass tort load at about 4,000 cases, where a missed deadline or disclosure day is the risk.
