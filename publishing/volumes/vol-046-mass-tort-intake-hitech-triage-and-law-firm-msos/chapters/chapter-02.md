## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every camp agrees the MDL is now the default container for mass harm. LawShelf puts MDL cases at 39% of open federal cases, mostly product liability, and counts more than one in five pending federal cases as an MDL mass tort [sozfq7mjvKE @ 00:00:25]. Sullivan & Cromwell reports MDLs climbing from about half to 'closer to 70 percent' of federal civil cases [X7eQYHQmnm8 @ 00:02:35]. The mechanics are uniform: 28 USC §1407 ships cases sharing common fact questions to one of 94 districts for pretrial purposes only, and the original court loses jurisdiction until remand, while the transferee judge owns discovery, dismissal, summary judgment, Daubert, and class certification [ZZ-JHbBTjyw @ 00:06:56]. LawShelf adds that shared legal issues alone never qualify; the panel wants common, material, contested facts, sets no minimum case count, and weighs transport and hotels, defendant location, witnesses and evidence, other MDLs already in the district, and the judge's competence. Each side banks something: defendants get manageability, plaintiffs get global-settlement pressure, and courts get a single judge. Justinian & Associates adds that drug-injury plaintiffs have no class-action route at all, so the MDL becomes the vehicle, with the plaintiffs' steering committee acting as the firms 'in charge' [mIB8iIDsWhY @ 00:00:18].

Inside the machine, the build-out sequence is consistent across plaintiff, defense, and neutral voices. Nigh Goldenberg Raso & Vaughn lays out the phases: scattered early filings in federal and state courts, consolidation through an MDL filing, leadership either court-selected or agreed among plaintiffs' attorneys, structuring orders on service and preliminary plaintiff information, then a discovery phase that is long and expensive [AxtNAnlfdLI @ 00:00:31]. LawShelf maps the same skeleton from the bench side: an early status conference, interim lead and liaison counsel, then a plaintiffs' steering committee with sub-committees, leadership chosen by application or a proposed slate, and a common benefit fund [sozfq7mjvKE @ 00:09:19]. Plaintiff fact sheets are sworn and carry perjury exposure, courts may demand Lone Pine affidavits, late fact sheets can trigger dismissal, and defendant fact sheets come due only after the plaintiffs'. Defense trial attorney Kevin Mayer confirms either side can petition for the MDL, after which the judge runs pretrial, selects bellwethers, or remands [fMKPM7zg2OI @ 00:03:38]. KC Defense Counsel frames the payoff for the individual client: the MDL eliminates duplicated depositions and discovery, yet the client is not bound to join and stays free to reject a settlement even after the committee and defendants agree on a plan [RKiB8uc_yWk @ 00:01:30].

The endgame consensus is blunt: trials are a pricing tool, not the destination. LawShelf reports most MDLs end in settlement or dispositive motions and cases are 'never sent back,' quoting one MDL judge who said 'you have failed if you transfer it back' [sozfq7mjvKE @ 00:02:10]. Judge Robreno says more than 98% of cases never reach trial, adding flatly that 'Trials have disappeared pretty much' [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:46:03]. What fills that gap is the bellwether. Mayer describes the defense-side build: roughly 10 cases, plaintiffs pick 5 and defendants pick 5, each given full fact and expert workup and tried 'probably one at a time,' with the results feeding a global resolution that sorts plaintiffs into injury and damages 'buckets' carrying assigned values [fMKPM7zg2OI @ 00:09:48]. Schuler, Weisser, Zoeller, Overbeck & Baxter describes the same pipeline from the claimant side: the judge and both steering committees prioritize 'trial balloons,' a favorable verdict brings defendants to a mass settlement, and opt-in plaintiffs then 'fit into a grid' that is 'not as individualized' as a solo case [zCygMrcjoOQ @ 00:01:54]. The working assumption for every operator is the same: the discovery record and the first verdicts set the price, and the grid distributes it.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The transfer play is procedural and clock-driven. LawShelf spells out the motion to transfer: show convenience, justice, and efficiency, attach a numbered schedule listing parties, district and division, action number, and judge, and expect a 21-day response window, with tag-along actions reported as they surface [sozfq7mjvKE @ 00:04:58]. When the panel moves sua sponte, a show-cause order gives 14 days to appear and 21 days to respond, and silence counts as consent [sozfq7mjvKE @ 00:03:52]. Panel members are appointed by the Chief Justice, no two from the same circuit, with an office in DC and bimonthly hearings held around the country. Once transferred, the first management event is being formalized: Sullivan & Cromwell walks through the June 2023 Rule 16.1 proposal of an initial management conference, coordinating counsel, a joint report covering leadership, factual bases, and consolidated pleadings, and a management order [X7eQYHQmnm8 @ 00:06:14]. The April final rule dropped the coordinating counsel provision without prohibiting it and kept the factual-bases exchange, effective 2025 or later. Defense tactics ride that exchange: seek early briefing on non-use or pre-use injury, press for medical records in the census registry for tolling, and keep Rule 11 sanctions on the table [X7eQYHQmnm8 @ 00:12:29].

Bellwether execution is where the hardest numbers in the machine live. The testosterone MDL funnel, laid out on the Federalist Society trial panel, started with a pool of about 600, randomized 100, put 32 through core discovery at about four depositions each, and tried 8, and any selected case could be dismissed only with prejudice [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:35:27]. Nigh Goldenberg Raso & Vaughn sequences the plaintiff workup: tedious plaintiff depositions first, then treating and prescribing doctors, who often say they would not have prescribed or would have warned [b_t0BzdDfl8 @ 00:01:19]. Fleming, Nolen & Jez describes the pick logic in party-selection pools: the PSC chooses strong cases with few comorbidities while defendants choose problematic ones such as smokers, and the law of averages still produces a pattern [qOnM2bNh01E @ 00:02:25]. LawShelf reports that Vioxx ran six bellwethers, and bellwethers can be bifurcated or trifurcated; in Stryker in D. Minn., 10 bellwether mediations became the template for thousands, and global deals are conditioned on a pre-agreed acceptance percentage [9kniD1-Jx8E @ 00:09:25]. Fleming, Nolen & Jez also counts bellwether workup as '100' toward resolution, says pressure alone can produce a global deal, and asks clients for patience while the process grinds forward.

The money mechanics expose exactly how heavy the transaction costs run. Seeger reports Vioxx settled for $4.85B with about one-third going to fees under retainers, and treats those high transaction costs as the reason to explore settlement early and build trust [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:17:07]. The cost of getting there is steep on both sides: Burnick puts mass-tort discovery spend at $20–40M and calls for proportionality, while Seeger says Vioxx cost more than $40M to try 18 cases [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:22:27]. The Upchurch Watson panel prices the class side in detail: about 97% of class actions settle, the default structure is non-reversionary with cy pres for leftover funds, incentive awards typically run 2,500–5,000 dollars in consumer class actions in the Eleventh Circuit, Takata used a 500-dollar claims-made structure to force inflator replacement, and the overdraft MDL left about 80 million dollars in uncashed checks [10ygaMXLKfM @ 00:33:40]. Peter adds that getting fees paid only after final approval deters objectors. Mayer's defense metric is cost of defense, telling clients to 'pony that up... nothing more' when that number is the benchmark, because these claims 'don't go away for zero and they don't all go away for a billions of dollars' [fMKPM7zg2OI @ 00:14:31].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Bellwether selection is the sharpest fault line. Kevin Mayer of Logan Mayer describes the textbook split: ten cases, plaintiffs pick five, defendants pick five, full fact and expert workup, then trials probably one at a time [fMKPM7zg2OI @ 00:07:34]. Fleming, Nolen & Jez concedes the obvious criticism. The plaintiffs' steering committee picks strong cases with few comorbidities, defendants pick smokers and other problem files, and the result is “best of the plaintiffs... best of the defendants.” Their answer is that the law of averages still produces a usable pattern [qOnM2bNh01E @ 00:02:25]. Defense lawyer Brian Jackson rejects that answer outright. Party-pick selection is “gamed,” parties dismiss or settle cases on the eve of trial, and little is known about 90% of the plaintiffs in the pool, so randomization is the fix [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:21:13]. The testosterone MDL ran the randomized alternative as a funnel: a pool of roughly 600, 100 drawn at random, 32 pushed through core discovery at about four depositions each, and 8 tried. Any selected case could be dismissed only with prejudice, so nobody could quietly pull a bad draw [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:35:27]. That prejudice lock is the operational answer to eve-of-trial dumping. For operators, the selection method decides whether the verdicts you get mean anything at all for the remaining inventory.

What a bellwether verdict is actually worth splits the bar just as hard. John Beisner traces bellwethers to a defense tactic meant to stave off settle-only MDLs. Beisner then warns that they now shunt preemption and Daubert fights aside and turn into a cudgel through six-plaintiff trials and plaintiff-only picks [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:03:52]. Chris Seeger, who has tried more than two dozen bellwethers, says the process is valuable but the verdict numbers are less so [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:12:57]. At the Upchurch Watson mediation panel, Peter points to 3M verdicts running from 1.5 million to tens of millions, calling it “not an exact science.” The real payload, in Peter's telling, is information on liability, the damages range, and the defense's trial costs [10ygaMXLKfM @ 00:12:15]. Jonathan Selbin goes furthest. In the Whirlpool litigation the parties tried Ohio first instead of filing 27 state certification motions, and the lesson came from what the trial taught rather than from the scoreboard [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:25:13]. The same discussion admits that some issues the team considered much weaker held up in front of the jury. The playbook divergence is clear. Treat verdicts as price tags and you get whipsawed by outliers; treat them as calibration data and the range becomes negotiable across the whole docket.

Front-end vetting of the inventory is the third battleground. David Burnick of Paul Weiss pushes Lone Pine orders and minimal verification, meaning a qualified doctor attests that the product caused the injury without a full expert report. Burnick also admits Lone Pine only cuts 5,000 cases to about 2,000, which is “still a mass tort” [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:10:27]. Chris Seeger opposes Lone Pine and counters with a lighter screen drawn from the PPI MDL: a plaintiff fact sheet carrying a pharmacy record plus proof of injury [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:18:29]. Seeger also pitches summary jury trials as a pricing shortcut, and Burnick dismisses them as having “zero empirical evidence” [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:23:49]. The rulemaking fight mirrors the courtroom one. Sullivan & Cromwell reports that the defense bar backed the June 2023 Rule 16.1 proposal while the plaintiffs' bar fought coordinating counsel and any “blanket rule.” The final version dropped the coordinating-counsel provision without prohibiting it and kept the early exchange on the factual bases for claims [X7eQYHQmnm8 @ 00:09:29]. The takeaway is that the screen itself is negotiable. Defense wants proof of causation up front, plaintiffs offer proof of use and injury, and the factual-bases exchange is where that fight now lands early.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The ugliest trap is the inventory nobody vetted. Trial selection in one MDL surfaced a neighbor's video of a plaintiff chopping wood and a speedboat-race trophy belonging to plaintiffs who claimed total disability. Once trial-readiness certification was imposed, the inventory shrank [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:52:08]. David Burnick puts the dead weight at roughly 25 to 30 percent of cases that should never have been brought [LVcpWtomEvU @ 01:20:03]. Sullivan & Cromwell spells out the cost: unvetted meritless cases inflate settlement numbers and impede negotiations, which is precisely the problem the MDL Subcommittee formed in 2017 to address [X7eQYHQmnm8 @ 00:03:57]. Even the plaintiffs' side has tightened up. Chris Seeger now requires a pharmacy record and proof of income before carrying a claim, after watching medical-record collection run into the tens of millions [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:56:39]. The economics check is blunt. A case file that cannot survive a fact-sheet audit is not an asset; it is a liability that drags down the per-claim number for every legitimate client in the same grid. Acquisition volume without proof of product use, proof of injury, and proof of damages is the fastest way to get an entire docket discounted, delayed, or dismissed once the defense starts pulling files for trial.

Transaction costs eat recoveries alive. Vioxx settled for $4.85 billion, and Chris Seeger notes that roughly one-third went to fees under the retainers. Seeger treats that cost load as the reason to explore settlement early and build trust [vk0M8TfTG9g @ 00:17:07]. Trying cases is no cheaper: Vioxx cost more than $40 million to try 18 cases [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:22:27]. David Burnick pegs mass-tort discovery spend at $20 to $40 million and demands proportionality [LVcpWtomEvU @ 00:07:28]. At the single-case level, the Center for Litigation and Courts warns that expert and discovery costs in an individual suit may simply not justify the damages, which is why claimants get pooled at all [ZZ-JHbBTjyw @ 00:03:33]. The defense side runs its own math. Burnick credits the testosterone MDL model, which combined fact sheets, pool stratification, cross-cutting issues, and bellwether discovery, with avoiding roughly $10 million in medical-record gathering. Seeger counters that defendants simply stopped ordering those records. Either way, whoever pays for records controls the pace. Model fee share, case-specific expert spend, and record retrieval per claim before signing, because a settlement grid divided across an inflated inventory after a one-third fee cut can leave individual payouts thin.

Procedural and control traps hit the claimant file directly. LawShelf warns that plaintiff fact sheets are sworn with perjury exposure, and late fact sheets can lead to dismissal [sozfq7mjvKE @ 00:12:39]. At the mediation table, Sharon cautions that a lawyer outside lead counsel and the plaintiffs' steering committee may see a client's case settled “without a lot of input” [10ygaMXLKfM @ 00:05:38]. Once a global deal lands, Schuler Weisser explains that opt-in plaintiffs “fit into a grid” that is “not as individualized” as a standalone recovery [zCygMrcjoOQ @ 00:01:54]. That grid only pays if enough claimants sign. LawShelf notes that global deals are conditioned on a pre-agreed acceptance percentage, so straggling or dissatisfied clients can stall an entire resolution [9kniD1-Jx8E @ 00:10:13]. Layer in the expert gate: under the amended Rule 702 regime, a forklift expert was excluded for lacking forklift-specific expertise, so a case-specific causation theory can die before trial. The defense side faces its own failure modes, from government scrutiny to stock-value hits to bankruptcy as an existential event. The operating rule is simple. File fact sheets on time, secure a seat or a voice near leadership, and set client expectations on grid math and acceptance thresholds well before the settlement lands.
