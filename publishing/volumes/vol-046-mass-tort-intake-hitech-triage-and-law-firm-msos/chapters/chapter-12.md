## Executive Summary

Healthcare and law-firm practitioners describe the same split: a licensed professional entity that owns the practice and a management services organization that anyone can own, linked by a management service agreement. Jackson LLP, Holt Law, and Dike Law Group root it in CPOM; Holt ports it to law firms with three guardrails and a Texas bright line: flat monthly fee or cost plus fair margin passes, percentage of revenue is a no-go. Shikhman puts initial monthly figures at $500 to $1,500. Frederick Shelton turned down about 90% of 70-plus PE-backed MSOs and benchmarks offers against 6 to 8x market multiples. Arizona's ABS track lets non-lawyers own 100% under a compliance lawyer and 10% disclosure. Practitioners split on approval speed, from Rodriguez's March-to-August run and three-minute hearing to 6 to 8 month queues, and on whether PE control of intake captures the firm.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every healthcare-side practitioner starts from one root: the corporate practice of medicine. Erin Jackson of Jackson LLP frames it bluntly: in CPOM or fee-splitting states a person who is not a physician cannot practice medicine, so unlicensed operators cannot own or run the practice, and the MSO lets them manage while steering clear of clinical work, backed by additional contracts and a medical director [4niOtoceEKc @ 00:00:19]. Holt Law describes the move as dividing a healthcare practice in half and says it all boils down to CPOM, which exists to stop non-doctors from making medical decisions [Oa-6OBD0Gak @ 00:00:55]. Jackson's build is two companies: the medical entity employs the doctors, while the MSO takes janitorial, reception, billing, and non-medical hiring and firing [wUqthLNx6Ro @ 00:01:00]. Doris Dike of Dike Law Group adds the threshold question: first decide whether the business is the practice of medicine, because if it is, the operator needs an MSO plus a physician partner, and most states run some form of CPOM [aZDpDRV6-KA @ 00:01:06]. Everyone lands on the same governing document, the management service agreement, which Jackson calls a really complicated contract that must address Stark, Anti-Kickback, the False Claims Act, fee-splitting, and CPOM.

The second point of consensus is the money rule. Jackson says the MSO gets paid a flat fee, monthly or weekly, not based on revenues from patient care [wUqthLNx6Ro @ 00:02:02]. Holt Law ports the identical rule into law firms, where the model was borrowed from healthcare CPOM: the law firm stays 100% lawyer-owned, the MSO takes investors and runs finance, HR, marketing, and IT as a vendor, and three guardrails hold the line: no non-lawyer ownership, no fee sharing, no UPL or influence over lawyer judgment [DNk78wbKy5s @ 00:01:23]. Fee sharing is the risk Holt flags hardest, citing the Texas State Bar's bright line: any compensation model that's a percentage of a law firm's revenue is a no-go, while a flat monthly fee or cost plus a fair margin is the safe path forward [DNk78wbKy5s @ 00:02:51]. Frederick Shelton of Shelton & Steele describes the same split as a law co and a service co, and calls the whole wave a remake of what healthcare went through about 40 years ago, followed by dentistry and accounting [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:08:43]. Across both industries, the MSO earns through a defined management fee, never through a cut of professional revenue.

On the Arizona ABS side, every practitioner agrees that non-lawyer money buys ownership, never control of the practice. Lynda Shely, who sits on the Arizona Supreme Court ABS committee, says non-lawyers may own 100% of a firm but cannot direct the representation or choose which clients the firm takes, and that Arizona ABS firms do not permit non-lawyers to practice law [poe1CAsUc4U @ 00:11:33]. Allen Rodriguez, the non-lawyer majority shareholder of Singular Law Group, runs exactly that firewall: product, client experience, operations, automations, scaling, and capital sit with Rodriguez, while lawyers handle legal services [ajveDNkk7dg @ 00:08:59]. Denton Peterson Dunn states the core principle as attorney independence to avoid ethical violations, naming profit-versus-ethics pressure on attorney judgment as the first risk in its rundown [5NeidO5OU60 @ 00:00:47]. Shely repeats the line across both Heart of Law interviews: an ABS law firm is a law firm, it cannot be a referral service, and an office manager cannot sign clients [OdPOa3osKC0 @ 00:12:01]. Shely frames the whole program as voluntary: this is being done for you as an option, not to you, and an Arizona lawyer can say 'I have a law firm in Arizona, it's not an ABS, I'm doing just fine.'

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Inside the MSA, Sara Shikhman, a former general counsel of a multi-state med spa, says the MSO itself is as simple as a standard LLC or corporation that anyone can own; it holds the assets, the equipment, and the brand name, and manages the non-clinical side [JadIHn9B3vk @ 00:02:14]. The MSA is more robust than a medical director agreement, covering scope, assets, leases, and distribution of funds, and it details the management fee, which is how the MSO owner captures revenue. The initial monthly figure Shikhman generally sees is $500 to $1,500, and the medical-director fee gets renegotiated as the business picks up [JadIHn9B3vk @ 00:04:28]. Holt Law draws the asset line with a med spa: Botox, filler, clinical salaries, and, in a conservative view, medical equipment stay clinical, while billing, facility management, and administrative staffing go to the MSO; tension starts when owners push lasers into the MSO [Oa-6OBD0Gak @ 00:03:22]. In California, Holt says non-MDs must use an MSO because CPOM requires greater-than-50% physician ownership, and expanding to more locations may take $10,000 to $100,000 in funding that investors can safely route through the MSO [bXO6V29iXPs @ 00:04:43]. Holt also warns MSOs are not needed for every clinic.

On the law side, Shelton supplies the deal math. Shelton has spoken with over 70 PE-backed MSOs and turned down over 60, about 90% [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:06:17]. The red flag Shelton listens for: once we control client intake, then we control the firm. The valuation illustration: a firm whose profit climbs from $3M to $4M to $5M might be valued at about $30M, take $20 to $25M up front plus rollover equity, and see a total package that could exceed $50M [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:09:17]. Buyers initially wanted PI firms with $8 to $20M of EBITDA; corporate-backed buyers now look at about $1M, and deals sort into platforms versus bolt-ons. Retiring owners can negotiate put rights, for example $20M up front plus $5M guaranteed in three years. Shelton benchmarks offers hard: 4x profit against the 6 to 8x seen in the market, and waiting until January to close to avoid handing 30 to 40% to taxes [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:27:11]. Kvesic's Aprio Legal shows the operating payoff: financial sponsor Charles Bank, an HR team, three-way trust reconciliations, and $500M on AI and tech over five years, with Kvesic admitting a 10-lawyer firm couldn't have afforded that AI software [_P68fUIZN48 @ 00:17:03].

Getting an Arizona ABS license is its own workflow. Shely says the application requires disclosing every person or company with a 10% or greater ownership or economic interest, including holding companies, LLCs, and decision-makers such as a fund manager on the board, plus criminal convictions, civil litigation from the last 10 years, agency investigations, and license revocations, along with plans for confidentiality, trust accounting, conflicts, and docketing [poe1CAsUc4U @ 00:18:08]. On Law Leaders, Shely put applications at 100 to 1,000 pages and approval time stretching from about 4 months to 6 to 8 months, while passive investors under 10% who are not decision-makers skip background checks [Tvn12qG9i5s @ 00:12:25]. Rodriguez reports a leaner experience: a roughly 10-page application, a six thousand dollar hurdle up front, public approval sessions around the first Thursday of each month, and approval in three minutes, with the firm filing in March, winning approval in August, and operating by the end of August [ajveDNkk7dg @ 00:26:16]. Once licensed, the compliance lawyer runs a self-audit every six months, renewal now comes every two years after the June 2025 changes, and fees rose so the program is self-sustaining [OdPOa3osKC0 @ 00:23:51].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fork is route selection, and the operators split hard. Holt Law frames the ABS as a non-starter for most firms because Arizona and Utah each needed a law change, while an MSO is viable in most US jurisdictions right now, with the law firm kept 100% lawyer-owned and the MSO taking investors as a vendor [DNk78wbKy5s @ 00:03:52]. Andy Kvesic, CEO of Aprio Legal and an early Arizona ABS licensee, draws the line differently: an MSO cannot own a law firm, an ABS can, and private equity drifted to MSOs mainly because ABS applications take six to eight months [_P68fUIZN48 @ 00:08:46]. Kvesic prices the ABS route bluntly: self-compliance audits twice a year, infractions reported to the ABS committee and the state bar, a mandatory compliance lawyer acting as ethics officer, a license that can be pulled, and renewal every two years. Allen Rodriguez, the non-lawyer majority shareholder of Singular Law Group, reports a much lighter experience from the 2021 cohort: a roughly 10-page application, filed in March, approved in August, and voted through in three minutes at a public session [ajveDNkk7dg @ 00:29:38]. Lynda Shely counters with the current reality: applications now run 100 to 1,000 pages, and time to approval has stretched from about four months to six to eight months [Tvn12qG9i5s @ 00:12:25].

Fee mechanics are where the healthcare and legal camps look aligned on paper but diverge in practice. Holt Law calls the Texas State Bar opinion a bright line rule: any compensation model that's a percentage of a law firm's revenue is a no-go, while a flat monthly fee or cost plus a fair margin is the safe path [DNk78wbKy5s @ 00:02:51]. Erin Jackson of Jackson LLP mirrors that on the medical side, structuring the MSA around a flat fee paid monthly or weekly, not based on revenues from patient care, and built to address Stark, the Anti-Kickback Statute, the False Claims Act, state fee-splitting rules and CPOM [wUqthLNx6Ro @ 00:02:02]. Sara Shikhman, a former general counsel of a multi-state med spa, is more candid about the purpose: the management fee is how the MSO owner captures revenue, generally $500 to $1,500 a month initially, with the medical-director fee renegotiated as the business picks up [JadIHn9B3vk @ 00:04:28]. Kvesic attacks the legal-side asymmetry directly: recruiters routinely take a percentage of first-year revenue from law firms, yet an MSO doing comparable vendor work cannot be paid that way [_P68fUIZN48 @ 00:40:33]. The operator read: flat and cost-plus fees are the shared floor, but the camps disagree on whether the percentage ban is principle or inconsistency.

The deepest split is over whether outside capital on the business side corrupts the practice side. Frederick Shelton of Shelton & Steele reports speaking with more than 70 PE-backed MSOs and turning down over 60, about 90%, and the tell is a single sentence from buyers: once we control client intake, then we control the firm [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:28:35]. Kvesic treats that fear as overblown, arguing a well-built MSO only consolidates vendors firms already pay, such as Westlaw or LexisNexis, IT, couriers, AI tools like Harvey, and recruiting, and that non-lawyer ownership isn't automatically a trigger for unethical behavior; Kvesic pegs the loudest critics as personal-injury lawyers [_P68fUIZN48 @ 00:13:23]. Lynda Shely, who sits on the Arizona Supreme Court ABS committee, draws the line at control rather than equity: non-lawyers may own 100% of an Arizona ABS but cannot direct representation or choose which clients the firm takes [poe1CAsUc4U @ 00:11:33]. Denton Peterson Dunn flags high-volume, low-cost work as a quality risk inside ABS firms, then undercuts the alarm by noting that this pressure already exists for traditional lawyers and that lower costs could be good for most clients [5NeidO5OU60 @ 00:01:43].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The ABS failures already on the record cluster around firms that forgot they are law firms. Lynda Shely reports that two compliance lawyers have been disciplined and reads that as proof the system works, but the operator takeaway is that the named ethics officer is the first person exposed when a firm slips [OdPOa3osKC0 @ 00:10:44]. Shely also warns against the cheap compliance-lawyer model: the role needs management experience, and one person should not be stretched across five to seven firms [OdPOa3osKC0 @ 00:19:39]. The sharpest trap hits mass-tort and PI operators, who made up 26 of the 65 ABS firms counted in the three-year update: a bar complaint is pending over a firm allegedly just referring cases out, and Shely states an Arizona lawyer must give substantive input on intake, medical-record review and client communication, so an empty Arizona office funneling cases to Florida is not allowed [poe1CAsUc4U @ 00:36:28]. On the Law Leaders update, Shely closes with the line every tort aggregator should keep in view: this is not a get-rich-quick scheme, even though ER 7.2's elimination now lets Arizona lawyers pay non-lawyers referral fees as a percentage of fees, with no bar charges so far [Tvn12qG9i5s @ 00:27:40].

On the healthcare side, the failures that blow up MSO structures are mundane and operational. Holt Law's warning example is a clinic posting before-and-after photos without patient consent and running QuickBooks payroll that was misfiled with missed taxes, exactly the back-office work an MSO is supposed to absorb [bXO6V29iXPs @ 00:03:37]. The structural leak Holt Law flags is owners pushing clinical assets such as lasers over to the MSO side, when a conservative read keeps Botox, filler, clinical salaries and even medical equipment inside the clinical entity [Oa-6OBD0Gak @ 00:04:04]. Erin Jackson cautions that an MSO is never a single filing: it needs additional contracts and a medical director to keep non-physician operators clear of the practice of medicine, and the private-equity exit story carries no guarantees on what PE may be interested in [4niOtoceEKc @ 00:01:15]. CorporateAttorneysCA lists the downstream penalty stack: regulatory issues can arise around the corporate practice of medicine, fee splitting and the Stark law, which can result in serious consequences if violated [cTWDG8n6ks0 @ 00:01:56]. The economics check is simple: if the MSA, the asset split and the medical director are not airtight, the efficiency pitch becomes the liability.

Deal-side traps hit before and after closing. Frederick Shelton warns that some investment bankers overpromise 10x multiples to collect $1 million to $2 million success fees, while an actual offer of 4x profit compares poorly with the 6x to 8x Shelton sees in the market, and closing before January can hand 30% to 40% to taxes [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:24:08]. Shelton's governance kill-switch is a board with one attorney facing four PE directors who control equity-partner decisions, which Shelton labels controlling the practice of law [I4F5OYZQJuU @ 00:31:10]. Holt Law adds two post-close risks: investor pressure on the firm, and the fact that lawyers often cannot be bound by non-competes, so the only shield is an ironclad MSA [DNk78wbKy5s @ 00:05:58]. Kvesic frames the traditional partnership as its own failure mode: partners sweep cash to zero in December, run on a January credit line, and resume draws in March or April, which Kvesic calls a ridiculous way to run a business, while a bank loan simply adds a creditor with liens on receivables [_P68fUIZN48 @ 00:24:17]. Before signing, operators should price the multiple, the closing date, the board seats and the non-compete gap together, not one at a time.
