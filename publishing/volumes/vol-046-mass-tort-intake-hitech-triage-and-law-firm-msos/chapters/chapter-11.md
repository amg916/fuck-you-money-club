# Chapter 11: Capital Markets Meet the Law Firm: Portfolio Funding, PE & Law-Firm MSOs

*Litigation as an asset class, funding market process, portfolio and law-firm financing, investor perspectives, ethics of non-lawyer capital, PE consolidation, and MSOs for contingency-fee firms*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Holland & Knight's Trisha Rich and Josh Port, Lucian Pera, Tim Mackey and the Esquire Bank panel converge on one blueprint. Spin non-lawyer staff, equipment, IP and founder likeness into a Delaware LLC MSO. Lease it back for a fixed monthly or cost-plus fee, with 10-15% markups, validated by a third-party fairness opinion. Keep lawyers, engagement letters, trust accounts, hiring, case selection and settlement inside the firm. Accounting trades near 15x EBITDA versus 4-8x for law firms, and Joe Prencipe pitches buying in at 5x to roll up PI firms running 35% margins. On the funding side, Burford funds 6-8% of reviewed cases at mid-20s IRRs, and 39 funders committed $2.8B in 2025. Disagreements center on mass-tort cash-flow unpredictability, the timing of Am Law deals, securitization, and whether portfolio deals are truly diversified.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Holland & Knight's Trisha Rich frames the MSO bluntly: it is 'simply a vendor,' and nobody is buying law firms [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:03:09]. Every practitioner describing the build lands on the same split. Tim Mackey of Vista Consulting spins all non-lawyer staff, leases and the licensed brand out of the law firm into the managed service organization, which leases them back while the firm makes a lease payment [lgURVgZL9IY @ 00:01:14]. On Mattern LLC, Holland & Knight's Josh and Trish add equipment, IP and the founders' name, image and likeness to the transfer list, while lawyers, engagement letters and trust accounts stay inside the firm [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:05:26]. HR and accounting can migrate to the management company; hiring and firing, case selection and settlement authority never do. On The LegalTech Fund, Rich calls the vehicle a 'two company model' or LSO, a parallel management company that handles administrative and business functions and that anyone, including non-lawyers, can own, provided it never owns or controls the lawyers and is never paid in a way that amounts to a fee split [kF2XWwBObZ8 @ 00:04:08]. The operator takeaway is consistent across these speakers: the investor buys the business chassis, never the practice, and every dollar of value has to be engineered on that boundary.

The second consensus point is payment. Rich is categorical that no percentage of profits or revenue is allowed: the fee must be a fixed monthly number or cost-plus, backed by a third-party fairness opinion [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:23:21]. The Esquire Bank panel with Chad Dudley and Tim Mackey describes the same discipline, with the valuation done by outside professionals and 'not tied to the revenue of the law firm,' run through licensing agreements or cost-plus arrangements [epFmlanyChw @ 00:01:28]. Lucian Pera of Adams and Reese says MSOs are legal and, structured correctly, trigger no Rule 5.4 problem, even though ethics opinions are the thinnest precedent and Texas only blesses the pieces rather than the whole [wXttJe-HCVM @ 00:05:40]. Rich dismisses the common owner fear of 'anybody coming in here and telling me what to do' as 'a little bit overblown,' because the two ethical pillars, no control and no fee-splitting, wall the investor off from the lawyering [kF2XWwBObZ8 @ 00:10:08]. Every state except Arizona carries a version of Rule 5.4, and the concern is control as well as ownership, so the fee mechanism and the control map are where every deal is judged. The Mattern walkthrough adds that regulators elevate 'substance over form,' so a cost-plus label will not rescue a fee that behaves like a revenue share.

The third consensus thread is that outside capital only works with a plan attached. Pera calls the MSO 'a tool... not for everyone,' rejects 'management by inflight magazine,' and insists the business plan comes first, citing an AI tool for construction clients as the example, and says a bank or 'uncle Jim' may be easier [wXttJe-HCVM @ 00:07:22]. Rich runs the same fit test from the deal desk: if the capital would just sit in savings, an MSO 'might not be for you' [kF2XWwBObZ8 @ 00:08:10]. On the litigation-finance side of the capital stack, the funders agree on the terms that make the money palatable. GLS Capital's Hannah Sadler explains that funding is non-recourse, so if the case is lost or dismissed 'the litigant doesn't owe anything to the funder,' and that funders do not control the litigation [9teRv9eFpEc @ 00:05:13]. Bentham's Dave Kirstine says the only rights retained after investing are to be kept informed, to weigh in on settlement and to be paid, and Drumcliff's Jim Little and Michael Weiss likewise exercise no control [rQPZlRgHcfE @ 00:56:12]. Whether the money arrives as MSO equity or case funding, the common denominator is passive capital backing a firm that keeps running its own cases.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The Mattern LLC walkthrough gives the build order. Step one is forming the MSO, 'typically just a Delaware LLC' [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:04:03]. Non-legal assets then move across, and the MSO licenses or leases them back under a business services agreement for a monthly fee, with an accounting firm validating fairness; the stated goal is to move as much economics as is defensible, net of attorney compensation [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:06:30]. Only then does the investor buy all or part of the MSO, either for partner liquidity or growth capital. Valuation is built vertical by vertical, marketing, HR and so on, and the rules do not require fair market value; it functions as a defense. On fee levels, Rich puts typical cost-plus markups at 10-15%, and says fee changes should be forward-looking only, with the fairness analysis refreshed; an annual cadence 'seems more or less sensible,' though there are no hard and fast rules [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:25:36]. The Esquire Bank panel structures the agreement by defining the services, the MSO-owned IP and the resources used, then paying a fixed monthly amount like any vendor, re-evaluated as MSO headcount grows, usually yearly and sometimes sooner [epFmlanyChw @ 00:01:08]. Annual re-pricing is the shared default across both camps.

Because lawyers cannot be bound by non-competes, even in an asset sale, and Josh Port warns there are 'no silver bullets,' the deal paper has to do the retention work. The Mattern walkthrough lists the levers: rollover equity, equity forfeiture, and five-year vesting of cash, for example returning 60% if a lawyer quits in year two [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:19:20]. Name, image and likeness licenses are another lever, and the Jon and Marc panel reports these run 'in perpetuity' and can permit AI versions of the founder [U2xFBBUm_IU @ 00:20:06]. Rich flags two drafting traps that show up in investor paper. First, personal liability for unpaid MSO fees should be rewritten as deferred fees [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:42:21]. Second, non-solicitation covenants that reach lawyers or clients violate Rule 5.6 and have to come out. Plans cut both ways: an investor that arrives without a performance plan is a 'yellow flag,' and a firm that takes money without its own plan faces 'a tremendous amount of pressure' from equity partners to pay it out in compensation [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:42:49]. On the exit side, the Jon and Marc panelists say less founder reliance earns a higher EBITDA multiple and that a couple of years of track record matters.

Litigation funding has its own mechanics on the same capital map. Bentham's Dave Kirstine sets a $1-1.5M minimum with a $5-15M wheelhouse, wants case value at 8-10x the commitment, and runs intake, NDA, an in-person meeting, a non-binding term sheet within about a week, then binding 30-45 day exclusivity [rQPZlRgHcfE @ 00:11:07]. Much Shelist's Jonathan Friedland lays out the formal market process: rifled rather than shotgun outreach, an anonymized teaser, CA, CIM, term sheet, diligence, then the funding agreement [9teRv9eFpEc @ 00:26:04]. Selectivity is brutal: Hannah Sadler reports about 4 funded per hundreds reviewed, and Statera's Stewart Ackerly prices on a multiple of capital actually drawn, often less than the commitment. Burford's Chris Bogart says Burford funds only 6-8% of the cases it reviews and describes the default waterfall: capital back first, then a time-based multiple starting around 0.5x and rising, then a share of net recovery; on $10 million deployed, Burford looks for '$10 million back and then another $5 million' on the time-based leg [1kAfGIkF10g @ 00:27:53]. Bogart reports mid-20s IRRs, ROIC in the 80s and a per-case loss rate under 10%, with appeal-stage SDNY cases priced cheap and multi-jurisdiction arbitrations priced expensive.

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over what an investor actually buys. Trisha Rich of Holland & Knight calls an MSO 'simply a vendor' and says nobody is buying law firms; the MSO cannot hire or fire lawyers, set settlement parameters, or control client intake [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:03:09]. Law firm owner Joe Prencipe pitches the same vehicle to PE, strategic acquirers, and family offices as the opportunity 'to buy all or part of a law firm,' backed by partners averaging $4M, PI niches running 35% margins, and a roll-up thesis of buying in at 5x EBITDA and expanding into nearby cities [R60PdSP1C24 @ 00:00:28]. The healthcare precedent splits the room too. Tim Mackey of Vista Consulting, sell-side advisor on the biggest public deal, warns that PE in medicine 15 to 20 years ago degraded service and that the fear is 'profits over plaintiffs,' while insisting lawyers retain all legal control and Vista will demand alignment [lgURVgZL9IY @ 00:11:11]. Rich answers that critics cite medical MSOs 'on purpose' as the worst case and that accounting, where deals trade around 15x EBITDA against 4 to 8x for law firms, is the honest analog [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:09:49]. Same legal skeleton, two sales pitches: vendor contract on one side, ownership stake with a growth mandate on the other.

Capital providers split just as hard on whether to lend or to own. Alex Garnier of North Wall Capital wants downside protection: a loan to the law firm, debt repaid before any profit share, diversification, and near-settlement cases to offset duration, and warns that single-case funding tempts firms to run up costs [QpKDhqASBqM @ 00:03:31]. On securitization the same Brown Rudnick panel fractured: Garnier says yes eventually, at hundreds of millions minimum with North Wall taking the junior tranche, while Burford's Leo disagrees, citing disclosure, size, and the point that capital is not the problem [QpKDhqASBqM @ 00:56:17]. Josh Meltzer of Infinitive, who runs an IP Finance group, attacks the comfort of law-firm lending from another angle: PIK interest erodes LTV and over-collateralization every year until the loan is 'Equity like' [l-KmnCtiBMg @ 00:13:12]. David Perla, after about 8 years at Burford, goes further and says litigation finance already 'behaves like an equity investment,' and Burford takes minority, passive law-firm stakes with no mandate to sell and pitches itself as a permanent partner rather than a 3 to 6 year PE seller [CdJKmwCQvOw @ 00:25:26]. Lender, quasi-equity, or permanent partner: that is the real choice buried in the term sheet.

Nobody agrees on how far up-market this goes or who should bother. Lucian Pera calls an MSO 'a tool… not for everyone,' rejects 'management by inflight magazine,' demands a business plan first, and says a bank or 'uncle Jim' may be the easier money [wXttJe-HCVM @ 00:08:47]. Rich runs the same filter from the deal desk: if the capital would just sit in savings, an MSO 'might not be for you,' even though about two-thirds of the roughly 50 live deals on that Holland & Knight team are consumer-facing SSDI and plaintiff PI shops [kF2XWwBObZ8 @ 00:08:10]. On scale, the Jon and Marc panel predicts hourly and Am Law 100 MSOs, then hundreds of deals followed by thousands, and flags that an MSO exit sells a service provider while an ABS exit sells a law firm [U2xFBBUm_IU @ 00:39:41]. David Perla pushes back on the big-firm hype: boutiques are the early movers over 3 to 5 years, big checks need firms with 1 to 5 owners, there is no critical mass of Am Law 100/200 PE deals, and Perla rejects a podcast guest's call for 3 to 5 Am Law 200 equity deals in 2026 backed by sovereign wealth funds [CdJKmwCQvOw @ 00:47:06]. Pera reduces the ABS-versus-MSO fork to a single question: how much control do you want.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is assuming the money locks the lawyers in place. Josh Port of Holland & Knight says lawyers cannot be bound by non-competes, even in an asset sale, the risk cannot be eliminated, and there are 'no silver bullets' [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:06:46]. On the Mattern LLC session, the Holland & Knight workaround stack is rollover equity, equity forfeiture, and 5-year vesting of cash, with a lawyer who quits in year two returning 60%, plus name/image/likeness licenses that keep the founder's brand inside the MSO [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:19:20]. Rich's red-flag list for deal drafts catches the overreach: personal liability for unpaid MSO fees should be restructured as deferred fees, and non-solicitation covenants covering lawyers or clients violate Rule 5.6 [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:42:21]. The fee itself is the second tripwire. No percentage of profits or revenue is allowed; fees must be fixed monthly or cost-plus, typically at 10 to 15% markups, backed by a third-party fairness opinion, and any change should be forward-looking with the fairness analysis refreshed [5TjsKzLtyc4 @ 00:23:21]. Rich says there are no hard and fast rules on cadence, but annual resets seem 'more or less sensible.' Each clause is where a sloppy draft breaks the ethics rules the whole structure leans on.

Control and cash discipline produce the war stories. The Jon and Marc panel points to the UnitedLex bankruptcy, where the court found the MSO exerted improper control, and insists majority and minority rights be contracted up front rather than improvised later [U2xFBBUm_IU @ 00:17:08]. On the Mattern LLC session, Holland & Knight warns that taking the money without a plan invites 'a tremendous amount of pressure' from equity partners to pay it out in compensation, and an investor arriving without a performance plan is a 'yellow flag' [8wwcTCIWOXM @ 00:39:23]. Mass torts carry their own cash-flow problem: the same Jon and Marc panel says the challenge for PE is 'the unpredictability of cash flow,' compounded by fund duration, so far only 'bucket B' deals, PI firms with systematic mass-tort programs, are getting done, with one closing within weeks [U2xFBBUm_IU @ 00:08:35]. Marketing economics can sink the roll-up thesis from below: the panel reports $300-per-click bidding pricing out small firms, cost per case rising, and almost no PI clients still running traditional Google Ads [U2xFBBUm_IU @ 00:38:11]. The squeeze runs both ways, because regional firms also face 'Death Star' capital as Morgan & Morgan and Sweet James enter markets, and the panel calls PE money 'an accelerant' for that pressure.

On the litigation finance side, the failures are pricing and duration errors. Judgment preservation insurance was born in 2021 and gone by 2023, mispriced because appellate risk runs about 50/50; 'So the product is now in my view gone,' says a National Law Review panelist, while Stewart Ackerly of Statera warns post-judgment collection is riskier than litigants assume [9teRv9eFpEc @ 01:01:19]. Josh Meltzer runs the IRR check: making two times your money over more than 10 years is 'less good on an irr basis' [l-KmnCtiBMg @ 00:09:32]. Chris Bogart of Burford flags low-but-positive outcomes, such as a $7M verdict or $11M settlement on a case expected to be worth $100M, as the problem case, and Burford's contracts carry worked payout examples at different outcome levels; Bogart also concedes $5M SME claims are effectively squeezed out [1kAfGIkF10g @ 00:55:54]. Sean Campbell of Valiant, selling 24-month notes at 15% per year secured at roughly 1.5x collateral against post-bellwether mass-tort fees, has not yet found a post-bellwether loss, but says that if one happens it is 'game over,' and investor statements simply rise 'a little over a percent' a month with no mark-to-market [FQLjhMdShl0 @ 00:24:22].
