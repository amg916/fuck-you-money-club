## Executive Summary

Mass-tort practitioners agree that funding has shifted from single-case bets to loan-like portfolio facilities lent to law firms, mostly to buy advertising and work up thousands of claims, with the funder paid from the lawyers' 20 to 40 percent fee rather than the plaintiff's share. Ted Farrell prices the workup at about $3,000 per case, or roughly $30 million for 10,000 clients; Bloomberg puts portfolio pricing around a 20 percent interest rate, and Rocade writes $10 million to $50 million facilities with a $10 million minimum. Funders say they take no control, select only 5 to 10 percent of deals, and underwrite by injury subtype because "Data is everything." Disagreement runs through the mechanics: Bloomberg's sources call mass-tort deals win-or-lose repayment loans, while commercial funders sell non-recourse ratchets of 2x to 3x and a 10x value-to-commitment rule.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Across Bloomberg's reporting and the advisers who structure the deals, the core consensus is that mass-tort money is not the old single-case bet. Bloomberg Television frames the shift plainly: single-case funding was the original model, but mass-tort funding is structured "more like a loan win or lose the investor gets paid," with portfolio funding that hedges risk across thousands of cases [dg6Bt8bmJmQ @ 00:05:24]. Ted Farrell, who founded Litigation Funding Advisors in 2019 after working both as a funding-user lawyer and as an MD at a global legal finance firm, confirms that mass-tort money is mostly loans to law firms, used heavily for advertising [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:07:20]. That advertising spend is measurable: advertisers know how many calls each ad generates, and social media and AI play a growing role in intake [D9nqJXMXzRI @ 00:04:32]. The economic engine underneath is the contingency fee. Lawyers typically earn 20 to 40 percent of settlements or awards but are paid only at settlement or trial, which is the cash gap funders fill. On Camp Lejeune, Bloomberg notes the funder's cut comes out of the lawyers' slice, not the plaintiff's, so the firm, not the client, bears the cost of capital [D9nqJXMXzRI @ 00:06:36].

The second point of agreement is the risk transfer itself. Express Legal Funding calls it "the absolute key": the deal "is almost always non recourse," meaning a third party pays legal bills for a share of the win, and on a loss the funder gets nothing [AVBbpi6lPFY @ 00:01:03]. Dave Kirsty of Arcadia, who came up through Gibson Dunn, Bentham IMF and Validity, adds the control piece: "funders don't take any control over the matter," holding only a right to updates [fTOe5KR6QSE @ 00:04:31]. Casey Gard of GFO Asset Management, with 30-plus years in finance and roughly eight years in litigation finance, says the same from the funder chair: funders cannot dictate strategy, and half his team are legal professionals, including five lawyers [g66VtR5b5gQ @ 00:32:29]. The Oregon Law Lab frames the financial position simply: a funder acts like a contingency attorney financially, and established funders are not gambling because they back "more likely than not" winners [TyGsfcYU1dw @ 00:08:34]. For a mass-tort shop, that means outside money arrives without an outside voice on strategy, as long as the firm clears underwriting first. Gard's own summary of the arrangement is blunt: "It's balancing the playing field."

The third consensus is that funders say no far more often than yes, and the yes depends on data. TownCenter Partners ranks the underwriting criteria as merits first, then the claim holder, then counsel, and reports that only 5 to 10 percent of deals get selected, with funders wanting non-emotional claim holders who will settle and have skin in the game [5XWpCPDxugc @ 00:02:00]. The Oregon Law Lab's number is tighter still: funders take about 5 percent of cases or less, leaving a "huge unmet need" [TyGsfcYU1dw @ 00:05:59]. Adam Rosen, MD of Underwriting at Rocade and a former Ashcraft & Gerel MDL litigator, reduces the mass-tort version to three words: "Data is everything." He wants firms to plan data capture beyond intake, including fee splits, expenses and surgery dates, and to run quality control and SOPs [Zz1HlCrfQPY @ 00:30:27]. TownCenter pitches this selectivity as a benefit: funders' data analysis improves the firm's own case selection, funding increases demand for legal services on both plaintiff and defense sides, and it counters downward pressure on fees because funders have no reason to demand discounted hourly rates [yYpZT2DR1fE @ 00:01:04]. The baseline prerequisite is clean, granular docket data.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start with the cash-burn math, because that is what the loan actually pays for. Farrell itemizes the hard costs per mass-tort case: $400 to $500 in filing fees, medical records orders and nurse review, with full case workup running "thousands" per case [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:08:27]. Scale it and the number gets ugly fast: at $3,000 per case, 10,000 clients means about $30 million, and Farrell paraphrases the casual pitch of "at 3,000 bucks a pop you know just come up with 15 million bucks and you too could you know be doing those cases" [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:10:38]. Camp Lejeune adds a layer, since those claims require an individual administrative process before filing. Gard runs the same arithmetic from the funder side: 500 cases at $10,000 each is $5 million laid out before any money comes back [g66VtR5b5gQ @ 00:22:25]. Rocade's ticket sizes reflect that scale. Rosen describes the core product as bank-like law-firm debt, with facilities typically running $10 million to $50 million, a $10 million minimum, and some deals above $100 million, under a "low volume, high quality" posture [Zz1HlCrfQPY @ 00:07:26]. Firms that show up ready close in four to six weeks; messy firms take months. Farrell's own matchmaking range spans $2 million single cases up to $50 million whole-firm loans.

Pricing splits by structure. On mass-tort portfolio loans, Bloomberg reports returns usually around a 20 percent interest rate [D9nqJXMXzRI @ 00:06:01]. Single-case and commercial deals price off ratchets instead. Kirsty lays out a typical ladder: 2x or 15 percent at one year, 2.5x or 20 percent at two years, and 3x or 25 percent at three years, with an alternative structure of 2x plus 10 percent; the target is for the client to keep 50 to 60 percent or more of the recovery [fTOe5KR6QSE @ 00:10:00]. His worked example: a $10 million budget against a $100 million expected resolution, the client puts in $100,000, the funder takes 3x or $30 million, the firm takes 20 percent or $20 million, and the client keeps $50 million, a "500x" return on its own outlay. That example was built to fit his 10x rule, where case value should run about 10 times the commitment and funders cap at 50 to 70 percent of the budget [fTOe5KR6QSE @ 00:12:59]. Farrell explains why docket structure cuts price: mature, diversified dockets with a track record price as credit-style deals, while single cases can go to "negative eight or negative ten" on summary judgment and even a trade-secret case carries about a 20 percent total-loss risk [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:15:05].

Underwriting a mass-tort docket is granular work. Rosen values every case by injury subtype rather than a settlement average, and in 2020 Rocade modeled three separate 3M grids [Zz1HlCrfQPY @ 00:28:47]. On the buy side, Farrell says a $20 million non-recourse loan against a docket likely returns 40, 60, 80 or 100, calls the asset uncorrelated with markets, and describes the money going in as "very smart" [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:12:37]. James Koutoulas, who runs the Typhon hedge fund and recovered about $7 billion for 48,000 accounts in MF Global and PFG, is building liquidity for that buyer pool through Juris Trade, a secondary market and securitization platform. His PCP bond pays insured principal plus 12 percent simple interest, with a preferred tranche reaching up to 20 percent IRR, and he splits buyers into MOIC investors chasing 2x to 40x and IRR investors [4PHHiyG2S5k @ 00:21:32]. Express Legal Funding's commercial example shows how tiered returns land at exit: a funder commits $3 million to a patent case running four years with appeals, the return climbs to the top 4x tier, and a $25 million settlement pays the funder $12 million while the company keeps $13 million [AVBbpi6lPFY @ 00:03:22].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Control over the case is the sharpest split among the operators. Dave Kirsty of Arcadia, a Gibson Dunn and Bentham IMF veteran, states flatly that “funders don't take any control over the matter,” with the funder holding only a right to updates [fTOe5KR6QSE @ 00:04:31]. Casey Gard of GFO Asset Management backs that line from the mass-tort side, saying funders cannot dictate strategy and pointing out that half of the GFO team are legal professionals, including five lawyers [g66VtR5b5gQ @ 00:32:29]. Express Legal Funding draws a different map: commercial deals worth millions in patent and antitrust cases are where the funder may have a say in strategy, and the cons list explicitly includes possible funder input on settlement decisions alongside complex contracts and disclosure demands [AVBbpi6lPFY @ 00:05:26]. Bloomberg Law frames the same fight as an open market controversy, reporting critics who fear funder control over settlement while funders deny it, and predicting that disclosure regulation is coming to an industry still described as the Wild West [z3Bd4lWO460 @ 00:03:22]. Operators therefore face contradictory testimony on the default: non-recourse capital with zero control on one side, and strategy and settlement input written into commercial contracts on the other.

The second fault line is whether funding manufactures cases. Bloomberg Law reports critics who say mass-tort money leads firms to file claims “without Merit,” set against a market where lawyers collect 20 to 40 percent and MDLs typically settle “sometimes without much judicial oversight” [D9nqJXMXzRI @ 00:07:15]. Bloomberg Television carries the same critique in sharper form, with opponents arguing the capital fuels “unnecessary cases… without a clear injury” while advertisers track exactly how many calls each ad generates [dg6Bt8bmJmQ @ 00:07:15]. Casey Gard answers from the funder chair with a blunt counter: “It has not created a single claim,” framing the money as a way of balancing the playing field, echoing the same panel's point that insurers have always funded the defense [g66VtR5b5gQ @ 00:26:09]. Stephen O'Dowd of Harbour goes further and dismisses the “alarmists” who forecast an explosion of claims, pointing to loser-pays costs as a natural check and noting that in the UK “not a single claim has been certified, which I think is quite telling” after four years of the competition-only regime [22mFLcrQrE8 @ 00:03:58]. O'Dowd's forecast is “measured growth,” while the Bloomberg critics treat MDL consolidation itself as the engine that raises settlements, funds the next wave of cases, and pulls in outside investors.

Structure is the third split, and it changes who eats the loss. Bloomberg Law defines litigation finance as a bet where a losing case leaves the funder “out all their money” [z3Bd4lWO460 @ 00:00:39], but Bloomberg Television reports that mass-tort money has moved to loan-like portfolio funding hedged across thousands of cases, where “win or lose the investor gets paid,” usually at around a 20 percent interest rate [dg6Bt8bmJmQ @ 00:05:24]. Ted Farrell of Litigation Funding Advisors prices the same gap from the buy side: mature, diversified dockets with a track record trade as credit-style deals, while single cases can go to “negative eight or negative ten” on summary judgment and firm loans rarely lose everything [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:15:40]. James Koutoulas of Juris Trade splits the capital itself into two camps, MOIC investors hunting 2x to 40x outcomes versus IRR investors, and builds a PCP bond with insured principal plus 12 percent simple interest and a preferred tranche reaching up to 20 percent IRR for the yield crowd [4PHHiyG2S5k @ 00:31:16]. Single-case commercial deals run on ratchets and multiples; mass-tort dockets run on loan math. Firms choosing a playbook are choosing which investor profile sits across the table.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first trap is the cash hole that opens before a single dollar comes back. Casey Gard walks through the arithmetic: 500 cases at $10,000 each means $5 million laid out up front, and Dodd-Frank-era limits keep banks from lending against contingency assets, which is why firms land at funders' doors [g66VtR5b5gQ @ 00:22:25]. Ted Farrell itemizes the hard costs behind that number, with $400 to $500 filing fees, medical record orders, and nurse review pushing workup into the thousands per case, so that 10,000 clients at $3,000 each becomes roughly $30 million [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:10:38]. Farrell mocks the advertising pitch directly: “at 3,000 bucks a pop you know just come up with 15 million bucks and you too could you know be doing those cases” [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:09:21]. Dave Kirsty names the two internal gaps that turn that burn into a crisis: poor budgeting and no partner-compensation mechanism for cases carried on risk, a problem that bites because funders cap their commitment at 50 to 70 percent of the budget [fTOe5KR6QSE @ 00:14:45]. Camp Lejeune adds a timing drag on top, since every claim requires an individual administrative process before filing, stretching the period in which cash is out and nothing is returning.

Valuation errors are the second recurring loss. Adam Rosen of Rocade, an ex-Ashcraft & Gerel MDL litigator, warns that naive per-plaintiff averaging overvalues “product in place” dockets, and the firms that lean on it keep refinancing instead of getting paid [Zz1HlCrfQPY @ 00:52:13]. Rocade's fix is to value every case by injury subtype rather than a settlement average, which is how the shop modeled three separate 3M grids in 2020, and Rosen's blunt summary is “Data is everything,” meaning fee splits, expenses and surgery dates tracked well beyond intake [Zz1HlCrfQPY @ 00:28:47]. TownCenter Partners breaks personal-injury value into damages, liability and insurance, noting that damages only come clear after maximum medical improvement and that liability is the hardest piece to calculate [1Xs5nnkcuL4 @ 00:01:35]. Dave Kirsty adds the final check: winning is not the same as collecting, and firms must build their case math around collectability, with case value sitting at roughly 10x the commitment so the waterfall still works after the funder's multiple [fTOe5KR6QSE @ 00:15:42]. A docket that looks rich on averages, unvalidated by subtype, MMI status and collection prospects, is the docket that ends in a refinancing loop rather than a payout.

The third category is counterparty and rule-change risk that firms rarely see coming. Ted Farrell warns that some funders are past their fund's investment period and will not admit it, which is why advisers time approaches for moments such as right after a “fund two” closes [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:18:57]. Farrell also flags odd clauses buried in documents that have no standard form, including attorney-in-fact rights triggered on a “material risk of a bankruptcy” [gdDT2WSKYs0 @ 00:20:13]. Adam Rosen tells firms to hire transactional counsel and to raise capital only when reinvestment returns beat its cost, and saves the harshest warning for MSO deals: “You're trading away a ton of upsides for a little upfront cash,” with California cited as a regulatory wipeout risk for those structures [Zz1HlCrfQPY @ 01:24:31]. Rosen also notes that North Carolina's ban blocks some appeal deals outright, so jurisdiction decides which products are even available. Oregon Law Lab adds that a change in law or treaty mid-case can make an investment worthless “or even negative,” the flip side of the hedge-fund pitch that litigation holds value whether the market goes up or down [TyGsfcYU1dw @ 00:04:45].
