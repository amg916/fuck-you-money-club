## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one verdict: in data-center AI silicon, memory capacity and bandwidth price the asset, not headline teraflops. Hitesh C., Screwdriver Science and AI Andrew all show H100-class parts starving at under one percent utilization during decode, while Cloud Ninjas and Supermicro confirm the tradeable unit is an 8-GPU SXM/HGX baseboard with a 1:1 GPU-to-NIC ratio. Street mechanics run from SysTelligence's ipmitool fan override and a 1,000-hour payback on a $250K box, to LOCKBREAK's $8 voltage-glitch board turning a $2,500 CMP 170HX into 80 GB and 85.9 TF. Disagreements are sharp: Cyfuture AI claims the H200 runs up to 45% faster, Gimel12 measured near-parity on Ollama, and AI Andrew plus Tomorrow Engineered argue challenger chips beat GPUs on energy per token.

## Section 1 - The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator on this bench lands in the same place: the scarce resource inside an AI server is memory capacity and bandwidth, not raw flops. Hitesh C. pins the H100 at 80 GB of HBM3 moving 3.35 TB/s with FP8 at 3,958 TF and FP16 at 1,979 TF, then warns that both headline numbers are sparse figures that halve the moment you run dense [fKrvPg4P6KY @ 00:00:28]. Screwdriver Science explains why that matters physically: a memory fetch burns roughly 100 times the energy of a multiply, so the H100 stacks HBM twelve-high at more than 1 TB/s per stack and uses eight stacks to reach about 8 TB/s, all inside a module that pulls about 700 W [b3RlbR2xqfM @ 00:07:32]. AI Andrew runs the roofline that closes the argument: Llama 3 8B in FP16 is 16 GB of weights, one full read at 3.35 TB/s takes 4.8 ms, and because the H100 wants about 295 operations per byte while token-by-token decode delivers about one, utilization sits under one percent [vj8GAlW7mQo @ 00:02:42]. Extrude Studio reaches the same conclusion from the other side, reporting compute idle up to 80% of the time under autoregression on $30,000-plus cards [xkHs2GUaKro @ 00:01:18].

The second agreement is about physical shape, and it dictates what you can actually buy and resell. Scott Flowers is blunt that SXM5 and HGX parts are not discrete cards: "you're going to see a baseboard as opposed to a PCIe card," one board carrying all eight GPUs, with the host's ten PCIe slots allocated as one NIC per GPU plus two for networking [NvI2zdq5I3Q @ 00:02:09]. Supermicro states the same topology as a design rule, putting 400 GB NICs into eight PCIe x16 slots to give "a 1:1 GPU to Nick ratio to avoid Network bottlenecks," then aggregating 32 nodes onto a non-blocking spine-leaf fabric [K7eFRXwd_9U @ 00:00:57]. Murli quantifies why the baseboard exists at all: NVLink moves 900 GB/s while PCIe 5.0 manages 64 GB/s, and ConnectX-7 is what enables GPUDirect RDMA out of the box [2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:02:47]. ServeTheHome shows the generational step in the same slot: B200 HGX runs eight ConnectX-7 at 400 Gb, one per GPU, while B300 moves to ConnectX-8 at 800 Gb per GPU built directly into the baseboard, doubling network throughput and bringing 288 GB of HBM3E per part [CHEbom4opXg @ 00:03:13].

Third, the baseline prerequisite is not the GPU budget, it is power and heat, and most rooms do not have either. Screwdriver Science puts 72 GPUs in a rack at about 120 kW, roughly a hundred homes, and calls a single H100 module "a microwave oven running flat out all day" [b3RlbR2xqfM @ 00:10:20]. Murli does the same math at the chassis level: eight 700 W parts is 5,600 W before overhead, the server exceeds 10,000 W in practice, air-cooled models need inlet air below 30 degrees, and "hot aisle containment is mandatory" because the hot aisle exceeds 70 degrees [2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:07:25]. ServeTheHome walks the plant that answers this, an in-row CDU rated at about 1.8 MW, "enough for more than 10 of these NBL 72 racks," plus rear-door heat exchangers at 50 kW and 80 kW and cooling towers that push water toward ambient rather than refrigerating it [CHEbom4opXg @ 00:12:14], and calls liquid cooling "standard in 18 months" at Lambda's 36 MW Columbus site [N5AJJ0tAoxc @ 00:01:23]. AI Andrew supplies the gap that makes this a sourcing problem: 69% of operators still run racks under 10 kW [vj8GAlW7mQo @ 00:07:38].

## Section 2 - Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start with the spec-and-price ladder the sellers themselves quote. Hitesh C. puts the H200 at 141 GB and 4.8 TB/s with identical compute to the H100 and NVLink at 900, giving a decode ceiling about 1.4x higher, and flags that the widely repeated Llama 2 70B 1.9x result compares batch 32 against batch 8 [fKrvPg4P6KY @ 00:01:18]. Cyfuture AI frames the same part as +76% VRAM and +43% memory speed inside the same 700 W envelope, claims up to 45% faster LLM inference and 1.7x in multi-GPU, then prices it at 20-25% more than an H100 with cloud rental starting around $3.80 per hour and the pitch that "total cost per workload can actually be lower" [8nL31VyVz8s @ 00:01:00]. Design Guy scores B200 over H200 six rounds to two: 180 GB against 141 GB, 7.7 TB/s against 4.8, FP8 at 9 PF sparse against 3,958 TF, but 1,000 W against 700 W and FP64 falling to 37 TF from 67 TF [PnUJffgghas @ 00:05:35]. Gimel12 supplies the street price floor, calling H100 and H200 "30 or 30 something" thousand and hard to find, against an A6000 at roughly $7-8K delivering 17.5 tok/s [gD-lTOjHc04 @ 00:06:54].

The most aggressive mechanic in this chapter is LOCKBREAK's account of Kate turning mining silicon into A100-class compute. The CMP 170HX carries the same GA100 die as a $20,000-plus A100; GA100 has 128 SMs and retail A100 enables 108, while the mining SKU launched in 2021 at $4,299 crippled to 70 SMs, 8 GB usable memory, PCIe Gen1, no NVLink and a 1/32 math rate enforced by 16,384 eFuses, even though about 80 GB of HBM2E is physically present [k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:01:25]. Munikote's CMP Unlocker, following Pry's "A Canary in the Crypto Mine" paper, exposed 64 GB, restored math and doubled link speed, pushing card prices from a couple hundred dollars to over $1,000, but it could not touch fuses [k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:03:26]. The hardware path is voltage glitching with an roughly $8 microcontroller board that controls the VRM and dips power during the fuse read to disable write-protect, after which new fuse records clear the disable masks while leaving genuinely defective cores off, taking one hour to two days per card [k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:04:23]. Nine of ten cards hit 80 GB with 120-126 SMs [k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:05:44].

On the operations side the tooling is specific and short. Murli overrides thermals with `ipmitool raw 0x30 0x45 0x01 0x01` to pin fans at full speed for training runs [2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:08:08], caps draw with `nvidia-smi -p`, plans electrical for 120% of nameplate load [2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:09:34], keeps IPMI on a separate out-of-band VLAN because "mixing IPMI management traffic with GPU workload traffic causes serious performance issues," holds GPUs below 83 degrees and HBM below 95, treats memory use above 90% as near capacity, and keeps 200 Gb InfiniBand off the management switch [2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:05:17]. Craft Computing's version of the same discipline is version-pinning: Proxmox 8 with GRID 17.6, because 19 drops V100 and 18 is unstable, and 17.6 demands kernel 6.8 or lower, which rules out Proxmox 9, on an Inspur DGX V100 that idles near 950 W and hits 3,300 W loaded with GRID entry licensing at $3,000 or $10,000 [9c1c3JPZ2ps @ 00:05:19]. Scott Flowers closes the resale loop: an order for 1,000 units is "very difficult," they filled 128 with painful OEM matching, and held value beats their ROI models [NvI2zdq5I3Q @ 00:05:08].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Fix the memory budget before shopping for compute
   - Compute weights plus KV cache for the target model, then select the part by capacity and bandwidth rather than flops.
   - A 70B model needs roughly 140 GB, which is why H100 at 80 GB forces sharding while H200 at 141 GB and 4.8 TB/s or B200 at 192 GB do not [SysTelligence — 2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:04:12]; FP16 at that size wants about 132 GB, so Q4 quantization drops the same model to roughly 43 GB and changes the whole hardware decision [Gimel12 — gD-lTOjHc04 @ 00:01:29].
2. Buy the system as a baseboard plus fabric, never as loose parts
   - Specify an 8-GPU SXM/HGX baseboard with one NIC per GPU and confirm slot count before the purchase order.
   - Expect ten PCIe slots allocated as one NIC per GPU plus two for networking on a single eight-GPU baseboard [Cloud Ninjas — NvI2zdq5I3Q @ 00:03:25], matched to eight 400 GB NICs in eight x16 slots for a 1:1 GPU-to-NIC ratio and 32-node non-blocking spine-leaf aggregation [Supermicro — K7eFRXwd_9U @ 00:00:57].
3. Prove the power and cooling envelope on paper first
   - Add up PSU capacity, inlet temperature limits and containment, then size the plant for the real rack draw.
   - Plan on 4,000 to 6,000 W power supplies, inlet air below 30 degrees for air-cooled units, and a chassis that exceeds 10,000 W once eight 700 W parts and fans are running [SysTelligence — 2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:02:05]; at rack scale that becomes six 5,250 W titanium supplies per server and 130 kg of weight inside 1.6 MW UPS blocks [ServeTheHome — N5AJJ0tAoxc @ 00:08:31].
4. Run the buy-versus-rent arithmetic both ways before committing
   - Price the box against hourly rental at realistic utilization, then sanity-check against resale behaviour.
   - A roughly $250K server against $240 per hour of equivalent rental pays back in about 1,000 hours, while the same operator states typical enterprise break-even runs 12 to 18 months [SysTelligence — 2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:13:01]; three-year-old H100s and H200s are 'still monsters' whose held value beats the ROI models, with H200s scarce because hyperscalers are holding them [Cloud Ninjas — NvI2zdq5I3Q @ 00:06:10].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split in Hopper buying is how much the H200 actually buys you. Cyfuture AI pitches the card as up to 45% faster on LLM inference and 1.7x in multi-GPU runs inside the same 700 W envelope. It argues that a 20–25% price premium and cloud entry near $3.80/hr still produce a lower cost per workload [Cyfuture AI — 8nL31VyVz8s @ 00:00:40]. Gimel12 ran Llama 3.3 70B at Q4 on Ollama with Open WebUI and got 36.6 tok/s on the H100, with the H200 landing in the same band. Both cards pulled about 600 W at 87–90% utilization. The speaker's verdict was 'not a big difference,' which they blamed on the Ollama inference server; they called the result inaccurate and promised a vLLM rerun [Gimel12 — gD-lTOjHc04 @ 00:03:51]. Hitesh C. sits between the two. Compute is the same, but memory goes from 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s to 141 GB at 4.8 TB/s, so the decode ceiling rises about 1.4x. Nvidia's 1.9x Llama 2 70B headline compares batch 32 against batch 8 [Hitesh C. — fKrvPg4P6KY @ 00:01:18]. The working read: the H200 premium pays off when the extra memory lets you raise batch size, not when a quantized model already fits in 80 GB.

On the Hopper-versus-Blackwell call, Design Guy scores the B200 a 6–2 winner. It has 180 GB versus 141 GB, 7.7 TB/s versus 4.8 TB/s, 9 PF sparse FP8 versus 3,958 TF, and 1.8 TB/s NVLink versus 900 GB/s. The H200 keeps two rounds: FP64, at 67 TF against 37 TF, and power, at 700 W against 1,000 W TDP. That keeps FP64 shops and tight power envelopes on the H200 [Design Guy — PnUJffgghas @ 00:05:35]. Hitesh C. frames the same fork as a workload rule. If KV cache is what caps batch size, buy H200; if you need FP4 or faster NVLink, buy Blackwell. B200 figures are Nvidia projections, and sparse numbers are double the dense ones [Hitesh C. — fKrvPg4P6KY @ 00:03:01]. Gimel12 takes the budget road entirely. The A6000 clocked 17.5 tok/s at roughly $7–8K, against H100 or H200 cards at '30 or 30 something' thousand that are hard to find. ## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The hardware-unlock trade is the sharpest example of one-way risk. LOCKBREAK walks through Kate's voltage-glitch workflow on CMP 170HX mining cards. An ~$8 microcontroller board dips VRM power during the fuse read to disable write-protect. New fuse records then clear the disable masks while truly defective cores stay off, at one hour to two days per card [LOCKBREAK — k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:04:23]. The traps are spelled out on camera. Fuse writes are permanent, and 'No driver update can unblow a fuse.' Results are a lottery: 9 of 10 cards reached 80 GB with 120–126 SMs, but one topped out at 64 GB. The 85.9 TF headline comes from a single non-tensor OpenCL FP16 test, sits only about 10% above the 78 TF spec, and ECC and MIG were never tested [LOCKBREAK — k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:07:03]. The margin also compresses fast. The software unlock alone pushed cards from a couple hundred dollars to over $1,000, and the 10 GB card now sits near $2,500 with the 8 GB near $3,000 [LOCKBREAK — k-M24u2WcZw @ 00:09:11]. Cloud Ninjas applies the same irreversibility logic to HGX servers. They refuse to pull baseboards because only a few removals are safe, and they are holding off on an install walkthrough until they have a failed unit [Cloud Ninjas — NvI2zdq5I3Q @ 00:00:32].

Hosting traps hit before any revenue lands. Craft Computing's eight-V100 DGX cloud-gaming build is pinned to Proxmox 8 and GRID 17.6. Version 19 dropped V100, version 18 is unstable, and 17.6 needs kernel 6.8 or older, which locks out Proxmox 9. The vGPU unlock only spans Maxwell through Turing/Volta [Craft Computing — 9c1c3JPZ2ps @ 00:05:19]. Once running, GRID caps output at 60 FPS and VRAM splits statically. VMs cost about 10%, plus 5% for GRID. The box idles near 950 W and hits 3,300 W at full load. The verdict was disappointing: roughly a GTX 1070 per gamer. GRID entry costs $3,000 for an A5000 or $10,000 for a Blackwell 6000 Pro, while Intel's B70 Pro offers SR-IOV with no licensing [Craft Computing — 9c1c3JPZ2ps @ 00:17:04]. SysTelligence lists the data-center failure points. Mixing IPMI management traffic with GPU workload traffic 'causes serious performance issues.' GPUs must stay under 83°C and HBM under 95°C. Hot aisles exceed 70°C, so containment is mandatory, and power should be planned at 120% of load [SysTelligence — 2tGJAc9AgQI @ 00:05:17]. ServeTheHome adds that a 1.8 MW in-row CDU needs its pH, inlet and outlet temperatures, and pump speeds watched [ServeTheHome — CHEbom4opXg @ 00:12:14].

Supply and concentration risk is the economics check most buyers skip. Cloud Ninjas trades used H100 through B300 servers. They report that H200s are scarce because hyperscalers are holding them, while H100s only trickle out. An order for 1,000 is 'very difficult,' and even a filled order of 128 was hard because OEM and configuration had to match [Cloud Ninjas — NvI2zdq5I3Q @ 00:05:08]. AI Andrew flags HBM as the choke point: it is sold out through 2026, and the squeeze is expected to run to late 2027. He also notes that 69% of operators run racks under 10 kW, while an NVL72 costs about $3M and draws about 120 kW [AI Andrew — vj8GAlW7mQo @ 00:07:38]. Tomorrow Engineered stacks up the Cerebras warnings. Memory has been stuck at 44 GB since 2021. DeepSeek V4 Pro needs about 40 systems, over $20M and 1 MW. The prefill/decode hardware ratio is fixed at order time. UAE customers made up 86% of 2025 revenue [Tomorrow Engineered — yYBoW-BKDl8 @ 00:19:11]. CyberPulse shows what a stack miss costs. Gaudi 3 missed $500M, Intel posted a $16.6B quarterly loss and an $18.8B annual loss, Intel was dropped from the Dow, and Falcon Shores was cancelled [CyberPulse — Z69EERVnY6w @ 00:08:39].
