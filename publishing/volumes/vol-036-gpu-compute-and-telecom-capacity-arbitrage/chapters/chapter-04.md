## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who priced real capacity lands on the same axis: power, not space. IPTP Networks calls power usually the largest variable on a colocation quote, billed either as a flat allocation of reserved capacity or by the meter on actual consumption, with redundant power adding roughly 15 to 20 percent; they flag that a standard server draws under half a kilowatt while a GPU server pulls 5 to 15 [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:01:17]. Alec Glover, who ran a thousand GPUs, explains why that hurts: colos bill on power provisioned, so a GPU box needing a full 6 kW circuit pays for the whole circuit monthly whether it runs at 20 or 30 percent, and only larger deals unlock fixed price plus energy [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:01:30]. Colovore frames density as the second lever: an operator stuck in a decade-old facility capped at 4 kW per rack needs 10 racks and then 20 after growth, while a 20 kW-per-rack colo lets the same load fit in four 10 kW cabinets and grow vertically inside them [JMz8luHLVxc @ 00:00:20]. The shared baseline is simple: model kilowatts first, then rack units, because rack units alone will not explain why two quotes for the same footprint diverge.

Redundancy is the second point of agreement, and the vocabulary is the same whether the building is a Tier III colo or a warehouse. Alec Glover defines it as buying more units than you need, notated N+1 or N+2, and applies it bluntly to cooling: if the math says one air conditioner, you still buy two, because a single failure puts your SLAs at risk [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:07:34]. MEP BOOSTER gives the same arithmetic for Tier III design: if the load needs three units, install four, so one can fail while the remaining three carry full load, with dual power and cooling paths letting UPS maintenance, generator testing, or pump replacement happen while IT load keeps running [O0T9qQ1TuGU @ 00:15:24]. Gareth Knight pins Tier III at 99.982 percent uptime, under 1.6 hours of downtime a year, and positions it as the cost-versus-availability sweet spot below Tier IV [mNQb5B_voNw @ 00:03:51]. Data Center News adds the operator caveat everyone should internalize: simply building a Tier IV facility does not guarantee Tier IV uptime, because the site has to be consistently maintained and monitored, from temperature and humidity to power usage [8DzqbL0HKG0 @ 00:02:53]. Consensus: buy redundancy where a single failure kills customer uptime, and back it with monitoring.

The third shared conclusion is that connectivity and carrier presence decide whether a site is usable for paying customers. IPTP Networks notes that location and carrier ecosystem never appear in the rack rate, yet they determine what you can connect to and at what cost, from the number of ISPs and on-site internet exchanges to cloud on-ramps reached through cross-connects [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:00:41]. Alec Glover treats the network as the next most important component after power: the building must support dedicated internet plans that let you run your own BGP router, and their warehouse ran a 10 Gbit primary backed by a 1 Gbit link, carrying about 100 servers per 10 gig [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:05:05]. Techno Tim saw the upside of carrier density firsthand at a Cologix site in downtown Minneapolis, where sub-millisecond pings came back over a site-to-site VPN because the home ISP also sat in that data center a few miles away [zbkqRPIUYAo @ 00:05:49]. Deft's team, touring with Lawrence Systems, warns that carriers sometimes run through the same manhole or conduit, so they build redundant fiber sides and route customers over diverse carriers so a single cut cannot take out both paths [gsN_CJJDy_o @ 00:12:21].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Start with the unit economics the operators actually paid. Alec Glover ran a thousand GPUs in a warehouse for almost three years at about $25,000 a month, and estimates a typical Tier III facility would have run three to five times that, a gap they say saved millions [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:00:00]. The math behind it: colocation has a hard time pricing below $150 per kilowatt per month, and Glover argues it has only gotten more expensive, while every DIY model they ran came in at $50 per kilowatt per month or less, because power that was not used was not paid for [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:01:49]. On top of power, IPTP Networks says connectivity is where colo quotes diverge most: every link to another provider or partner needs its own cross-connect, carrying an installation fee of typically $500 to $1,500 plus a monthly charge of around $100 to $300, so four cross-connects mean $2,000 to $6,000 upfront alone [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:02:34]. The last recurring line is remote hands, the facility engineers doing reboots, drive swaps, and cable checks on your behalf, typically billed at $150 to $350 an hour with a one-hour minimum [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:02:56]. Budget every one of these lines before comparing any warehouse lease.

For small footprints, the street move is to stop buying retail space and share a rack. Techno Tim called several data centers before colocating a homelab: one quoted half a cabinet, 24U, with 50 Mbps burstable to a gigabit, two switch ports, and 16 IPs for $900 a month plus a $4,500 one-time fee, and another offered 4U with 50 Mbps burst to 1 gigabit and one public IP for about $350 a month [zbkqRPIUYAo @ 00:00:50]. The workable option came from Will, who runs Plover Digital and rented a whole rack with friends to split the cost, which kept dropping as more people joined; Tim recommends that route outright [zbkqRPIUYAo @ 00:01:29]. IPTP Networks explains the structure behind it: top-tier facilities rarely sell less than a full rack, so 1U and partial cabinets typically come through a reseller or managed hosting provider rather than the data center itself [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:00:56]. Some operators do slice inventory directly: Deft, on the Lawrence Systems tour, offers half cabinets and quarter-cabinet compartments to put two customers in the same physical space, secured with customer-changeable combination locks and optional fingerprint or card readers at the cabinet level [gsN_CJJDy_o @ 00:21:37]. The more tenants on the rack, the lower each share, which is the whole play.

Network plumbing is where operators cut the most cost with config rather than cash. Alec Glover first NATed public IPs to private addresses on machines, then switched to assigning public IPs directly on each server's interface, which made wiping a box, re-IPing it, and handing it to the next customer fast [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:06:26]. When a building cannot get BGP, Null0 Labs builds a plain GRE tunnel from the basement router to a router racked in a provider's data center, skipping IPSec because the tunnel only carries public transit, then splits the lent /24 into a /25 for the DC LAN and a /30 for the tunnel interfaces [Pr9psxw4Z_o @ 00:17:32]. The session starts on private ASN 64592; the upstream, running public AS900, strips the private ASN, sends only a default route, and filters inbound to the lent /24, so nobody on the internet sees the private ASN [Pr9psxw4Z_o @ 00:31:22]. Owning your own ASN is not stifling: Jeremy Cioara shows ARIN's autonomous system number at a $550 initial fee and $150 a year to maintain, while IPv4 blocks now come from a carrier, not ARIN, with authorization to advertise them over BGP [5WW8432TcR4 @ 00:03:15]. Underlay ISPs then become interchangeable plumbing.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock a BGP-capable underlay
   - Secure dedicated internet plans at the building with a primary and a backup link.
   - Step one is finding a provider that offers BGP and dedicated internet at the bandwidth you need; Glover ran a 10 Gbit primary backed by a 1 Gbit link and carried about 100 servers on 10 gig [Alec Glover — TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:05:52]
2. Build the GRE tunnel to a transit-rich rack
   - Remove the default route, pin a static host route to the remote tunnel endpoint via the ISP, and bring up the tunnel.
   - Delete the default route, add a static route to the remote router's public IP out of the ISP interface, then set tunnel source G1, the remote destination, and path MTU discovery; no IPSec is needed for public transit [Null0 Labs — Pr9psxw4Z_o @ 00:14:32]
3. Peer on a private ASN with a pull-up route
   - Run iBGP over the tunnel on a private ASN and nail the announced block to Null0.
   - Configure router BGP 64592 with update-source tunnel 0, announce the /24, and add a static pull-up route to null 0 so the prefix appears in the BGP table; set next-hop-self toward the basement router [Null0 Labs — Pr9psxw4Z_o @ 00:26:29]
4. Cut over to a public ASN and your own block
   - Re-peer with the upstream on your public ASN and have them update prefix filters.
   - After verifying block ownership with the upstream, the transit provider adds your new /22 to their inbound prefix list and outbound route map so it is announced to the internet instead of being filtered [Null0 Labs — Pr9psxw4Z_o @ 00:53:01]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Alec Glover and the colocation sellers split hardest on who should own the building. Glover ran a thousand GPUs in a warehouse for almost three years at $25,000 a month and says a typical tier three data center would have cost three to five times as much [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:00:00]. His math: colos bill the full provisioned circuit, so a GPU server on a 6 kW circuit pays for all 6 kW whether it runs at 20% or 30%, and he says nobody will quote under $150 per kilowatt per month, while his own models landed at $50 or less because idle power was not billed [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:01:30]. IPTP Networks frames the same lever as a contract choice rather than a reason to leave: power can be a flat reserved allocation or metered on actual consumption, redundant power adds roughly 15 to 20%, and a GPU server draws 5 to 15 kW against under half a kilowatt for a standard box [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:01:17]. Colovore argues the opposite fix, density: a legacy colo capping Don at 4 kW per rack forces ten racks, while Marty in a 20 kW-per-rack hall consolidates into four cabinets and grows vertically instead of buying more floor space [JMz8luHLVxc @ 00:00:20].

How much redundancy to buy is the second fault line. Glover argues the engineering in tier-rated buildings serves a valid purpose, but good enough is good enough and the savings from skipping functions can outweigh the drawbacks, then admits his own crew took it too far [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:00:27]. Gareth Knight pitches tier three as the sweet spot: N+1 on every critical component, concurrent maintainability, and 99.982% uptime, which he translates to less than 1.6 hours of downtime per year at reasonable cost compared with tier four [mNQb5B_voNw @ 00:03:51]. PCweb pushes the floor lower for small players, saying not every company needs tier four and that startups or small businesses can live with tier one or two, while enterprises where every second counts are stuck with three or four [0acbHqkQe80 @ 00:01:26]. Data Center News throws cold water on paying for the certificate alone: simply building a tier four facility does not guarantee tier four uptime, and without comprehensive monitoring even a tier four hall can go down unexpectedly, like a car with no dashboard [8DzqbL0HKG0 @ 00:02:53]. The split is plain: Glover buys redundancy only where customers feel it, the tier camp buys it as insurance, and the monitoring camp says the label is worthless without live telemetry.

On getting public IPs and routing into a GPU room, four playbooks compete. Glover went direct: buy dedicated internet plans that let you run your own BGP router, a 10 Gbit link backed by a 1 Gbit link, roughly 100 servers on the 10 gig, and his own public IPs that he eventually bound directly to server interfaces instead of NATing, so boxes could be wiped and re-handed to new customers [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:05:05]. Null0 Labs argues you do not need BGP at the building at all: run a plain GRE tunnel over a commodity ISP to a router in someone else's rack with real transit, peer with a private ASN that the upstream strips, and skip IPSec because the traffic is public anyway [Pr9psxw4Z_o @ 00:02:11]. Jeremy Cioara warns IPv4 no longer comes from ARIN, only from a carrier who authorizes you to advertise it, while an ASN runs $550 up front and $150 a year in maintenance [5WW8432TcR4 @ 00:03:15]. FEPLabs Radio shows the lightest option for ham operators stuck behind CGNAT on Starlink or a Verizon hotspot: a WireGuard tunnel to 44Net that lands a public 44.x address with no port forwards [ou57Rm_y3fs @ 00:08:04].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The loudest firsthand failure comes from Glover, who says the warehouse model was right but his execution was wrong: they cut so deep that scaling and growing the business became very difficult, and the build set the business back even while saving money [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:00:43]. A second utility feed from another substation was never an option because only one utility company served the area, so the failover layer big colos rely on simply did not exist [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:14:42]. Without generators, the only answer to customers during an outage is that power returns when the utility turns it back on, and cloud buyers already assume you sit in a real data center with backup power [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:13:34]. Lawrence Systems' Deft tour shows backup power failing even when you own it: generators run 500 kW to 3 MW each and must reach speed in about 16 seconds, but if the first unit latches onto the paralleling bus with a bad sine wave, the others refuse to sync and a site can end up with one of seven generators trying to carry the load [gsN_CJJDy_o @ 00:07:08].

Environmental shortcuts kill hardware quietly. Glover warns that running stock air conditioners strips moisture, and if humidity lands near 5% you risk a static shock that destroys a ton of hardware, so non-data-center units need bolt-on humidifiers and dehumidifiers [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:09:43]. His sharper warning is on free cooling: hardware coming out of old crypto-mining sites is absolutely destroyed after three years from wet, dirty air, with corrosion and salt buildup, so accelerated depreciation and lost resale value can outrun what skipping air conditioning saved [TZp1yBea0o4 @ 00:10:48]. Cakka | Cybersecurity flags the electrical version of the same trap at GPU densities: a 120 kW rack at 480 V three-phase pulls 152 amps per phase versus 38 for a 30 kW rack, cable bundles choke trays and airflow, and a rack loading one phase heavily creates harmonic distortion and dangerous overheating at the upstream transformer [psGPevLhvpI @ 00:05:22]. Colovore shows the slow-motion version: Don, capped at 4 kW per rack, refreshes into power-hungry blade servers, fits even fewer per cabinet, and sprawls from ten to twenty cabinets that now all need managing [JMz8luHLVxc @ 00:01:15].

The recurring bill is where colo budgets leak. IPTP Networks puts every cross-connect at a $500 to $1,500 install plus $100 to $300 a month, so four cross-connects mean $2,000 to $6,000 upfront, and remote hands for reboots, drive swaps, and cable checks runs $150 to $350 an hour, usually with a one-hour minimum [3EnjHW-36GI @ 00:02:34]. Lawrence Systems' tour shows why on-site spares matter: crews drive two or three hours, discover the DAC cable or rack kit never shipped with a $40,000 storage array, and an 800 GB versus 960 GB SSD mismatch decides whether you have a good day or a bad one [gsN_CJJDy_o @ 00:24:01]. Techno Tim's countermeasure is to standardize every server's components and document serial numbers and disk positions so parts can be ordered before the drive downtown [zbkqRPIUYAo @ 00:02:20]. Beam Networks reports a software-side trap in a six-server colo rack: the Ceph cluster on shared 10 gig is extremely slow, adding disks slows the whole cluster for a while, RAM is at capacity, and storage sits at 66% with a warning, so Ceph is slated to be ripped out [4NmQ_0-0BC8 @ 00:04:21].
