## 1. The Practitioner Consensus: Rented VRAM Replaced Hashrate

The operators who lived through the Ethereum era agree the hashing business is finished and rented VRAM is the replacement. Red Panda Mining puts it without hedging: 'GPU mining is dead, and it's not profitable at all, especially at any residential electric.' The same operator has redirected cards, servers and traded-in ASICs into rental fleets [mfQE_CptyLA @ 00:04:26]. Modern Mining reaches the same conclusion from a single-card consumer build. A long parts-and-profit walkthrough ends with 'Do not buy crypto miners,' and the channel runs eight rigs on rental software instead [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:42:42]. Mining-native channels quietly concede the point. Honey With Crypto frames a 5 to 6 lakh rupee rig as dead capital if its coin collapses and names AI GPU renting as the escape hatch [Wp6L77ZvZw8 @ 00:00:22]. Crypto Miners Rigs, running about 50 miners, adds a hard rule: if revenue minus power doesn't leave a cushion, don't buy the cards for mining alone. The channel also notes that GPUs, unlike ASICs, keep their resale value [1q_WZwOjByQ @ 00:07:22]. That resale angle is what makes the pivot workable. Silicon that stopped paying on hashrate can be relisted to renters who pay by the hour for memory and compute.

The second point of agreement is that renters pick machines on infrastructure, not just on the GPU model. Red Panda Mining runs its rental boxes on fiber internet with a static IP and reports Vast machines getting verified within one to two days on that setup. The channel is also fitting a 4 TB Samsung NVMe to the V100 server [ET1JYocpu0M @ 00:08:32]. Modern Mining sets the connectivity floor at 500 Mb, runs eight rigs on a 1 Gb symmetric line, and stresses that upload is the number that matters [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:10:01]. BearMarketMiner measured the memory effect directly on OctaSpace. A 26 GB rig landed 7 rentals in roughly 30 days, while a 63 GB rig landed 82. Both test builds carried 64 GB of system RAM, paired with a 1 TB disk and at least 100/100 internet as the OctaSpace baseline [egH5hKD913Y @ 00:10:53]. At warehouse scale Alec Glover applies the same thinking to storage. Every node gets four U.2 drives through an SFF-8654 backplane, treated as scratch space or RAID0 rather than primary storage [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:18:33]. The shared lesson is that a fast card stuck behind thin RAM, slow disks or weak upload simply sits unrented.

Power discipline is the third shared baseline, and the mining and data-center camps use the same number. Alec Glover runs all power infrastructure at a consistent 80% steady-state load. A 4090 server is sized at about 4.6 kW (GPU TDP plus 1,000 W) on four 1,600 W supplies, and 5090 servers move to five supplies with one PDU each [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:27:42]. Crypto Miners Rigs applies the same 80% rule to an open-frame rig. Count cards at their planned power limit plus about 150 W for the platform, and keep continuous load near 80% of the PSU rating. A 15 A/120 V circuit supports only about 12 A, or 1,440 W, so bigger rigs need 20 A or 240 V [1q_WZwOjByQ @ 00:03:38]. Red Panda Mining builds in headroom the same way, planning only six to eight of ten servers on a rack fed by three 30 A/240 V circuits [mfQE_CptyLA @ 00:15:12]. Heat is the other half of the equation. Modern Mining exhausts rig heat through a window with a 50 W fan. Dumping it indoors roughly doubles the power cost, though it offsets heating in winter [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:10:33]. Nobody treats electrical and thermal planning as optional once multiple cards run around the clock.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Modern Mining's single-card build is the cleanest consumer template for Salad. The parts list: a used ASUS TUF 4090 at $2,100, a no-name 1000 W PSU with a native 12V 600 W cable for about $100, an $80 MSI B550 board, a $90 Ryzen 5500, a $15 frame, a $10 Govee smart plug, 64 GB of used eBay DDR4 for $320, a $20 Peerless Assassin cooler and a 1 TB NVMe [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:02:05]. The unit economics are spelled out step by step. Eighteen cents an hour, which Modern Mining calls the cap, equals $4.32 a day. Salad's 10% cash-out fee and a 40-cent deduction cut that to $3.88. Roughly 0.4 kW of draw over 24 hours costs $0.96, leaving $2.92 a day. That is about $854 a year at 80% uptime and a 3.5-year breakeven, before counting 80-90% card resale [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:37:51]. BearMarketMiner lists Salad's entry bar as 32 GB RAM, 500 GB storage and about 50 Mb each way. A 5090 prices around 0.237 there, and a 4060 Ti around 6 cents, averaging 7 to 13 cents [egH5hKD913Y @ 00:12:53]. The tuning trick: locking the 7950X at 1600 MHz dropped system draw from 600 W to 318 W. Power-limiting below 90% on Salad, though, gets the rig degraded [egH5hKD913Y @ 00:21:18].

Red Panda Mining runs the opposite playbook: cheap ex-enterprise servers packed with older high-VRAM cards. The core buy was a pallet of ten Gigabyte G481-HA0 servers at $500 each plus $600 freight. The upgrades were Xeon Gold 6152s at about $350 for 22, plus 32 sticks of 16 GB DDR4-2400 ECC RDIMM at $40-50 apiece [mfQE_CptyLA @ 00:12:51]. Into the Gigabyte chassis go eight passively cooled Tesla V100 16 GB cards at about CA$400-420 each. They rent for 8-20 cents an hour, against 8-15 cents for a 5060 Ti that costs nearly CA$1,000 after tax. The V100 buys similar rent at half the price, with a projected $25-40 a day and a 4-6 month ROI [ET1JYocpu0M @ 00:07:02]. The 170HX server is the payoff: ten cards unlocked to 64 GB each for 640 GB of VRAM, rented at 20 cents per GPU per hour. It earned $30.42 for 9 GPUs over 19 hours and $36.37 over about 24 hours. After SimplePod's 20% fee, 20 cents nets about 16 cents, or $38.40 per full day, on cards bought for $150-170 [SJbOF9Ufm0Q @ 00:08:10]. Alec Glover maps the sales channels above that. Vast, RunPod and ShadeForm take about 25%, while resellers such as HydroHost, ShadeForm and FluidStack take 7.5-15% on one-to-two-year contracts [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:17:08].

At warehouse scale, Alec Glover's hardware rules come from weeks lost to unstable risers and adapters. Buy ASRock boards with native SlimSAS (19 ports, eight GPUs at x16, a 14-GPU limit), or MCIO for Gen5. Cheap slot-to-SlimSAS adapters threw AER errors and driver crashes after weeks, and 10GTech Gen5 risers failed within 30 minutes. Only C-Payne risers, at over $100 per GPU, and One Chassis assemblies, at about $80, stayed stable [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:13:22]. Gen4 moves 2 GB/s per lane against Gen5's 4 GB/s, about 24 versus 48 GB/s at x16 in practice. Even so, dropping Gen5 cards into Gen4/DDR4 systems saved roughly $10K per server, and the 5090 inference customer didn't care [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:05:12]. Red Panda Mining draws the line lower down: no 4090s on Gen3, which the channel calls a waste. Its 8x4090 plan is therefore a 7U PCIe 5.0 MCIO build rather than a reuse of the Gen3 Octominer box [X2uIX3DDPu0 @ 00:07:29]. Crypto LLC adds the riser diagnosis from mining farms. When new cards crash Windows 11 and System interrupts sit at 7-10%, the culprit is 'most of the time' a riser, even though BIOS shows every PCIe port green. Swapping the bad riser dropped interrupts to 0-1% [nHwXk9HCuYI @ 00:04:18].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Spec the box for what renters filter on
   - Lock in upload bandwidth, system RAM and NVMe storage before choosing the card count.
   - Run at least 500 Mb, ideally 1 Gb symmetric, because upload matters [Modern Mining — WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:10:01]. Carry high system RAM: a 63 GB rig got 82 rentals versus 7 at 26 GB [BearMarketMiner — egH5hKD913Y @ 00:10:53].
2. Configure BIOS and remote access for unattended uptime
   - Set BIOS for automatic recovery and install remote and update-blocking tools.
   - Turn XMP off, set restore after AC power loss to on, and enable SVM. Then install Stop Updates 10, Chrome Remote Desktop and Nvidia drivers [Modern Mining — WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:23:42].
3. Burn in and hunt down riser faults before listing
   - Stress-test every GPU for days and watch for throttling or interrupt spikes.
   - Run the C-Payne stress tool for 48 hours to a week and flag drops like a 4090 falling from 450 W to 230 W [Alec Glover — CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:46:26]. On Windows, System interrupts at 7-10% point to a bad riser [Crypto LLC — nHwXk9HCuYI @ 00:01:27].
4. List low, get rented, and clear verification
   - Undercut comparable listings to win first rentals, then relist if the machine stalls unverified.
   - Red Panda priced a 3080 rig lowest among eight-GPU rigs on Vast and it rented instantly [Red Panda Mining — ET1JYocpu0M @ 00:12:03]. For unverified listings, unlist for 5-10 minutes and relist [Red Panda Mining — mfQE_CptyLA @ 00:07:30].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Alec Glover and Red Panda Mining run opposite hardware playbooks. Glover's rule for any rental business is to buy the newest generation: it costs a premium, depreciates in a friendlier way, and stays rentable longer, while low-end stock is what sits idle when the market slows [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:01:08]. Looking back on a warehouse cloud scaled to 1,000 GPUs, Glover says the move now would be decommissioned H100s and H200s in a DIY facility that can be certified [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:11:02]. Red Panda Mining goes the other way on purpose. They bought a pallet of ten Gigabyte G481-HA0 servers at $500 each, run PCIe 3.0 boards, and openly flag that this gear may command lower rates than AMD Epyc systems [mfQE_CptyLA @ 00:12:51]. Their V100 buy follows the same logic: Tesla V100 16 GB cards at roughly CA$400 to 420 each rent for 8 to 20 cents an hour, against 8 to 15 cents for a 5060 Ti that costs about CA$800 before tax, so the older card earns similar rent for half the capital [ET1JYocpu0M @ 00:07:02]. Glover is optimizing for durability and certification; Red Panda is optimizing for payback speed, targeting a four-to-six-month ROI on the V100 box.

The build-versus-buy line splits by card class. Alec Glover says that for enterprise cards a vendor charges only a 10 to 15 percent margin over building, so paying roughly $10K extra on a $100K server for the warranty is the smart trade, and building only makes sense for consumer GPUs [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:03:03]. On bus bandwidth, Glover measured about 24 GB/s on Gen4 x16 versus 48 on Gen5, yet putting Gen5 cards on Gen4/DDR4 hosts saved about $10K per server and their 5090 inference customer never noticed [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:07:16]. Glover still concedes Gen5 is always more expensive than Gen4 but carries twice the throughput. Red Panda Mining draws a harder line for the top cards: 4090s on a Gen3 Octominer are a waste, so they are planning a separate 7U PCIe 5.0 MCIO build for eight 4090s and reserving the Gen3 box for 3070s or CMP 170HX cards [X2uIX3DDPu0 @ 00:07:29]. Yet the same channel's ten 170HX cards, unlocked to 64 GB each and running LLM inference over a PCIe 2.0 x4 link, are pulling real rentals, matching their own read that renters want multi-GPU nodes to pool VRAM for large LLMs [SJbOF9Ufm0Q @ 00:05:28].

Platform choice is where the playbooks fork hardest. Alec Glover calls the roughly 25 percent cut taken by Vast, RunPod, and ShadeForm cheap, because the marketplace absorbs marketing, sales, and support, while resellers such as HydroHost, ShadeForm, and FluidStack take only 7.5 to 15 percent but want one- to two-year contracts [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:15:18]. Red Panda Mining pays that same 25 percent on Vast but splits their fleet: invite-only SimplePod demands redundant internet and generators, wants 30-series cards rather than more 4090s and 5090s, and keeps their 3090s and 3060s rented at low draw, while Vast renters appear to be crypto-mining instead [mfQE_CptyLA @ 00:08:39]. Modern Mining runs Salad instead, accepting a 10 percent cash-out fee plus a 40-cent deduction and an 18-cent hourly cap on a 4090 [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:37:51]. BearMarketMiner, after $10,000 of testing, gives the most split verdict: on OctaSpace a 4060 Ti rented about as often as a 5090, so start with a 4060 Ti, yet they also conclude high-end is definitely the way to go, and their Salad 5090 kept earning until its three-month job ended [egH5hKD913Y @ 00:04:39]. The right card depends on which marketplace's renters you are courting.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Risers and boards are where the firsthand losses cluster. Alec Glover's cheap slot-to-SlimSAS adapters threw AER errors and crashed drivers only after weeks in service, and the 10GTech Gen5 risers they tested were completely unusable, failing within 30 minutes, while C-Payne risers at over $100 per GPU and One Chassis assemblies at about $80 stayed stable [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:13:41]. Their blunt verdict was that they wasted time avoiding ASRock boards with native SlimSAS [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:02:25]. Crypto LLC sees the same pattern on Windows 11 farms: three freshly installed GPUs crashed the whole rig as soon as they mined, System interrupts sat at 7 to 13 percent, and BIOS still showed every PCIe port green, falsely signalling all was well. Their fix is to reseat the risers, rerun the miner, then swap one riser at a time, and from their experience it is risers most of the time [nHwXk9HCuYI @ 00:04:18]. Red Panda Mining's 600 CAD Octominer never booted at all: no VGA signal, a flashing LED, and an M1 error after a CMOS reset, RAM and CPU reseats, a swap to E5-2640 V3s, and pulling the NVMe, leaving a motherboard swap on the to-do list [X2uIX3DDPu0 @ 00:16:34].

The second trap is gross-rate math. Alec Glover warns that newcomers simply divide hardware cost by online hourly rates, when a cloud is far more than hardware [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:00:18]. Red Panda Mining's own 170HX server shows the gap: the $60-a-day figure was shoot-for-the-moon pricing, while real rentals paid $30.42 for nine GPUs over 19 hours and about $36.37 over a full day, and SimplePod's 20 percent fee turns a 20-cent rate into roughly 16 cents [SJbOF9Ufm0Q @ 00:08:10]. Modern Mining walks the same erosion on Salad: a 4090 capped at 18 cents an hour grosses $4.32 a day, drops to $3.88 after the 10 percent fee and 40-cent deduction, then to $2.92 after power, and at 80 percent uptime lands near $854 a year and a 3.5-year breakeven, which they offset by counting on 80 to 90 percent resale value [WJnsgbZsg0U @ 00:39:50]. Mining-side operators face a faster squeeze: MysticOrb289's 3090 is barely covering power on Quantus at 22 cents per kWh, and a working FPGA bitstream for the Osprey E-300 now threatens the GPU miners, with daily coin output halving if difficulty doubles [w5vhWKlqN_k @ 00:01:21].

Network and power shortcuts break under real load. Alec Glover's earlier network ran daisy-chained 1G links to single ports on each machine and caused outages, so they rebuilt around redundant routers, MC-LAG switch pairs, and Arista 10G 48-port switches at about $200 each [iuLuDflfEE4 @ 00:10:03]. Red Panda Mining hit the same wall at smaller scale when a renter pulled 400 GB in 20 minutes over 3 Gb fiber: throughput swung between 550 Mbps and 3 Gbps, packet loss hit 10 to 12 percent, and an X550 NIC firmware error surfaced, leaving a possible Ubiquiti upgrade on the table [SJbOF9Ufm0Q @ 00:12:23]. On power, Glover keeps every circuit, PSU, and PDU at a consistent 80 percent load and burns in new servers for 48 hours to a week, because a 4090 sliding from 450 W to 230 W can crash on overheating or throttle an entire multi-GPU server back [CgsDEFaGLJ0 @ 00:47:22]. BearMarketMiner flags two settings that quietly kill income: power-limiting below 90 percent on Salad gets the rig degraded, and thin RAM starves demand, with a 26 GB rig landing 7 rentals in about 30 days against 82 for a 63 GB rig [egH5hKD913Y @ 00:10:53].
