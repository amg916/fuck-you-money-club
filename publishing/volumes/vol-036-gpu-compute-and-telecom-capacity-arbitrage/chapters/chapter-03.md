## The Practitioner Consensus: Vast Wins the Sticker, RunPod Wins the Predictable Bill

Every operator who has spent real money on both platforms describes the same structural split. Bryan Software frames it before running five head-to-head tests: RunPod is a managed GPU cloud with fixed rates and compliance coverage, while Vast.ai is a marketplace where independent hosts set the price [Ez2jsa33jFY @ 00:00:15]. GPU Battle, who checks both price lists every day for a tracker covering 62 cards, found Vast listing 55 of them against RunPod's 31, and where both carried the same card, Vast came in cheaper 17 out of 24 times, with the RTX 3090 at 14 cents versus 22 cents, the RTX 5090 at 45 cents versus 69 cents, and the RTX 5080 at 22 cents versus 39 cents [156XFkwFpwY @ 00:01:16]. The same operator calls Vast the cheapest and also the most expensive provider, because it is the only one where bandwidth pricing shifts with each host, which makes it the pick for cheap low-VRAM consumer cards while RunPod stays the best all-rounder for medium to high workloads [SqfQZYYMPj4 @ 00:01:30]. TWiz reaches the same verdict from the usability side: RunPod for a user-friendly interface and straightforward pricing, Vast for extensive customization and potentially lower costs, paid for with a steeper learning curve on the marketplace model [_UBOzLyl2b0 @ 00:02:05].

The second point of agreement is that the Vast discount is a host lottery, and RunPod does not lose every price fight. GPU Battle's plot twist was RunPod winning on several big cards, with the H100 at $2.69 against $3.59 on Vast, the RTX 4090 at 34 cents against 40 cents, and the A6000 at 33 cents against 40 cents, then watching one Vast 4090 host run at half speed because it was underclocked [156XFkwFpwY @ 00:01:37]. Across 291 Vast rentals the channel logged 47 pods that never ran a single test, a 16% dud rate against 14 out of 200, about 7%, on RunPod, while noting the duds together cost only a dollar [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:00:17]. The West Reviews relays the same pattern from developers: one Reddit user found Vast's cheap GPUs attractive but saw machines fail mid-job and require restarts, while another found RunPod more stable with more predictable performance and fewer unexpected shutdowns [ZNRLXhDdbW4 @ 00:00:39]. HostingCompared with Natalie turns this into an order of operations: set a reliability minimum before sorting by price, because if one retry ruins the deadline the lowest row becomes the most expensive decision, and even a 99.78% host score is useful history, not magic [cEAhnKuawL8 @ 00:00:40].

The third consensus is about what the bill actually measures, and every speaker says the hourly rate alone lies. Bryan Software notes both platforms bill per second, so a 16-minute job at $1.99 per hour costs 53 cents rather than the full $1.99, a mechanic identical on both sides [Ez2jsa33jFY @ 00:01:25]. GPU Battle flags pricing the hour instead of the job as a core mistake, since per-second billing means a faster card can be cheaper per job, and in their earlier LLM token testing the RTX 3090 rather than the H100 came out cheapest per million tokens [WhTJ6B-YCmw @ 00:04:46]. HostingCompared with Natalie insists on writing down four numbers before launch, productive compute, paid idle time, storage lifetime and expected transfer, and points out that if setup takes five minutes but the GPU sits idle for two hours, setup was never the expensive part [_VqIjD7qiIg @ 00:01:43]. The same channel hammers the exit discipline everyone repeats: stop on RunPod releases the GPU but the volume disk keeps billing at 20 cents per gigabyte per month, so 100 gigabytes is $20 a month, a separate network volume survives even termination, and on Vast stopping halts compute while storage keeps billing as long as the machine is online [OQpEhkeZZp4 @ 00:00:19].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Vetting Scripts, Whole-Job Math, and Storage Discipline

GPU Battle runs rentals through an AI coding agent in Visual Studio Code holding the provider API keys, and the street mechanic is a written instruction set the agent must follow, starting with confirming CPU specs and current RAM before any job lands [WhTJ6B-YCmw @ 00:04:15]. The first trap is power-capped cards that listings never disclose: a 5090 throttled to 450 watts out of 575, a 4090 at 420 watts, a 3090 at 250 watts against 315, plus cheap 3060s sitting behind retired mining rigs on 2016-era CPUs where a host hands over 16 of 64 cores and the job loads forever, then fails to compile [dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:02:25]. The fix is the search prompt itself: search by quality, not price, reliability 99% plus, 16 plus CPU cores, 800 plus megabytes per second, data center host preferred, price the whole job, check power limit, CPU and download speed in the first two minutes, destroy the machine if anything is off, always destroy, never stop, and keep the API key in a local folder instead of pasting it into chat [dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:05:11]. The same channel's one-minute check caught a 4090 drawing 350 watts against a stock 450, and a listing promising about 3 gigabits down that pulled only 14 megabytes a second from Hugging Face [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:00:53].

The unit economics only work when the whole job is priced line by line. HostingCompared with Natalie models a 36-hour run with four paid idle hours, 100 gigabytes kept seven days and 50 gigabytes downloaded: RunPod compute at the public 74-cent 4090 rate is $29.60 and the full job lands around $33.71 with no transfer fee, while the Vast compute floor at a 28.6-cent host is $11.44, so Vast still wins only if that host's disk and transfer extras stay below roughly $22 [cEAhnKuawL8 @ 00:01:40]. Bryan Software puts the marketplace range at 2 cents to $6.25 per hour depending on host, with hidden costs adding 30 to 60% to the base fee and bandwidth and egress tacking on another $5 to $50, against RunPod's zero egress [Ez2jsa33jFY @ 00:01:06]. GPU Battle shows how fast those line items bite: on one Vast 4090, 6 cents of a 17-cent bill was the download charge, and one bad host running ten times slower wipes out the whole discount [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:01:44]. That is why the channel says a 53-cent host can beat a 47-cent host, because slow startup on weak CPUs and thin RAM is still billed on the same clock [dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:04:28].

Setup time is the hidden meter, and the speakers attack it with storage choices. GPU Battle warns that downloading a 50 GB model on a GPU pod bills GPU hours the whole time, so the model goes on a network volume and gets downloaded once [WhTJ6B-YCmw @ 00:03:59]. hrithik verma, running Isaac Sim from a public Docker template with an 80 GB disk, measured instance builds of 15 to 20 minutes plus another long wait for the first Isaac Sim load on both platforms, even though data center hosts booted noticeably faster, and recommends volume mounts or storing the instance for a week of work instead of reloading every session [CkxWiz2Qzi8 @ 00:33:04]. On spec selection, HostingCompared with Natalie tells renters of multi-GPU offers to check GPU type and count on RunPod, and on Vast the GPU count, per-GPU memory, total listed memory, PCIe or NVLink bandwidth and total hourly cost, because two 24 GB cards only act like one 48 GB pool when the exact framework supports model or tensor parallelism [b0fXfKwASvI @ 00:01:37]. Data Receipts applies the same discipline at the H100 tier: compare inside one SKU only, since the SXM 80 GB, PCIe 80 GB and NVL 94 GB all get sold as an H100 [ewu0un1Iy8g @ 00:00:49].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Shortlist by quality, not price
   - Filter offers on reliability, CPU cores, download speed, and host type before sorting by price.
   - Search with reliability 99% plus, 16 plus CPU cores, 800 plus megabytes per second, and data center hosts preferred, then sort what survives [GPU Battle — dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:05:11].
2. Price the whole job in four lines
   - Multiply compute, idle time, storage, and transfer separately for the exact offer you can rent now.
   - Use productive hours, idle hours, storage kept per day, and gigabytes transferred, and compare a minimum and maximum when any fee is unknown [HostingCompared with Natalie — _VqIjD7qiIg @ 00:02:24].
3. Vet the host in the first two minutes
   - Check power limit, CPU, and download speed immediately after launch, and move on if the host stalls.
   - Destroy the machine if anything is off and jump to the next host rather than paying for a throttled card [GPU Battle — dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:05:34].
4. Run the exit check
   - Back up or sync, terminate the RunPod pod or destroy the Vast instance, then clear leftover volumes.
   - Open storage and delete any RunPod network volume or Vast volume you no longer need, because those live beyond compute and bill separately [HostingCompared with Natalie — OQpEhkeZZp4 @ 00:01:19].

The loudest split is over which platform deserves to be the default. Meanwhile GPU Battle also calls Vast the ultimate value pack for consumer GPUs and budget H100s, provided the agent renting on your API key is told how to dodge the marketplace's traps [dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:02:04]. The operator takeaway is that the headline verdict depends entirely on whether you score sticker price or total predictability.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure mode is trusting a single marketplace host. GPU Battle rented 291 Vast pods and 47 never ran a single test, a 16% dud rate against 14 out of 200, about 7%, on RunPod; the duds were cheap, costing a dollar combined, so the fix was simply delete and grab another [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:00:17]. The real damage came from hosts that did run but ran slow: the same operator rented a 4090 that drew 350 watts when a stock 4090 is 450, and listing disk speeds ranged from about 180 megabytes a second to over 30,000 [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:00:53]. Old machines behind cheap cards are the second trap, with 3060s often sitting on retired mining rigs with 2016-era CPUs where the host hands over 16 of 64 cores, so jobs load forever and then fail to compile [dWC3n2Bn3r8 @ 00:03:06]. Newer cards are not immune: every 5060 GPU Battle tried to rent came back underclocked, saddled with a crappy CPU, or short on RAM, making it useless for testing [WhTJ6B-YCmw @ 00:07:40]. The economics check is blunt: duds cost pennies, slow hosts cost real money, and one bad host can wipe out the whole discount.

Interruptible capacity is where cheap turns into lost work. GPU Battle bid on Vast spot pods 17 times and eight got kicked off before they finished, about half, which the operator rates great for a quick test and terrible for anything you cannot restart [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:01:13]. Bryan Software describes the nightmare version: a training run spins up, runs 40 minutes, the host drops, and you lose both the compute time and the checkpoint, with Vast's provisioning score at 3.6 out of five and no platform-wide contractual SLA [Ez2jsa33jFY @ 00:02:38]. Worse, the same operator flags that Vast's interruptible tier bills the license cost even on interrupted hours, so the 10 to 30% interruption loss is a charge already paid, not a refund, and an unsaved checkpoint means paying for compute you cannot recover [Ez2jsa33jFY @ 00:09:16]. HostingCompared with Natalie adds the duration trap: always add setup time, idle time, and a safety buffer to the offer's maximum rental window, because a cheap offer that expires before the checkpoint finishes is just a very affordable way to fail, and if one retry ruins the deadline, the lowest row may become the most expensive decision [cEAhnKuawL8 @ 00:01:02]. The rule that falls out: spot is for restartable work only, and anything with a deadline needs a host that can finish.

The quietest bleed is the bill that keeps running after the work stops. HostingCompared with Natalie spells out that stopping a RunPod pod releases the GPU but the volume disk keeps billing at the stopped rate of 20 cents per gigabyte per month, so 100 gigabytes is $20 a month, while on Vast storage keeps billing while the machine is online until you destroy the instance and delete any separate volume [OQpEhkeZZp4 @ 00:00:19]. Stopping also does not hold your hardware: hrithik verma tried restarting a stopped instance and was told it was CPU only because the GPU could not be started, so each fresh relaunch means waiting through Docker layers and Isaac Sim loading again while the meter runs [CkxWiz2Qzi8 @ 00:04:29]. GPU Battle found bandwidth eating 6 cents of a 17 cent Vast 4090 bill on the download alone [kvP__Lh53oM @ 00:01:44], and warns that downloading a 50 GB model on a GPU pod bills GPU hours the whole time, while an AI agent left unsupervised may park a pod on standby instead of terminating it and drain credits [WhTJ6B-YCmw @ 00:03:59]. Price the exit, the download, and the idle storage before you price the hour.
