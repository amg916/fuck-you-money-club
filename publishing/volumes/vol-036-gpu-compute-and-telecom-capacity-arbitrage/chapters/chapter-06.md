## Executive Summary

Neocloud operators and multi-cloud brokers converge on one law: idle or stalled GPUs bleed money, so utilization, scheduling and portability decide who survives. Caleb Writes Code models a $25K H100 rented at $2.30/hr returning a 28% CAGR only at full utilization, with broad stocks winning below roughly 55%. Neoclouds list GPUs at roughly half to two-thirds of hyperscaler prices and up to 30-50% cheaper on contract, while SkyPilot's broker finds up to 3x on-demand and 9x spot price spreads across zones. Street mechanics center on queues with priority preemption, checkpointed spot runs saved near a 34-minute interval, and one YAML spec that fails over across clouds. Disagreement persists over provider picks, Slurm versus Kubernetes, and whether neocloud debt echoes the telecom bust, where a NY-LA circuit collapsed from $1.8M to under $150K a year.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator in this chapter starts from the same brutal arithmetic: a GPU only earns while it is busy. Caleb Writes Code prices a single H100 at $25K plus $5K opex, gives it roughly 4 years of useful life, and shows that renting it at $2.30/hr yields about $55 a day, near $20K a year and about $80K over four years, which works out to a 28% CAGR only at 100% utilization; at or below roughly 55% utilization, broad stocks beat the neocloud outright [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:01:22]. The gpuMaster frames the same pain from the renter side: stalled GPUs still bill, and large-scale GPU workloads are 'far less forgiving' [_tV0Elv_jIk @ 00:01:29]. Codemia puts a dollar figure on scheduler quality for a 10,000-GPU fleet: default Kubernetes lands near 45% utilization, a workload-aware design reaches about 85%, and that gap is worth roughly $150k per day [a_zou7QahAo @ 00:04:38]. The Code Notebook compresses the whole consensus into one line, 'The cheapest GPU is one that is switched off,' backed by a scale-to-zero simulation that cut 24 GPU-hours to 4.04, an 83% reduction, at a cost of 3.9 cold starts per day lasting 60 seconds each [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:12:17].

The second point of agreement is that neoclouds win on price and workload fit, not on breadth of services. Cloud Computing Insider defines a neocloud as an AI-first, GPU-centric provider and argues that hyperscalers were built for all IT, while neoclouds were built for the compute, networking and storage patterns modern AI needs, and hyperscalers charge more for AI because they run it less efficiently [mAgWtefgeP8 @ 00:01:02]. Romil Bhardwaj of SkyPilot, speaking at TMLS, lists the neocloud edge as instant self-serve capacity, 3.2TB/s InfiniBand, top SemiAnalysis ClusterMAX tiers, and pricing up to 30 to 50% cheaper depending on contract length, while warning that the long tail of neoclouds runs 2 to 3x cheaper but less reliable [_oWJ0t-RW7o @ 00:06:30]. The Code Notebook, working from vendor figures dated October 2026, pegs neocloud GPU list prices at roughly one-half to two-thirds of big-cloud rates [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:05:29]. Caleb Writes Code, whose own model assumes a market rate of $1 to $5 per hour across CoreWeave, Runpod, Vultr and Thunder Compute, adds the hard corollary: neoclouds are heavily commoditized, customers typically look for the cheapest pricing, and any operator who charges a premium watches utilization fall [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:01:37].

The third consensus is that no serious buyer stays on one provider. Zhanghao Wu and Kevin Mingtarja of SkyPilot open their frontier-capabilities talk by pointing to Meta signing multi-billion-dollar deals with CoreWeave and Nebius and give three reasons to run multi-provider: capacity, cost, and no single point of failure [uwLkrJb5pEA @ 00:01:47]. Romil Bhardwaj, presenting at CNCF, traces OpenAI's move from Microsoft exclusivity to Oracle, CoreWeave and GCP [-czZES1U_Us @ 00:00:49]. The USENIX NSDI '23 SkyPilot team makes the economic case for brokerage: silos reduce availability and leverage, a single-cloud business is more vulnerable to large-scale outages, and it has lower negotiation leverage to get a better deal because 'choice is power' [3Edw88b1bRg @ 00:01:22]. Their broker example routes SGX workloads to Azure, TPU training to GCP and Inferentia inference to AWS. Cloud Computing Insider expects the end state to be a hybrid, heterogeneous mix, telling buyers that a best-of-breed play is going to be complex and heterogeneous, and that if they shortlist two providers it is probably Nebius and CoreWeave, while disclaiming any single-provider recommendation [mAgWtefgeP8 @ 00:13:14]. The prerequisite all of them share is a portability layer that lets workloads move when prices, quotas or availability shift.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

On the supply side, the build math gets heavier fast. Caleb Writes Code notes that a GPT-3-class model needs more than 350GB of VRAM, so a serious customer needs at least 8 H100s, which puts the minimum rentable unit at $250K: $200K for cards and $50K opex; eBay cards are risky, and supplier relationships act as the moat [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:03:59]. Scaling is non-linear: efficiency drops to 77% at 8 GPUs under model parallelism and 74% at 512 per an NVIDIA paper, fragmented nodes create bottlenecks, and scheduling turns into Tetris. CUDA and ROCm workloads are not interchangeable, so a mixed fleet carries heterogeneity risk. On financing, Caleb Writes Code points out that resale value stays near purchase price, which enables asset-backed loans, while contracts trade a discount for flexibility [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:13:55]. Codemia sizes a 10,000-GPU fleet at about $400M across 1,250 hosts in 32 racks, with cards renting at $20 to $40 per hour [a_zou7QahAo @ 00:03:35]. Reliability becomes a sales weapon: Deok Filho of CoreWeave cites Meta's figure of roughly 8 hours between interruptions per 1,000 GPUs and claims CoreWeave runs 70 to 80 hours for an equivalent job size [vdP0fhqpYZs @ 00:06:03].

On the buy side, The Code Notebook lays out the pricing ladder: flex start runs about half price, commitments go down to about 45%, and spot goes down to about 9% [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:06:06]. The commitment rule is simple: utilization must exceed 1 minus the discount. With $10 on-demand, a $4.50 commit costs $5 per used hour at 90% busy but $45 per used hour at 10% busy, hence 'Never commit to your peak.' Spot carries a different tax: AWS gives 2 minutes of warning and Azure gives 30 seconds. In a 2,000-run simulation of a 100-hour job with interruptions every 8 hours, 6-minute restarts, 72-second saves and a 70% discount, never saving produced 2,203,564 hours, saving every 30 minutes finished in 109 hours at a cost of 32.6 against 100 on-demand, and saving every 30 seconds took 345 hours at 103.5. The optimal save interval, the square root of 2 times MTBI times save cost, lands near 34 minutes [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:10:20]. The USENIX SkyPilot team measured the spread that makes brokering pay: up to 3x on on-demand pricing and up to 9x on spot across zones [3Edw88b1bRg @ 00:07:21]. Codemia's scheduler polls for spot reclaim every 5 seconds against the 2-minute warning [a_zou7QahAo @ 00:29:02].

The brokerage layer itself is mostly scheduling and provisioning plumbing. SkyPilot's Zhanghao Wu and Kevin Mingtarja run `sky launch --gpus B200` for a dev box, bump `num_nodes 16` to scale out, and use `sky jobs launch` so a GPU failure, node maintenance, quota reclamation or spot preemption is recovered hands-free; in their demo the optimizer picked the cheapest option, an already-provisioned CoreWeave H100 node [uwLkrJb5pEA @ 00:08:31]. At Meta FAIR, Lucca Bertoncini found a 10-step setup lost about 90% of users by step 2 and 98% by the end, and 2FA on every cluster drove adoption to 0%, so the team shipped a single binary that writes the configuration and holds SSH/ET tunnels in tmux with 2FA done once, across clusters of roughly 20K GPUs each [Pas2NE76220 @ 00:05:54]. Seung Jin Yang of SkyPilot replaces spreadsheets and node taints with a real queue, priority preemption backed by checkpoints, Slack alerts on preemption, and quota borrowing that lets a team grab 256 GPUs idling in another team's queue [tb-Yg8PNXT4 @ 00:05:02]. Alex from Nebius shows SkyPilot task definitions running 3 to 4x shorter than the Kubernetes equivalent, driving a 2-node, 16-H100 Llama 3.1 fine-tune with TorchTune [QLSYI5Ali8Y @ 00:02:24].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Model Utilization Break-Even
   - Price the card, opex and hourly rate, then compute returns at realistic utilization rather than full occupancy.
   - Use the baseline of a $25K H100 plus $5K opex rented at $2.30/hr, which returns a 28% CAGR at 100% utilization while broad stocks win at or below roughly 55% [Caleb Writes Code — 9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:01:22].
2. Benchmark Shortlisted Neoclouds
   - Run your own workload on each candidate provider before committing any contract.
   - Run a roughly week-long POC to confirm end-to-end performance, since neoclouds can be 30-50% cheaper depending on contract length and multicloud gives negotiating leverage [Toronto Machine Learning Society (TMLS) — _oWJ0t-RW7o @ 00:07:47].
3. Size Commitments Below Peak
   - Commit only the capacity you will keep busier than the break-even line.
   - Commit only when utilization will exceed 1 minus the discount; a $4.50 commit on $10 on-demand costs $5 per used hour at 90% busy and $45 at 10% busy, so never commit to your peak [The Code Notebook — IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:07:52].
4. Launch Through A Broker With Checkpointed Recovery
   - Define the job once in YAML, launch as a managed job, and mount checkpoints to durable storage.
   - Launch with `sky jobs launch`, mount checkpoints (the demo used S3 at /checkpoints), and let SkyPilot recover from GPU failure, node maintenance, quota reclamation or spot preemption hands-free [SkyPilot — uwLkrJb5pEA @ 00:08:31].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first split is whether to own GPUs or arbitrage other people's capacity. Caleb Writes Code runs the owner math cold: a $25,000 H100 plus $5,000 opex, a four-year life, and $2.30 per hour yielding roughly $55 a day, about $20,000 a year and $80,000 over the card's life, a 28% CAGR only at 100% utilization [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:00:50]. Drop to roughly 55% utilization and broad stocks beat the neocloud, and because the market is commoditized, any premium price simply bleeds utilization [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:01:22]. Romil Bhardwaj attacks from the buyer side: the long tail of neoclouds runs two to three times cheaper but is less reliable, and neoclouds can land 30 to 50% cheaper depending on how long a contract you sign [_oWJ0t-RW7o @ 00:06:30]. Cloud Computing Insider frames the same gap structurally, arguing hyperscalers charge more for AI because they were built for all IT and are less efficient at it [mAgWtefgeP8 @ 00:01:49]. The owner needs full racks and steady renters; the broker profits when that owner's margin collapses under competition. Both camps agree price is the battlefield, but they sit on opposite sides of the same spread, and an operator has to decide which side of that spread pays the bills before buying a single card.

The second fight is concentration versus brokerage. Cloud Computing Insider will name names, saying if you look at two providers it is probably Nebius and CoreWeave, with CoreWeave as the gold standard, yet the same speaker refuses to back a single provider and warns that a best-of-breed play will be complex and heterogeneous [mAgWtefgeP8 @ 00:13:14]. CoreWeave's Deok Filho pushes concentration hard: Meta reports about eight hours between interruptions per 1,000 GPUs, CoreWeave claims 70 to 80 hours for an equivalent job size, and the pitch closes with 'It just will run better on us' [vdP0fhqpYZs @ 00:06:03]. The Sky Computing team at USENIX argues the opposite, that silos cut availability and negotiation leverage because choice is power, and that clouds will never commoditize themselves through standardization, so a broker must sit on top [3Edw88b1bRg @ 00:01:22]. Modelplane adds a sharper twist: the multi-cloud layer is too important to be proprietary, and clusters should be treated as one fleet where a deployment never names the cluster at all [Wq9Ov_jlxlM @ 00:02:46]. Single-vendor depth buys reliability numbers and integrated tooling; brokerage buys leverage and failover. Neither camp disputes the other's data, only which risk an operator should eat.

The third split is how to buy time on the iron. Caleb Writes Code treats contracts as a plain trade of discount for flexibility [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:11:30]. The Code Notebook maps the buyer's full ladder: flex start at about half price, commitments down to about 45%, spot down to about 9%, and a hard rule that a commitment only wins when utilization exceeds one minus the discount, summed up as 'Never commit to your peak' [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:07:52]. The USENIX SkyPilot team bets on the spread instead, measuring up to three times difference in on-demand pricing and up to nine times in spot pricing across zones, then routing jobs with an ILP solver that minimizes total cost or time [3Edw88b1bRg @ 00:07:21]. Underneath the pricing sits a cluster-manager argument. Romil Bhardwaj says Slurm hits isolation and serving limits, Kubernetes brings a steep learning curve with no SSH and no gang scheduling, and raw VMs dump key management and manual orchestration on the team, so the recommendation is managed Kubernetes plus VMs [_oWJ0t-RW7o @ 00:11:41]. CoreWeave answers by running Slurm on Kubernetes through SUNK, while Meta FAIR still flags schedulers stacked on schedulers as an open problem. Each answer reshapes how much of the discount actually reaches the bottom line.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is cheap spot capacity run without discipline. The Code Notebook simulated a 100-hour job with interruptions every eight hours, a six-minute restart, 72 seconds per save, 2,000 runs, and a 70% discount: never saving stretched the job to 2,203,564 hours, saving every 30 minutes finished in 109 hours at a cost of 32.6 against 100 on-demand, and saving every 30 seconds ballooned to 345 hours at 103.5 [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:09:58]. Their verdict is that spot without checkpoints is not cheap, saving too often costs more than not using spot at all, and the optimal interval is the square root of two times mean time between interruptions times save cost, about 34 minutes [IZtzUfhm8LU @ 00:10:20]. The same speaker notes the warning windows are brutal: AWS gives two minutes and Azure gives 30 seconds. Codemia's scheduler design polls the two-minute reclaim notice every five seconds and tells operators to give each job a random offset so checkpoint timers do not line up, because correlated failures are where these systems break [a_zou7QahAo @ 00:31:58]. SkyPilot's managed jobs layer pitches hands-free recovery from GPU failure, node maintenance, quota reclamation, and spot preemption so nobody gets paged at 3:00 a.m. [uwLkrJb5pEA @ 00:08:31].

The second trap is paying for GPUs that are not doing work. The gpuMaster's core warning is that stalled GPUs still bill, and that large-scale GPU workloads are far less forgiving [_tV0Elv_jIk @ 00:01:29]. Caleb Writes Code measured the scaling tax: efficiency drops to 77% at eight GPUs under model parallelism and 74% at 512, and fragmented nodes turn scheduling into Tetris [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:05:04]. Seung Jin Yang of SkyPilot debugged the failures researchers actually hit when they ask why their job is not scheduled: fragmentation, rogue taints left by agents, a request for 128 CPUs on nodes with only about 126.32 allocatable that shows up as blocked, and quota oversubscription where 12 GPUs are promised against 8, which, in their words, can happen in practice [tb-Yg8PNXT4 @ 00:13:27]. Codemia prices the gap: default Kubernetes gets about 45% utilization, a workload-aware design about 85%, a spread worth roughly $150,000 per day on a 10,000-GPU fleet [a_zou7QahAo @ 00:04:38]. The same speaker caps defragmentation at 5% per hour, because the cure has to stay cheaper than the disease. Every one of these leaks lands as billed GPU hours with zero training progress behind them.

The third trap is the balance sheet. Tulipnomics walks through the telecom bust as a template: Lucent promised $6.7 billion in customer credit by September 2000, with Nortel and Cisco running similar programs, summed up as the seller lending the buyer the money to buy the seller's own products [71yv5MpdX1s @ 00:02:24]. When capacity flooded in, a New York to Los Angeles circuit fell from $1.8 million a year to under $150,000, telecom made up over half of 2002 bond defaults with 21 cents of recovery, and the same channel now counts roughly $36 billion of CoreWeave debt plus Moody's tally of $1.2 trillion in lease commitments, $820 billion not yet started [71yv5MpdX1s @ 00:09:54]. theCUBE flags the operator-level exposure: a 2024 net loss of $863.4 million, NVIDIA dependence, and over 60% of revenue from Microsoft, with the warning that sustaining profitability is going to be another challenge [VjfoLNKgo3A @ 00:03:51]. Caleb Writes Code offers the counterweight, noting that GPU resale value stays near purchase price, which enables asset-backed loans [9u2hCf8FlW0 @ 00:13:55]. Tulipnomics' own coda cuts both ways: the cable was never the problem, but new owners bought the networks for a fraction of what they cost to build, and the banks lost money on the fiber.
