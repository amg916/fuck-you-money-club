## The Practitioner Consensus

Every STIR/SHAKEN practitioner lands on the same three-grade ladder, and the grade is a commercial weapon. Chris of Crosstalk and ClearlyIP CEO Tony Lewis define A as a known customer on an owned number, B as a known customer on an unverified number such as follow-me forwarding, and C as a gateway call, the grade most likely to be marked spam [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:02:43]. Briana Sullivan of Bandwidth frames A around known right-to-use, B as a known customer with an unknown number source, and C as originated onto the network but unauthenticated, with the SIP Identity Header carrying calling number, called number, timestamp, attestation level, and a unique origination ID for traceback [G91y9mjo-pk @ 00:01:49]. Tim Lin of Sangoma ties the grade directly to inventory: A means the DID was bought from Sangoma, B a third-party DID, C an international gateway [3XJ9OkK8aFw @ 00:07:34]. Techmode, which sends every one of its numbers out with A attestation, adds the operator reality check: STIR/SHAKEN does not itself block calls or paint the spam-likely label, analytics companies do that, and non-IP hops can strip the seal entirely [KjFy5hTaOcM @ 00:01:25]. Crosstalk's standing rule follows from all of it: anything other than an A on the checker number gets fixed.

The second point of agreement is that reselling does not buy anyone out of the paperwork. Crosstalk ran a SIP resale side business for roughly 60 to 70 customers on VoIP Innovations upstream, and it still required FCC and state registration, tax remittance, and billing [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:06:53]. Rick at ZCorum reads the January 25 public notice the same way: every provider must file certifications and plans in the Robocall Mitigation Database, including intermediate providers and resellers that own no infrastructure, with existing filers forced to update by February 26, 2024 [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:02:27]. Tim Lin lists the self-signing prerequisites as a 499-A filing, an OCN from NANPA, and direct SOMOS number access [3XJ9OkK8aFw @ 00:19:14]. Legal Registration hammers the 499-A angle from the filing side: every US telecom provider, VoIP included, needs a DC agent secured through the form, it is filed every year before April 1st to report USF revenue, and it grants no license on its own, so a separate license application is still required [Lqlbag5CQMU @ 00:01:21]. The consensus baseline for anyone selling voice minutes is therefore stacked: registration, annual revenue reporting, an RMD listing with a mitigation plan, and an FRN behind all of it, because the upstream carrier's compliance does not carry the reseller's name.

Across voice, wireless, and IoT, practitioners agree that regulatory cost flows straight into the unit economics and lands on the customer bill. Eric Yuzi at ZCorum puts USF fees at about four or five dollars per bill, funded by landline, mobile, and VoIP revenue on a shrinking base, and argues that every fee passes to consumers anyway: there's no such thing as a free lunch [QPBt987oumw @ 00:14:38]. Ariel Roth's Federalist Society framing confirms the mechanism, with contributions assessed on interstate and international voice revenue, passed through as a line item, and the contribution factor climbing from low single digits to 25 to 33 percent [EL34vsXYuGQ @ 00:03:00]. On the wireless side, Brian Lachford of Atomic Mobile, who has helped launch 100-plus MVNOs, warns that billing and tax surprises show up with the first invoice and that state filings must be done before launch, because after launch you are cleaning up a mess [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:08:58]. IoT (eSIM) Studio defines the structural split every model sits on: MNOs own the network, MVNOs resell connectivity without owning the underlying network patrimony, and aggregators contract with both, so the first diligence question is always who you can switch to and at what cost [GZtF-I2M1ZE @ 00:01:27].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Tony Lewis lays out the self-signing bill line by line. The iconectiv token runs thousands a year, priced as a percentage of 499 revenue with a minimum fee, getting it means clearing a multi-page sandbox test case, and the certificate itself costs thousands a year against roughly $100 for SSL [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:10:42]. Tim Lin confirms the governance chain behind those invoices: ATIS is the governance authority, iconectiv is the policy administrator that issues tokens and sets an annual fee based on FCC-filed revenue, and multiple certificate authorities are listed by iconectiv [3XJ9OkK8aFw @ 00:05:29]. The cheap path small operators reach for is forking the call to a signing provider, which requires a PJSIP patch plus a first and second trunk in FreePBX outbound routes, a setup Chris calls a bubble gum and Band-Aid solution [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:12:53]. The proper path Tony describes is a real SBC build with redundancy, geographic failover on both coasts, DID, credential, and IP mapping, and 911 caller-ID logic. Against that cost stack, Crosstalk's resale business peaked at 130,000 to 140,000 calls per month and netted only 15 to 20 percent margin every month once profitable, while ClearlyIP handles tens of millions [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:07:43].

Rick at ZCorum turns the RMD rule into a repeatable know-your-customer routine. Verify identity, address, years in business, and time at that address, then ask what the service is actually for, because a pizza shop is a different risk from a call center or telemarketer [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:07:52]. Terms of service should allow immediate termination on reported abuse, the filer certifies full, partial, or no STIR/SHAKEN implementation, and the mitigation plan itself is usually just one page long. The sample WOW plan Rick walks through commits to traceback cooperation, vets businesses on name, physical and mailing address, tax ID, phone, email, and PIN, and blocks unassigned numbers, with an FCC Registration Number required to file [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:13:22]. Tim Lin layers on the carrier-side obligations: cooperate with the Industry Traceback Group and law enforcement, and expect calls from providers without a certificate on file to be refused from September 28, 2021 [3XJ9OkK8aFw @ 00:15:13]. Lin's routing rule is blunt: sign your own calls if you are multi-vendor or an outbound contact center, otherwise you face caller-ID-based routing. On the foreign side, Chris and Tony Lewis say carriers handling foreign gateway traffic face a binary choice, know your customer or block [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:25:56].

On the wireless and eSIM side the mechanics are platform-driven. Brian Lachford says MVNOs otherwise juggle three to six vendors, launches that once took six months with a one-week port now port in minutes, and Atomic Mobile hands out a free 100-item launch checklist with no signup [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:13:33]. Lachford flags real-time usage as the margin lever: lags of 3, 6, or 9 hours make usage plans risky, so Atomic hired Amanda Herman, 22 years at AT&T, to fix it for margins, fraud, and spam control [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:14:59]. Vincent Custany of Kigen pitches the IoT version: a starter kit with complimentary eSIMs and three months of connectivity across 9 to 12 MVNOs, and Kigen Pulse with API parity to the UI so an operator can run a campaign switch, such as moving all of Germany from MNO A to MNO B, without a truck roll [u_m7kYjI4ow @ 00:12:19]. The Top New Code demo shows the retail reseller stack: upload profiles from any carrier, build global, regional, and country plans with custom pricing, deliver by QR with top-ups across 190-plus countries, and run unlimited resellers and sub-resellers with commissions, balance limits, KYC, and no monthly fees [e8R4z-ISWAo @ 00:01:45].

1. Register and file the 499-A

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the reseller trench is whether a shop riding someone else's switch owes its own signing. Tim Lin of Sangoma told VoIP Innovations resellers they were not required to implement STIR/SHAKEN themselves, while still telling them to get a regulatory expert's read on their specific setup [3XJ9OkK8aFw @ 00:16:48]. Chris of Crosstalk Solutions lived the other side of that answer. VoIP Innovations and a telecom lawyer both told Chris the business was covered, and "neither of them were correct," after FCC legal had already mailed what Chris called "pretty threatening" letters [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:20:06]. Tony Lewis of ClearlyIP drew the line in one sentence: whoever is charging the end user is the originating service provider under the spec, no matter whose network carries the minutes [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:20:52]. Rick at ZCorum sits between the camps. Resellers can select the "without a STIR/SHAKEN obligation" box in the Robocall Mitigation Database, yet Rick sees many tick "complete" simply because their upstream is compliant, and he tells them to run that choice past an attorney before filing [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:16:08]. The operator takeaway is blunt: the vendor's assurance and the regulator's reading diverged, and the reseller who trusted the vendor is the one who received the letter.

On how to actually get calls signed, three playbooks compete. Tony Lewis priced the do-it-yourself route: the iconectiv token runs "thousands a year," pegged as a percentage of 499 revenue with a minimum fee, and the certificate is another "thousands a year" against roughly $100 for an ordinary SSL cert [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:10:42]. The cheap alternative is forking each call to a signing provider, which needs a PJSIP patch plus a first and second trunk in the FreePBX outbound routes; Chris dismissed it as a "bubble gum and Band-Aid" fix rather than real infrastructure [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:12:53]. Tim Lin of Sangoma argues self-signing pays when you are multi-vendor or running an outbound contact center, because otherwise you are stuck with caller-ID-based routing, and the entry ticket is a 499-A filing, an OCN from NANPA, and direct SOMOS number access [3XJ9OkK8aFw @ 00:17:44]. Rick at ZCorum pitched a third lane through Voice Select, where the reseller keeps only the customer premises equipment and the know-your-customer work while the platform carries everything else [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:19:17]. The choice comes down to vendor count, outbound volume, and whether you can afford to own certificates, tokens, and test cases yourself every year.

MVNO builders split on speed and on whether to own the stack. Brian Lachford of Atomic Mobile says launches that once took six months with week-long ports now port in minutes, while host John Horvitz pushed back that going direct to a carrier took a full year [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:10:05]. Lachford insists there is "not really a window of capitalizing on developing your own software," adding that designing a platform is easy and that AI-built MVNE platforms routinely ship without the billing logic that actually runs the business [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:17:36]. Top New Code sells the opposite instinct: a white-label eSIM storefront with multi-vendor mode, unlimited resellers, commissions, KYC, payment gateways, currency and branding controls, pitched as "modular and customizable" with no monthly fees [e8R4z-ISWAo @ 00:02:02]. On the IoT and connectivity side, IoT (eSIM) Studio tells buyers to pressure-test any MNO, MVNO, or aggregator with one question: which provider can I switch to today, and what will it cost? If you cannot switch, you are locked by technology and the lack of service; if switching costs unreasonably, you are locked commercially [GZtF-I2M1ZE @ 00:02:51]. Lachford's camp buys speed and billing maturity from a specialist, while the storefront camp bets that packaged software plus reseller tiers is enough to start selling data.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The resale math broke first. Chris ran the Crosstalk SIP side business on VoIP Innovations for 60 to 70 customers while carrying FCC and state registration, tax remittance through a paid third-party tax engine, and billing, and once it finally turned a profit it cleared only "15 to 20 percent profit margin every month" [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:07:43]. That margin leaves no room for the next layer of cost. Tony Lewis says Inteliquent will only sign C attestation when the customer has no certificate of their own, which means the reseller pays for carriage and still ships the worst grade available [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:21:20]. The penalty shows up at the handset: Tony traces T-Mobile "likely spam" flags back to a C signing "nine times out of ten," and SS7 hops along the route can strip the identity header entirely [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:23:01]. Techmode adds the uncomfortable detail that a clean signature does not settle the matter either. Non-IP hops can strip the seal, STIR/SHAKEN itself never applies the "spam likely" label because analytics companies do, and even all-A traffic is only one signal among several that the terminating side weighs [KjFy5hTaOcM @ 00:01:25]. Thin margin plus a fixed signing bill plus downgraded attestation is how small VoIP carriers get squeezed out.

The 2024 Robocall Mitigation Database order turned paperwork into a kill switch. Rick at ZCorum read it plainly: as of May 28, 2024, voice and intermediate providers "must refuse to carry traffic from any provider that's not listed," and he warned resellers to file before May or watch their upstream partner refuse their traffic, even if fines for the February deadline never land [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:05:20]. The plan itself is where operators get caught. Rick's know-your-customer bar asks what the service is actually for, because a pizza shop is a different risk than a call center or telemarketer, and the terms of service should allow immediate termination on reported abuse [ZLzeR3N0mpc @ 00:07:52]. Tony Lewis applies the same logic to foreign gateways: know the customer or block the traffic, because that inbound flood is exactly what traceback lands on [ycGPHG_ANrE @ 00:25:56]. Underneath sits the annual filing treadmill. Legal Registration stresses that the 499-A is not a one-time event; it must be filed every year before April 1, VoIP providers included, a provider without a DC agent faces massive fines and penalties, and the filing itself grants no license [Lqlbag5CQMU @ 00:01:21]. Miss any layer and the network, not the regulator, is what cuts you off first.

MVNO founders bleed in places they never modeled. Brian Lachford says billing and tax surprises arrive with the first invoice, and that filing in every state is something you "plan before you launch, not after you launch, cuz after you launch, you're essentially cleaning up a mess" [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:08:58]. Usage feeds that lag three, six, or nine hours make usage-based plans risky, which is why Atomic hired Amanda Herman, a 22-year AT&T veteran, to chase real-time data for margins, fraud, and spam control [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:14:59]. Lachford's fastest tell of a doomed brand is a founder chasing Mint-level pricing, visible within five to ten minutes of the first conversation, alongside founders who keep changing their minds about the plan [Gye5g8KT7Oo @ 00:18:30]. B2B roaming carries its own exposure. A Virtuser panelist described a wealthy, travel-heavy firm that banned roaming outright because costs escalate fast beyond the 30 to 40 big countries into zones two through six, and staff needed line-manager sign-off proving a time-sensitive deal just to switch roaming on [6sIXgGXsu3E @ 00:00:29]. Airport and hotel Wi-Fi does not rescue that traveler either; the same panelist called it untrusted, maxed out, slow, and patchy between the room and the lobby.
