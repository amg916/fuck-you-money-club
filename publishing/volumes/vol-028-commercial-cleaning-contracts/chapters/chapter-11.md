## 1. Assign every seat before promising another building  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Treat Candidate #10's pod as a capacity ledger, not an organization chart decorated with job titles. The four mapped plays used here are recurring B2B route density, medical janitorial specialization, ISSA-based production-rate bidding, and supervisor pods supported by scheduling, inspection, and billing automation. In the nine-person configuration, FT1 is the working owner-supervisor, responsible for bids, account reviews, payroll review, dispatch exceptions, and selected production shifts. FT2 owns the first recurring route; FT3 owns the second route; FT4 handles medical-site execution; FT5 supplies floating coverage, equipment readiness, and documented inspection support. These are five people, not five departments. C1 performs scheduled floor restoration, C2 performs carpet extraction, C3 performs accessible commercial glass cleaning, and C4 accepts separately scoped overflow janitorial packages. Each contractor seat represents one identified individual operating an independent business for this headcount model. A subcontractor arriving with three helpers adds three people to the capacity analysis. Put building access, task qualifications, equipment ownership, and available service windows beside every seat. The owner cannot simultaneously inspect a clinic, cover an absence, and conduct a sales walkthrough; the dispatch board must show which promise moves when that conflict appears.

Expand to eleven people only when a measured bottleneck justifies FT6 and C5. FT6 becomes a producing route lead who takes repeatable dispatch and inspection work away from FT1; C5 provides a second independently contracted relief specialty with a distinct service package. Do not convert permanent employee vacancies into nominal contractor seats. Price capacity from task inventories and ISSA production references, then replace reference assumptions with timed walkthroughs and actual first-month results. Include loading, paid intersite travel, security delays, restocking, and equipment cleanup when measuring available hours. A capacity stress test illustrates the burden: at an assumed $70 of recognized revenue per productive hour, the $725,000 Base case needs approximately 10,357 productive hours. Four producing employees at 1,600 hours each plus 600 owner production hours supply 7,000; contractors must supply the remaining 3,357. At an assumed $90 yield, the $1,200,000 Scaled case needs approximately 13,333 hours. Five producing employees at 1,600 hours plus 600 owner hours supply 8,600, leaving 4,733 contractor hours. These yields are planning inputs, not observed market rates. Verify that each named contractor can deliver their share without hidden helpers or overlapping service commitments.

Preserve the canonical economics while challenging their operating feasibility. Conservative revenue of $250,000 less $230,000 total costs produces $20,000 owner net profit; Base revenue of $725,000 less $638,000 produces $87,000; Scaled revenue of $1,200,000 less $1,008,000 produces $192,000. Those are 8%, 12.0%, and 16% net margins respectively. The stated five-person employee team in both lower cases does not prove that local wage floors, benefits, contractor fees, and travel fit the cost envelope. Build a wage-loaded schedule before treating any case as executable. Under 29 U.S.C. section 207, covered nonexempt employees generally receive overtime after forty hours in a workweek; apply stricter applicable state rules as well. Record compensable travel between work locations under 29 CFR 785.38 instead of deleting it to rescue a bid. Route density creates value only when it removes actual paid travel or unlocks saleable service capacity. Scheduling software cannot clean another restroom by itself. If the capacity ledger fails, reprice scope, improve route geometry, or decline the work; do not revise the published scorecard or disguise unpaid owner shifts as automation.

## 2. Buy defined contractor outcomes and verify medical-site quality  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Contractor compensation should purchase a defined result rather than imitate an employee timecard with a different tax form. Give C1 a measured floor area, finish specification, furniture responsibility, approved access window, and acceptance test; give C2 a carpet inventory, extraction method constraints, drying requirement, and spotting exceptions. Pay a fixed accepted-package price with a separate change-order rate for newly discovered conditions. For C3 and C4, define reachable surfaces or room-level service scope with equivalent specificity. An illustrative $1,000 periodic-service invoice might support a $550 contractor package, $80 supplies, and $70 allocated inspection and travel, leaving $300 contribution before central overhead. That example is job math, not an additional annual scenario. Require written authorization for extra scope and documented acceptance within two business days; pay undisputed accepted amounts on the agreed schedule. Specify reinspection and correction responsibility without arbitrary invoice penalties. Track correction costs separately so a cheap subcontractor does not appear profitable while FT5 repeatedly repairs the work. Where the company actually controls an individual's ongoing employment relationship, use the appropriate employee arrangement and wage treatment instead of relabeling the same job as a package.

Evaluate worker status under each applicable wage, tax, unemployment, and workers' compensation test; a signed independent-business agreement does not decide all of them. The FLSA definitions in 29 U.S.C. section 203 and federal employment-tax rules in 26 U.S.C. section 3121(d) address different statutory questions. California Labor Code section 2775 can make subcontracting the company's usual cleaning work especially difficult unless an applicable exception and its conditions are satisfied. Document the actual relationship, not merely incorporation papers and insurance certificates. Review who markets services, negotiates prices, supplies equipment, bears business risk, and serves other customers. Customer security procedures and acceptance standards still apply, but do not use those requirements to camouflage employee-like daily control. Before mobilization, obtain current liability evidence, applicable workers' compensation evidence, and required endorsements directly through a verifiable insurance channel. A certificate alone does not amend coverage. Track expiration dates and excluded operations against the actual scope, particularly floor treatments, elevated glass work, and medical environments. If the nine-person model needs more reliable controlled labor than lawful contractor arrangements provide, identify the headcount conflict and rework the operation rather than claiming the same pod still fits.

Make quality measurable at the room and task level. For routine offices, inspect entrances, restrooms, breakrooms, waste stations, and agreed high-touch points against a versioned checklist. For medical sites, obtain the facility's environmental-services protocol and define which rooms, isolation conditions, sharps situations, and regulated waste activities are excluded or separately qualified. Follow disinfectant label directions for surface compatibility, dilution, and wet contact time; 7 U.S.C. section 136j addresses pesticide use inconsistent with labeling. Under 29 CFR 1910.1200, maintain the required hazard communication program, labeling, safety data sheets, and training. Where reasonably anticipated occupational exposure triggers 29 CFR 1910.1030, implement the applicable exposure-control, training, protective equipment, vaccination, and post-exposure requirements. Set an illustrative SLA of immediate isolation and notification for critical hazards, acknowledgment within thirty minutes during contracted coverage, and routine miss correction before the next agreed occupancy window. Specify who dispatches qualified help when that window cannot be met. Use timestamped inspection evidence without capturing patients, charts, screens, or access codes. A checklist score never overrides a critical failure: one unsecured chemical container can fail acceptance even when every other task passes.

## 3. Run one weekly scorecard and close accrual cohorts monthly  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Run a thirty-minute weekly operating review from a single frozen reporting cut, with FT1 accountable for decisions and FT5 preparing inspection exceptions. Use Connecteam or Swept for authorized schedules and field records, and QuickBooks Online or Xero for the accounting ledger; exports must share stable customer, building, contract, and service-period identifiers. Calculate labor-hour variance as actual paid service and travel hours minus bid hours, divided by bid hours. Keep productive-hour yield separate so reduced service does not masquerade as better efficiency. Track first-pass inspection acceptance, critical failures, callback hours, unfilled shifts, overtime hours, invoice disputes, days sales outstanding, and aged receivables. Initial management triggers can include labor variance above 5%, first-pass acceptance below 95%, any critical failure, or any receivable exceeding its contractual due date by fifteen days. These are proposed controls, not historical performance claims. Every exception needs one owner, a dollar or hour exposure, a corrective action, and a dated verification. Review the same exception next week until evidence closes it. Do not average away a failing clinic inside a strong office route or reward a supervisor for low hours when skipped tasks explain the apparent improvement.

Apply a maximum 25% customer concentration policy to each related customer group, measured on trailing twelve-month recognized revenue and again on signed forward recurring revenue. Aggregate buildings controlled by the same payer or termination decision-maker; six locations under one cancellable master agreement are one exposure. Measure each independent contractor counterparty against a separate 25% ceiling on total productive delivery hours and total direct delivery costs, using the larger percentage. Include affiliated contractor entities together and monitor critical specialty dependence even below the cap. The denominator includes employee delivery, so four contractors need not each consume one quarter of subcontractor spend. Model the immediate cash and coverage impact of losing the largest customer or contractor. At Base, 25% of $725,000 is $181,250 annual revenue; that is the customer ceiling, not acceptable profit at risk. During startup, a first customer necessarily breaches a diversified-book target. Record the breach, limit additional exposure, and pursue unrelated accounts until the book qualifies; never report the target as achieved prematurely. Decline incremental scope that increases an existing breach unless a documented restructuring removes the shared termination risk. Diversification must exist in contracts and cash receipts, not just building names.

Close the books by service period, not by the day an invoice happens to be sent. Recognize recurring cleaning revenue as contracted services are delivered; retain advance collections as liabilities until earned and accrue completed uninvoiced work when supported by enforceable contract terms and delivery evidence. Accrue earned payroll, payroll taxes, contractor services, and consumed supplies to the same period. Record equipment purchases, depreciation, deposits, credits, and expected collection losses consistently rather than moving them to improve a sale month. Create contract-start cohorts by month and separate offices from medical sites, recurring work from periodic projects, and original scope from approved changes. Each cohort should show opening accounts, retained accounts, recognized revenue, direct contribution, callback cost, and collection timing. Report gross revenue retention excluding expansion and net revenue retention including expansion, using the same opening customer set. Tie monthly cohort totals to the general ledger and reconcile receivables to subsequent bank deposits. Keep owner distributions outside operating expense and separately disclose owner wages and working hours. The canonical owner-profit figures remain fixed; this accounting bridge explains what was actually earned, what was collected, and which adjustments a buyer would need to evaluate.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the service-week evidence
   - Export schedules, paid hours, inspection results, accepted packages, and customer exceptions.
   - Use one cutoff and stable contract identifiers; record late corrections separately rather than silently replacing the reviewed data.
2. Price each exception
   - Calculate labor variance, callback cost, overdue exposure, and concentration shares.
   - FT1 checks denominators and distinguishes recognized revenue, cash receipts, direct contribution, and annual owner profit.
3. Assign a verifiable correction
   - Name one responsible seat and a completion deadline for each material exception.
   - Specify evidence such as corrected inspection results, an approved scope change, a reconciled accrual, or a collected invoice.
4. Reconcile and carry forward
   - Close verified actions and retain unresolved exceptions in the next review.
   - At month-end, reconcile contract cohorts to the ledger, payroll, contractor accruals, receivables, and bank deposits.

## 4. Build a transferable earnings file before discussing a multiple  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

A buyer purchases enforceable customer relationships and reproducible delivery, not a folder labeled recurring revenue. Build a transfer register containing each signed agreement, current scope, pricing schedule, renewal date, termination rights, assignment language, change-of-control provisions, and required customer permissions. Track agreements held through property managers separately from agreements with building owners, because management changes can interrupt the relationship. Assemble inspection histories, insurance claims, employee records, contractor agreements, equipment ownership, and open disputes in a permission-controlled diligence room. Version your estimating templates, training materials, inspection forms, and operating manuals. Under 17 U.S.C. section 204(a), a copyright ownership transfer generally requires a signed writing from the rights owner or authorized agent. Obtain explicit assignments for contractor-created materials where ownership is intended, identify preexisting materials, and secure sufficient licenses for embedded components. An assignment does not create copyright in facts, methods, or purely functional ideas. Document employee authorship and work-made-for-hire treatment where applicable rather than assuming every file belongs to the company. Keep customer access credentials out of buyer previews; transfer authorized access through controlled account changes at closing. Test whether FT6 can operate the service week without undocumented instructions from the owner.

Use valuation ranges as negotiation illustrations, not asserted transaction comparables. A small owner-operated janitorial book might be screened at 2.0 to 3.0 times verified seller's discretionary earnings, while a genuinely management-supported operation might warrant a separate 3.0 to 4.0 times normalized EBITDA sensitivity. These are different earnings bases and cannot be swapped to obtain a higher headline. The canonical $20,000, $87,000, and $192,000 figures are owner net profit, not automatically either measure. As a transparent illustration only, if diligence establishes that each canonical profit equals verified discretionary earnings, the 2.0 to 3.0 times screen produces $40,000 to $60,000 Conservative, $174,000 to $261,000 Base, and $384,000 to $576,000 Scaled enterprise values. Reject that shortcut if owner compensation, interest, taxes, depreciation, or genuine nonrecurring expenses require a bridge. For an absentee buyer, deduct the incremental market cost of replacing necessary owner work before applying a management-supported multiple. For example, a $75,000 replacement cost absent from the Scaled expense base reduces $192,000 to $117,000 before other normalizations. Never deduct it twice when equivalent management expense is already recorded in the operating accounts.

Negotiate proceeds and obligations alongside the multiple. Enterprise value must be bridged to equity proceeds through agreed debt, cash, working-capital adjustments, transaction expenses, and any holdbacks. Distinguish cash paid at closing from seller notes and earnouts whose collection depends on future performance or buyer credit. A buyer may offer a larger headline price while shifting customer retention risk back to the seller; define retained revenue, service failures, pricing changes, and reporting access before accepting that structure. A premium requires evidence: contracts that can transfer, no customer or delivery counterparty above the stated 25% caps, credible route coverage, clean accruals, and limited dependence on the owner. Compare a sale with the cash-cow alternative using the same replacement labor, maintenance capital expenditure, tax assumptions, and working-capital needs. Neither the $87,000 Base profit nor the $192,000 Scaled profit is automatically distributable cash after equipment replacement and receivable growth. Run an owner-absence trial, document every intervention, and price the remaining dependency. Keep the business when risk-adjusted ongoing cash and manageable owner workload outweigh credible net sale proceeds; pursue an exit when the transfer file and competing funded offers demonstrate that ownership can change without breaking service.
