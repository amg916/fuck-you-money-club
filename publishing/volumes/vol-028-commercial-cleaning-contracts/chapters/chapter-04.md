# Chapter 04: Bidding Methods: Production Rates, Square Footage, and Hourly Formulas

*Production and billable rates, per-square-foot versus per-hour bidding, going sq-ft rates, bid formulas, three ways to price for win and profit, density and beginner pricing mistakes, and ISSA bid-like-a-pro sessions*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Commercial cleaning bidders from OctoClean, Clean Biz Network, The Janitorial Store, CleanGuru, 7 Figure Cleaning, and Clean Ops Guide agree that recurring janitorial work is priced on labor hours, not a flat cents-per-foot number: measure cleanable square footage yourself, divide by a production rate you have tested on your own crews, then multiply hours by visits, 52 weeks, divided by 12, and a loaded billable rate. Rates in play run from 3,000 to 5,000 square feet per cleaner-hour, bill rates of roughly 1.2x to 2x the going janitor wage, a 40/30/30 labor-expense-profit split, and 3 to 5 percent for supplies. The divide comes on square footage: AJ Simmons and CleanGuru reserve per-foot pricing for floor and specialty work, while Darian Jarrett and Rudy Perez run a 19-to-20-cent-per-foot check alongside the hourly bid and split the difference.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Nearly every operator teaching bids lands on the same conclusion: you are selling hours, and square footage is only the input that gets you to hours. AJ Simmons of the Clean Biz Network compares two identical 5,000-square-foot buildings, one all hard floor with glass and tons of cubicles that eats four hours, one light office with three occupants that gets knocked out in an hour to an hour and a half, and concludes you cannot bid commercial cleaning by the foot [7VHAkhs_HG4 @ 00:03:35]. Dan at CleanGuru says that across more than 20 years building a sizable company, the number that mattered was how long a building should take per visit, multiplied by an hourly billing rate [UdGEmUdlNJs @ 00:02:42]. Clean Ops Guide runs the same test on two 10,000-square-foot buildings, a carpeted professional office with two restrooms versus a medical office with 12 exam rooms, and lands on double the labor for the second [9ojOIxznBUc @ 00:02:16]. Square footage gives context, Clean Ops says; the scope tells you the work, and labor determines the cost. Matt Stowe of OctoClean names density as the blind spot beginners keep hitting: bidding an open car showroom like a cube farm, then losing money on trash cans and desks nobody counted [45JohGIULbI @ 00:00:54].

The second point of agreement is that a production rate, meaning square feet one cleaner covers in an hour or minutes per fixture, is the prerequisite to bidding anything, and that borrowed rates are a crutch. Matt Stowe tells new owners to learn the business before bidding regularly, because knowing how long it takes you to clean a toilet, a sink, a floor, or a carpet matters more than any bidding app [SXNq_81x8SY @ 00:00:52]. At ISSA 2024, Matt called the ISSA cleaning times a lowest-common-denominator figure for average employees and said a trained crew should test, test, and test until it beats them [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:12:07]. Steve Hansen of The Janitorial Store is blunter: the heck with industry averages, because running somebody else's numbers means you never reach the true profit number on a job, and bidding software may just use those averages to calculate your time [baQYWkWt7lQ @ 00:01:28]. The shared warning underneath is that the owner's own pace is not a production rate until hired staff can repeat it. Matt says companies in this industry fail miserably once the core owners walk away from the job and the employees they hire cannot reproduce the work [SXNq_81x8SY @ 00:01:30].

Third, the walkthrough is a measuring job. Matt tells salespeople not to trust the square footage the prospect hands over, to measure every single thing, and points out how often a rep comes back with a great bid only for operations to find four restrooms nobody accounted for [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:20:54]. AJ's method is a laser measure pointed wall to wall, length times width for every area, summed into cleanable square footage rather than the whole building [vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:05:55]. Once the hours exist, the speakers agree the hourly figure cannot be the cleaner's wage alone: Matt's follow-up breakdown says bidding is production rates, labor costs, material costs, back-office expenses, and other overhead, plus your profit [cVCpNgEj_zc @ 00:01:04]. There is also wide agreement on where per-foot pricing does belong. Carolyn Arellano quotes routine janitorial and commercial cleaning by the hour but prices carpet, tile, and VCT stripping and waxing by the square foot, the same split AJ uses for strip-and-wax, carpet cleaning, and pressure washing [IJlyq2UAXqU @ 00:01:14]. The prerequisite list that falls out is short: a tested production rate, a measured building, a tight service schedule that spells out what the crew will and will not touch, and a loaded rate before a number goes on paper.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The arithmetic is the same chain everywhere: cleanable square feet divided by production rate equals hours per visit; hours times visits per week times 52, divided by 12, times a billing rate equals the monthly recurring charge. Steve Hansen's production-rate method shows it with real numbers: a 12,500-square-foot account that takes 3.25 hours works out to 3,846.15 square feet per hour, cleaned three days a week that is 42.25 hours a month, and at a $22 billing rate the invoice is $929 plus any city or state sales tax [rGpgBfBHKg0 @ 00:00:58]. Steve also bends the rate for frequency: 3,500 square feet an hour on a five-night office drops to about 3,000 at three nights, because more soil builds up between visits [baQYWkWt7lQ @ 00:04:07]. Rudy Perez of 7 Figure Cleaning uses 3,000 to 5,000 square feet per cleaner-hour, picks 4,000 on a 50,000-square-foot building for 12.5 hours a night, bills $40 an hour for a $500 daily rate, and multiplies by 22 cleaning days instead of four weeks to land $11,000 a month [1_z-Kgj0GYM @ 00:01:35]. Clean Ops Guide rounds up deliberately, turning a 4,500-an-hour office into 2.5 labor hours and a 2,000-an-hour medical office into 5 [9ojOIxznBUc @ 00:04:17].

The billable rate is where the numbers get specific. Matt Stowe defines the cleaner minimum wage as the going rate incoming janitors actually earn in your territory, not the state, city, or county minimum, then multiplies it by 1.2 to 1.5 and has gone as high as 1.75 in California; $18 times 1.5 gives a $27 bill rate [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:23:04]. In OctoClean's formula breakdown Matt says companies typically run 30 to 35 percent for overhead plus profit, and builds high, medium, and low prices at 50, 35, and maybe 25 percent markup [SXNq_81x8SY @ 00:06:06]. AJ Simmons itemizes instead: pull the after-hours cleaner wage from Indeed or Monster, say $15, add about $3 for payroll tax and roughly 50 cents an hour for supplies to reach $18.50, then stack a flat $12 for profit to bill $30.50, which lands close to double the wage, the shortcut AJ leans on [vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:09:26]. Steve Hansen has seen commercial billable rates from $18 to $55 an hour, holds one general-cleaning rate regardless of building size or frequency, raises it only for floor care such as strip-and-wax or marble polishing, and tells owners to survey competitors and sit in the middle of the local span [baQYWkWt7lQ @ 00:06:50].

Three worked examples show how the math closes. Carolyn Arellano's formula adds headcount: hourly rate times hours per visit times employees times days per week times 52, divided by 12. At $25 an hour, 4 hours, 5 days, that is a $2,166.66 base, plus 3 percent, or $64.98, for monthly supplies, and the client sees one monthly figure and the visit count, never the man-hours [IJlyq2UAXqU @ 00:06:46]. Matt's fixture method anchors on the restroom person: ISSA puts a toilet, sink, or urinal at about three minutes, OctoClean rounds to four, so 10 fixtures is 40 minutes, rounded up to 0.75 hours, times three team roles, times five days, times 52, divided by 12, roughly 49 labor hours a month, with the manager told the job takes no more than 48 [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:25:26]. AJ reverse-checks margin: a one-day-a-week, two-hour job pencils to $264 a month, gets rounded up to an in-house minimum of $275, and instead of a 70/30 subcontractor split handing over $192.50, AJ pays about $160, roughly $18 an hour for two hours at 4.33 visits a month, keeping $115, a 42 percent margin, because on accounts that small AJ wants 40 to 50 percent [vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:15:43].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify on the phone
   - Extract pain, days per week, day or evening service, and cleanable square footage or headcount before scheduling a walkthrough.
   - Target three-to-five-day accounts, favor evening work where efficiencies go up, and multiply headcount by about 200 sq ft when the prospect does not know cleanable space [OctoClean Media — zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:18:31].
2. Walk and measure everything
   - Tour with notepad, pen, and laser; log square footage and flooring per room and count sinks, toilets, urinals, and stalls.
   - Measure every square inch of cleanable space, office by office, conference room, common areas, then fixture counts in each restroom [OctoClean Media — SXNq_81x8SY @ 00:03:24].
3. Convert footage to hours
   - Divide cleanable square feet by your own production rate, adjusted downward for lower frequency, and round up.
   - A five-night rate of 3,500 sq ft per hour drops to about 3,000 at three days a week because soil builds up between visits [The Janitorial Store — baQYWkWt7lQ @ 00:04:07].
4. Price, floor, and reverse-check
   - Multiply hours by visits, 52, divide by 12, times bill rate; apply the in-house minimum and back out cleaner pay to confirm margin.
   - Round a $264 result up to a $275 minimum, compare a 70/30 sub split against paying about $160, and keep roughly 42% on a small account [AJ SIMMONS — vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:13:09].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether a cents-per-square-foot figure belongs in routine janitorial pricing at all. AJ Simmons calls it winging it: rates seen in the wild run from two cents to twenty cents per square foot, and ten cents on a 5,000 square foot building serviced five nights a week inflates to about ten grand a month, while the same building timed at a 3,000 square foot production rate and billed at double the cleaner wage lands near $60 a visit [7VHAkhs_HG4 @ 00:01:13]. Darian Jarrett runs the opposite playbook, carrying a portfolio average of about 19 cents per square foot, pricing a 10,000 square foot building at roughly $1,900, then cross-checking against a 5,000 square feet per hour pace at $50 an hour and meeting in the middle [QlIScHN-MPE @ 00:00:30]. Rudy Perez of 7 Figure Cleaning formalizes that blend as a third method: a 50,000 square foot, five-day building priced at $11,000 by labor hours and $10,000 at 20 cents per square foot gets split to $10,500 [1_z-Kgj0GYM @ 00:02:43]. Dan at CleanGuru adds the missing context that the going square foot rate traditionally means a monthly, Monday-through-Friday figure used by large contractors on Class A buildings, so quoting it on a three-day account misfires [UdGEmUdlNJs @ 00:02:04].

Practitioners also split on whose production numbers to trust. Steve of The Janitorial Store says the heck with industry averages, because borrowed numbers, including the defaults baked into bidding software, make it impossible to see true profit on a job; beginners may borrow rates only until their own accounts can be tracked [baQYWkWt7lQ @ 00:01:28]. Matt Stowe of OctoClean treats ISSA cleaning times as a floor rather than a ceiling, describing them as lowest-common-denominator numbers for average employees that a trained crew should beat through repeated testing [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:12:07]. AJ Simmons takes the most beginner-friendly line: lean on a bidding calculator such as Calculated Clean, which estimates a 5,000 square foot office at 1.67 hours on a 3,000 rate and adds toilets, urinals, mirrors and sinks, until real experience produces a real rate [7VHAkhs_HG4 @ 00:04:28]. Carolyn Arellano lands in between, having bought the ISSA book and still using it, yet trusting accumulated experience from comparable accounts over the printed times [IJlyq2UAXqU @ 00:04:24]. The practical fork for an operator is clear: borrowed rates get the first bid out the door, but every one of these operators expects the stopwatch on live accounts to replace them.

The markup math diverges just as hard. Matt at OctoClean multiplies the going janitor wage by 1.20 to 1.50, has pushed it to 1.75 in California, and builds a high, median and low price so reps can drop the number on the spot without a sales-manager routine [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:23:04]. AJ Simmons simply doubles cleaner pay, or itemizes a $15 wage, $3 payroll tax and 50 cents of supplies before stacking $12 of profit to reach $30.50 an hour [vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:09:26]. Frequency splits them too. Steve at The Janitorial Store holds a flat $30 billable rate for general cleaning regardless of building size or visits, adjusting only the production rate downward, from 3,500 to about 3,000 square feet an hour, when a five-day account drops to three days because soil builds up; only floor care like strip and wax earns a higher rate [baQYWkWt7lQ @ 00:06:17]. Darian Jarrett instead lets the per-visit price slide as frequency rises, charging $51 for a 35-minute weekly warehouse but about $48 a visit for a 40-minute office cleaned twice weekly, and about $160 for a weekly church that runs two and a half hours [QlIScHN-MPE @ 00:05:56].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Underbidding is the first wound most operators report. Carolyn Arellano states flatly that newcomers will underbid, that it happened to them too, and that the fix is timing every task from the first account onward [IJlyq2UAXqU @ 00:03:18]. Clean Ops Guide walks the economics: a $2,000 monthly contract looks like a win until wages, supplies, operating expense and the extra hours spent managing the account leave almost nothing, and shaving a five-hour estimate to three hours to look competitive only moves the shortfall into rushed cleaning, employee frustration, declining quality or the owner working extra hours [9ojOIxznBUc @ 00:01:04]. A $20 hourly wage is not a $20 labor cost either, once payroll taxes, workers compensation, insurance and training land on top. Karen Denton names the copy-paste trap, recalling copying franchise bids and watching every client tear those proposals apart [h7lD-HNIQ_o @ 00:00:43]. Matt Stowe adds the anchoring error seen across consulting clients: asking a prospect what they pay now and setting the price there, or blurting a number off a price list before any due diligence, when the correct answer is that a lot of questions come first [nBB03PNbUHI @ 00:04:50].

The second cluster is scope blindness on the walkthrough. An OctoClean coach says density is what beginners miss most: bidding an open car dealership showroom, where the crew mostly dust mops and mops, the same as a cube farm loaded with trash cans and desks ends with an operator asking why they are not making money [45JohGIULbI @ 00:00:54]. On the ISSA Media panel, Matt describes the distribution-facility version, bidding cleanable square footage while ignoring the hour of walking to reach a single restroom on the far side of the building; customers understand non-productive time but will not raise it, so the contractor must [nBB03PNbUHI @ 00:05:47]. Bad inputs compound it. A prospect claiming 655,000 square feet turned out to have an 823 square foot office with the rest warehouse, and salespeople incentivized on commission have handed operations accounts with four restrooms nobody counted, which is why OctoClean makes reps measure every square inch themselves [zAGjNnpTUcY @ 00:21:16]. Rudy Perez flags the gut-versus-formula gap as its own trap: walking a site and sensing five hours a night while the bid math says three leaves the number badly off, which is why that operator averages the hourly and square foot results [1_z-Kgj0GYM @ 00:03:38].

The third trap is pricing for today's company instead of tomorrow's. In OctoClean's detailed analysis, Matt warns that pricing at the I-do-it-myself rate means immediate losses once bookkeeping, account management, supervision and customer service get outsourced or hired, admitting to having lived it and seen it hundreds of times; churn and overtime are listed as hidden costs, and product, equipment and materials are pegged near 5 percent of contract value [cVCpNgEj_zc @ 00:06:12]. OctoClean's formula video names replication as the other killer: too many companies fail miserably after the core owners walk away and hired employees cannot reproduce the work [SXNq_81x8SY @ 00:01:30]. AJ Simmons guards the bottom of the ledger with an in-house minimum, refusing to go below roughly $275 a month on once-weekly work even when the formula spits out $264, then reverse-checks the split: at a straight 70/30 the subcontractor gets about $192.50, but paying $160 instead leaves $115, roughly a 42 percent margin [vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:12:34]. The same operator also stopped telling people to simply double cleaner pay because too many were trying to pay people way too much, so the full itemized build replaced the shortcut [vqE3IAR7B18 @ 00:17:21].
