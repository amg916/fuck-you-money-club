## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on a blunt reality: roughly 90% of commercial prospects already have a cleaner, so the first contract is won on dependability, pain diagnosis and follow-through rather than the lowest bid. AJ Simmons runs a phone-first machine of 100 calls a day, projecting 10-20 appointments and 1-2 closes a month, while Mike Mak and Commercial Cleaning CEO lean on power-base referrals, BNI and free proof cleans. Pricing runs on production rates, not square footage: Joseph D Adams uses 3,500-4,000 sq ft per hour for offices and targets a 30% margin. OctoClean Media pushes a pain-decision-budget qualification and floats a number 25-30% higher when budgets stall. The splits: AJ's relentless 'wearsome' follow-up versus Clean Ops Guide's respect for the client's timeline, and whether a website and Google profile matter at all.

## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

The first contract is a trust sale before it is a cleaning sale. Joseph Adams of Clean Ops Guide frames the buyer's core question as whether you are 'dependable,' because a cleaning crew gets unsupervised, after-hours access to the building [XySWgKh0nEY @ 00:00:17]. Janitorial Empire says the same thing in commercial terms: clients buy reliability, and the starting kit is a professional email, phone number, capability information and insurance documentation, aimed first at small offices before medical, dental, daycare, churches, retail and small warehouses, with the first account doing double duty as the reference that sells the next one [8ireVJkHvoo @ 00:00:29]. AJ Simmons adds the hard market fact that shapes every pitch: '90%' of prospects already have a cleaner, and a prospect with no cleaner usually has no budget, so that prospect gets skipped [Xvc-7yo1lng @ 00:03:35]. AJ also narrows the target map. Skyscrapers, anything that says 'go through corporate' or 'vendors list,' and connected buildings run by a management company that may only issue RFPs 'once a year' are out; individually managed standalone or medium-size buildings are in [ZDdd0TDbbFo @ 00:02:58]. The operator takeaway is consistent across these voices: start small, start reachable, and walk in assuming you are displacing an incumbent whose weak spot is reliability and service, not price.

Price is not the battlefield these practitioners choose. OctoClean Media's 30-year sales veteran qualifies every deal on three axes, pain, decision and budget, and opens by asking 'why am I here'; if the answer is the lowest possible bid, the account is not a fit and the seller walks [1tf8JMWxi98 @ 00:01:04]. Cleaning Launch, whose host reports building a $5M+ company, states flatly that clients do not pick the cheapest quote and that lowballers should be ignored [V84a_KWRYU4 @ 00:00:15]. OctoClean Media's proposal video goes further, noting that some winners are the highest bidder and that proposals lose by being interchangeable rather than expensive [OzSRs5osKKI @ 00:00:32]. AJ Simmons reports the switching order from the field: service first, then price, then communication and relationship, and AJ digs it out with one question, 'How are you guys handling your cleaning services right now?' [Xvc-7yo1lng @ 00:04:39]. The answers to AJ's question decode the incumbent's weakness: 'We got a lady' signals under-the-table help, and in-house cleaning signals dust, which is why high dusting sits in AJ's routine. The shared rule is to diagnose the incumbent's failure in the buyer's own words and price against that failure, not against the cheapest competitor in the local market.

The walkthrough is where practitioners agree the contract is actually won or lost. Janitorial Empire's checklist covers square footage, restrooms, offices, flooring, headcount, frequency, kitchens, consumables, security and complaints about the current cleaner, and the channel's verdict on bidding is blunt: 'Winning a contract that loses money isn't winning' [8ireVJkHvoo @ 00:04:28]. Joseph D Adams of Green Line Operations, with 25 years in the trade and a full-time job still running, admits on the ONLY MAIDS PODCAST hot seat that the very first job was underbid [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:05:40]. Clean Ops Guide warns that two 10,000 sq ft offices can carry completely different workloads, so flooring, restrooms and high-touch areas get inspected and the client gets heard instead of the footage simply being plugged into a formula [XySWgKh0nEY @ 00:02:50]. Follow-up closes the consensus loop. Janitorial Empire observes that 'a surprising amount of business is won simply because one company followed up while another didn't,' and pairs it with a simple rule: send scope, frequency, monthly price and terms, then follow up after a few business days [8ireVJkHvoo @ 00:04:59]. Across all three channels the pattern holds: measure honestly, price for profit, and be the company still in contact when the incumbent slips.

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

AJ Simmons runs the most explicit numbers game: 100 calls a day, 500 a week, 2,000 a month, which AJ projects into 10 to 20 appointments and at least one to two closes, each adding $1,000 to $2,000 a month until the book reaches $10,000 to $12,000 monthly and six figures inside a year [ZDdd0TDbbFo @ 00:03:56]. The opener asks for 'the person in charge of hiring the cleaning company,' and appointments are always proposed two to three days out at 10:00. Joseph D Adams works a slower, part-time cadence while holding a full-time job: five businesses a week, roughly 20 a month, leaving a business card and capability statement to land two or three walkthroughs a month [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:01:00]. Janitorial Empire builds the list from Google Maps, logging name, address, phone, website, contact and date, and argues that '50 qualified prospects' beat hundreds of random calls [8ireVJkHvoo @ 00:02:03]. Mike Mak, ten years in, found cold calling, door-to-door, Bark and Yelp produced cheapest-bid clients and pivoted to warm channels: the doctor, car dealership or chiropractor already in your life, plus BNI groups of 15 to 30 people that Mak expects to yield three to four offices [G2qoM4LqGdM @ 00:04:46].

Pricing mechanics center on production rates rather than square-foot guesses. OctoClean Media calls bidding by the square foot a 'rookie mistake' and has estimators go room by room, counting sinks and toilets and photographing dispensers and hot spots before any number is built [j_wLBMK7Flk @ 00:02:25]. Joseph D Adams supplies the actual rates: 3,500 to 4,000 sq ft per hour for offices, medical spoken as '22 to 28,' and a worked example of 6,000 sq ft divided by 25 landing at 2.4 hours [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:05:57]. On margin, Adams targets 30 percent: a $2,100 cost becomes a $3,000 price and $900 profit, going as low as about 25 percent for strategic accounts [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:07:34]. Janitorial Empire lists the cost stack that has to sit under that price, labor hours, payroll burden, supplies, equipment, transport, insurance, overhead and profit, and shows the hour math with three hours a night across five nights coming to about 65 hours a month [8ireVJkHvoo @ 00:04:28]. The same channel warns that underbidding damages the company. The street sequence is therefore fixed: count fixtures, convert footage into hours with a production rate, load the full cost stack, then add margin before the proposal is written, and refuse to bid below it.

The close runs on qualification scripts and speed. OctoClean Media never asks 'what's your budget'; the question is what solving the problem is 'worth,' and when a buyer resists, the line is 'we're going to be too high, should we stop now,' followed by a number floated 25 to 30 percent higher [1tf8JMWxi98 @ 00:04:18]. Joseph D Adams sends the proposal within 24 hours of the walkthrough, so the numbers land while the visit is still fresh in the buyer's mind [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:09:17]. AJ Simmons compresses it further into a 'wearsome' cadence: walkthrough in the morning, proposal by the afternoon, a call before the contact gets off work, never more than a week between touches, and no stopping until the prospect says stop [Xvc-7yo1lng @ 00:07:43]. Cleaning Launch adds a framing move: present a top-to-bottom deep clean, normally 'a premium charge,' as included because the contract is recurring, knowing the first clean takes about double the time anyway [V84a_KWRYU4 @ 00:04:16]. Decision mapping matters too; OctoClean Media calls 'are you the decision maker' a rookie move and asks instead how the last decision was made, by committee or by one person. Each tactic shortens the gap between interest and signature while keeping price anchored to value rather than to the cheapest bid.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is how the first contract gets sourced. AJ Simmons runs a pure phone machine: 100 calls a day, 500 a week, 2,000 a month, feeding 10 to 20 appointments and at least 1 to 2 closes, with each month stacking $1,000 to $2,000 until the book reaches $10,000 to $12,000 a month. He says social posts, ads, a Google My Business profile and a fancy website are not needed [ZDdd0TDbbFo @ 00:04:27], and he ranks the phone over door-to-door because it is quicker and burns no gas [ZDdd0TDbbFo @ 00:09:53]. Mike Mak, ten years in, reports the opposite result from cold outreach: cold calling, door-to-door, Bark and Yelp delivered clients who only wanted the cheapest bid [G2qoM4LqGdM @ 00:01:12]. His replacement is the power base, meaning the doctor, the car dealership or the chiropractor seen every two weeks, plus a BNI chapter of 15 to 30 people where he expects 3 to 4 offices. Matt of Commercial Cleaning CEO leans toward Mak, insisting the business is relationship-based even when leads arrive through Google Ads or cold email, and he pushes beginners to work referrals and introductions first [SU1OB2thaKo @ 00:05:04]. The operator choice is volume dialing versus warm network leverage, and the two camps report very different lead quality.

Follow-up intensity is the second fault line. AJ Simmons preaches being 'wearsome': walkthrough in the morning, proposal by the afternoon, a phone call before the prospect gets off work, and never stopping until they tell you to, with no more than one week between contacts [Xvc-7yo1lng @ 00:07:43]. Joseph Adams of Clean Ops Guide runs a softer cadence: confirm receipt, offer clarification, track every communication and 'respect the client's timeline,' while warning, 'Don't assume you lost the contract' [XySWgKh0nEY @ 00:05:07]. Both agree silence kills deals; they disagree on how hard to lean. The two camps also report different odds. AJ tells operators nobody closes 100 percent and that a single lead is '50/50' [Xvc-7yo1lng @ 00:11:53]. The Cleaning Launch host, who reports building a $5M-plus company, frames a typical closing rate of 10 percent and claims anti-cheap positioning lifts it to 25 to 35 percent [V84a_KWRYU4 @ 00:01:22]. That positioning tells the prospect outright, 'We won't be the cheapest,' then backs it with good wages, frequent inspections, same-day response and fixing problems at the company's own cost. Run AJ's math and one appointment is a coin flip; run the Cleaning Launch math and you need roughly three to four proposals per win, so budget prospecting volume accordingly.

Qualifying and pricing method split along the same line. OctoClean Media's 30-year salesman calls 'what's your budget' the wrong question: ask what solving the problem is worth, and if the prospect stonewalls, say 'we're going to be too high, should we stop now' and float a number 25 to 30 percent higher [1tf8JMWxi98 @ 00:04:18]. AJ Simmons goes direct, asking about budget or what the account currently pays during the walkthrough itself [Xvc-7yo1lng @ 00:11:19]. On the bid math, AJ's door-to-door method grabs the square footage and runs it through the Clean Business Network bidding calculator [ZDdd0TDbbFo @ 00:01:01]. OctoClean labels bidding by the square foot a rookie mistake and builds price from production rates after a room-by-room inventory that counts sinks and toilets and photographs dispensers and hot spots [j_wLBMK7Flk @ 00:02:25]. The practical difference: the calculator camp can quote from a door knock and a number, while the production-rate camp needs a full walkthrough before any price leaves the building. OctoClean adds that if the prospect's stated reason for the meeting is the lowest possible bid, you are not a fit and should walk. AJ instead keeps low-intent prospects warm with a complimentary quote kept on file in case the incumbent drops the ball.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Underbidding is the failure practitioners own up to most directly. Joseph D Adams of Green Line Operations, with 25 years in the trade, says his first job was underbid [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:05:40]. His fix is production-rate and margin discipline: offices run at 3,500 to 4,000 square feet an hour, and his worked example divides 6,000 square feet by 25 to land at 2.4 hours. Then comes a 30 percent margin target: $2,100 in cost priced at $3,000 leaves $900 in profit, and he has dropped to about 25 percent only for strategic accounts [aQj7G__L-iE @ 00:07:34]. Janitorial Empire prices from labor hours, payroll burden, supplies, equipment, transport, insurance, overhead and profit, using a 3-hour, 5-night account at roughly 65 hours a month, and warns flatly that underbidding damages the company [8ireVJkHvoo @ 00:04:42]. Clean Ops Guide attacks the same trap from capacity: a small office can beat a medical facility, so check facility needs, staff capacity and profitability, because winning work you cannot manage 'isn't growth' [XySWgKh0nEY @ 00:02:33]. The economics check is simple: if labor hours times your loaded cost leave less than the margin floor, the contract is a loss you are paying to keep, and every extra night of it compounds the damage.

The second cluster of losses happens before price is even weighed. The Cleaning Launch host reports losing a $50,000-a-year contract, a quarter million dollars over five years, because the proposal never mentioned security practices: the alarm, the locks, the lights and calling security. Despite the most competitive quote, they finished 'second best' [V84a_KWRYU4 @ 00:07:28]. AJ Simmons flags targeting traps: skyscrapers, any building that says 'go through corporate' or points to a vendors list, and connected buildings run by a management company that may only run RFPs once a year. He tells operators to work individually managed standalone or medium-size buildings instead [ZDdd0TDbbFo @ 00:02:58]. On his own first bank walkthrough, AJ arrived 20 minutes early with a laser measure and learned the branch manager was not the decision-maker; a regional manager would judge the bid straight on the number by phone [IMsJ1NxKfUk @ 00:04:39]. OctoClean Media's closer calls 'are you the decision maker' a rookie question and instead asks how the last decision was made, by committee or by one person [1tf8JMWxi98 @ 00:03:25]. Map the buying chain on the first visit, or the proposal lands on a desk that cannot sign it.

After the signature, the traps shift to operations and labor. Steve Hansen of The Janitorial Store warns that small favors snowball: a client adds one extra here and another there until crews are doing 6 to 7 unbilled tasks and more than 30 minutes of free work. His fix is work orders, billed additional work, and starting that conversation at the prospect stage [EL4NN_-rX7o @ 00:09:27]. Matt of Commercial Cleaning CEO endorses free cleanings to build proof assets, meaning two to five written reviews, Google five-star reviews or video testimonials, but draws a hard line: 'Now, I wouldn't clean for free recurring forever' [SU1OB2thaKo @ 00:15:20]. On subcontracted labor, Matt's caller Dalen vets cleaners on their own supplies, insurance, and a test clean at an aunt's house, where the aunt asks about rebooking directly to see whether the cleaner poaches the client [o6xwPfuhTI4 @ 00:05:50]. Matt also tells operators to reject net 30 and net 60 terms and pay within 5 to 7 days of being paid, so subcontractors stay loyal instead of drifting [o6xwPfuhTI4 @ 00:18:59]. Slow pay and an unbilled scope both erode the margin you fought for at the proposal stage, and neither shows up until the first month closes.
