## 1. Identify the Debtor, Budget Owner, and Person Who Can Accept Work  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

The person complaining about dirty restrooms often cannot commit the entity that pays. In an office building, the facilities manager defines performance, the property manager administers purchasing, the ownership entity funds operating expenses, and accounts payable releases approved invoices. Ask which legal entity will sign, whether the manager signs as agent, and whether the contract actually creates an obligation for that principal. A management company's logo does not establish its liability for an owner's unpaid balance. Obtain the entity's exact name, billing address, purchase-order requirements, authorized signer, and acceptance contact before staffing starts. Inspect signing authority through an executed management authorization or direct confirmation from the principal where warranted. For multi-site customers, identify whether one master agreement binds every location or each subsidiary owes separately. A regional manager's verbal promise cannot repair an invoice issued to the wrong affiliate. The buyer's acute pain is usually operational: tenant complaints threaten renewals, inspection failures consume management time, and absentee cleaners force expensive emergency coverage. Sell a measured response to that pain, then connect the response to an authorized budget and a named payment owner.

Medical buyers add exposure-control requirements, but a clinic's regulatory duties do not automatically make every janitorial service mandatory. Under 29 CFR 1910.1030, employers with covered occupational exposure must maintain appropriate exposure controls, housekeeping practices, and training. Determine whether your workers reasonably anticipate contact with blood or other potentially infectious materials; define sharps, regulated waste, and clinical equipment exclusions accordingly. OSHA's hazard-communication requirements under 29 CFR 1910.1200 also affect chemical inventories, labels, safety data sheets, and employee training. An infection-prevention lead may set protocols without holding purchasing authority. Map that technical approver alongside the practice administrator and accounts-payable contact. The medical supervisor pod play works when several nearby sites share trained relief coverage and inspection capacity, rather than one exhausted lead absorbing every absence. Price each site's actual task frequencies, surface requirements, access restrictions, and documentation burden. Explain precisely which records the customer receives and when defects are corrected. Avoid promising an infection outcome that ordinary inspection logs cannot establish. The check purchases specified work and accountable execution, not an unsupported claim that outsourcing transfers every duty away from the facility.

Counterparty screening begins before the walkthrough becomes a free consulting engagement. Search the applicable secretary-of-state register, verify the operating entity, request two trade references, and ask for actual payment experience rather than a generic recommendation. With appropriate access, review commercial credit information from Dun & Bradstreet or Experian Business and public court records for relevant insolvency proceedings. Distinguish a slow administrative process from a buyer whose budget is already exhausted. Ask whether invoices require a funded purchase order, monthly service receipt, property-level coding, or submission through Coupa or SAP Ariba. A $5,000 monthly account on forty-five-day terms can leave roughly $7,500 outstanding at steady billing cadence, before disputes or startup timing extend exposure. Set a written credit limit against that balance and your payroll reserve. For a thinly capitalized property entity, negotiate advance payment, a deposit with explicit application rules, or a separately executed support obligation from a creditworthy party. If those protections are rejected, reduce the initial service period instead of hiding credit risk inside a higher square-foot price. Record the decision and review it whenever ownership, management, or payment behavior changes.

## 2. Separate the Payment Rail from the Conditions That Release Money  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Payment design has three layers: the contractual debt, the event making it due, and the mechanism moving funds. Wire and ACH are transfer rails. Retainers, milestone holds, escrow, factoring, and statutory disbursement describe funding or release structures that ultimately use a transfer rail. Do not present them as interchangeable protections. A wire can move money quickly while leaving a disputed invoice unresolved; an escrow balance can be fully funded while release conditions remain impossible to satisfy. For recurring service, specify the billing period, submission date, acceptance procedure, due date, and treatment of disputed versus undisputed amounts. Where commercially agreed, require specific written defect notices within a defined period and preserve the customer's genuine contractual remedies. Use Jobber or QuickBooks Online to issue consistent invoices, but verify that the customer's portal actually received them. A delivery email from your accounting system is not evidence that a procurement portal accepted the invoice. Store the portal receipt, purchase-order match, and service evidence together. Tie each payment to an invoice and customer entity, rather than clearing several unrelated deposits against whichever balance looks closest in the ledger.

ACH usually fits repeat commercial billing when the customer authorizes the arrangement and the bank or processor supports the intended transaction. Obtain the required authorization, disclose timing, preserve its evidence, and follow the applicable network and processor rules. Business-account returns and consumer-account protections differ; do not assume one universal reversal deadline. An ACH initiation notice is not collected cash, and availability does not eliminate every return or recovery risk. For wires, confirm beneficiary details through a previously verified telephone number whenever instructions change. Never trust replacement bank details merely because they arrive inside an existing email thread. For platform checkout, evaluate processing charges, reserves, dispute handling, payout timing, and whether recurring billing is supported for the account. If a negotiated card-processing example costs 2.9% plus $0.30, a $5,000 payment costs $145.30; that is 24.2% of a $600 monthly profit before any permitted recovery. Treat those figures as an illustration, not a vendor quotation. Prefer verified ACH where appropriate, while retaining a documented alternative for customers whose procurement systems require another method. Reconcile gross receivables, processor fees, and net bank deposits separately.

Public-prime subcontract collections require a different mental model from direct agency billing. The federal Prompt Payment Act and implementing rules address covered federal payment obligations; they do not turn every private subcontract invoice into a Treasury receivable. Read the actual award and subcontract, including applicable clauses, acceptance procedures, and any contingent-payment provision. For qualifying direct federal contracts, FAR 32.905 describes payment documentation and proper invoice requirements, while 5 CFR part 1315 addresses prompt payment administration. A missing acceptance record can delay the practical collection process even when the service crew performed correctly. Escrow is more useful for a disputed remediation assignment or unusually large startup than for routine nightly cleaning. Name the independent holder, permitted deductions, release evidence, deadlines, and dispute process. A retainer should specify whether it is an advance applied to future invoices or security held until termination; accounting follows the arrangement. Factoring changes who advances cash and may change who collects it, not whether the customer owes for accepted work. Examine recourse, reserves, notice requirements, and all charges before assigning invoices. A three-percent charge on a $5,000 invoice consumes $150 before additional fees, leaving only $450 of the illustrative $600 profit.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Register the paying entity
   - Match the signed customer entity to its purchase order and vendor record.
   - Confirm authorized contacts, billing route, tax documentation, and independently verified payment instructions before deployment.
2. Capture the service event
   - Record completed tasks, exceptions, supervisor review, and authorized extras.
   - Use timestamped inspection records while excluding patient information and unrelated confidential material.
3. Submit a proper invoice
   - Send the invoice through the agreed channel and retain its acceptance receipt.
   - Match service dates, location codes, purchase-order balances, and supporting evidence before starting the collection clock.
4. Reconcile and escalate
   - Match bank settlement to the invoice and investigate shortages immediately.
   - Separate fees, disputed deductions, unapplied advances, and returned payments; escalate according to contractual notice periods.

## 3. Protect the Fee with Task Evidence, Change Orders, and Real Labor Math  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

The direct recurring facility play begins with measured tasks, not a competitor's square-foot price. Use ISSA production-rate references as a starting hypothesis, then time representative work under the site's actual furniture density, access constraints, soil load, and equipment conditions. For an illustrative $5,000 monthly contract, twenty visits requiring two workers for three hours each create 120 direct labor hours. At a fully burdened $26 hourly assumption, direct labor costs $3,120. Allocate $480 for supervision and relief, $250 for supplies, $300 for equipment and insurance, and $250 for administration. Total allocated cost is $4,400, leaving $600, or 12%. Those allocations must include all relevant expenses within the model's definition; unpaid owner labor should not silently subsidize the bid. A calendar requiring additional visits needs a different monthly allowance or an annualized schedule. Sixteen unpriced extra hours at the same cost add $416 and reduce that month's profit to $184. Protect the fee through explicit frequencies, periodic-work schedules, consumable responsibilities, and signed extra-work authority. A customer requesting free weekend coverage is changing the purchased capacity, not merely asking the crew to be helpful.

Reconcile the portfolio to the canonical scorecard before presenting upside. Conservative annual cleaning revenue is $250,000, costs are $230,000, and owner net profit is $20,000 at 8%, with 1 FT + 3 C. Base revenue is $725,000, costs are $638,000, and owner net profit is $87,000 at 12.0%, again with 1 FT + 3 C. Scaled revenue is $1,200,000, costs are $1,008,000, and owner net profit is $192,000 at 16%, with 2 FT + 4 C. Twelve annualized contracts matching the $5,000 example produce $720,000 revenue, $633,600 cost, and $86,400 profit; a $5,000 annual extra-work package costing $4,400 completes the Base totals exactly. This is an arithmetic reconciliation, not evidence that those accounts are available. The Conservative staffing case needs particular scrutiny: five employees working 2,080 paid hours each at $24 burdened hourly cost already require $249,600, exceeding its entire cost allowance before contractors. Preserve the published roster and economics, but identify this feasibility conflict rather than relabeling full-time workers as occasional labor. A real launch budget requires verified wages, hours, benefits, coverage, and utilization before that scenario can support an operating commitment.

Fee protection is strongest when the records explain both performance and the price. Attach a room-and-task matrix, inspection method, cure procedure, extra-work rates, and designated change-order authority to the agreement. Record blocked access, missing utilities, hazardous conditions, and customer-requested deferrals on the service date. A supervisor's photo of an occupied conference room explains an exception better than a retrospective argument about whether the cleaner skipped it. Use Connecteam or Swept for scheduling and inspection evidence where their features fit the workflow; restrict access and export essential records so evidence survives a vendor change. Do not use broad employee restrictions as a substitute for customer service or enforceable contract drafting. A customer non-solicitation provision requires jurisdiction-specific review and does not justify withholding workers' wages. Under 29 U.S.C. 207, covered nonexempt employees generally receive overtime after forty hours in a workweek; state requirements can add obligations. Calling workers subcontractors or issuing Form 1099 does not settle classification. Examine the applicable federal and state tests and actual relationship. For nonpayment, follow contractual notice and suspension procedures while protecting access credentials, customer property, and safe site handover rather than abruptly abandoning a sensitive location.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Verify Four Conditions Before Funding the First Shift**
> 
> Mobilization requires evidence that scope, credit, labor, and collection mechanics agree.
> - Verify the debtor, signer authority, purchase-order capacity, acceptance contact, and invoice delivery requirements.
> - Validate task hours through a walkthrough and test shift, including relief coverage, overtime exposure, and travel obligations.
> - Confirm classifications, applicable wage rates, insurance coverage, chemical training, and any site-specific exposure controls.
> - Approve a written credit limit and demonstrate payroll liquidity through the agreed payment cycle plus a modeled collection delay.

## 4. Control Storm Referrals, Public Receivables, and Collection Concentration  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

The required mapped adjacency is public_adjuster_appraisal_clause_storm_arbitrage: Commercial Hail-Swath Public Adjuster & Appraisal-Clause Storm Arbitrage, TRACK_1. For a cleaning operator, its responsible implementation starts with customer-authorized observations of apparent storm damage, not unsolicited claim advocacy or a promise of insurance recovery. Route supported observations to appropriately licensed public-adjuster capacity, subject to the jurisdiction's solicitation and compensation rules. A policy's appraisal provision generally concerns valuation issues, but its scope and interaction with coverage disputes depend on policy language and governing law. A cleaner must not present appraisal as a universal way to compel coverage. Texas Insurance Code Chapter 4102, for example, regulates public insurance adjusting and contains licensing and conduct requirements that must be checked before participation. Keep the cleaning agreement, customer authorization, and any adjuster engagement separate. Do not negotiate the claim, collect an adjuster's contingent fee, or accept referral compensation without establishing that the specific arrangement is permitted. The fallback is explicit: pause solicitation and refer coverage disputes to qualified counsel while licensed adjusters handle supported valuations. Ordinary cleaning service can continue only within its authorized, safe scope.

Unit math keeps this adjacency from contaminating the cleaning scorecard. Suppose a customer authorizes a discrete post-storm cleaning assignment priced at $1,250. Four workers providing eight hours each create thirty-two hours; at $26 burdened hourly cost, labor totals $832. Add $168 for allocated equipment, supplies, and supervision, producing $1,000 total cost and $250 contribution before any expenses excluded from that assignment model. Treat this as a separate illustration, not an increment silently added to the canonical annual cases. If the property has a $200,000 insurance claim, that claim amount is neither cleaning revenue nor operator receivables. Collect the cleaning charge from the contracting customer under agreed terms rather than assuming an insurer owes it. Joint-payee checks, lender interests, mortgage servicing controls, and disputed coverage can obstruct access to proceeds. Never route the customer's claim proceeds through the cleaner's ordinary operating account to simplify payment. When the customer cannot fund the assignment independently, use a properly documented funding arrangement or defer work. Do not expand into structural restoration, hazardous-material remediation, or electrical work merely because storm-related demand makes those services appear commercially attractive.

Concentration controls finish the payment system. A $20,000 monthly customer represents $240,000 annually, or approximately 33.1% of the $725,000 Base revenue case. At forty-five days outstanding, that account alone can absorb roughly $30,000 of receivables. Do not confuse that balance with profit or assume a factoring facility will accept a disputed invoice. Review receivables weekly by legal debtor, not merely by location, and distinguish unsubmitted work, rejected invoices, accepted invoices, disputes, and overdue balances. Call the acceptance contact before escalating to accounts payable when the obstacle is service documentation. Escalate to the contract owner when the obstacle is funding or habitual withholding. For public-prime work, monitor the prime's payment behavior without assuming a bond or direct agency remedy exists for every janitorial contract. Build supervisor pods around route density and relief capacity, not one dominant account's convenience. Set customer-specific stop-extension limits, document any exception, and plan safe contractual suspension before cash becomes critical. Scheduling, inspection logs, and billing automation improve evidence and reduce administrative waste; they do not eliminate paid crew hours or turn an insolvent customer into a collectible debtor.
