## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one point: the contract, not the mop, is the business, and unit franchisees rent it rather than own it. They add 3x to 4x finder fees financed as debt, and underbid accounts that push real pay toward $7 an hour. Mike Mak calls laptop residential cleaning a roughly $30-per-job grind and steers toward $3,000 to $5,000 commercial contracts. ## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who dissected the janitorial franchise machine landed on the same mechanic. The cleaning is the visible service, but the money sits with whoever controls the recurring account. Win Driggins says it bluntly: the hidden business is the contract itself and access to recurring paying accounts. Inspection performance protects those contracts, because streaky mirrors and dull sinks turn straight into complaints [83XHXw79m3E @ 00:00:41]. Ledger & Ruin traces Jani-King back to a night auditor in Norman, Oklahoma, who collected finder's fees for bringing customers to a janitor. That became a system where the regional office signs accounts and hands them to franchisees as initial business. The office keeps billing and collections and sells additional accounts on top, which the channel sums up as the customer is the product [UNUQT2N9Brc @ 00:05:40]. Their closing lesson is to sell the customer, not the work [UNUQT2N9Brc @ 00:12:13]. Rob Lanced of Stratus pitches the same engine from the top of the pyramid. Masters sell unit franchises, win contracts, award them to units, and provide sales, marketing and operational support. They technically sit in commercial cleaning yet never clean anything [eJ5s6HRr1HE @ 00:07:05]. Whoever owns sales owns the business.

The second point of agreement is ownership. Commercial Cleaning CEO, who openly runs a competing territory brand, warns that unit franchisees do not own their accounts and that the franchisor can reassign them. The speaker concludes that with a unit franchise you just don't really own a business, you more so have a job [xqw9zag57Uk @ 00:10:37]. AJ Simmons was a Jan-Pro Franchise Owner of the Year, also held Jani-King and Coverall units, and worked as a Jan-Pro operations manager. Simmons says the franchise owns the contracts and can pull them even when the customer is happy [Bbay8f0-XFM @ 00:03:40]. In a separate breakdown, Simmons recalls a franchisee who drew a single complainer and lost every contract they had. The reason is simple: you don't own the contracts, which leaves you undiversified [BK0p3k8OpRo @ 00:18:22]. Mike Mak, whose first business with a partner was a franchise, describes the lived version. Mak felt like a manager or employee instead of an owner and could not drop nightmare clients. Now, as an independent, Mak warns a bad client once and then stops service, and says they would not buy a franchise again [6MDrTK46_8g @ 00:11:24]. In every account from these operators, the customer belongs to the system, not to the person doing the cleaning.

Practitioners also converge on a narrow buyer profile. Ben Bragg of Clean For A Dream started in 1999 with a $400 vacuum and later founded the franchisor Diamond Pash. Bragg states that the franchising industry is designed for you to supplement your income, not to be a business owner. Bragg also reminds buyers that office cleaning happens at night, so they should take stock of their skills, time and money first [fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 01:00:50]. AJ Simmons draws the line by capability. A franchise suits someone rough around the edges with no email, no computer skills and discomfort in business settings. Anyone articulate who can use a computer and social media should go independent, as Simmons did by hiring callers, cold calling and learning to price [BK0p3k8OpRo @ 00:02:02]. Vetted Biz describes the typical unit as roughly $40K in revenue, often run by a husband and wife part-time after their regular jobs. Vetted Biz advises buying a unit for a side gig and a master or area developer for growth [rQxrqnty1Cs @ 00:02:45]. Commercial Cleaning CEO adds that franchising fits first-time owners but not people who resist systems, and not serial founders [xqw9zag57Uk @ 00:21:17]. The shared baseline is to buy a unit for a paycheck supplement, not an empire.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The fee math is where the speakers get granular. Commercial Cleaning CEO lists a unit royalty around 10 percent, marketing at 2 to 10 percent, and admin and collection at 5 to 10 percent, plus insurance. That totals 20 to 35 percent and sometimes 40, so every $1,000 billed nets $600 to $800 [xqw9zag57Uk @ 00:05:18]. Ben Bragg walks through a buy-in example: an $8K package with $3,600 down and $4,400 financed over three years. On $1,000 gross, $145 goes to financing and $150 to royalty, leaving a $705 payout with almost 30 percent gone. Bragg then layers on special service fees of up to an extra 25 percent, quality control at $50 an hour plus expenses, transfer fees of 8 to 10 percent or a $1,500 minimum, and 12 to 18 percent interest [fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 00:46:28]. Ledger & Ruin pulls Jani-King's disclosure numbers: 10 percent royalty, 7 percent accounting and sales, and 1.5 percent advertising. That comes to roughly 18 percent, or about $21K a year on $118K in average unit gross. The channel also flags that the popular $61K owner-earnings figure comes from a salary site, not the disclosure document [UNUQT2N9Brc @ 00:07:54]. Mike Mak paid a support fee near 20 percent, which meant $2,000 came off the top of every $10,000 [6MDrTK46_8g @ 00:03:41].

The second mechanic is account debt. Mike Mak bought in at $15,000 for $2,000 a month in volume, then paid four times the monthly value for every new account. A rare $10K-a-month account therefore cost $40,000. Labor, supplies and the 20 percent fee came out first, so the more they grew, the deeper the debt went [6MDrTK46_8g @ 00:04:31]. AJ Simmons confirms the 4x multiple, which turns a $1,000-a-month contract into a $4,000 charge financed as another monthly payment. Simmons also caught a franchise bidding work at 16 or 17 dollars an hour [Bbay8f0-XFM @ 00:02:47]. Commercial Cleaning CEO models a $2K account at 3x: $500 a month over 12 months plus $400 a month in fees leaves $1,100 a month. The speaker warns that the franchisor's incentive is their fees, which drives underbidding [xqw9zag57Uk @ 00:07:02]. Ben Bragg converts the payout to hours. A 5-night account averages 21.67 visits a month, so a $705 payout should cover 44 hours at $16 or 24 hours at $30. In practice franchisees work about 100 hours, roughly $7 an hour. Handing the account back ends the master's obligation, so you're stuck [fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 00:51:00].

Operators who skipped franchising publish their own numbers. Mike Mak models a remote residential 3-bed, 2-bath clean in the Austin suburbs at $225, split 60 percent to the cleaner and 40 percent, or $90, to the owner. Google LSA leads at $20 to $50 become roughly $40 to $50 per booked customer. After card fees, software, insurance, refunds and recleans, the owner keeps about $30 per job, so $10K a month in take-home pay needs 300 to 400 jobs [kbRmM3sctgA @ 00:03:13]. Mak's alternative is three commercial contracts at $4,000 each for $12K MRR, which yields about $4K profit at a 30 to 35 percent margin [kbRmM3sctgA @ 00:05:57]. Logan Robison runs Airbnb turnovers without cleaning a unit. Robison pays cleaners 48 percent for a 52 percent gross profit, with three VAs in the Philippines and one in-person manager running the operation [mrd8LK8fcFM @ 00:03:13]. Business From Zero builds a five-account commercial P&L: $9,000 revenue against $2,900 OPEX leaves $6,100, a 67.7 percent margin. That model prices a 4,000 sq ft dental clinic at $1,800 a month and staffs W-2 cleaners at $18 an hour plus a 12 percent burden [fvJFWetfuFA @ 00:07:08].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pull the full fee stack
   - List every percentage and flat fee in the offer before comparing any revenue promise.
   - Total up royalty at 5 to 10 percent, admin at 5 to 10 percent, additional volume fees of 3.5 to 6x, special service fees up to an extra 25 percent, quality control at $50 an hour, transfer fees of 8 to 10 percent or a $1,500 minimum, and 12 to 18 percent interest [Clean For A Dream — fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 00:40:04]
2. Convert the payout into an hourly wage
   - Divide the net monthly payout by the real hours each account takes.
   - A 5-night account averages 21.67 visits a month. A $705 payout should equal 24 to 44 hours at $16 to $30 an hour, but franchisees report about 100 hours, roughly $7 an hour [Clean For A Dream — fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 00:51:00]
3. Price the finder-fee debt
   - Model the finder fee as a monthly loan payment that stacks on top of the fees.
   - A $2K account at 3x costs $500 a month over 12 months, plus $400 a month in fees, leaving $1,100 a month. Remember that the franchisor's incentive is their fees [Commercial Cleaning CEO — xqw9zag57Uk @ 00:07:02]
4. Choose the side that owns sales
   - Either buy at the master level or learn to win contracts yourself instead of renting accounts.
   - Learn how to go after businesses and contracts yourself, no matter how long it takes, even if that means keeping your day job a little longer [Winter 🧊 — 6Ee7M9O9djo @ 00:03:00]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is which seat in the same pyramid you occupy. From the master chair, Rob Lanced of Stratus pitches a business where 'as a master operator, you're not doing any cleaning': the master sells unit franchises, wins contracts, awards them down, and banks 90% recurring revenue on contracts running 4+ years with about 90% retention [eJ5s6HRr1HE @ 00:07:05]. Vetted Biz backs the top-of-pyramid math, pegging a master at roughly $1M revenue earning 150K to 200K and scalable to three to five million, while calling the unit a roughly $40K husband-and-wife side gig netting 70% to 80% because they do the labor themselves [rQxrqnty1Cs @ 00:01:05]. Ben Bragg of Clean For A Dream runs unit numbers from the floor and lands somewhere else entirely: at commercial rates of $16 to $30 an hour, the $705 payout on $1,000 gross should take 24 to 44 hours a month, but franchisees actually grind about 100 hours, roughly $7 an hour, and handing the account back ends the master's obligation, so 'you're stuck' [fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 00:56:29]. Ledger & Ruin strips the model to its core: the regional office signs accounts and hands them out as initial business, because 'the customer is the product' [UNUQT2N9Brc @ 00:05:40]. Pick the seat before you pick the brand.

Practitioners also split on whether anyone should buy in at all. Winter takes the hard line from firsthand experience: a franchise or management company 'will rob you blind,' and the only play is to learn to go after businesses and contracts yourself no matter how long it takes, even if that means keeping the day job longer [6Ee7M9O9djo @ 00:03:00]. AJ Simmons, who has owned multiple franchises and worked as an operations manager at 'the biggest one,' refuses the scam label and sorts by temperament instead: a franchise fits someone 'rough around the edges' with no email or computer skills, while anyone articulate who can run a computer and social media should go independent, and franchising works like a record label that gets you started but that you will probably regret long-term [BK0p3k8OpRo @ 00:05:31]. Mike Mak sits between the two: not knocking franchises and knowing people who did well with them, yet unwilling to buy one again after feeling like a manager rather than an owner [6MDrTK46_8g @ 00:13:42]. The Commercial Cleaning CEO, who discloses founding a territory brand, splits the model itself: the unit franchise is more 'a job' than a business, while territory franchises requiring $50K liquid and $100K net worth suit first-time owners who will follow systems [xqw9zag57Uk @ 00:21:17].

The remote-ownership fight runs on unit economics. Mike Mak calls remote residential cleaning BS for anyone chasing real income: a $225 Austin-area clean splits $135 to the cleaner and $90 to the owner, then Google LSA acquisition at about $40 to $50 per booked customer, card fees, software, insurance, refunds and recleans grind that to about $30 a job, so $10K a month take-home needs 300 to 400 jobs [kbRmM3sctgA @ 00:04:02]. Dan, the Myrtle Beach MaidThis franchisee profiled by UpFlip, counters with $40K a month against a 15% to 25% industry margin, a remote operations team in South Africa, a 5-to-20-hour work week, and a 70% vacation-rental mix [PUIZGbefocg @ 00:08:08]. Logan Robison scaled Airbnb turnovers without cleaning a single unit personally, running 3 VAs in the Philippines and one in-person manager at 52% gross profit with cleaners paid 48% [mrd8LK8fcFM @ 00:07:00]. Mike Mak's counter-playbook is commercial: three car dealership or medical contracts at $4,000 make $12K MRR and about $4K profit at a 30% to 35% margin, and six or seven contracts push past $10K profit, though Mike Mak concedes 'of course it works' for a college student wanting about $2K a month [kbRmM3sctgA @ 00:07:49].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most repeated firsthand loss is the fee stack compounding into debt. Mike Mak bought in at $15,000 for $2,000 a month in volume, then paid a support fee near 20% of billing plus four times the monthly value for every new account, so a rare $10K-a-month account cost $40,000; because labor, supplies and the 20% come out first, that debt never clears in four months, and growth meant 'debt on debt on debt' [6MDrTK46_8g @ 00:06:06]. Ben Bragg's worked package tells the same story: an $8K package at $3,600 down with $4,400 financed over 3 years leaves $705 from every $1,000 gross once $145 of finance and $150 of royalty come off, before volume fees of 3.5 to 6 times, $50-an-hour quality control and 12% to 18% interest stack on [fs86ZhCBfJ8 @ 00:48:41]. AJ Simmons puts the finder fee at 4 times monthly gross, financed as yet another monthly payment, until owners walk away with pretty much nothing [Bbay8f0-XFM @ 00:02:47]. Ledger & Ruin adds a reading trap: Jani-King's disclosure shows about $118K average unit sales and roughly 18% in fees, yet the circulating $61K owner-earnings figure comes from a salary site, not the disclosure document [UNUQT2N9Brc @ 00:08:30].

Underbidding and account ownership are where franchise owners most often report getting trapped hardest. Mike Mak says about 70% of their accounts felt underbid because quota-driven salespeople who had never cleaned could not tell a $2K space from a $4K one; an account worth $5,000 a month sold at $3,500 bleeds $1,500 a month that should fund quality assurance, equipment and profit, and unprofitable accounts make cleaners hard to find [6MDrTK46_8g @ 00:08:14]. AJ Simmons caught a franchise bidding 16 or 17 an hour and warns the franchise, not the owner, can reassign contracts even when the customer is happy [Bbay8f0-XFM @ 00:02:00]; in a separate breakdown, AJ Simmons reports one complainer losing every contract, because 'You don't own the contracts' [BK0p3k8OpRo @ 00:18:33]. Winter lived the end state of a percentage fee on a thin margin firsthand: the 15% looked manageable until a real net profit of about 20% meant it took nearly all of the profit, leaving Winter inspecting buildings and buying supplies personally with no money left, paying employees while unable to pay their own bills [6Ee7M9O9djo @ 00:02:21]. That is how a franchise owner ends up feeling, in AJ Simmons' own words, like a glorified employee.

Going independent or remote carries its own firsthand traps, mostly around marketing, labor and launch spending. Dan of MaidThis cut marketing in year two, watched growth flatline, and calls it plainly: 'It was a mistake'; Dan also kept a waiting list instead of hiring more cleaners [PUIZGbefocg @ 00:21:16]. Mike Mak warns that scaling remote residential toward hundreds of jobs and 200 to 300 clients breeds chaos from complaints and cleaners who quit, because 'every single job is its own problem to solve' [kbRmM3sctgA @ 00:04:33]. Business From Zero lists the launch traps that sink commercial starts: a $30,000 cargo van or warehouse lease on day one, bids missing the $10K surety bond that gets them rejected by 90% of property managers, 1099 misclassification instead of W-2 staff at $18 an hour plus a 12% burden, and lead aggregators with net-30 terms instead of walking medical plazas between 10am and 2pm and collecting a first-month retainer upfront [fvJFWetfuFA @ 00:05:37]. Win Driggins locates the quality failure point that kills contracts: restrooms are the highest-risk area in every building because 'restrooms drive complaints very fast,' and inspection performance is what protects the contract, since branding does not replace execution [83XHXw79m3E @ 00:02:12].
