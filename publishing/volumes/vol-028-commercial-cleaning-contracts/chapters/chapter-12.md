## 1. Days 1-7: Define the Four Plays and Protect the Launch Cash  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Use four execution plays throughout the sprint, each with a different proof requirement. Play one is a dense recurring office route: select one compact territory, compatible evening access windows, and buildings whose service frequency supports repeatable staffing. Play two is bounded medical environmental cleaning: pursue outpatient common areas and explicitly described rooms only after the facility defines exposure risks, cleaning responsibilities, and escalation contacts. Play three is measured scope-and-price reset: time the actual tasks, remove unpriced extras, and sell separately authorized periodic work. Play four is the supervisor pod: combine scheduling, inspection logs, and billing around one accountable field lead. These are the chapter's four operating mappings, not four additional revenue streams layered onto the scorecard. On Day 1, assign an owner to each play and create a shared prospect register with building address, decision maker, service window, usable cleanable area, current contract expiry, and next action. Keep one opportunity identifier from first contact through invoice. A quote without measured scope belongs in discovery, not committed backlog. Start with offices; medical work advances only when its additional training and handling requirements fit the same cash and supervision limits.

The canonical launch pod contains five full-time roles and four contractor relationships, not nine people to hire before a customer signs. Plan the five employee roles as one working supervisor and four cleaners; use genuine independent businesses for defined relief or specialty packages rather than disguising controlled employee shifts as subcontracting. The owner handles selling and cash control outside this delivery roster and records their own operating time for costing. Budget the supervisor at no more than twenty cleaning hours weekly until inspections, training, and relief coverage are measured. Reserve ten percent of scheduled field capacity for absence and rework, and use actual available hours rather than nominal headcount when accepting sites. Before mobilization, establish workers' compensation where required, customer-required liability coverage, payroll registration, timekeeping, and documented work authorization procedures. Under 29 U.S.C. 207, covered nonexempt employees generally receive overtime after forty hours in a workweek; apply stricter state requirements where relevant. Count compensable travel between sites and required training. A contractor's invoice or insurance certificate alone does not establish independent status. If the facts require employment treatment, move that capacity into payroll before assigning the work.

For an illustrative $15,000 opening cash balance, cap first-month launch burn at $6,000 and protect the remaining $9,000. Allocate up to $1,200 for coverage deposits and registrations, $1,600 for basic equipment and PPE, $400 for scheduling and accounting setup, $800 for travel and prospecting, $1,200 for paid training or trial labor, and $800 for contingency. These are spending limits, not vendor quotations; obtain actual prices before committing. Launch burn means cumulative cash outflows less collected customer cash, excluding owner contributions and borrowing. Separately list every unpaid commitment so delayed bills cannot manufacture apparent headroom. If opening capital is larger, the first-month ceiling still holds until contracted demand justifies additional deployment. If mandatory coverage or equipment exceeds an allocation, reduce discretionary spending or delay the affected service rather than raid payroll cash. Require unrestricted cash sufficient for the greater of $9,000 or the next six weeks of committed payroll, payroll taxes, and subcontractor payments. By Day 7, the owner must produce a thirteen-week cash forecast, verified insurance requirements, and a timed sample scope. Do not count an unsigned proposal or a customer's purchasing budget as available liquidity.

## 2. Days 8-14: Turn Walkthroughs into Measured, Collectible Contracts  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Days 8 through 14 turn the territory list into evidence rather than a bulk outreach campaign. Make twenty individualized buyer contacts during the week and seek six substantive discovery conversations, three booked walkthroughs, and two measured proposals by Day 14. These are management targets, not assumed conversion rates. Ask who can authorize service, when the incumbent agreement ends, which complaints recur, and how the customer issues purchase orders. During each walkthrough, measure cleanable area by surface and task instead of copying gross building area. Count fixtures, waste stations, occupied workpoints, dispensers, locked rooms, and stairs. Record whether furniture moves, whether supplies are customer-provided, and whether elevators or security escorts consume paid time. Use current ISSA Cleaning Times references as a starting point, then adjust with timed observations and documented obstacles. Never apply a single square-foot rate to restrooms, open offices, and clinical rooms as though they had equal labor intensity. The proposal must state frequency, acceptance criteria, excluded tasks, periodic services, access assumptions, and the price adjustment process. A procurement conversation becomes qualified only when the buyer confirms scope, timing, authority, and the next scheduled decision.

Build a transparent illustrative office bid before discounting anything. Assume three visits weekly, three productive labor hours per visit, and fifty-two service weeks: that produces 468 cleaning hours annually. Add 0.25 paid travel and setup hours per visit, or thirty-nine hours, for 507 total paid hours. At a $20 hourly wage and a twenty-percent employer burden allowance, loaded labor is $24 per hour and $12,168 annually. Add $1,200 for supplies, $1,800 for direct supervision, and $600 for equipment allocation. Total modeled direct cost becomes $15,768. A $24,000 annual contract therefore produces $8,232 of contribution, or 34.3%, before central overhead and owner profit. Monthly recognized service revenue is $2,000 when that month's contracted service is delivered; a deposit is cash received, not automatically earned revenue. Recheck wage burden against actual taxes, insurance, benefits, and overtime. If observed paid hours rise twenty percent, labor becomes $14,601.60 and contribution falls to $5,798.40, or 24.16%. That account enters Fix even if its customer praises the crew. Separate extra visits and periodic floor work through written orders rather than hiding their labor inside the recurring fee.

Medical opportunities need a narrower sales promise and stronger site evidence than ordinary office work. On Day 11, ask the facility's infection-prevention contact to define room categories, permitted products, spill escalation, waste boundaries, and equipment ownership. Do not accept sharps handling, regulated waste transport, or instrument reprocessing by implication. Where occupational exposure is reasonably anticipated, 29 CFR 1910.1030 drives an exposure-control plan, training, appropriate PPE, hepatitis B vaccination provisions, and post-exposure procedures. Under 29 CFR 1910.1200, hazardous chemical use requires the applicable written program, labels, accessible safety data sheets, and employee training. Price disinfectant contact time into the work sequence; EPA-registered products must be used consistently with their labeling under 7 U.S.C. 136j(a)(2)(G). Keep patient records out of inspection photographs and establish a facility-specific process for accidental access. By Day 14, each medical proposal should include a responsibility matrix signed by the facility contact and accepted internally by the supervisor. If those boundaries remain unresolved, continue office prospecting rather than improvising clinical procedures. The medical play earns its place through measured contribution and controlled exposure, not a premium label attached to otherwise identical cleaning.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Four Field Tactics That Protect Janitorial Contribution**
> 
> Experienced operators remove hidden minutes before adding another building to the route.
> - Time the walk from parking to the janitor closet; badge delays and distant loading doors can erase a cheap square-foot bid.
> - Count restroom fixtures separately from floor area, then verify refill responsibility and dispenser compatibility before pricing supplies.
> - Photograph approved existing floor damage without capturing people or records, and separate restorative work from recurring maintenance.
> - Ask the buyer to demonstrate their invoice portal during onboarding; rejected invoices often reveal missing purchase orders rather than customer insolvency.

## 3. Days 15-21: Run a Paid Pilot Without Overbuilding the Pod  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

A paid pilot starts only after written authorization defines price, scope, access, and acceptance. On Day 15, conduct the handover with the buyer and supervisor; reconcile keys, alarm codes, supply locations, and emergency contacts. Day 16 is the first observed clean, with separate time entries for cleaning, travel, setup, training, and rework. On Day 17, compare actual task minutes with the estimate before changing any rate assumption. Day 18 is the correction shift: adjust sequence, equipment placement, or task frequency only within the agreed scope. Day 19 tests absence coverage using a briefed employee or a properly scoped subcontractor business. On Day 20, ask the buyer to score the same inspection checklist used internally. Day 21 closes the pilot's cost ledger and invoice package. If no authorized pilot exists, run paid internal demonstrations, complete additional walkthroughs, and preserve cash; do not manufacture booked revenue or offer unlimited unpaid cleaning. First-deal timing for this candidate is one to four months, so the sprint must produce credible selling evidence without treating a slow procurement cycle as automatic failure. Internal demonstrations cannot satisfy the customer acceptance requirements used for a Scale decision.

Use a modest integrated stack rather than a custom application. Connecteam or Jobber can hold schedules and mobile checklists; QuickBooks Online can issue invoices and track receivables; a controlled spreadsheet can preserve estimate versions and contribution calculations. Select one source of truth for each record, limit access by role, and avoid collecting unnecessary location or patient information. Schedule the supervisor's inspections as paid work, not an invisible favor performed after the shift. Define first-pass acceptance as visits passing all critical checks without a return visit, divided by completed visits. Define completion reliability as visits finished within the contracted window, divided by scheduled visits. Measure labor variance as actual paid hours divided by budgeted paid hours, minus one, using an identical task scope. Capture complaints, rework minutes, and missed visits against the same site identifier. A sanitation-critical failure overrides a good average inspection score. Run a ten-minute daily exception meeting and a weekly payroll reconciliation. When a cleaner leaves, freeze new starts until trained replacement capacity exists. Scheduling automation can expose a shortage, but it cannot supply the hands needed to clean another building or eliminate compensable time between two customer locations.

Keep the canonical economics visible while testing whether the staffing design is affordable. Conservative revenue is $250,000 at an eight-percent net margin, leaving $20,000 annual owner profit and $230,000 total annual costs. Base revenue is $725,000 at twelve percent, leaving $87,000 profit and $638,000 costs. Scaled revenue is $1,200,000 at sixteen percent, leaving $192,000 profit and $1,008,000 costs. These are cleaning contract revenues, not customer sales, property values, or money passing through a customer's facility. Both Conservative and Base specify five FT plus four C; Scaled specifies six FT plus five C. At the illustrative $24 loaded hourly cost, five employees each paid 2,080 hours consume $249,600 before contractors, supplies, or overhead. That exceeds the Conservative cost envelope by itself. Preserve the published scenario but flag this deployment mismatch: do not claim the stated full-time roster is affordable at those illustrative wages. Stage hiring until signed workload and actual costs support it, or reject that implementation. Never relabel full-time roles as part-time merely to force the arithmetic. The sprint proves a viable route; it does not validate every scorecard staffing assumption.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Authorize the exact service unit
   - Link the signed scope, work order, site identifier, and crew assignment.
   - Confirm access, safety requirements, task frequencies, and the paid labor budget before releasing the schedule.
2. Capture paid effort and exceptions
   - Record cleaning, travel, setup, training, and rework in distinct time categories.
   - The supervisor resolves missing punches before payroll and attaches scope changes to the originating work order.
3. Inspect and close corrective work
   - Score critical checklist items and assign each defect an owner and deadline.
   - Track first-pass acceptance separately from the final corrected result so rework remains visible in contribution.
4. Reconcile delivery to collection
   - Match accepted service, invoice value, direct costs, and subsequent cash receipt.
   - Review contribution and payment aging weekly; recurring losses or disputes trigger the documented Fix or Kill response.

## 4. Days 22-30: Close the Evidence File and Apply Day 30/60/90 Decisions  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Day 22 reconciles every pilot time entry and missing supplier bill. Day 23 recalculates site contribution and prepares written price or scope corrections. Day 24 submits invoices through the customer's actual purchasing channel and verifies receipt rather than merely recording an email as sent. Day 25 tests payroll and absence coverage against the next four weeks of scheduled work. Day 26 obtains the buyer's written acceptance, complaint list, or renewal decision. Day 27 audits chemicals, training records, equipment condition, and access credentials. Day 28 inspects the route's longest travel segment and measures whether a second building can fit without overtime or service-window collisions. Day 29 closes the cash forecast, updates concentration, and reconciles earned revenue separately from deposits and unpaid invoices. Day 30 produces a one-page decision record showing actual results, missing evidence, corrective owners, and the next review date. If the first contract remains unsigned, attach buyer-confirmed next steps and completed walkthrough records instead of inventing delivery statistics. Complete at least three measured proposals by this review. A credible pipeline can justify continued testing within the cash ceiling, but it cannot justify hiring the entire nine-person pod or advertising unused capacity as an operating achievement.

Apply decisions in order: a Kill condition overrides everything, any Fix condition blocks expansion, and Scale requires every applicable control to pass. Kill means stop the affected offer, account, or expansion commitment; terminate service through contractual procedures and preserve safe continuity where necessary. Fix means assign one owner, a measurable correction, and a fourteen-day deadline. A negative contribution account gets one correction attempt; if the next two full service weeks remain negative, use its exit provisions. Scale means one adjacent site at a time, not immediately jumping to the eleven-person scenario. The standard delivery threshold is at least thirty-percent direct contribution, ninety-five-percent first-pass acceptance, ninety-eight-percent completion reliability, and no more than ten-percent labor overrun. Require ten inspected customer visits across two service weeks for Day 30, twenty visits across four weeks for Day 60, and forty visits across eight weeks for Day 90. Missing samples mean Fix and continued observation, never an assumed pass. Pause the affected task immediately for an uncontrolled serious exposure, unpaid earned wages, or lost essential coverage. Resume only after the specific deficiency is resolved and documented; averages cannot offset those failures.

Review cash and concentration alongside service results. After Day 30, cap cumulative launch burn at the lesser of $18,000 or opening unrestricted cash minus the $9,000 minimum reserve, while retaining the larger six-week committed-labor reserve whenever required. At $15,000 opening cash, cumulative burn therefore remains capped at $6,000 unless collected operating receipts offset new spending; fresh borrowing does not improve measured burn. A forecast reserve breach inside fourteen days stops new commitments immediately. Through Day 60, require no undisputed invoice more than fifteen days past its contractual due date; at Day 90, also require receivable days of thirty-five or less. Calculate receivable days as ending trade receivables divided by trailing sixty-day earned credit revenue, multiplied by sixty; exclude deposits and calculate only with a positive revenue denominator. Track customer concentration by signed monthly recurring fees, not pipeline value. One pilot customer is acceptable during validation; discretionary equipment or permanent staffing expansion requires the largest customer at fifty percent or less by Day 60 and forty percent or less by Day 90. Maintain ten-percent field capacity reserve and inspect every added site's first three visits before releasing further route growth.
