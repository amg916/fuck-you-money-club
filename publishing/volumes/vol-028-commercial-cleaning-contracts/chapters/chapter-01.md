# Chapter 01: Starting Lean: Founder Playbooks from Zero to Seven Figures

*Launching under $1,000 or with no money, solo operation, essential supplies and equipment, top-to-bottom commercial cleaning training, UK business plans, and founder case studies from million-dollar cleaning, Queen Bee, and pool-cleaning operators*

## Executive Summary

Founders building from zero toward seven figures agree on a lean sequence: learn the work hands-on, price for the labor you will eventually hire, and win recurring contracts through reputation and fast follow-up. AJ Simmons runs a sub-$1,000 launch built on a $99 CBN membership, a $200 LLC budget, a $25 domain and 400 cold calls in week one, deferring insurance and supplies until a deal closes. DJ The Boss landed a first lead for $1.75 in Google Ads that grew into roughly $500K over seven years, then burned $6K on the same campaign. UpFlip's operator runs 60% labor and nets 15-20%. Practitioners split on whether owners must clean first or whether inexperience is an advantage, and on paperwork: Nicole Thomas holds seven commercial buildings without an LLC, EIN, insurance or website.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Scaled operators keep returning to one premise: you must understand the work before you can sell or manage it. The $15M owner interviewed by AJ Simmons tells first-year founders to do everything themselves first, mastering chemicals, equipment, supplies, training and hiring, and warns that owners who have never cleaned are not going to make it far [7pVd69Als0I @ 00:00:57]. That company forces every six-figure management hire, including the CTO of its software arm, to clean for 30 days so they learn changed locks, forgotten keys and locked supply closets in the field. AJ backs that up from their own franchise start at 19, when no training meant smeared windows and skipped tasks on a children's clinic worth about $2K+ per month [srB5UatlJlU @ 00:07:49]. Clean Ops Guide frames the same lesson from the buyer side: commercial cleaning is a professional service business, not just cleaning buildings, and property managers are paying for reliability, communication and peace of mind so they never have to chase the crew [IgZO8_dFaeU @ 00:00:16]. The shared baseline is simple: know the labor, know the site problems, and sell certainty rather than mop strokes to every buyer.

The second point of agreement is that the real asset is the recurring contract, not the one-off job. Clean Ops Guide lays out the commercial cadence, an office three evenings a week, a medical facility five nights, a large building daily, and notes that a single client can mean hundreds of visits over several years [IgZO8_dFaeU @ 00:02:35]. DJ The Boss proves the compounding: a gymnastics facility signed on a 12-month agreement grew from two days to five days a week, and when the point of contact moved to another facility, the contact brought the company along, turning client one into contract two [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:00:53]. AJ Simmons tells owners to optimize for keeping a contract five years rather than squeezing profit from it today, backed by a policy that a no-call/no-show is an automatic firing [srB5UatlJlU @ 00:21:19]. The threat to that asset is rarely cleaning quality. AJ warns that a competitor met at a Chamber of Commerce event can take an account on relationship alone, so solo operators still run daytime inspections at least quarterly and keep communication constant [mp0PpDrOpYM @ 00:04:57]. Retention, in this playbook, is a sales activity that never stops after the signature.

Speed and public reputation close the loop. Ricky Regalado, running a roughly $10M Chicago operation, says walkthroughs die when proposals are not turned around quickly, so the proposal goes out within two to three hours of the walkthrough [QBuGc_Q36dg @ 00:05:39]. AJ Simmons pushes the same discipline downstream: call within 24 hours, tell the prospect to check spam, follow up again after about a week, and keep going until there is a clear yes or no, because deals have closed six months later and many owners lose business simply by not following up [pqTeTRcPhKg @ 00:20:13]. Reputation is the other half. Joel Davis says Google reviews are what sells the business, with the web page plus Google PPC and reviews producing the best return on their pool route [j3l2-xKJKb8 @ 00:29:01]. UpFlip's $120K-per-month residential operator calls clients the next day for feedback and responds to every review, crediting the protected online reputation as one of the keys to fast growth [d4Iip7BHXwg @ 00:18:25]. Across commercial, residential and pool service, the consensus is identical: answer fast, follow up until the prospect decides, and treat star ratings as the storefront prospects actually check before calling.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

AJ Simmons lays out a launch target of under $1,000 and a first customer inside 30 days. Step one is the $99 CBN membership through a special link to dodge the setup fee, canceled if no customer lands in 30 days; bookkeeping runs on free waveapps.com; the LLC typically costs $100 to $125 with $200 budgeted, while the EIN and BOI filing are free; five bid appointments cost $625 at $125 each; the domain runs $25 max and a Canva print order about $50 [VrlDbQP4iQI @ 00:05:27]. AJ's 2026 course adds the insurance spec to quote but not yet buy: $1M per incident and $2M aggregate, with the line that the business should pay for itself [pqTeTRcPhKg @ 00:06:01]. The residential benchmark from UpFlip ran higher at $5K: $4K for a 2003 Accord, a $500 vacuum, $300 in supplies and the rest on flyers, or about $1K for anyone who already owns a car [d4Iip7BHXwg @ 00:03:07]. Kadz Money started UK domestic work with a Home Bargains box of about £20, sponges and five or six cloths, carrying no vacuum or mop because the insurance did not cover a vacuum [dEkotRT-f90 @ 00:01:36]. Capital is not the bottleneck in any of these builds.

The acquisition math is explicit. AJ's calling plan is 100 businesses Monday through Friday: 400 calls in week one should yield one to four walkthroughs, and 1,500 more over the remaining weeks should produce four to 20 appointments, one to four contracts and about $2K per month recurring [VrlDbQP4iQI @ 00:14:20]. DJ The Boss went paid instead: after wasting $600 on door hangers, they built a website because competitors had no online presence, ran Google's free $100 ad credit on a badly configured campaign drawing clicks from the Philippines and China, and still caught the first lead at $1.75 of spend [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:06:17]. UpFlip's operator budgets roughly $4K a month on Google Local Leads, which bills per call and allows disputed leads, plus $1K on Facebook and $700 to $750 on Yelp, with Google performing best; in slow December-to-February months they text a $50-off offer to 1,500 contacts through HighLevel and see at least 10% reply [d4Iip7BHXwg @ 00:04:21]. Kadz Money's cheap UK channels mirror this: a Gumtree ad at £5 to £10 a month pulled about 28 inquiries, and a £10 Mumsnet ad still generates calls years later [dEkotRT-f90 @ 00:03:01].

Pricing starts at the walkthrough, and the practitioners script it tightly. Ricky Regalado arrives on time, shakes hands, asks about the client's hot points, never requests a scope of work, prices the facility and the person's expectations, and closes with a same-type reference, restaurant to restaurant or luxury high-rise to luxury high-rise [QBuGc_Q36dg @ 00:04:54]. AJ Simmons wears a logo shirt, brings a second person to measure and record, asks about pain points, budget and frequency, never promises a delivery time, never prices on the spot, and runs the numbers through calculatedclean.app afterward [pqTeTRcPhKg @ 00:17:46]. Measurement drives the bid: AJ's supply list puts a laser measure on the cleanable square footage only, because a 10,000-foot building where you clean 2,000 feet will get bid way too high [mk-8avM72Ms @ 00:02:52]. DJ The Boss adds the margin rule that built a $500K account: ask the client's budget, price it to include the employees you will eventually put in, come in under the incumbent ServiceMaster after its roughly 35% hike, bill in advance and collect upfront; that client now pays about triple the original rate [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:09:39]. Price for future labor, not for your own free hours.

## 3. Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the founder playbook is how much legal scaffolding goes up before the first invoice. AJ Simmons tells even solo operators to still form an LLC, both to shield personal assets when someone slips on a wet floor with no sign posted and because businesses prefer paying licensed and insured vendors [mp0PpDrOpYM @ 00:01:28]. The 2026 course runs the full stack: articles of organization for a single-member LLC, a free EIN, a business bank account for deductions, and a business license or sales-tax registration only where required [pqTeTRcPhKg @ 00:03:33]. Nicole Thomas in Lake Charles runs the opposite experiment and reports seven commercial buildings with nobody asking for an LLC, an EIN, insurance, or a website, while still admitting "I know I need to do that" [NHWdv_WG2B8 @ 00:05:46]. Insurance timing splits even inside the AJ camp: the under-$1,000 plan says answer yes when a prospect asks about coverage but only buy the policy once a deal is ready to close, and buy no supplies until then either [VrlDbQP4iQI @ 00:15:12]. The working read for an operator: the lean camp defers spending, not structure, and every speaker who skipped paperwork still treats it as a debt to settle once contracts land. Thomas covers the gap with client references instead of a website and has clients order Thomas's preferred supplies.

Whether the founder needs to have pushed a mop is the second live disagreement. The $15 million cleaning company owner interviewed by AJ Simmons says new owners have to do everything themselves first, covering cleaning, chemicals, equipment, supplies, training and hiring, and that owners who have never cleaned are not going to make it that far [7pVd69Als0I @ 00:00:57]. That owner sends every six-figure management hire, including the CTO of the software company, into the field for 30 days to learn changed locks, forgotten keys, locked supply closets and alarm issues [7pVd69Als0I @ 00:01:29]. Jazmine Tates flips the premise: never having cleaned professionally might be the advantage, because experienced cleaners think like a cleaner and not like a CEO, and cleaning skills can be outsourced while systems cannot [g8OVdfYHogY @ 00:01:19]. AJ lands between the two, having cleaned roughly nine years personally yet telling founders to hire people from day one and to drive Uber or take a job rather than live off the company too early [srB5UatlJlU @ 00:16:38]. Tates's own hiring filter demands contractors with at least two years of residential experience, their own supplies and reliable transport, so field knowledge still enters the business. It just does not have to live in the owner, whose job becomes coordination rather than supervision.

Customer acquisition splits into an outbound grind and an inbound reputation play. AJ Simmons scripts the grind: 100 business calls Monday through Friday, roughly 400 in week one for one to four walkthroughs, then 1,500 more for four to twenty appointments, one to four contracts and about $2,000 a month recurring [VrlDbQP4iQI @ 00:14:20]. Ricky Regalado, running about a $10 million Chicago operation, says the company does no marketing, cold calling or email campaigns, names Google reviews as the number one source, and says every new caller mentions the reviews [QBuGc_Q36dg @ 00:03:06]. Paid search sits in the middle and cuts both ways: DJ The Boss landed the gymnastics facility off a lead that cost $1.75 in ad spend, then burned $6,000 on the same campaign with no other business to speak of [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:08:47]. The UpFlip residential operator spends about $4,000 a month on Google Local Leads, pays per call, disputes junk leads, and ranks Google above Facebook at $1,000 and Yelp at $700 to $750 [d4Iip7BHXwg @ 00:04:21]. DJ's verdict reconciles the camps: Google Ads is the answer but not the answer, because you still have to be good at Google Ads, and cost per click drops when your SEO is at least somewhat set up.

## 4. Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common first-year loss is money spent on the wrong channel or a price set before the operator knows the job. DJ The Boss opened with $600 of door hangers and calls it a dumb move, because hangers suit residential and the target was commercial; flyers in mailboxes did produce the first post-construction jobs [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:04:14]. The Google Ads campaign that followed ran with negative-keyword problems and the wrong geo-targeting, pulling clicks from the Philippines and China before the $1.75 lead arrived [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:05:59]. Underpricing hits just as hard. The UpFlip operator's first move-out paid $90 for about 12 hours of work, and the fix was calling competitors for a three-bed, two-bath quote before pricing again [d4Iip7BHXwg @ 00:17:26]. Kadz Money started at £10 to £12 an hour in the UK, now recommends £15 to £20 with the first clean at half price, and pushes per-job or annual pricing such as £600 a year [dEkotRT-f90 @ 00:05:57]. Kadz also flags clients who try to squeeze a three-to-six-hour clean into two hours, countered with a free walkthrough using a room-by-room clipboard and a direct question about what the last cleaner failed at, which is usually reliability.

Cash conversion is where lean founders get squeezed. Charles Banks hit $30,000 a month inside a franchise yet kept roughly half after deductions and fees, with a crew still to pay out of that half [du4OAGZv9AE @ 00:06:12]. Banks also reports jobs that went unpaid and says they are still waiting on the money [du4OAGZv9AE @ 00:10:53]. AJ Simmons puts the timing bluntly: commercial franchise work pays effectively net 60, so everything you do this month arrives at the end of next month, while residential pays faster with better margins [srB5UatlJlU @ 00:32:58]. Clean Ops Guide warns that a 50,000 square foot building demands multiple employees, more equipment, supervision, and enough cash to cover payroll before the invoice is paid, and tells new owners to start under 5,000 square feet [IgZO8_dFaeU @ 00:03:14]. The counter-moves practitioners actually used: DJ The Boss billed in advance and got paid upfront after pricing under a ServiceMaster that had raised rates about 35 percent, and AJ asks for upfront payment or net 10 to net 15. AJ's own exit from field cleaning came with debt and repossessed cars, offset only by having a system that finally ran without AJ in it.

The failure that does not show up on a profit sheet is people. DJ The Boss, roughly seven years and about $500,000 into a single contract, says sales is easy and managing people is the hard part [ZVDOV6jG8Dw @ 00:10:51]. AJ Simmons warns solo operators that even great cleaning loses to a relationship: a competitor met at a Chamber of Commerce event can take the account, so daytime inspections at least quarterly and steady communication are the defense [mp0PpDrOpYM @ 00:04:57]. Ricky Regalado names the sales-side version, walkthroughs wasted because the proposal did not go out quickly, and sets a two-to-three-hour turnaround; Regalado also admits showing up late ruined one walkthrough [QBuGc_Q36dg @ 00:05:39]. Nicole Thomas hit the reverse problem: clients got too attached to Thomas personally, which blocks handing off accounts, so Thomas's daughter now comes along from the first visit [NHWdv_WG2B8 @ 00:18:21]. AJ's no-call/no-show rule is automatic firing, and a nine-hour relocation forced backup plans B, C and D plus invoice credits to keep contracts alive. The goal AJ sets is keeping a contract five years rather than squeezing profit from it today.
