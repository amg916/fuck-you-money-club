## Turn keyword lists into groups, then decide which groups need pages

Travis Wilkie ([0qP8UUWuBSc @ 00:01:09]) demonstrates a Semrush workflow that starts with Keyword Magic Tool results and adds selected phrases to a keto-diet list. He describes clusters as similar variations that one page can rank for, then moves into the strategy builder and clusters the list. Tutorial Stack ([fLGvfe6OTgc @ 00:01:00]) shows the other entry point: supply related but varied seeds directly to Keyword Strategy Builder, using ergonomic office chairs and chairs for back pain as examples. The channel asks viewers not to repeat themselves too much when choosing those seeds. Robbie Richards ([P5ET0IvFReQ @ 00:07:01]) demonstrates importing an existing set instead of generating everything from scratch: his pasted list contains 214 keywords and produces 38 clusters. Across these demonstrations, the shared operation is organizing a collection that already has a business or topic connection, whether that collection comes from selected results, supplied seeds, or prior research. The speakers show more than one interface and starting point, so their instructions should retain those distinctions. Wilkie's list workflow explains how selection feeds clustering; Tutorial Stack's seed workflow explains how ideas enter generation; Richards' import example explains how an existing research asset can be reorganized without beginning again with an empty list.

Ahrefs Product Updates ([JMyPIbu7vhw @ 00:00:53]) distinguishes grouping by terms from grouping by parent topic, with sidebar numbers showing the total search volume associated with a word or phrase in the target country. Parent topics help the presenter scan a broad area, while term groups offer a way to investigate more specific angles. The dalgona-coffee example develops an informational post containing relevant subtopics rather than treating every wording as a separate assignment. Travis Wilkie ([wSkIVyKv2rY @ 00:00:53]) demonstrates a different narrowing operation in Keywords Explorer: apply a minimum of 50 monthly searches, then commercial and transactional intent, leaving about 65 phrases in the example. He calls this a cluster and exports it for further targeting. Robbie Richards ([P5ET0IvFReQ @ 00:03:04]) supplies the next decision that filtering alone does not settle: whether a head-on car-accident attorney phrase requires a dedicated page or can be covered by the main service page. He answers by examining the URLs already ranking. These demonstrations preserve an important difference in what the speakers actually do: a filtered list collects candidates, a sidebar group organizes exploration, and the observed competing pages help determine the eventual page structure. Their examples do not make every collected phrase an automatic new page.

Robbie Richards ([P5ET0IvFReQ @ 00:03:19]) hovers over the competing URLs for the head-on collision cluster and observes dedicated pages nested beneath broader accident services. That repeated pattern leads him to propose a dedicated page beneath the main car-accident attorney page, incorporating the cluster's variations. Travis Wilkie ([0qP8UUWuBSc @ 00:03:06]) performs a related inspection with keto cookies: opening the competing results reveals recipe pages, which shapes his suggestion to create a better recipe page. Ahrefs Product Updates ([JMyPIbu7vhw @ 00:03:20]) considers a narrower audience inside a competitive topic, using dog food for allergies as a potential separate post when the site is not ready to compete for the broader dog-food query. Tutorial Stack ([fLGvfe6OTgc @ 00:02:52]) adds the explicit caution that appearing in a sidebar group does not make a keyword good; difficulty, volume, and the rest of the research still matter. Together, these examples show practitioners moving from a group label to the particular search result and audience. Richards examines dedicated-page targeting, Wilkie examines the content format already appearing, and the Ahrefs presenter examines a specific need within a broader category. The resulting assignment follows those observations, rather than the mere presence of a phrase in generated output.

## Evaluate difficulty, traffic, advertising cost, and marketplace competition separately

Tutorial Stack ([1Q38-qqOu3c @ 00:01:00]) looks for lower keyword difficulty with useful volume, suggesting 100 to 500 searches for someone starting out rather than requiring a very large head term. The tutorial uses question results and the easy difficulty filter to surface candidates. Kyle on My First Website ([Z2ya7VWFvh4 @ 00:01:56]) demonstrates a more explicit combination: a maximum difficulty of 35 and minimum volume of 100, followed by sorting and examining longer, problem-oriented phrases. WeStudyFunnels ([PpHWPSjRYPI @ 00:01:51]) then shows why an attractive filter result does not complete the analysis. The treadmill-under-$1,000 phrase has difficulty 30 and volume 3,600, yet the speaker finds Wirecutter well below the leading results and later observes extensive exact targeting among competing pages. The point is not that the earlier filters disappear; the demonstrations give them different jobs. Tutorial Stack and Kyle use them to produce a manageable set of possibilities, while WeStudyFunnels examines what the score can miss about a particular result set. The reported figures belong to those examples and do not supply one universal threshold across every site. After filtering, the actual ranked pages still determine whether the apparent opportunity resembles the competition the researcher is prepared to tackle.

WeStudyFunnels ([PpHWPSjRYPI @ 00:04:06]) counts fifteen leading treadmill results with the target phrase in their titles and reads that as evidence of active targeting. The speaker contrasts this with a difficulty calculation heavily influenced by backlinks: fewer links to those specific articles do not mean strong domains are absent from the query. Kyle on My First Website ([Z2ya7VWFvh4 @ 00:05:48]) likewise says difficulty is an estimate and describes checking results even when a keyword displays KD 15. His inspection considers strong domains, weak backlinks, thin content, and outdated information. Travis Wilkie ([D7f-Pid5IhM @ 00:02:10]) investigates a different number altogether in the advertising workflow: cost per click. He filters the plumbing example from $1 to $15 and then narrows by commercial and transactional intent, later widening the price range. His earlier explanation distinguishes cheaper competitor-business searches from traffic he expects to be more useful. These examples keep organic difficulty and advertising cost attached to their respective tasks. A low difficulty candidate still receives a result-page inspection; a cheaper ad keyword still receives an intent and competitor-name inspection. The speakers are not replacing relevance with the smallest available number. They use the numbers to reduce the list, then inspect what those surviving searches actually represent.

Hamish on Income stream surfers ([C6HdlbqGuF4 @ 00:10:58]) applies the research to print-on-demand marketplaces by entering a marketplace domain, filtering ranking positions, and narrowing titles or URLs to product language such as shirts, necklaces, or earrings. He then checks the keyword in the target country's Google results and examines the marketplace itself. His example distinguishes a crowded marketplace result from a small result set lacking exact-phrase titles, where he considers a specifically titled product. Kyle on My First Website ([Z2ya7VWFvh4 @ 00:04:51]) adds another way to inspect apparent demand: compare the keyword's stated volume with estimated traffic to the leading pages. His example contrasts 200 monthly searches with a page receiving 1,500 visits from 30 related keywords. Ahrefs Product Updates ([JMyPIbu7vhw @ 00:03:20]) similarly looks beneath a broad term for an audience-specific angle, finding dog-food allergy queries that appear less competitive. These are different kinds of opportunity, not interchangeable measurements. Hamish checks marketplace visibility and competing product titles; Kyle examines the broader traffic a page receives; the Ahrefs presenter examines a narrower need. Hamish also says there is no minimum search-volume requirement in his marketplace workflow, preserving a different selection approach from Kyle's demonstrated minimum-volume filter rather than forcing both into one rule.

## Close silo gaps and advance only with the traction the speakers describe

Topical Authority SEO | Floyi ([0XswjN-Lops @ 00:00:38]) classifies silos using comparative ranking coverage: leading means matching or exceeding the top competitor's count of topics ranking in the top 20, while contested means at least 60% of that count or being ten topics or fewer behind. Its investment ordering starts with contested silos and the smallest gaps, then moves to leading, open, and trailing silos. Kyle on My First Website ([Z2ya7VWFvh4 @ 00:03:41]) demonstrates a keyword-gap workflow by comparing a site with two to three competitors and filtering the resulting opportunities by difficulty and volume. Hamish on Income stream surfers ([C6HdlbqGuF4 @ 00:07:39]) describes comparing one website against four others to uncover phrases the first site is missing. All three approaches use competitors to expose absences, but Floyi adds an explicit map-level ordering to the resulting work. Its recommendation is to finish leading and contested areas before spreading effort across trailing ones, rather than treating every missing keyword as equally urgent. Kyle's and Hamish's comparisons explain how candidates enter the research; Floyi explains which existing silo receives attention first. That distinction keeps a large gap export from replacing the stated decision about where a focused publishing effort should go next.

David G. Quaid on James Dooley ([mE2Y-3fNorE @ 00:06:07]) describes putting down the next cornerstone and waiting a week: if it does not receive clicks, he does not keep advancing. Instead, he starts another topic cluster or revisits where he misread the authority required. James Dooley ([mE2Y-3fNorE @ 00:13:53]) explicitly separates topical coverage from topical authority, describing authority as coverage plus historical data from traffic, links, social activity, and engagement. Edward Sturm ([8DuIlUdfY_w @ 00:09:34]) presents purchase-intent landing pages as contributors to topical authority and places link building and marketing alongside them as off-site contributors. These accounts do not reduce authority to the number of articles produced. Quaid describes progressing through achievable queries and responding to observed clicks; Dooley names the history missing from mere coverage; Sturm emphasizes both commercially focused pages and the language surrounding external mentions. Their different routes also preserve different immediate publishing choices. Sturm does not require a large educational blog to establish the topic, while Quaid describes smaller related terms as an entrance when the desired money term is out of reach. In both accounts, the relationship between the chosen topic and subsequent activity matters more than declaring authority simply because a content map has been filled.

Topical Authority SEO | Floyi ([0XswjN-Lops @ 00:03:50]) makes each non-ranking topic actionable according to its existing state: create a brief when nothing exists, create a draft from a ready brief, or continue editing the work already started. The tutorial also explains that silo analysis updates when scores recalculate or search results refresh. David G. Quaid on James Dooley ([mE2Y-3fNorE @ 00:15:46]) describes building page clusters around the words needed for a target and linking those pages to the final money page. Edward Sturm ([8DuIlUdfY_w @ 00:16:06]) carries the chosen commercial language into link building, public relations, and branding, especially off-site activity. These are distinct next actions after research, rather than a single instruction to publish every remaining phrase. Floyi resumes the actual content work already present; Quaid connects supporting pages with the commercial destination; Sturm aligns external descriptions with the searches that bring customers. The resulting chapter workflow retains the speakers' own checks: identify the business language, group candidates, inspect competing pages, choose the appropriate page coverage, and revisit the topic using ranking or click evidence. Completion of a brief or draft is therefore a different recorded state from a topic entering Floyi's top-20 coverage count, just as publishing coverage differs from the authority Dooley describes.
