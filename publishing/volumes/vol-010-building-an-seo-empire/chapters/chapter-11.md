## Network Selection: Requirements, Revenue Share, and Working Style

Casey Botticello ([uSKcihbY0hs @ 00:00:59]) begins the comparison with different denominators: Mediavine requires 50,000 monthly sessions in that recording, while Raptive requires 100,000 monthly page views. They explicitly distinguish sessions from page views rather than treating the thresholds as interchangeable. David Kowalski ([OzPRqoG7brI @ 00:02:10]) describes a different entry ladder: Journey at 1,000 sessions, Monumetric at 10,000 page views, Raptive at 25,000 page views, Ezoic at 250,000 monthly active users, and Mediavine at $5,000 in annual ad revenue. In that account, the criteria differ not only in size but also in what gets measured. David Kowalski ([OzPRqoG7brI @ 00:06:44]) dates the Raptive reduction to October 2025 and describes geography requirements that vary with traffic size. Casey Botticello ([uSKcihbY0hs @ 00:13:30]) instead discusses a newly introduced 50,000-page-view Raptive program alongside the standard requirement. These are the speakers' descriptions of different program stages, not a single simultaneous eligibility schedule. Casey also distinguishes first-site applications from secondary-site opportunities elsewhere in the comparison. The selection question in these accounts is therefore specific: which program, which traffic measure, which audience geography, and which stage of the network's offering is the speaker describing? The reported entry number alone does not capture that comparison.

Casey Botticello ([uSKcihbY0hs @ 00:21:27]) describes Raptive's publisher revenue share as a flat 75%, while Mediavine starts at the same level and offers increases through performance and loyalty. Their comparison separates the percentage retained by the publisher from the headline earnings shown in a dashboard. Casey Botticello ([uSKcihbY0hs @ 00:24:53]) adds a consequential switching detail: leaving Mediavine resets the loyalty bonus, so returning does not restore the previous consecutive-years position. Performance-based increases and loyalty increases are described separately. She Knows SEO ([dL9VBY-Gj0Y @ 00:14:06]) puts more emphasis on payment timing, contrasting Raptive's net 45 schedule with Mediavine's net 65 schedule and explaining why receiving money sooner mattered when blog income was needed for bills. Max Kuch ([kkVGjM2Fpok @ 00:01:23]) likewise reports the 75% share and net 45 terms, illustrating the delay with April earnings received in mid-June. Their account calls attention to cash flow even while praising RPMs. The speakers thus weigh different components of the same relationship: the publisher's share, the payment delay, and what survives a move away and back. Casey's preference for accumulating Mediavine benefits and She Knows SEO's appreciation of faster Raptive payments remain distinct practitioner priorities rather than one combined verdict on which arrangement every publisher should choose.

Casey Botticello ([uSKcihbY0hs @ 00:35:17]) describes Mediavine as the more hands-on environment for changing ad settings, including density, directly in the account. Raptive generally requires an email for those changes in their experience. That difference is about how settings are changed, not simply whether a publisher can influence them. She Knows SEO ([dL9VBY-Gj0Y @ 00:42:25]) reports being happy with Raptive's control despite using email rather than a dashboard percentage selector. They asked for a particular structure and valued the support team's implementation. How To Mr ([nIeVxkyipjw @ 00:01:48]) frames managed operation as a tradeoff: onboarding includes a site audit, and having the network handle operations leaves less manual control for publishers who prefer frequent adjustments. Meghan's Media ([NRcTFL8qghY @ 00:03:03]) describes a similarly hands-off Mediavine setup followed by accessible dashboard toggles for individual placements. Their account emphasizes that the publisher can still decide what remains enabled. Across these descriptions, managed setup and publisher choice coexist, but the working interface differs. Casey wants direct experimentation, She Knows SEO is comfortable requesting changes, How To Mr favors an established publisher seeking a managed solution, and Meghan's Media appreciates straightforward controls after the initial setup. Those preferences explain why comparable ad formats can still produce different experiences of the service.

## RPM Operations: Read the Measure, Improve Engagement, Inspect Placements

Vitaliy Gershfeld ([NwV-eO-VZe8 @ 00:02:15]) explains RPM's practical importance with an unchanged audience: 1,000 visitors at $30 produces $30, while the same visitor count at $40 produces $40. Their example concerns earning more from the same traffic rather than requiring another traffic increase. Meghan's Media ([NRcTFL8qghY @ 00:01:04]) describes Mediavine's reporting in terms of revenue per thousand sessions and contrasts that with the per-click or per-impression account they give of AdSense. She Knows SEO ([xRuzXK8HIFo @ 00:05:51]) demonstrates switching the Raptive display between sessions and page views, noting that the reported averages change substantially because a session can contain multiple page views. Their walkthrough therefore shows why two dashboard numbers can describe the same site differently. David Kowalski ([OzPRqoG7brI @ 00:07:50]) says the networks do not publish the official RPM figures they sought and describes individual dollar figures as estimates rather than a rate card. They name niche, geography, seasonality, placement viewability, and audience engagement as influences. Together, these explanations keep the measurement attached to its denominator and context: Vitaliy's visitor example teaches the earnings effect, Meghan identifies session-based reporting, and the Raptive walkthrough exposes the page-view alternative. David's discussion also leaves room for different publishers to report different outcomes without turning any one outcome into a network-wide price.

Vitaliy Gershfeld ([Gb-4YRsXxSk @ 00:01:52]) recommends listicle content because readers scroll through successive items and more ads load as they continue. Their example is a numbered collection of hikes near Las Vegas, built around a searched topic rather than numbering unrelated material. Vitaliy Gershfeld ([Gb-4YRsXxSk @ 00:04:13]) extends the engagement approach to internal links: a reader interested in a named destination can follow a link to another article and remain on the website longer. The same discussion recommends images that interrupt repetitive reading and videos that visitors may spend time watching. HeyRahat ([2oU82BZ6KOU @ 00:00:43]) works on the presentation around that content, recommending larger text and describing a preferred range of 18 to 20 pixels. HeyRahat ([2oU82BZ6KOU @ 00:02:08]) also recommends adjusting line height and breaking longer paragraphs into shorter pieces, presenting these as changes tested on their own blog. The two accounts address different parts of the reading experience: Vitaliy offers reasons to keep exploring, while HeyRahat changes how the material is displayed. Neither account supplies a separate measured contribution for every individual change. Their recommendations are reported as the speakers present them: content and formatting choices associated with keeping readers engaged and improving the RPM experience on their own sites.

Meghan's Media ([NRcTFL8qghY @ 00:04:05]) recommends checking how much individual placements earn before disabling them. The dashboard's dollar breakdown helps identify whether a placement being considered for removal contributes substantially to income. That advice accompanies their concern about readers struggling to reach a recipe through excessive ads, rather than a blanket instruction to remove or retain every format. She Knows SEO ([xRuzXK8HIFo @ 00:08:48]) examines Raptive's page-level visibility score, describing possible problems with loading, early departures, or insufficient scrolling when readers are not seeing ads. They flag a score below 50% as something to work on. Max Kuch ([kkVGjM2Fpok @ 00:05:13]) uses device and format breakdowns to examine where earnings originate, noting that desktop visitors can see more ads at once. She Knows SEO ([xRuzXK8HIFo @ 00:17:19]) supplies an example of a format test that did not impress them: mobile interstitials were enabled after hearing about other publishers' results, but the speaker disliked the experience and reported little benefit on their own site. These accounts make placement decisions specific to the publisher's evidence. Earnings by unit, visibility by page, device differences, and the observed performance of a newly enabled format each answer a different question before the publisher requests another change.

These accounts do not erase one another. Meghan and She Knows SEO describe switches they liked, while Casey emphasizes similarities and differences beyond headline RPM. ## Affiliate Alternatives and the Traffic-Dependence Challenge

David Kowalski ([OzPRqoG7brI @ 00:09:41]) distinguishes affiliate income from display income by describing payment for a purchase decision rather than a view. They point to product roundups, comparisons, and buying-oriented articles as places where affiliate monetization can outperform display on the same traffic. David Kowalski ([OzPRqoG7brI @ 00:10:53]) then links ads, affiliates, products, and email: traffic supports ads, intent supports affiliates, trust supports products, and email allows another contact with a visitor. Their recommendation is to build an email list and one product while moving through ad-network options. Revenue Tactics ([7pZQrsYVmt4 @ 00:02:50]) presents a challenging companion account. The Tennis Bros already had affiliate commissions, Mediavine display advertising, coaching courses, consultancy, and a custom racket-fitting service before the reported search collapse. Multiple revenue streams were therefore already part of that business. Meghan's Media ([XXTUzO1Y_1U @ 00:06:09]) reinforces the separate traffic question through their Pinterest decline: Google traffic helped support the site while they worked on Pinterest recovery. These accounts distinguish expanding monetization from expanding acquisition. David explains ways to earn beyond impressions; Revenue Tactics describes those methods existing inside a search-dependent asset; Meghan emphasizes having more than one source bringing readers to the website.

Revenue Tactics ([7pZQrsYVmt4 @ 00:04:17]) describes selling the tennis business for a strong five-figure amount after previously discussing a multiple-six-figure valuation. The speaker attributes the gap to the collapse in organic traffic and treats it as the central lesson of the business. Revenue Tactics ([7pZQrsYVmt4 @ 00:09:41]) proposes paid traffic to a landing page to an offer as the alternative foundation. They emphasize decisions about spend, offers, creative, landing pages, and testing rather than waiting for search rankings. Revenue Tactics ([7pZQrsYVmt4 @ 00:10:06]) also states that this requires a testing budget and that early campaigns may not be immediately profitable. Their preference is for risking a defined testing budget rather than years of content work. Meghan's Media ([XXTUzO1Y_1U @ 00:06:57]) offers a less sweeping response to platform disruption: begin with a couple of traffic sources, learn them, and expand later. Their starting combination was Pinterest and Google. The positions remain different. Revenue Tactics argues for changing the foundation of an affiliate operation, while Meghan continues the blog model with broader traffic acquisition. Both accounts connect income decisions to what happens when the platform supplying visitors changes, rather than discussing RPM as an isolated measure.

David Kowalski ([OzPRqoG7brI @ 00:07:20]) describes Mediavine and Journey exclusivity as control over programmatic advertising, while saying affiliate links, sponsored posts, and the publisher's own products remain compatible. The warning is specifically against treating another display network as an effortless side-by-side test. CanEye ([kZ7NX7BR1X8 @ 00:08:34]) shows a different selection issue: Monetag offers separate zones, so a publisher can remove intrusive formats instead of retaining the all-format configuration used in the speaker's test. They suggest this may matter for sites unable to enter other networks, while keeping their own negative reaction to the ads explicit. Carl Broadbent ([feVaD3YiPFE @ 00:15:12]) reports testing a website with and without ads and seeing no change in traffic or reading duration; removing ads from buyer-intent content also did not improve sales in their account. She Knows SEO ([dL9VBY-Gj0Y @ 00:15:10]) adds a switching pitfall unrelated to reader response: losing Mediavine dashboard access after departure and needing to request a spreadsheet. The speakers' practical checks therefore cover coexistence rules, actual ad behavior, observed reader and sales effects, and continued access to historical data. Each check comes from a described experience rather than an assumption that one configuration fits every website.
