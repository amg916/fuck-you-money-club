## Separate crawl capacity, crawl demand, and the evidence of a problem

Daniel Waisberg at Google Search Central ([x_GoMrl6_u0 @ 00:02:35]) defines crawl budget as the number of URLs Google can and wants to crawl, bringing together crawl rate and crawl demand rather than presenting a target every website should maximize. In the tutorial, responsiveness determines how much crawling the server can handle, while demand concerns new addresses and changes to known content. Andrew Halliday on James Dooley ([cv7lfh1AjZc @ 00:01:12]) puts stable hosting first: repeated server errors reduce crawling, so the foundation precedes attempts to attract additional visits. Their practical emphases overlap without making every increase in requests a ranking result. Halliday discusses backlinks, schema, paid traffic, and social signals as ways of building trust and encouraging activity; Waisberg explains the request capacity and indexing demand behind Google's decisions. Daniel Heredia on BoostRoas ([kuSNDOAsr50 @ 00:27:13]) explicitly says crawling is necessary for appearing in results but is not itself a ranking signal. Read these accounts as explanations of different parts of the process: whether the server can respond, whether Google wants the content, and whether fetched content proceeds into indexing. A technically responsive site and an increased request count therefore answer different questions from whether an individual page has become searchable.

The speakers do not give one shared threshold for concern. Gary Illyes at Google Search Central ([LIMqmzuzpVg @ 00:02:35]) estimates that over 90% of sites need not worry about crawl budget; the discussion repeatedly distinguishes large-scale constraints from anxiety about a small website's latest post. Andrew Halliday on FatRank ([GVJrNHhAXME @ 00:07:18]) similarly contrasts a five-page local plumber website with a substantial ecommerce catalog, making site size central to the usefulness of tracking. Misha Manko ([nBrto2B9RyE @ 00:08:55]) explicitly takes a different position, saying their experience makes consideration worthwhile once a site has more than 100 pages. Greenlane Search Marketing, LLC ([NyNv-u7KkFw @ 00:01:37]) also emphasizes larger, database-driven sites but warns against dismissing smaller ones. These positions remain attributed rather than being collapsed into a universal page-count rule. Their examples direct attention toward actual symptoms: important pages not receiving visits, excessive generated URLs, server limitations, or accumulated content drawing continued attention. The Google discussion also describes demand and quality considerations, not just exhausted server capacity. Consequently, the chapter's contrasting accounts support inspecting the site's recorded behavior before choosing which explanation fits, while preserving each speaker's stated view about where that investigation deserves priority.

Daniel Waisberg at Google Search Central ([x_GoMrl6_u0 @ 00:03:54]) walks through three Crawl Stats trends: total requests, downloaded data, and average response time. Requests include unsuccessful attempts, while resources hosted outside the property do not appear in that site's totals. The response-time measure concerns retrieving page content, not rendering the page or retrieving its associated resources. Ismael at Pearl Lemon SEO ([aRuxCdZQ1Yo @ 00:06:50]) demonstrates why file-type percentages need interpretation: the example shows JavaScript at 41% and CSS at 32%, yet they explain that the WordPress implementation needs those files for rendering. The tutorial does not treat those proportions as an emergency. Waisberg at Google Search Central ([x_GoMrl6_u0 @ 00:08:10]) separately distinguishes discovery of new URLs from refresh crawling of known pages, and shows breakdowns by crawler type. Together these demonstrations make the report a way to identify what changed, rather than a scoreboard where every resource request counts against useful crawling. Examine the requested addresses, response patterns, and purpose behind the totals. Pearl Lemon's example particularly warns against blocking assets merely because their share looks large; the speaker describes that action as giving Google a fragmented version of the website rather than solving the suspected problem.

## Preserve, validate, and segment the requests that actually reached the site

Log investigations begin with retaining the evidence. Carl Dalton on FatRank ([9YOo5zDOsP8 @ 00:05:00]) describes shared hosting that overwrites logs after a short period and a cron job that moves the current log before the next file begins. Their preferred longer view is about six months, rather than treating isolated daily activity as the whole picture. Andrew Halliday on James Dooley ([cv7lfh1AjZc @ 00:04:35]) asks ideally for three to six months, but says 28 days can begin an investigation, especially for visible server errors. Jamie's presentation on Semrush Live ([eelEuYoflG0 @ 00:03:04]) adds a different completeness issue: CDNs, load balancers, and web servers maintain separate records, so collecting only one location may miss where Googlebot actually interacts with the site. Cached edge requests particularly matter when choosing which logs to aggregate. The acquisition task is therefore both historical and architectural: retain enough time to compare behavior, and include the places serving the requests under investigation. Ask the existing log management team what is already captured and how fields are defined. Semrush Live's presentation favors using functionality already in place rather than rebuilding collection simply because an SEO investigation has introduced a new question.

The brightonSEO presenter ([Q9Nkmc8Cx6I @ 00:09:02]) warns that apparent bot identities must be checked against provider IP information. Their workflow fetches published address data live during ingestion rather than assuming a downloaded list remains current, and keeps rejected spoofed traffic available for a separate investigation. Semrush Live ([eelEuYoflG0 @ 00:06:11]) likewise makes Googlebot validation a step before asking SEO questions of the logs, offering manual checks, scripts, or tools according to the amount of data. Once requests are validated, the point is to organize them around a question. TM Blast ([1v7Wiw-ptLU @ 00:02:33]) exports crawled URLs into a pivot table and counts events by blog section, comparing that activity with priority service pages. The example finds 266 blog events over 30 days against 893 total events, leading Greg to examine legacy posts and plugin URLs. Those figures describe that site's investigation, not a target allocation for every business. The presenter examines actual destinations and the value of the content before describing removals and blocking tests. This combination of verified identity, URL grouping, and page-level inspection keeps a large volume of requests connected to the specific question of what the site's important pages are receiving.

The hospital investigation at brightonSEO ([Q9Nkmc8Cx6I @ 00:11:32]) compares recorded ChatGPT crawling with the XML sitemap and finds only 1.3% coverage, concentrated near the top of the site. The presenter then investigates 499 responses and JavaScript-rendered internal links that prevent the relevant bots from reaching deeper content. A temporary dynamic-rendering arrangement serves cached rendered HTML, with refreshes every six hours; the presenter reports improved crawling and uses the case to support a later server-side-rendering upgrade. They explicitly describe the workaround as a difficult-to-maintain bandage, not the root repair. Semrush Live ([eelEuYoflG0 @ 00:14:42]) provides a different example: a broad response-code view looks mostly healthy, but segmenting 500 responses by subfolder exposes a failing AJAX endpoint essential to rendering content. FatRank ([9YOo5zDOsP8 @ 00:10:17]) adds an outcome check after link work: if crawler revisits rise without impressions or clicks improving, inspect the content and page weight rather than stopping at the crawl increase. These cases show why overall totals do not finish the diagnosis. Each speaker moves from a broad pattern to a particular destination, dependency, or content problem, then evaluates what changed after the intervention.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Preserve the relevant request history
   - Collect retained logs and establish where requests are recorded.
   - Include CDN, load-balancer, and web-server records where those layers serve the traffic being investigated. [Semrush Live — eelEuYoflG0 @ 00:03:04]
2. Validate the crawler population
   - Separate verified crawler traffic from merely claimed bot identities.
   - Use current provider IP information during ingestion and keep the spoofed requests available for their own analysis. [brightonSEO — Q9Nkmc8Cx6I @ 00:09:02]
3. Segment requests around important destinations
   - Group URL events by the sections and pages under investigation.
   - Follow Greg's export-and-pivot approach to compare blog activity with the service pages whose crawling matters to the business. [TM Blast — 1v7Wiw-ptLU @ 00:02:33]
4. Check what the intervention changed
   - Compare crawler revisits with impressions and clicks after the work.
   - If additional crawling does not produce movement in impressions or clicks, investigate the page's content and weight. [FatRank — 9YOo5zDOsP8 @ 00:10:17]

## Build discovery routes and scale the checks with Python

BKA Content ([n68rh1s-ZdY @ 00:03:22]) starts website structure with the customer's journey and top navigation, using an RV park to illustrate a homepage, rates, amenities, locations, attractions, company information, and a blog. The presentation recommends placing the blog in the main navigation instead of burying it beneath another page, then developing subpages around the information customers seek. Long-term and short-term stays, serviced neighboring locations, and nearby attractions become specific destinations rather than unrelated topics crowded onto one page. Abdul Latif ([pjmp4gM12iQ @ 00:02:28]) complements that structural account with routes into important pages: link from the homepage and well-visited blog posts to relevant service pages inside useful sentences. Their audit examples concern existing money pages that have few or no internal routes, not simply a shortage of published content. Misha Manko ([vcLkBQxf9Hs @ 00:12:22]) adds a condition to the location-page approach: pages that merely exchange city names while retaining the same text, photos, and questions look like duplicates in their account. They recommend genuinely local material or a consolidated service-area page when meaningful differentiation is unavailable. The speakers therefore describe both a discovery structure and the distinct content that belongs at the destinations it exposes.

Chris Palmer SEO ([qLr_HlF7dKw @ 00:01:19]) shows the Links section in Search Console as a starting point for examining internally linked pages, then uses Screaming Frog when more anchor detail is needed. Their analysis distinguishes links pointing into a particular page from the wording distributed across the whole site. Chris Palmer SEO ([qLr_HlF7dKw @ 00:08:09]) demonstrates the inlink anchor-text visualization, where larger words represent more frequent wording, and connects that inspection to the term variations ranking pages already cover. Abdul Latif ([pjmp4gM12iQ @ 00:03:01]) offers the simpler labeling check: a reader seeing only the anchor should understand the destination, instead of encountering repeated generic instructions to click or learn more. Palmer also describes more aggressive internal optimization through jump links, headings, and follow-noindex pages, reporting their use in competitive examples. Chris Palmer SEO ([qLr_HlF7dKw @ 00:11:10]) presents these as ways of exposing more term variations internally without adding ordinary indexed pages for every variation. These are Palmer's reported tactics, while Latif's account emphasizes clear contextual routes and avoiding indiscriminate linking. Keeping both accounts visible preserves their different emphases: sitewide anchor optimization on one side, and understandable paths to important existing content on the other.

Daniel Heredia on BoostRoas ([kuSNDOAsr50 @ 00:31:47]) demonstrates Python as a way to inspect linking structure and identify proposed connections between source and target URLs. Their cryptocurrency-site example produces up to 50,000 internal-link opportunities, with keywords, source pages, and destinations available for review. The presentation also describes combining that output with Internal Links Manager on WordPress to create links from supplied terms and targets. BoostRoas ([kuSNDOAsr50 @ 00:33:56]) extends the workflow to bulk PageSpeed Insights analysis, storing measurements so deployments can be compared across URLs rather than checking only the homepage. The brightonSEO presenter ([Q9Nkmc8Cx6I @ 00:07:24]) explains another reason for Python: a ten-day log collection occupies 30 to 40 gigabytes before filtering, and even the reduced dataset contains about eight million rows. Filtering around the actual question matters more than trying to inspect everything in a spreadsheet. Heredia also covers regular checks for indexing problems, log parsing, and browser-driven report exports, but does not prescribe programming for every task. BoostRoas ([kuSNDOAsr50 @ 00:41:50]) explicitly recommends an existing market solution when it already handles the process. Python serves repeatable collection, comparison, and scale in these demonstrations; it is not presented as a replacement for deciding which pages and symptoms deserve attention.

## Diagnose excluded pages without confusing notifications, content, and rendering

Misha Manko ([vcLkBQxf9Hs @ 00:08:44]) distinguishes crawled-currently-not-indexed from discovered-currently-not-indexed: the first describes a page Google fetched but did not retain in the index, while the second describes a known URL not yet crawled. Their walkthrough treats the report as information requiring interpretation, not a list where every entry must disappear. Feeds, filters, internal searches, account pages, checkout pages, and canonical variants can belong outside the index; important products, articles, categories, and previously trafficked pages deserve a different response. Greenlane Search Marketing, LLC ([NyNv-u7KkFw @ 00:00:48]) approaches the same inventory question through accumulated content, arguing that old indexed material can obstruct attention to newer, more valuable pages. TM Blast ([1v7Wiw-ptLU @ 00:06:47]) describes removing about 100 irrelevant older posts and taking them out of both HTML and XML sitemaps to concentrate attention on current content and core service pages. Those are concrete inventory decisions tied to the speakers' examples, rather than a requirement to delete a fixed share of every site. Manko's distinction supplies the initial sorting question: does the excluded URL represent something the business actually wants indexed, or is its exclusion consistent with the function of that page?

The speakers also disagree about how particular cleanup actions affect crawl allocation. FatRank ([9YOo5zDOsP8 @ 00:03:25]) reports that changing an obsolete URL to 410 was followed by more crawling of a struggling category and subsequent movement in impressions and clicks. Gary Illyes at Google Search Central ([LIMqmzuzpVg @ 00:26:37]) explicitly says 404, 410, and robots-blocked URLs do not waste crawl budget, then contrasts them with soft 404 responses. The chapter preserves the reported intervention and Google's explanation without turning them into a single agreed mechanism. Pearl Lemon SEO ([aRuxCdZQ1Yo @ 00:09:43]) recommends removing or noindexing low-value pages, fixing parameter duplication, strengthening internal links, and cleaning the sitemap. Misha Manko ([nBrto2B9RyE @ 00:19:19]) separately warns that noindex does not stop fetching because Google can continue accessing a known URL without indexing it. Their different accounts make the intended outcome important: removing an unwanted search result, changing which URLs exist, and influencing which requests occur are not described identically. Keep the diagnosis attached to the actual status, URL, and reported behavior instead of attributing every cleanup result to a universally accepted budget transfer. That preserves the transcript debate while retaining the concrete repairs each speaker teaches.

For a page that should be indexed, Misha Manko ([vcLkBQxf9Hs @ 00:30:20]) demonstrates inspecting the URL, running the live test, and viewing the tested page's HTML to see what Google can access. Their technical exception concerns content that arrives too late or depends on JavaScript interaction, leaving an apparently complete human-facing page empty to the crawler. Semrush Live ([eelEuYoflG0 @ 00:16:26]) recommends combining crawl information with logs to find indexable pages without bot hits and to examine whether internal links are actually implemented with an anchor and href value. Daniel Heredia on BoostRoas ([kuSNDOAsr50 @ 00:37:36]) adds recurring checks and notifications for accidental indexing directives, robots blocks, or important URLs returning 404 responses. These demonstrations cover different failure points without treating a repeated indexing request as the repair. Misha Manko ([vcLkBQxf9Hs @ 00:21:36]) specifically says requesting indexing again will not change the outcome when the page and website remain unchanged. Their content remedies include consolidating near-identical products, creating genuinely differentiated local pages, and replacing copied supplier material with distinct information. The working sequence in these accounts is to establish accessibility and discovery, inspect what was fetched, repair the identified technical or content issue, and then observe subsequent crawling and indexing behavior.
