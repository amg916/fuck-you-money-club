# Chapter 07: The Letter of Intent: Anatomy and Drafting

*Whether LOIs are necessary, binding vs. non-binding terms, the LOI's components and anatomy, terms to settle before diligence, structuring a winning small-business LOI, lawyer guidance on LOIs, the LOI's place among the M&A milestones, Acquire.com's LOI builder, and what an LOI does in a buy-sell process*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from law firms, investment banks and acquisition marketplaces treat the LOI as a short, mostly non-binding letter that locks price, structure, payment form, working capital, timing and closing conditions before anyone pays for diligence, with exclusivity and confidentiality carrying the binding weight. The street numbers are concrete: exclusivity of 60 to 90 days, purchase agreements of 40 to 70 pages waiting downstream, 80/10/10 payment structures, seller notes at 6 to 10%, a $5M working capital target that swings price $1M either way, targeted escrows of $500K to $1M, and a 72-hour expiration. A buyer who skipped the LOI lost three weeks and an $8,000 environmental report. Disagreements center on 60 versus 90 day closing dates, setting the working capital target in the LOI versus deferring it to the QoE, and whether an LOI is needed at all.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The cheapest insurance in a deal is a signed LOI. The Letters of Intent episode opens with a buyer who spent three weeks negotiating price verbally and paid for an $8,000 environmental report, only to watch the seller take a higher offer because nothing was signed and nothing was exclusive; the verdict was that an LOI would have cost them an afternoon [VuuLz-8NiYg @ 00:00:14]. Oberman Law Firm, which handles roughly 100-plus transactions a year, frames the LOI as the framework set before anyone spends money on diligence, and warns that skipping it misaligns expectations and can burn thousands of dollars in legal fees [6uqSoqOVnsg @ 00:01:17]. Eric Pacifici and Sam Rosati of SMB Law Group make the same economic argument: the LOI locks exclusivity before the buyer commits QoE spend, it sits upstream of a purchase agreement that runs 40 to 70 pages, and it separates a serious buyer from the roughly 600 inbound inquiries a broker fields on a listing [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:04:29]. Mike Rosendahl of PCE Investment Bankers puts the purpose plainly: let the parties confirm very early that there is basic agreement on key terms and no deal-breaker issues before either side devotes substantial time and resources to diligence and definitive documents [n_gxsr8QgBM @ 00:00:38].

On what actually binds, the practitioners converge hard. Brett Cenkus, who works deals between $0.5M and $20M, says exclusivity and confidentiality are usually the only binding terms, with exclusivity meaning the seller will not talk to other parties or keep shopping the deal for commonly 60 or 90 days [aNioqOmfOe4 @ 00:02:47]. Lockaby PLLC names confidentiality and exclusivity as the binding pair, then rounds out the letter with conditions precedent, a non-compete, a conduct-of-business covenant, limits on public announcements, governing law, dispute resolution and a merger clause [NCbzskaBTdQ @ 00:10:25]. Mike Rosendahl adds governing law to the binding list and leaves price, structure, contingencies, diligence and working capital explicitly non-binding [n_gxsr8QgBM @ 00:01:14]. The Letters of Intent episode calls non-binding the market standard, yet pushes breakup fees, governing law and dispute resolution into the binding bucket, and warns that re-trading the economics after signing can be bad faith and potentially actionable [VuuLz-8NiYg @ 00:13:17]. The operator read is that the non-binding label is not a license to walk back the numbers. The binding clauses carry the legal teeth, but reopening agreed business terms once the other side has turned away bidders invites exactly the bad-faith accusation these practitioners describe.

The term list is remarkably stable across channels. The Letters of Intent episode scopes an LOI at about 10 to 15 deal points and names the core set: price, deposit, diligence period, closing date, exclusivity, financing contingency, what is being sold, key economics, closing conditions, confidentiality, and the binding or non-binding designation [VuuLz-8NiYg @ 00:08:49]. Cenkus covers price, form of payment as cash or seller note, assets versus stock versus merger, employees and closing conditions, in a document of a few pages and up to about 10 [aNioqOmfOe4 @ 00:01:39]. PRIME exits demands clear structure and price, a defined adjusted EBITDA with add-backs, payment terms and a no-shop with commitment on both sides, warns that vague pricing signals you are not fully committed, and tells drafters not to over-legalize the letter [32xGWYMqfWk @ 00:00:57]. On length, nobody rewards bulk: Keira Hamilton says a 15-page LOI on a $1M HVAC business likely will not be read [JulvxMkY4VY @ 00:00:43]. The consensus build is short, priced precisely, and explicit about structure, payment form, timing and the conditions that must clear before close, with the heavy legal drafting saved for the purchase agreement that follows.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Exclusivity length is where the numbers get specific. Keira Hamilton ties it to lender speed: SBA loans take about 60 days, so ask for roughly 90 days of exclusivity, not 30 and not 180 [JulvxMkY4VY @ 00:05:21]. Ellie on the SMB PE episode says most deals close in 60 to 90 days, calls asking for 120 a bad bad move, notes that only 30 to 45 days are needed to reach a purchase agreement, and adds that deals almost never die because exclusivity runs out; the episode settles on 60 days plus automatic extensions [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:08:28]. Sam Rosati of SMB Law Group pegs SBA deals at about 90 days and expects a buyer who writes 60 to come back for an extension, so the exclusivity clause should carry extension cover; Eric Pacifici writes 60 days every time and would rather lose a rare deal later than lose every deal up front with a long projected closing date [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:18:58]. Escamilla Law Office treats exclusivity as a meaningful commercial concession and ties its duration to financing and diligence readiness, using defined deadlines and extensions the buyer has to earn rather than an open-ended hold on the seller [juS-FdGX2o0 @ 00:08:31]. The working rule is a short headline window with pre-negotiated extension mechanics behind it.

Price mechanics start with how the dollars move. SMB Law Group structures deals 80/10/10 or 90% cash, so an $8M deal puts $7.2M on the table at close, keeps the SBA debt amount out of the letter, and prices the seller note at typical 6 to 10% rates with monthly payments favoring the seller [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:36:34]. Pacifici and Rosati want the note modeled through standby, interest-only and balloon periods, flag a 50% note with a 12-month balloon as a trap, and tell buyers to foreshadow the subordination agreement because the note will have to subordinate to the lender. Escamilla contrasts two $5M offers, all cash versus $4M at close plus a $500K seller note plus a $500K earnout over two years, the second loading credit and performance risk onto the seller [juS-FdGX2o0 @ 00:02:35]. The acquire.com founder demos a $1.3M offer with $700K upfront, $500K seller financing over 12 months at zero interest, and a $100K holdback for 12 months conditioned on zero outstanding litigation [MYKKpeF0xMM @ 00:01:07]. Ellie hammers the logic behind every one of these splits: you cannot pay your full purchase price at closing, you need to hold back something [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:44:55].

Lockaby PLLC calls working capital the biggest deal-killer, and the practitioners attach hard numbers to it [NCbzskaBTdQ @ 00:05:34]. Rosendahl defines it as receivables plus inventory plus prepaid expenses, less accounts payable and accrued liabilities at close, with the seller keeping the cash; on a $5M target, delivering $6M pays the seller $1M more and delivering $4M cuts $1M from the price, trued up after close [n_gxsr8QgBM @ 00:04:31]. Ellie defers the target to the QoE, for example $1.5M, and uses escrow for the 60 to 90 day true-up and indemnification, replacing the fading 5 to 10% norm with targeted amounts such as $1M for ERC risk and $500K for sales tax [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:31:43]. The same episode moved definitions into an exhibit and cut comments by about 80%, sets a 72-hour expiration, and adds fee-shifting on exclusivity breach. On asset scope, SMB Law Group buys all assets owned or used by the business minus the seller's stated exclusions, a stance that won a Rhode Island Chancery fight over a seller-owned Amazon account carrying 4,500 five-star reviews, a 4.7 rating and about $2M of value [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:23:54].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Qualify the buyer and set up the signing vehicle
   - Form the acquisition entity and line up lender support before drafting the offer.
   - Business acquisitions typically close in 45 to 60 days, so attach a bank pre-approval letter to show the buyer can actually perform [Letters of Intent — VuuLz-8NiYg @ 00:07:12]; sign the NDA and LOI through a freshly formed entity and assign it to a NewCo later [SMB Law Group LLP — OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:17:14].
2. Lock price, payment split and seller note terms
   - Write one exact price with the cash-at-close, seller note, earnout and holdback components spelled out.
   - Model the seller note through standby, interest-only and balloon periods [SMB Law Group LLP — OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:41:01], call your bank before committing to note terms, and foreshadow that the note will be subordinated to the lender [SMB Law Group LLP — OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:42:14].
3. Define working capital and targeted escrows
   - State the working capital definition, the target or the method for setting it, and the post-close true-up.
   - Rosendahl treats the working capital target as one of the most important LOI terms [PCE Investment Bankers — n_gxsr8QgBM @ 00:03:40]; size escrow to specific risks such as $1M for ERC and $500K for sales tax rather than a flat 5 to 10% [Dennis Unrein / SMB PE LLC — PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:31:43].
4. Fix exclusivity, binding clauses and expiration, then sign
   - Mark exclusivity and confidentiality as binding, set the window with extensions, add a short expiry and execute.
   - Commonly 60 or 90 days of exclusivity [Brett Cenkus — aNioqOmfOe4 @ 00:02:47]; add a 72-hour expiration and always sign the LOI rather than leaving it as an unsigned draft [Dennis Unrein / SMB PE LLC — PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:56:57].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Exclusivity length and the projected closing date produce the sharpest split. Inside SMB Law Group, Sam Rosati says SBA deals take about 90 days, so a buyer who writes 60 should expect to request an extension and needs extension cover written into the exclusivity clause, while Eric Pacifici answers '60 days every time,' preferring to lose a deal later in a very rare situation than lose every deal up front with too long a projected closing date [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:20:12]. Ellie on the SMB PE panel sides with the short clock: most deals close in 60 to 90 days, asking for 120 is a 'bad bad move,' and only 30 to 45 days are needed to reach a purchase agreement, because deals almost never die from running out of exclusivity; the panel's resolution is 60 days plus automatic extensions [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:08:28]. Keira Hamilton runs different math: SBA loans take about 60 days, so ask for about 90 days of exclusivity, not 30 and not 180 [JulvxMkY4VY @ 00:05:21]. Escamilla Law Office rejects a fixed number entirely, tying duration to financing and diligence readiness through defined deadlines and earned extensions, and frames exclusivity as a meaningful commercial concession the seller is giving up in exchange for the buyer's proof of readiness [juS-FdGX2o0 @ 00:09:21].

Working capital splits the bankers from the lawyers. Mike Rosendahl of PCE Investment Bankers sets the target inside the LOI and calls it one of the most important terms to negotiate: on a $5M target, delivering $6M pays the seller $1M more and delivering $4M cuts $1M from the price, with the payment trued up or down after the close [n_gxsr8QgBM @ 00:03:40]. Ellie on the SMB PE panel defers the number to the QoE, using a $1.5M example, and flags that sellers want to sweep receivables, which a working-capital line of credit can offset [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:28:28]. Lockaby PLLC calls working capital the biggest deal-killer and insists that the earlier the parties talk through how it works inside the structure of the deal, the better [NCbzskaBTdQ @ 00:05:34]. Structure is just as contested. KreischerMiller frames the baseline fight bluntly: buyers want assets, sellers want stock [Q6exObbgCgk @ 00:00:30]. Ellie prefers an asset deal unless contracts or licenses cannot be assigned, warns that sales tax and environmental liabilities still carry over, then says a buyer can move from asset to equity but not back, and in the same breath advises to always start out with an equity purchase. Buyers have to pick which opening anchor to throw.

Seller financing draws opposite reads. The Letters of Intent episode treats it as a red flag that the buyer cannot get a loan and expects the seller to demand a premium for carrying paper, accepting it mainly for related parties or law-firm pension sales [VuuLz-8NiYg @ 00:11:56]. acquiredotcom pitches the reverse to sellers: offering seller financing can increase the buyer pool and the chances of closing, demoed as $700K upfront plus $500K over 12 months at zero interest to de-risk the acquisition [MYKKpeF0xMM @ 00:01:27]. Eric Pacifici of SMB Law Group says you never actually have to have a seller note, yet still values one as proof of seller belief, added liquidity, and an indemnity offset, and warns it will have to be subordinated to the lender [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:40:04]. Document length is the second open fight. Transact Capital wants more detail in the LOI rather than deferring it, expecting about 10 pages, because the LOI phase is the seller's point of maximum leverage, accepting it bars talking to other buyers, and signing releases control to the buyer, who is now 100% committed but not 100% bound [Axofinu0FSU @ 00:00:56]. That leverage logic pushes sellers to win every economic point before the ink dries, not after.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failure is skipping the LOI. The Letters of Intent episode opens with a buyer who spent 3 weeks negotiating price verbally and paid for an $8,000 environmental report, only to watch the seller take a higher offer because there was no LOI and no exclusivity; an LOI would have cost them an afternoon [VuuLz-8NiYg @ 00:00:14]. The same episode walks through a $3M business where nobody discussed the diligence period, financing contingency, or asset versus stock sale, while the seller turned down other bids assuming an exclusivity that never existed. Oberman Law Firm, handling about 100-plus transactions a year, says skipping the LOI misaligns expectations and can burn thousands of dollars in legal fees [6uqSoqOVnsg @ 00:04:52]. SMB Law Group explains why the sequence matters: the LOI locks exclusivity before QoE spend, purchase agreements run 40 to 70 pages, and brokers field about 600 inbound inquiries, so an unlocked buyer is one of hundreds [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:04:29]. Eric Pacifici also warns never to sign an APA with a deposit and inspection period without help. Even with a signed letter, Letters of Intent cautions that a refundable $100K deposit can tie the seller up with a non-buyer, which is why they require proof of funds.

The number-one deal killer after signing is a quality-of-earnings miss. SMB Law Group cites $2M of EBITDA becoming $1.6M post-QoE, and pairs it with structure traps: don't list the SBA debt amount, and model the seller note across standby, interest-only, and balloon periods, because a 50% note with a 12-month balloon is a trap [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:33:34]. Escamilla Law Office's Texas $5M hypothetical stacks every failure at once: a 90-day exclusivity period, a $300K working-capital dispute, a seller-note mismatch, lender conflicts, a customer consent requirement, and broad indemnification [juS-FdGX2o0 @ 00:16:23]. Escamilla also warns that SBA lenders restrict seller notes through subordination and payment limits, so a note negotiated without the bank collapses. Two offers both priced at $5M carry very different risk: all cash versus $4M at close, a $500K seller note, and a $500K earnout over 2 years. Ellie on the SMB PE panel shows the forgivable-note downside in numbers: $3M of EBITDA at 5x is $15M, with $10M at close and $5M forgivable, and missing by $500K forgives $2.5M [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:25:13]. The same panel says a buyer cannot pay the full purchase price at closing and must hold back something.

Post-signing people risk is where economics quietly leak. On the SMB PE panel, a plumbing seller and their son started competing after the sale, and a sale-based non-compete needs roughly $100K to $200K of consideration to stick; Dennis adds a $100,000 tenant-improvement bill from a landlord, with landlords demanding deposits and personal guarantees [PF2SOneLrJ0 @ 00:42:16]. Transition terms fail on small numbers: an $80M seller quit over $150 an hour versus $300 an hour, and the panel uses sweeteners like $200K or $400 an hour to get through the seller death spiral. SMB Law Group recounts a seller-owned Amazon account with 4,500 five-star reviews and a 4.7 rating, worth about $2M, that the buyer only won in Rhode Island Chancery because the LOI bought all assets owned or used by the business [OIcTvUbSEgM @ 00:23:54]. Bootstrapping to Billions warns that good deals don't get done with bad people and that paralysis by analysis kills momentum [BDlt56r8cRk @ 00:15:59]. KreischerMiller flags the board-level trap: boards with passive investors may insist on a fiduciary out to accept a higher unsolicited offer and avoid litigation, which punches a hole in the buyer's exclusivity [Q6exObbgCgk @ 00:03:14].
