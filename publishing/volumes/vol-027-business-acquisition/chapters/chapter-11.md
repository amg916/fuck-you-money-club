## 1. Assign seven plays to seven employees without hiding owner labor  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with seats, decision rights, and recurring deliverables rather than a miniature private-equity organization chart. Candidate #13 supports seven full-time employees and three contractors in both Conservative and Base cases; the working owner occupies one of those seven seats. Use this chapter's seven-play mapping consistently: acquisition screening, quality of earnings, capital structuring, 100-day integration, customer retention, reporting automation, and exit readiness. The owner leads screening and capital structuring; the controller leads quality of earnings and reporting; the operations lead runs integration; the customer lead owns retention; and the owner coordinates exit readiness through records maintained by every seat. Sales and delivery employees execute these plays without becoming additional department heads. Give each critical process one accountable employee, one trained backup, and one measurable output. A responsibility shared by everybody usually becomes an unresolved seller dependency. During diligence, compare this roster with actual payroll, overtime, seasonality, seller hours, and outsourced work. A business requiring eleven permanent employees does not fit this pod simply because its historical payroll labels several workers as vendors. Price the actual staffing requirement before signing, and reject economics that depend on the seller continuing unpaid.

The first full-time seat is the owner-general manager, responsible for cash allocation, lender communication, pricing exceptions, and succession. The second is an operations lead who controls scheduling, capacity, safety procedures, and acceptance standards. The third is a controller who owns accrual close, payroll review, covenant calculations, and the evidence room. The fourth is a customer-success lead responsible for renewals, complaints, and account-level margin. The fifth is a sales and estimating lead who quotes within approved price bands and records acquisition source. Seats six and seven are delivery specialists with complementary technical skills and documented cross-training. Separate vendor creation, payment preparation, and payment release: the bookkeeper prepares, the controller reviews, and the owner releases through bank controls. Nobody should create a supplier and independently approve that supplier's payment. Maintain named backups for bank access and customer escalation without sharing credentials. At Scaled, add an eighth employee for delivery capacity and a fourth contractor for defined overflow work. Promote an existing employee only when their prior workload has a funded replacement; relabeling a technician as manager does not create free capacity. Every recurring hour must land in a seat, contractor scope, or explicit owner workload.

Run the 100-day transition as a transfer of operating control, not an indefinite seller apprenticeship. During days 1–30, capture credential ownership, customer commitments, quoting logic, supplier terms, and seller-only decisions. During days 31–60, require employees to perform those decisions using written tolerances while the seller observes. During days 61–100, test an owner-absence week and close every exception through a named backup. Record seller transition assistance separately from recurring operating capacity, with a finite scope and cost. The canonical Conservative case produces only $60,000 annual owner profit on $750,000 sales, so seven employees are affordable only with a compatible wage structure, delivery model, and nonlabor cost base. The Base case is $1,875,000 sales and $243,750 profit; Scaled is $3,000,000 sales and $540,000 profit. Those figures imply total modeled costs of $690,000, $1,631,250, and $2,460,000 respectively, including interest within the stated profit basis. Test compensation against those totals rather than inserting an additional management layer. Measure productive capacity using completed, accepted jobs per paid hour. Expansion requires sustained contribution after the proposed hire, not merely a fuller sales pipeline. Preserve the exact scenario profits when presenting the scorecard; show any revised staffing economics as a separate acquisition underwriting case.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Buy the operating system, not the seller's permanent availability**
> 
> Transferability appears in routine evidence before it appears in a buyer's valuation model. Make these four practices part of the acquisition plan.
> - Reconcile seller calendars against payroll and job logs; price recurring seller labor before accepting discretionary earnings adjustments.
> - Ask the second delivery specialist to execute the seller's hardest recurring task while the seller observes without intervening.
> - Have the company own the accounting tenant, domain, repository, and bank administration; give contractors revocable named access.
> - Run a five-business-day owner-absence test before adding management headcount, then cost every unresolved exception.

## 2. Pay contractors for accepted outputs and review seven weekly signals  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Contractor economics should purchase defined outputs, not conceal supervised employee hours. An illustrative Base allocation is $1,500 monthly for bookkeeping, $2,000 monthly for a bounded automation backlog, and $1,000 monthly for independent quality review: $54,000 annually in aggregate. These are proposed operating budgets inside the canonical cost envelope, not additional deductions from its published profit. Replace them with written quotations after inspecting transaction volume, system complexity, and technical requirements. Bookkeeping acceptance requires reconciled accounts and linked support by business day seven; automation acceptance requires passing tests, company repository delivery, and a demonstrated rollback; quality review acceptance requires documented sampling, severity classification, and corrective action evidence. Specify a two-business-day routine response target, while pricing urgent coverage separately rather than assuming constant availability. Pay monthly service fees against accepted recurring scope and milestone fees against accepted project deliverables. Include a defined cure period and objective rework responsibility, with payment timing consistent with applicable law. Worker status follows the actual relationship, including control and applicable federal and state tests, not the invoice label. Review 26 U.S.C. section 3121(d), wage obligations under 29 U.S.C. section 207, and relevant state classification rules before treating a recurring seat as independent.

Hold one weekly operating meeting using a locked scorecard extracted at the same cutoff each Monday. The controller owns definitions and reconciles financial measures; operating owners explain exceptions with source records, not presentation slides. Review seven signals: accepted revenue against plan, gross contribution by service cohort, delivery reliability, repeat-work defects, retention, concentration, and 13-week liquidity. Report numerator, denominator, comparison period, owner, and corrective action for each. Calculate delivery reliability as jobs accepted by the promised date divided by jobs due; do not remove late jobs by moving their dates after failure. Measure defects against completed work from the same cohort, including credits and unpaid rework. Retention should distinguish recurring-contract renewal from repeat purchasing in noncontract businesses. Review gross contribution after direct labor, materials, subcontractors, and attributable credits, while keeping interest outside that operating measure. A 98% on-time target and a 2% rework threshold are initial operating standards, not historical claims. Recalibrate only through a documented change that preserves prior-period comparability. Every red metric ends with one accountable person, a dated intervention, and evidence required for closure. A meeting that produces explanations but no changed operating action is overhead, not management.

Set a hard target of no more than 25% exposure to any customer group, measured separately on trailing-twelve-month recognized sales and open trade receivables. Aggregate related entities under common economic control; splitting invoices across subsidiaries does not diversify demand. Apply the same 25% ceiling to operating counterparties within each critical purchased-capacity category, and track customer referral intermediaries separately when they control demand access. Four equal suppliers leave no room for disruption, so five credible sources or genuinely substitutable capacity provide a more resilient design. Small businesses often inherit breaches: disclose them, stop increasing the dependency, and assign a dated remediation plan rather than pretending a temporary waiver meets the target. A buyer should see both current exposure and progress toward the ceiling. Track acquisition lenders and bank deposits in separate funding-risk schedules; these are not quietly included in an operating-supplier diversification claim. Stress-test the loss of the largest customer and interruption of the largest critical supplier in the cash forecast. Require verified alternative capacity, not a list of uncontacted vendors. Connect each resulting action to customer retention, capital structuring, or integration so the weekly scorecard remains tied to the seven mapped plays rather than becoming an unrelated reporting exercise.

**Seven Weekly Signals With Auditable Definitions and Specific Interventions**

| Weekly signal and accountable employee owner | Calculation and source evidence required weekly | Operating target or explicit exception trigger | Required intervention when the measure deteriorates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accepted revenue versus plan: sales lead owns | Recognized sales from completed obligations against approved weekly plan | Investigate any shortfall exceeding ten percent of weekly plan | Separate demand loss from delivery backlog before revising commitments |
| Cohort gross contribution: controller owns financial reconciliation | Cohort revenue less attributable labor, materials, subcontractors, and credits | Investigate margin falling three percentage points below quoted economics | Audit estimating assumptions and reprice future uncommitted customer work |
| On-time accepted delivery: operations lead owns execution | Jobs accepted by original promise divided by jobs due | Initial target requires at least ninety-eight percent timely acceptance | Resequence constrained work and contact affected customers before deadlines |
| Repeat-work defect rate: operations lead owns correction | Jobs requiring attributable rework divided by completed cohort jobs | Initial threshold permits no more than two percent rework | Isolate failure cause and test corrective work before release |
| Renewal and repeat purchasing: customer lead owns | Renewed eligible contracts or repeat buyers within defined intervals | Investigate every lost major account and deteriorating cohort retention | Interview departing customers and assign recoverable service failures immediately |
| Customer and operating-counterparty concentration: owner owns exposure | Related-group sales, receivables, and critical category spend measured separately | Each defined operating exposure must remain at or below 25% | Freeze dependency growth and execute dated account or supplier diversification |

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the reporting population
   - Export accounting, CRM, job, and receivables records at Monday's agreed cutoff.
   - Store dated exports with cohort definitions and source-system totals; preserve the original extract when subsequent corrections occur.
2. Reconcile and test exceptions
   - Have the controller reconcile revenue and cash while operations samples delivery and rework evidence.
   - Tag timing differences separately from errors, retain adjustment support, and route unsupported contractor deliverables for cure.
3. Assign intervention ownership
   - Review seven signals in a thirty-minute meeting and assign one owner to each material exception.
   - Record the intervention, deadline, financial exposure, and completion evidence without expanding the standing meeting roster.
4. Verify closure and update liquidity
   - Check completed interventions against source evidence and refresh the thirteen-week cash forecast.
   - Carry unresolved items forward visibly; escalate customer losses, capacity failures, and covenant issues to the owner immediately.

## 3. Build accrual cohorts, transferable systems, and a defensible evidence room  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Transferable books begin with an accrual ledger that distinguishes earned revenue, invoicing, and collections. Use QuickBooks Online or Xero with controlled account mapping, customer identifiers, service lines, and acquisition cohorts; the specific software matters less than consistent posting rules and exportability. Post customer deposits to liabilities until the relevant performance is delivered, and distinguish unbilled earned revenue from work that has not yet satisfied the recognition policy. Accrue received services, payroll, subcontractor work, and other incurred costs in the proper period. Reconcile bank accounts, receivables, payables, inventory where applicable, deferred revenue, fixed assets, and debt monthly. Business day seven is the initial close deadline, followed by controller review and period locking. Maintain a separate tax reconciliation rather than assuming management accrual treatment determines tax timing. Section 6001 of Title 26 requires supporting records, while section 461 addresses deduction timing subject to its applicable rules. Preserve invoice support and correction history so a QoE reviewer can reconstruct a period without interviewing the seller. Historical cash-basis accounts need a documented conversion bridge; changing software does not repair missing accruals. Treat unexplained opening balances as diligence exceptions with assigned resolution dates, not as permanent suspense accounts.

Use cohort accounting to expose acquisition damage that aggregate sales can conceal. Separate customers present at closing from customers won afterward, then segment by service line, contract type, and originating channel. Track recognized revenue, direct contribution, credits, renewal outcomes, and receivable aging within each cohort. A business can grow total sales while losing its acquired customer base and replacing it with lower-margin work. Reconcile cohort totals to the general ledger every month, with documented treatment for customer mergers and identifier changes. Build a monthly earnings bridge from reported operating results to proposed normalized earnings. Support every adjustment with invoices, payroll records, contracts, or a specific event. Recurring software, ordinary recruiting, routine repairs, and contractor costs are operating expenses even when the seller calls them discretionary. Replace owner labor at a defensible market cost before marketing manager-run earnings. Distinguish a genuinely completed integration project from continuing operational maintenance. Keep acquisition transaction costs and financing charges separately identifiable, with their accounting treatment reviewed rather than mechanically expensed or capitalized. This discipline supports quality of earnings, customer retention, and exit readiness simultaneously. It also gives the owner an actionable hold-case view: which customer cohorts actually fund debt service, replacement investment, and distributions after collection delays.

Control of operating assets must survive both contractor departure and an ownership change. Put automation scripts, templates, documentation, and proprietary process materials in company-controlled repositories with role-based access and tested exports. For copyright transfers, 17 U.S.C. section 204(a) generally requires a written instrument signed by the rights owner or an authorized agent. Do not assume paying an invoice transfers copyright, or that every commissioned work qualifies as work made for hire. Obtain assignments covering identified deliverables, and distinguish contractor pre-existing tools from newly created work. Where background technology remains contractor-owned, document a license sufficient for continued operation, modification, and the contemplated transfer. Maintain third-party license terms and dependency inventories; ownership of custom code does not eliminate restrictions in embedded components. Store signed customer and supplier contracts, assignment provisions, change-of-control clauses, permits, employment records, and lender documents in an indexed evidence room. Review SBA loan terms and applicable 13 CFR requirements before assuming an ownership transfer or asset disposition is permitted. Test that another authorized employee can restore the workflow and produce the monthly reports without the original contractor. A buyer discounts undocumented dependency even when the underlying code works, because operational continuity depends on enforceable rights and reproducible execution.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Verify four conditions before presenting the company as transferable**
> 
> Treat these checks as pre-flight evidence requirements for a sale process or an owner-light holding strategy. Record failures explicitly.
> - Reconcile twelve monthly accrual closes to bank activity, tax bridges, cohort totals, and supported balance-sheet schedules.
> - Confirm executed copyright assignments or sufficient documented licenses for every material contractor-created operating asset.
> - Calculate related-group customer and critical operating-counterparty exposures against the 25% ceilings, disclosing every breach and remediation date.
> - Complete an owner-absence test, price remaining replacement labor, and verify customer, license, and lender transfer requirements.

## 4. Bridge canonical profit into buyer earnings and realizable owner cash  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Keep the scorecard and valuation model separate but reconciled. Conservative recognized sales of $750,000 multiplied by 8% produce $60,000 annual owner profit; Base sales of $1,875,000 multiplied by 13.0% produce $243,750; Scaled sales of $3,000,000 multiplied by 18% produce $540,000. These are acquired-business sales, not customer GMV, transaction purchase price, loan proceeds, or an advisory fee pool. The margin basis is after interest and before principal repayments. Therefore none of these profit figures is automatically EBITDA or distributable cash. Start a buyer bridge with the actual definition of owner profit, then identify expensed interest, depreciation, amortization, applicable entity income taxes, owner compensation, and supported normalizations. If an expense was never deducted, adding it back manufactures earnings. Illustratively, assume Base profit includes $75,000 interest and $31,250 depreciation and amortization, includes market-rate compensation for all required owner duties, and includes no entity income-tax expense. Adding those two expenses produces $350,000 EBITDA before further adjustments. These are explicit bridge assumptions, not supplied scorecard facts. Validate them against the ledger before using the result. A buyer's replacement manager expense reduces normalized EBITDA whenever the assumed owner compensation does not actually cover the required duties.

Use multiple ranges as negotiation sensitivities, not as automatic rewards for clean folders. For a smaller owner-operated acquisition, an illustrative 2–3 times normalized seller discretionary earnings range may be a useful starting screen; SDE requires its own owner-benefit reconciliation and cannot be substituted for EBITDA. For a genuinely transferable small company, test 3–5 times normalized EBITDA, recognizing that size, industry, capital intensity, growth quality, concentration, and financing conditions can move outcomes outside that range. Obtain current comparable transactions and buyer indications before setting a reserve price. Applying 3, 4, and 5 times to the illustrative $350,000 EBITDA gives enterprise values of $1,050,000, $1,400,000, and $1,750,000. Those are not owner proceeds. At the middle sensitivity, subtract assumed bank debt of $600,000, seller-note principal of $150,000, and transaction costs of $70,000, then add $50,000 of transferable excess cash: estimated equity proceeds are $630,000 before taxes and any other adjustments. Define cash, debt-like items, and the working-capital peg in the transaction documents. Customer deposits, accrued bonuses, leases, and overdue payables require consistent treatment without double counting. Earnouts and rollover equity are contingent or illiquid consideration, not cash received at closing.

The durable cash-cow decision deserves the same rigor as the exit decision. Starting from Base owner profit of $243,750, an illustrative $90,000 annual principal schedule, $30,000 maintenance capital expenditure, and $20,000 working-capital increase leave $103,750 before owner-level taxes and other cash adjustments. Interest is already reflected in the scorecard profit, so subtracting it again understates cash. Conversely, ignoring principal exaggerates distributions. Calculate debt-service coverage using the precise lender definition and required measurement period; EBITDA divided by principal alone is not a complete coverage calculation. Compare holding cash flows with after-tax sale proceeds over an explicit horizon, including reinvestment, refinancing, owner time, and downside cases. Premium valuation becomes more plausible when the pod produces stable cohorts, independently reproducible reporting, low rework, bounded concentration, and an owner who is replaceable at a funded cost. None of those attributes changes the canonical scenario arithmetic by itself. Use the seven mapped plays as the operating loop: screening selects a staffable business; QoE verifies earnings; structuring protects liquidity; integration transfers decisions; retention preserves cohorts; automation reduces reporting friction; and exit readiness preserves strategic choice. Sell when actual buyer terms exceed a disciplined hold value, or retain the company when verified cash generation compensates for its continuing capital and labor demands.
