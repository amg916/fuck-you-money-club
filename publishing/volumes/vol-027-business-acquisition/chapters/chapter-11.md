# Chapter 11: Asset vs. Stock Purchases, Tax Allocation, and Buyer Protections

*Asset vs. share purchases and buyer/seller preferences, inherited liabilities, IRS Form 8594 and Section 1060 allocation, what a seller note can't spread, fixed-asset accounting for asset acquisitions, QSBS and F-reorgs, the three primary deal structures, representations and warranties, and set-off rights against seller notes*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners line up on one rule: sellers want to sell stock for capital-gains treatment, buyers want to buy assets for liability protection and a stepped-up basis, and almost every main street and lower middle market deal closes as an asset sale. Stock deals hand the buyer the entity's full history, so they demand heavier diligence; asset deals leave old debts with the seller but force third-party consents, new bank accounts, rehired staff, and a matching Form 8594 allocation. Hard numbers: a $1M intangible amortizes over 15 years, a standalone C Corp seller eats roughly 21% twice, and SMB protection runs on 10-15% indemnity caps, escrows, holdbacks, and set-off against the seller note. Disagreement centers on whether share purchases are administratively easier, whether asset buyers inherit liabilities, and whether reps and warranties are worth anything without a note to offset.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The split is the first thing every practitioner says out loud. Brett Cenkus puts it flatly: sellers want to sell stock, buyers want to buy assets, because stock delivers capital gains that go long-term after one year and possibly zero tax under QSBS, while an asset buyer re-depreciates everything at market value [ASUM5illI98 @ 00:01:56]. Tax Hack Accounting Group goes further, saying the seller is always going to want to sell stock and that sellers volunteering for asset sales is very rare, since an asset sale computes gain asset by asset and drags depreciation recapture, cash-basis receivables, and inventory into higher ordinary rates [Wd1__Z4Nz6k @ 00:00:21]. Jeremy A. Johnson, CPA frames the buyer side as liability protection plus a step-up in basis, and the seller side as capital gains, warning that sellers must know their basis to calculate the gain [xVt6suFrCqE @ 00:00:49]. Despite seller preference, the market lands on assets: Walker Deibel states that almost all lower middle market and main street transactions are going to be an asset sale and not a stock sale, and Deibel's framing is that the assets generate the cash, not the legal entity, so the buyer is really paying for what produces the cash [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:02:06].

The second point of consensus is liability. A share purchaser acquires the company with its history, as Thomas Efraim LLP puts it, which is why in the firm's scenario the buyer switches to an asset purchase after price is agreed and forces the economics to be revisited [l2gJwZbVVnc @ 00:01:27]. Walker Deibel uses a toxic waste example for inherited history: the stock buyer takes everything the entity ever did, while in an asset sale the buyer forms a new entity, the seller's entity is left hollow, and employees are terminated and rehired in the following days [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:05:28]. Tax Hack Accounting Group says stock buyers inherit all historic liabilities, federal, state, and sales and use, while asset buyers generally escape income tax exposure but can still pick up sales and use, payroll, and gross receipts taxes [Wd1__Z4Nz6k @ 00:03:07]. Paper Rich adds the escape hatches that collapse the asset shield: you assumed the debts, a de facto merger, a mere continuation, or a fraudulent dodge, and in New York the buyer gives the tax department 10 days' notice or risks liability for the seller's sales tax [Qqgfjq8CujE @ 00:01:11]. The operator takeaway is consistent: asset structure narrows exposure, it does not erase it.

Every adviser who spoke to timing says the same thing: lock structure before price. Josh Siegel, a CPA and tax attorney, tells Dennis Unrein that tax structure belongs in the LOI, because a silent LOI lets the buyer assume an asset deal while the seller assumes a stock deal, and that conversation is not productive post LOI [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:01:23]. Thomas Efraim LLP's checklist runs the same order: settle structure early, involve tax advisors before price, identify third-party consents, do legal diligence, and document which liabilities are assumed [l2gJwZbVVnc @ 00:02:23]. The consensus exceptions, where stock wins even for the buyer, are just as consistent. Walker Deibel lists a non-transferable essential contract or license, a minority equity investment, and larger deals where the seller retains 20% and the buyer takes 80% [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:06:34]. Chris Barsness flags non-transferable licenses such as alcohol licenses, change-of-control clauses, contract assignability, leases, landlord consents, and minority-shareholder consents as the items that decide whether an asset deal can physically close, and Barsness notes that if you only want part of the assets a stock purchase usually won't work [-DthgkqGsGA @ 00:06:31]. Structure is a diligence output, not a preference.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The tax mechanics explain why buyers fight for assets. Tax Hack Accounting Group runs a $1M intangible-only deal: as a stock purchase the $1M is trapped in stock basis until exit, say five years later, but as an asset purchase it is amortizable over 15 years [Wd1__Z4Nz6k @ 00:02:32]. Josh Siegel confirms that buying $1M of C Corp stock gives $1M of basis with no depreciation, no amortization, and no step-up, that price is allocated across seven asset classes recovered over 1 to 15 years with goodwill at 15, and that a $1M step-up on 3-year trucks throws off one third per year [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:07:44]. The seller side has its own math. Walker Deibel shows a $100 price netting the seller $65 in an asset sale versus $75 in a stock sale, so a buyer may pay about $90 for stock [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:07:41]. Siegel says a standalone C Corp asset sale is quite hard to make work: the seller pays 21% corporate tax, then roughly another 21% on distribution plus state tax, demands a gross-up, and that gross-up is neutral only with full year-one depreciation [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:09:32]. The workaround Siegel names is synthetic asset treatment through 338(h)(10), 336(e), or an F reorg.

Once the structure is set, the allocation becomes the battlefield. Paper Rich notes that in an asset deal both sides report the price split to the IRS [Qqgfjq8CujE @ 00:00:53], and Teach Me! Personal Finance walks Form 8594 line by line: seller and buyer both file it for a business purchase, not for single pieces of equipment, Class 1 is actual cash, goodwill sits in Class 6 and 7, and side deals for a non-compete, license, or lease are reported at maximum consideration, such as $50K a year for 3 years equaling $150K [G0f0r8tYMnk @ 00:09:15]. Both returns must match, or the IRS is probably going to catch that. Hans Goldstein ties the allocation to the seller note: under IRC 1060(a) a written allocation binds both sides, inventory in Class IV is ordinary income that is completely ineligible for installment reporting, and a seller note only spreads the goodwill, so the allocation and the down payment have to be negotiated together [I8L23gqP5to @ 00:02:57]. Goldstein's 2026 example runs a 72.0% gross profit ratio, a year-one payment of $410,047, interest of $201,600, and gain of $956,482. Martin Godfrey Legido adds the HMRC angle: in an asset deal every value needs accountant work and vendor agreement, and HMRC watches undervalue transfers [04Uy5gfO_PU @ 00:07:37].

Protection mechanics are where the SMB Law Group LLP team gets specific, drawing on 399 deals closed as a firm. Escrow money moves only on joint instructions or a court order; a holdback on a $4M deal wires $3.5M and keeps $0.5M, which shifts the burden to the seller; and set-off against the seller note is the most common tool [iNOj__jXGpE @ 00:10:09]. Their market terms: survival periods of 12 to 24 months with 12 to 18 the norm, an indemnity cap of 10 to 15%, a deductible of 0.5 to 1%, and in SMB deals an escrow equal to the cap, with the seller note sized at 10 to 15% so it matches the cap [iNOj__jXGpE @ 00:26:54]. The tactic when a $100K claim lands is to pause note payments, such as $11,000 a month, and not spend the withheld money. David C Barnett reaches the same tool from the street: reps and warranties are words on paper, suing over $10K costs too much, so protection number two after the seller relationship is a note you can offset, like skipping 10 payments of $1,000 [LStVgWOM2LI @ 00:02:08]. Brett Cenkus adds that sellers give 15 to 30 reps against 4 to 10 from buyers, and that sellers are usually liable jointly and severally [cnvG-qb6w9Q @ 00:01:11].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Fix the structure in the LOI
   - Write asset or stock, plus any 338(h)(10), 336(e), or F reorg path, into the LOI before agreeing price.
   - A silent LOI lets the buyer assume assets while the seller assumes stock, and the conversation is not productive post LOI, so pre-LOI is ideal [Dennis Unrein / SMB PE LLC — hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:01:23]
2. Clear contracts, licenses, and consents
   - Inventory change-of-control clauses, assignability, leases, non-transferable licenses, landlord consents, and minority-shareholder consents.
   - Change-of-control and assignability terms, plus licenses such as alcohol licenses, decide whether an asset deal can close or stock becomes the only route [Chris Barsness - Securities Law Media — -DthgkqGsGA @ 00:05:13]
3. Stand up the buying entity
   - Form the new entity, file a DBA to keep the trade name, and plan the employee termination and rehire.
   - The buyer forms a new entity, the seller's entity is left hollow, and a DBA costs about $10, renewed roughly every 3 years [Walker Deibel — NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:09:29]
4. Allocate, file, and lock protections
   - Agree a written allocation for matching Form 8594 filings, size the seller note at 10-15% to match the indemnity cap, and set escrow or holdback terms.
   - Sizing the seller note at 10-15% so it matches the cap gives the buyer a set-off source equal to its maximum indemnity claim [SMB Law Group LLP — iNOj__jXGpE @ 00:34:44]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split is over which structure an operator should reach for first. Martin Godfrey Legido, drawing on more than 30 years of deals, prefers buying shares as the easier route: the customers, creditors, suppliers and the bank account all come across in one move, and Legido's bottom line is that the share purchase is easier from the administrative side [04Uy5gfO_PU @ 00:08:32]. Walker Deibel runs the opposite default for small buyers, stating that almost all lower middle market and Main Street transactions are going to be an asset sale and not a stock sale, with the buyer forming a new entity and leaving the seller's entity hollow [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:02:06]. Brett Cenkus frames the tension as a standing conflict of interest: sellers want stock for capital gains, long-term after one year and possibly no tax under QSBS, while buyers want assets so they can re-depreciate at market value [ASUM5illI98 @ 00:01:56]. Cenkus adds a street-level reason asset sales dominate listings: brokers tend to market asset deals because they lack securities licenses, which means the structure on the teaser can reflect the broker's licensing rather than the best economics for either side [ASUM5illI98 @ 00:04:18]. Read every listing with that bias in mind before accepting the default structure the broker hands you.

Even asset-first buyers flip to stock when the deal forces it. Walker Deibel lists the triggers: an essential contract or license that cannot transfer, a minority equity investment, or a larger deal where the seller retains 20% and the buyer takes 80% [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:06:34]. Deibel then shows the price lever: on a $100 price the seller nets $65 in an asset sale versus $75 in a stock sale, so the buyer may only need to pay about $90 for stock to leave the seller whole [NM-ZwBNBHQE @ 00:07:41]. Josh Siegel, a CPA and tax attorney appearing with Dennis Unrein, argues the entity type decides it: from a standalone C Corp, the seller pays 21% corporate tax and then roughly another 21% on the distribution plus state tax, so if it's a standalone C Corp it's quite hard to make the economics work, while S corps and LLCs in the lower middle market generally do asset deals [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:12:19]. Siegel's alternative playbook is synthetic asset treatment through 338(h)(10), 336(e) or an F reorg, with the hard rule that 338(h)(10) and 336(e) both require buying at least 80% of the stock within one year, so a 70% or 79% purchase fails outright [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:19:33].

The deepest philosophical split is over whether the purchase agreement itself protects you. David C Barnett calls representations and warranties words on paper, pointing out that suing over a $10K problem costs too much, and ranks protection number one as a good relationship with the seller and number two as a seller note that can be offset, for example by skipping 10 payments of $1,000 [LStVgWOM2LI @ 00:02:08]. Brett Cenkus treats reps as the engineering of the deal: sellers give 15 to 30 reps, buyers give 4 to 10, reps are the basis for indemnity claims, and the real question they answer is who carries the risk rather than which facts are true [cnvG-qb6w9Q @ 00:05:51]. SMB Law Group LLP, closing roughly 130-plus deals last year and trending toward 150, layers hard cash mechanisms on top: an escrow that releases money only on joint instructions or a court order, a holdback such as wiring $3.5M of a $4M deal and holding $0.5M to shift the burden to the seller, and rep and warranty insurance at roughly a $140K premium on a $4M deal [iNOj__jXGpE @ 00:10:09]. The operator choice comes down to relationship plus note offset, or signed paper backed by parked cash.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is winning a claim you cannot collect. SMB Law Group LLP warns that if the individual shareholders are not parties to the indemnity, the seller's company empties its cash after closing and the claim is worthless; the first avenue of recovery is making sure the person with the money is someone you actually have a claim back against [iNOj__jXGpE @ 00:19:51]. The same firm flags that set-off against the seller note, the most common recovery tool, does not solve today's cash shortage, and that using it for a working-capital adjustment is a mistake to avoid [iNOj__jXGpE @ 00:11:52]. David C Barnett's pizzeria buyer lived the set-off play: the seller note was 40% of the deal, and the buyer stopped paying when cash sales came in short of what the seller had claimed [LStVgWOM2LI @ 00:06:26]. Barnett also reports the harder loss: hidden damage to an architect relationship that had no way to be valued as a claim, so neither a rep nor a note offset could recover it [LStVgWOM2LI @ 00:07:18]. The economics check is identical in every case: a claim only has real value when money is already parked in escrow or a holdback, or is still owed to the seller on a note you control.

Tax surprises land late and wreck the agreed price. Josh Siegel warns that a silent LOI lets the buyer assume an asset deal while the seller assumes a stock deal, and recalls a roughly $60M deal where the seller questioned the after-tax cash late in the process; the rule is that it's not a productive conversation to have post LOI, so pre-LOI is ideal [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:28:08]. Thomas Efraim LLP sketches the same collapse: the buyer later proposes an asset purchase to avoid historical corporate liabilities after the price is agreed, so the economics must be revisited from scratch [l2gJwZbVVnc @ 00:00:50]. On the seller side, Tax Hack Accounting Group notes an asset sale computes gain asset by asset, with depreciation recapture, cash-basis accounts receivable and inventory taxed at higher ordinary rates [Wd1__Z4Nz6k @ 00:01:01]. Hans Goldstein adds the installment trap: inventory sits in Class IV as ordinary income and is completely ineligible for installment reporting, so a seller note only spreads the goodwill, and allocation and down payment must be negotiated together; the 2026 example runs a 72.0% gross profit ratio, a year-one payment of $410,047, $201,600 of interest and a $956,482 gain [I8L23gqP5to @ 00:02:19]. Sellers who assume the note defers everything get blindsided at filing time.

The back-office traps hit after the wire clears. Teach Me! Personal Finance warns that buyer and seller Form 8594 filings must match, or the IRS is probably going to catch that, and that side deals for a non-compete, license or lease must be reported at maximum consideration, such as $50K per year over 3 years for $150K [G0f0r8tYMnk @ 00:05:35]. Siegel's diligence warning is blunter: the biggest surprises come from not doing tax diligence, because after Wayfair roughly $100,000 of sales into a state creates economic nexus, and with no return filed the statute of limitations never starts, while a VDA generally looks back 3 years and litigating a $100K issue can cost $200K to $400K or more [hDmKsEVGoBI @ 00:38:06]. Paper Rich adds a state-level landmine: in New York, skip the 10 days' notice to the tax department and the buyer risks liability for the seller's sales tax [Qqgfjq8CujE @ 00:01:30]. On the books, PwC US partner Beth Paul stresses that the screen test is not optional, that a bargain purchase gain is not recognized in an asset acquisition, and that the value of the long-lived assets acquired is reduced instead, while transaction costs are capitalized rather than expensed [b43cWRS8RKE @ 00:17:35].
