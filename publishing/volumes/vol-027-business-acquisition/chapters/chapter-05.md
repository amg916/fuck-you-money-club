*7(a) mechanics and pros and cons, equity injection minimums (a $1M business with about $57K down), the October 1 rule changes and technical updates, lender underwriting and the acquisition application, QoE expectations, what sellers must prepare, buying the business plus the building, and SBDC and lender training on change-of-ownership loans*

## Executive Summary

Lenders, brokers, CPAs and SBA policy staff converge on one message: SOP 50 10 8.1 makes historical cash flow the ceiling on acquisition debt. The DSCR floor moves from 1.15x to 1.25x on historical financials. The 10% injection on initial acquisitions cannot be reduced, and full-standby seller debt can cover at most half of it, which kills the old zero-cash structure. A lender-commissioned or vendor-reviewed QoE is mandatory at a $3M business purchase price, with quotes running $5,000 to $30,000. In one worked example, a $1M deal closes with roughly $57,500 of buyer cash. In another, a target earning $185K fails a 1.25x test that requires $196.2K. Practitioners split on three points: whether a buyer QoE can be reused, which deal costs count as equity, and how the $3M trigger is measured.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner working the equity side repeats the same rule: the 10% injection on an initial acquisition is now a hard floor. The Money Rules states flatly that the 10% cannot be reduced, that 25% is not an SBA rule, and that seller and limited sources are capped at half of it [DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:02:04]. Pioneer Capital Advisory runs the same math on a $4M project: a $400K injection, of which $200K must come from the buyer [WF2-6ODNQms @ 00:36:34]. Exit Ready Valuation adds the trap most first-time buyers miss. The 10% applies to the whole project, not just the price, so a $1M business plus $150K of other uses becomes a $1.15M project and $115K down [Tt3Ae9PDn8A @ 00:00:33]. Michael S Glaspie names the casualty. The old play was 90% SBA plus a 10% seller note on full subordination, with no cash out of pocket, and it is dead. Seller standby plus non-controlling investors holding 20% or less is now limited to 50% of the injection, or '5% of the entire purchase' [WfgrSa37vaA @ 00:02:50]. All four point to the same operator move. Budget at least 5% of total project cost in your own cash, treat any standby seller note as covering no more than the other 5%, and build the stack on total project cost rather than the headline price.

Lenders and advisors agree that cash flow sets the ceiling. Ed Weeks Jr. reports that DSCR on a first-time takeover rises to 1.25x from 1.15x. It is measured on the last fiscal year or a two-year average, and lenders can no longer rely on projections [aJcoLYwi6vo @ 00:00:35]. Matthias Smith explains why: 1.15 left little margin for error and led to defaults, so the new floor is 1.25 on historical numbers only [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:04:43]. Jack Pastrana of Credit Bench, with about 21 years in SBA lending, frames the underwriting logic. Banks are cash flow lenders, and when cash flow will not support the debt, the business is usually overpriced [NmgNpE_W_po @ 00:34:41]. Josh KS at Lion Business Advisors shows how add-backs collapse the math. A seller claims $700K of earnings, diligence supports $550K, and debt service is $500K. The deal lands at 1.10x and fails the 1.25x test [orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:15:11]. The lesson is that the earnings figure on a listing is not the figure the bank underwrites. Only documented historical cash flow survives the credit memo, and every unsupported add-back cuts directly into the debt the buyer can carry. The 1.25x floor is now the first filter on price, not a box checked after the LOI is signed.

Structure guardrails round out the consensus. Michael S Glaspie describes a partial buyout where the seller retains equity: the buyer can own only 50% or less. Taking control requires the seller's full exit, and seller consulting is capped at 24 months [WfgrSa37vaA @ 00:04:30]. The Money Rules confirms consulting of up to two years and adds that earnouts are banned outright [DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:06:43]. Ray Drew of SBA Ray, who lends on $1-5M acquisitions, puts it more bluntly: 'the sba just straight up doesn't allow this you need a fixed purchase price' [9Sl18GknuZE @ 00:01:42]. SBA policy staff Dan Pashet and Emily Dachon, presenting through Coleman Report, add the valuation layer. There are four change-of-ownership types, every one needs an independent valuation, and only 7(a) Small and Express are carved out [miA6NuzVV6Y @ 00:48:20]. Taken together, the operator rule set is simple. Negotiate a fixed price with no contingent kicker. Budget for an independent valuation on every change-of-ownership deal outside Small and Express. Limit the seller's post-closing role to a consulting agreement of 24 months or less. Build the full seller exit into the LOI from day one, so the structure survives underwriting instead of collapsing in the credit memo.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Practitioners run the numbers from total project cost, not sticker price. Exit Ready Valuation turns a $1M business with $150K of additional uses into a $1.15M project and a $115K injection. The injection splits into $57,500 of seller full-standby note and $57,500 of buyer cash, and the SBA loan lands around $1.035M [Tt3Ae9PDn8A @ 00:00:54]. Their payment line is the reality check: 'At an example rate of 10%, that's about $13,700 a month, about $164,000 a year' [Tt3Ae9PDn8A @ 00:01:32]. The Money Rules runs the same drill for a buyer named Jordan. The $1.1M project carries $110K of equity, built from $55K of seller standby and $55K of cash. The $990K loan at prime plus 3%, or 10%, costs roughly $157K a year in payments. Earnings of $185K fail the 1.25x test because $196.2K is required [DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:04:32]. Matthias Smith scales it up: a $2M project means $200K down, with $100K from the buyer's own pocket. The buyer's half can be cash, a grant, or a personal loan repaid from outside the business [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:01:12]. Two formulas fall out of these examples. Minimum buyer cash is 10% of project cost, halved. The earnings the target must already show are annual debt service times 1.25.

Deal timelines now live or die on the QoE, and the trigger is narrower than buyers assume. Josh KS at Lion Business Advisors measures it on business purchase price. That is the PSA price minus the appraised value of owner-occupied real estate, tested before equity or seller debt. A $4.2M deal with $1M of real estate is a $3.2M business price, so a QoE is required even on a $2.7M loan. Exactly $3M counts, but a $2.8M price inside a larger project does not [orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:07:05]. Pioneer Capital Advisory spells out the required scope: proof of cash, tax reconciliation, documented adjustments and a sustainability analysis. The lender is the client [WF2-6ODNQms @ 00:29:24]. Josh adds that proof of cash covers TTM plus two fiscal years. Under PLP, the engagement must be in place when the loan number is issued, even if the report finishes later. Chris Barrett, CPA, walks through the September technical update. Buyers may now commission the QoE, though sellers still cannot. The report qualifies through either a provider reliance letter or a review by a lender-vetted provider [Ru9e1wYKGHc @ 00:00:43]. Barrett's concern is the first path: reliance letters raise provider liability, cost and scope, which feeds straight into the price of the engagement [Ru9e1wYKGHc @ 00:01:30].

Injection sourcing is the next checkpoint. Ray Drew calls verification one of the top two or three sticking points. Funds must be seasoned for two full bank statement cycles, and gifts and account transfers draw extra scrutiny. A borrowed injection, such as a HELOC, needs an outside repayment source like a spouse's W-2 income [9Sl18GknuZE @ 00:04:31]. Pioneer Capital Advisory confirms that personal loans or HELOCs work if repaid from outside the business's cash flow. Investor money inside the 10% can take tax distributions only, while funds above 10% can take profit distributions [WF2-6ODNQms @ 00:42:35]. Matthias Smith then layers on two changes to the math. Real estate amortization now blends the real estate share at 25 years and the remainder at 10. That replaces the old rule, under which 51% real estate earned a full 25-year term [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:07:44]. Total SBA debt plus any amortizing seller note is also capped at valuation, so a $3.5M price on a $3M valuation forces $500K of extra equity [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:12:10]. For expansion acquisitions, a four-digit NAICS match unlocks a deal with no down payment, but working capital cannot be financed alongside it. Smith's fix for a $300K working capital need is to negotiate it into the purchase price, rather than give up the zero-down structure and inject 10%.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Size the Deal to Your Own Cash
   - Compute 10% of total project cost and fund at least half of it personally.
   - Apply the 10% to the full project, including the $150K of non-price uses in Exit Ready's $1.15M example [Exit Ready Valuation — Tt3Ae9PDn8A @ 00:00:33]. Size the deal to your own capital, because investors inside the injection must be long-term holders [Matthias Smith — 3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:02:07].
2. Stress-Test Historical DSCR
   - Divide documented historical earnings by annual debt service and confirm the result clears 1.25x.
   - Jordan's deal shows $185K of earnings against $196.2K required [The Money Rules — DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:04:32]. Haircut claimed add-backs the way Lion's example falls from $700K claimed to $550K supported, which ends at 1.10x [Lion Business Advisors — orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:15:11].
3. Engage the QoE and Valuation Early
   - If the business purchase price is $3M or more, lock in a QoE provider the lender accepts before the loan number is issued.
   - Under PLP, the QoE must be engaged by the time the loan number is issued [Lion Business Advisors — orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:10:13]. A buyer-commissioned report qualifies through a reliance letter or a review by a lender-vetted provider [Chris Barrett, CPA — Ru9e1wYKGHc @ 00:00:43].
4. Submit a Clean Prequal Package
   - Send the application, 3 years of tax returns, a resume and 2 months of bank statements, then deliver the LOI.
   - Credit Bench gives a same-day, non-binding prequal on that package [Tresle Inc — NmgNpE_W_po @ 00:13:30]. Approval takes about 2 weeks, with closing roughly 20 days later, for 30-35 days total, or 35-45 days when commercial real estate is involved [Tresle Inc — NmgNpE_W_po @ 00:14:20].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over who owns the quality of earnings report and whether a buyer can reuse one. Pioneer Capital Advisory's team told acquisition buyers flatly that the lender has to be the client, no assignment or reliance letter is possible, and a second QoE is likely [WF2-6ODNQms @ 00:25:49]. Josh KS of Lion Business Advisors reads the September technical update differently: a seller-commissioned QoE still cannot satisfy the rule, but a buyer QoE can be used after an approved vendor reviews it, and the lender keeps both reports on file [orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:09:28]. Brad Hedock at Commercial Lending X lists the buyer-ordered QoE among the 13 key changes and calls it positive overall, while flagging that nuances remain [Rsg9fKAzdAo @ 00:03:47]. Chris Barrett, the Midwest CPA, confirms buyers may now commission the report through either a provider reliance letter or a review by a lender-vetted provider, then warns that reliance letters raise the provider's liability, cost, and scope [Ru9e1wYKGHc @ 00:01:30]. The operator read: if you commission early diligence yourself, budget for the review path rather than assuming the reliance-letter path will be cheap, and confirm early with your lender which route its approved vendors will actually accept.

Practitioners also split on which dollars count toward the buyer's half of the injection. Pioneer Capital Advisory teaches that deal costs the buyer already paid count toward their share, so diligence spending does double duty as equity [WF2-6ODNQms @ 00:55:43]. Brad Hedock calls the opposite ruling the most negative change in the September technical update: education, advisory, and agent fees no longer count as equity [Rsg9fKAzdAo @ 00:11:30]. Dan Pashet and Emily Dachon of the SBA's Office of Financial Assistance, presenting for Coleman Report, confirm that education and advisory prepaids are excluded from the injection [miA6NuzVV6Y @ 00:25:25]. The Money Rules threads the needle by stating that valuation and QoE report costs do count toward equity, which keeps third-party diligence inside the ledger while coaching and buy-side advisory fees fall outside it [DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:06:01]. The working playbook is to separate invoices by category before the lender writes the credit memo: valuation and QoE receipts go into the injection column, while program tuition, coaching retainers, and broker or agent fees get treated as sunk cost. A buyer who leaned on paid coaching or advisory retainers to fill their half under the older reading now has to replace that money with seasoned cash, a grant, or a personal loan repaid from outside the business cash flow.

Even the trigger date for the new rules is argued three ways. Josh KS ties it to applications received by SBA on or after October 1, stressing that September 30 filings stay on SOP 8 and that LOI or listing dates decide nothing [orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:01:38]. Michael S Glaspie frames it as applications started after October 1, 2026 [WfgrSa37vaA @ 00:00:30], while The Money Rules keys it to the date SBA issues the loan number [DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:00:21]. For any deal straddling the cutover, the defensive move is to underwrite to the stricter 8.1 math so a slipped submission date cannot blow up the structure. On the financing mix itself, Ray Drew explicitly runs a different playbook from the buyers chasing minimum cash: SBA does not require seller notes, but many lenders do, and beyond the full-standby half of the injection those notes are negotiable tools used to offset a weakness such as customer concentration, with examples like a 10-year note, a 1–2 year standby, or a balloon at year 5 [9Sl18GknuZE @ 00:06:36]. That makes the note's terms a negotiation chip to trade against the price, not just a way to cut the buyer's cash at the closing table.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common deal-killer practitioners describe is earnings that collapse under scrutiny. Josh KS walks through a buyer claiming $700,000 of earnings when only $550,000 is supported; against $500,000 of debt service that is 1.10x, a fail under the 1.25x floor [orHTcAJcuAM @ 00:15:11]. The Money Rules runs the same autopsy on a buyer named Jordan: a $1.1M project, $110,000 of equity split between a $55,000 seller standby note and $55,000 of cash, and a $990,000 loan at prime plus 3%, roughly $157,000 a year in payments. Earnings of $185,000 fall short of the $196,200 the 1.25x test demands [DyCKwhKRtK0 @ 00:04:32]. Matthias Smith explains why SBA raised the bar: the old 1.15 floor left little margin for error and led to defaults, so the new floor runs on historical numbers only, and Smith pushes buyers to clear 1.25 with room to spare [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:04:43]. Exit Ready Valuation puts the seller-side version of the trap bluntly: messy books can kill the loan [Tt3Ae9PDn8A @ 00:02:29]. The economics check is mechanical: take historical earnings, strip every add-back you cannot document, divide by annual debt service, and either walk away or retrade the price whenever the result lands below 1.25x.

The second trap is paying a price the appraisal will not support. Matthias Smith's example: a $3.5M price against a $3M valuation leaves a $500,000 gap that must be covered with equity, because the SBA loan plus any amortizing seller note cannot exceed the valuation; Smith's fix is to research multiples before the LOI so a retrade never becomes necessary [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:12:10]. Pioneer Capital Advisory frames the same cap as total debt, SBA plus any non-standby seller note, limited to valuation [WF2-6ODNQms @ 00:49:57]. Ray Drew's sequence when the number comes in low is to renegotiate with the seller first and treat overpaying as a last resort, justified only occasionally for a strategic deal [9Sl18GknuZE @ 00:01:11]. Jack Pastrana of Credit Bench by First Home Bank explains why the gap usually exists in the first place: banks are cash flow lenders, and weak cash flow usually means the business is overpriced [NmgNpE_W_po @ 00:34:41]. The economics check is to run the lender's lens before signing anything: if the debt the business can carry at 1.25x is less than the asking price minus the 10% injection, the price is wrong, not the financing. Bring that math to the seller early, while there is still room to negotiate.

The third trap hits after closing. Ray Drew calls inadequate working capital the number one reason for an early default, tells buyers to project the cash-flow deficit during the transition, and warns that SBA will not fund working capital that is not substantiated [9Sl18GknuZE @ 00:07:05]. Matthias Smith flags a sharper version on expansion acquisitions: with 100% financing, working capital cannot be financed at all, so a buyer needing $300,000 of working capital is forced back into 10% equity unless that need is negotiated into the purchase price [3tkWRQX9WjI @ 00:10:40]. SBA University adds the liquidity side: lenders want to see money left after the injection, not a buyer emptying their bank account to close [92Nd2yD33As @ 00:05:12]. Michael S Glaspie lists the operational warnings that follow a thin closing: poor bookkeeping, interest-only early payments, payment fluctuation, and the risk that the lender calls the loan [WfgrSa37vaA @ 00:08:57]. The economics check is to model the first year with the full principal-and-interest payment, a transition dip in cash flow, and the cash reserve left after injection; if that reserve cannot carry several weak months, the deal is undercapitalized before day one. Drew also warns that stingy lenders who refuse to fund real needs hurt both sides of the table.
