## Executive Summary

Business acquisition buys control of an existing operating company and earns money from its delivered products or services, not from the purchase price or customer funds passing through its accounts. Candidate #13, Business Acquisition, holds Rank #27 of 29 and scores 57/100: capital and labor intensity constrain otherwise attractive cash generation. Durable ownership requires verified earnings, retained customer relationships, transferable operating rights, and back-office automation that strengthens controls rather than disguising missing people.

## 1. Buy an operating system, not a seller's income claim  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Business acquisition is the purchase of assets or equity that gives a buyer control over an existing company's productive resources, customer obligations, and earning capacity. Entrepreneurship through acquisition, or ETA, replaces the startup's search for initial demand with a search for transferable cash flow. Micro-private-equity ownership applies similar discipline to smaller companies, often with one operating manager rather than a portfolio bureaucracy. The buyer pays for equipment, working capital, relationships, intellectual property, and goodwill; those payments are investments, not operating revenue. An asset purchase selects assets and assumed obligations through the agreement, but does not automatically eliminate statutory successor exposure. An equity purchase generally leaves the company and its historical obligations intact while changing its owners. Neither structure substitutes for tax, employment, environmental, licensing, and contract diligence. Begin with a transaction perimeter: which entity invoices, which entity employs, which contracts transfer, which deposits remain owed, and which liabilities survive closing. Put that perimeter beside the purchase agreement and opening balance sheet. A profitable business that loses its qualifying license, landlord permission, or indispensable customer upon transfer is not the same business the seller marketed. Price the operation that can legally and practically continue, not the historical operation that existed under someone else's relationships.

Recognized operator revenue follows the acquired company's customer performance, not the amount financed or the volume of other people's money it handles. Under an accrual reporting policy, record service revenue as contracted performance occurs and product revenue when the relevant control-transfer conditions are met. Cash-basis tax records may differ, so reconcile them rather than treating deposits as earned sales. A property manager collecting $200,000 monthly rent at an 8% management fee ordinarily has $16,000 monthly management revenue, not $200,000 of company sales. A reseller controlling inventory before delivery may instead report gross product sales with corresponding cost of goods sold; a marketplace agent may report only its commission. Assess the actual principal-agent relationship, not the software's gross-volume dashboard. Customer retainers, refundable deposits, sales taxes collected for authorities, acquisition loans, and owner capital require separate balance-sheet treatment. Buying a company for $1,200,000 does not create $1,200,000 revenue. Build a bridge from signed orders to delivered work, invoices, credit notes, processor settlements, and bank receipts. QuickBooks Online or Xero can hold the ledger, but the operator must define the recognition policy and investigate settlement differences rather than silently booking every bank credit as sales.

The canonical scorecard describes acquired business sales and margin after interest, before principal repayments. Conservative sales of $750,000 at 8% produce $60,000 annual owner profit; Base sales of $1,875,000 at 13.0% produce $243,750; Scaled sales of $3,000,000 at 18% produce $540,000. The first two cases require seven full-time employees plus three contractors, while Scaled requires eight employees plus four contractors. Twelve people is a real management system, not a passive-income side project. These cases imply $690,000, $1,631,250, and $2,460,000 respectively of costs and charges within the stated profit boundary. Treat the separate $15,000-$70,000 monthly opex planning range as a scope-dependent budget input, not a complete expense reconciliation. Startup capital of $200,000-$1,000,000 must cover the actual equity requirement, transaction spending, liquidity reserve, and unfunded transition work; first-deal timing is six to eighteen months. Principal amortization, capital purchases, and working-capital expansion can consume profit without reducing that scorecard measure. Build a second cash waterfall for those uses. The 57/100 score reflects this burden: revenue potential contributes 24 points, margin 16, team efficiency seven, startup accessibility two, and automation eight. The opportunity is operating ownership with leverage, not an accounting shortcut.

## 2. Screen sub-verticals for transferable demand and controllable delivery  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Choose a sub-vertical by its renewal mechanics and operating dependencies, not by a listing broker's favorite multiple. BizBuySell, Axial, and Acquire.com can surface opportunities, but marketplace presence is neither earnings verification nor proof of transferability. Ask what customers actually repurchase, who performs the work, and what happens when the founder stops answering calls. Commercial cleaning can retain recurring sites yet lose its margin through overtime and contract underbidding. HVAC maintenance can build route density yet depend on a qualifying license holder. A managed IT provider can show monthly recurring revenue while harboring underpriced support obligations and unsupported infrastructure. Compare businesses using gross profit after direct delivery labor, customer-level retention, collection timing, service backlog, and replacement management cost. Revenue per employee alone rewards outsourcing and pass-through sales without proving operating quality. Obtain twelve to twenty-four months of invoice-line exports and group customers by acquisition cohort, location, service type, and technician. Then ask which margin disappears if the top customer, seller, or key employee leaves. A durable small-team moat consists of repeatable performance and accumulated operational knowledge that the buyer can retain, teach, and inspect without requiring the founder's daily intervention.

The comparison matrix uses named operating systems to make diligence concrete; its fee descriptions are customer-contract structures, not asserted software subscription prices. Request actual vendor agreements, seat counts, payment-processing schedules, implementation obligations, and change-of-control terms. A platform migration can destroy dispatch history, maintenance reminders, or customer-specific pricing if the acquisition team treats exports as an afterthought. Preserve a read-only source archive and reconcile opening balances before replacing software. Test customer concentration against contribution profit, not merely sales: losing one complex account can improve economics, while losing a high-margin anchor can strand an entire crew. Examine contract assignment, cancellation rights, service credits, warranties, and customer security requirements. For a franchise resale, verify franchisor transfer requirements and evaluate disclosure obligations under 16 CFR Part 436 where applicable; a familiar brand does not make the territory freely transferable. For government contractors, determine whether an asset transfer requires federal recognition through the FAR Subpart 42.12 novation process. Equity changes and asset transfers do not necessarily receive identical treatment. Preserve the specific customer permission, vendor support, and operating authorization that produces cash rather than assuming goodwill moves automatically with the purchase price.

Durability also requires distinguishing collectible contracts from contractual headlines. A three-year agreement with convenient termination rights can be economically weaker than a month-to-month relationship supported by measurable switching costs. Inspect cancellation cohorts, concessions, chargebacks, aged receivables, and post-period credits before assigning a recurring-revenue premium. For software, examine repository ownership, administrator access, hosting bills, dependency licenses, and support hours per account. For physical services, inspect truck condition, spare-parts availability, workers' compensation history, and actual payroll classifications. Federal wage-and-hour obligations under 29 U.S.C. 206 and 207 are concrete cost inputs where applicable, not items to remove through aggressive add-backs. State requirements may impose additional costs. Quality of Earnings, or QoE, should connect reported profit to sustainable delivery economics rather than simply produce a normalized earnings number. Require a written exception log showing the source, proposed adjustment, cash consequence, responsible reviewer, and purchase-agreement response. An unresolved exception must change price, structure, reserves, or the decision to proceed. When the seller cannot substantiate claimed renewals, underwrite documented collections and demonstrated retention. A lower valuation supported by evidence is more useful than a sophisticated model built on customers who can disappear immediately after closing.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Buy the operating evidence that survives the seller's departure**
> 
> Experienced acquirers test transferability through source records, retained people, and customer behavior before paying for claimed recurring earnings.
> - Request invoice-level credits issued after the diligence cutoff; reconcile them to earlier sales to detect inflated period-end earnings.
> - Rebuild one busy week's labor schedule with the seller removed and replacement management fully costed; compare promised capacity with actual coverage.
> - Request a consented, seller-coordinated customer reference sample weighted by contribution profit, including a recently lost account where feasible.
> - Export customer histories, administrator roles, renewal dates, and service obligations before migration; validate record counts and balances against the source system.

## 3. Map seven operating plays to measurable economics and bounded fallbacks  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

This chapter maps seven plays: the specified distressed_asset_recovery TRACK_1 play and six descriptive acquisition operating mappings shown below. The latter are chapter labels, not additional canonical catalog identifiers. All play-level figures are illustrative incremental economics, not replacements for the volume's three annual cases. Distressed asset recovery locates mispriced or administratively stranded value, including distressed micro-businesses, government surplus, dormant judgments, tax-sale overages, and state unclaimed funds. These are different legal and operational lanes, not interchangeable claims. An authorized $80,000 recovery with a legally permitted 15% contingent fee yields $12,000 fee revenue; $4,000 of direct case costs leaves $8,000 before overhead and tax effects. The $80,000 claimant principal is not operator revenue. Buying transferable surplus equipment for $18,000, spending $4,000 on transport and repair, and selling it for $30,000 produces $8,000 gross spread before other costs. Verify title, liens, redemption rights, fee restrictions, waiting periods, and claimant authority for the applicable jurisdiction. The fallback is diversified multi-state docket sourcing and attorney-backed filing partnerships where required, with state-specific procedures rather than copied forms. Diversification never cures an invalid claim or authorizes work reserved to licensed professionals.

The recurring-service acquisition play starts with retained customers rather than new-sales assumptions: 100 commercial accounts paying $500 monthly generate $600,000 annual sales, and a 30% direct contribution margin yields $180,000 before central overhead, financing, and other charges. If retention cannot be verified, reduce the price or use a properly documented retention adjustment rather than borrowing against the seller's forecast. The seller succession play purchases operating continuity from a retiring owner. Replacing $90,000 of genuinely removable owner compensation with $70,000 of manager compensation creates only $20,000 incremental annual improvement, not a $90,000 add-back; employer costs can narrow it further. If no competent manager will stay, retain the seller through a defined transition or defer the transaction. The route-density tuck-in play acquires adjacent demand that existing supervision can absorb. Saving 20 paid travel hours weekly at $30 loaded hourly cost across 50 weeks creates $30,000 potential annual labor savings. Confirm overtime, crew availability, and service windows before counting them. If customer locations do not fit, operate the acquired route separately and underwrite standalone costs. Each play must survive its fallback without requiring speculative revenue growth to pay scheduled debt service.

The working-capital repair play improves cash conversion rather than pretending collections are new profit. At $1,875,000 annual credit sales using a 365-day convention, reducing receivable days from 50 to 40 releases approximately $51,370 of cash once, assuming steady sales and collectible balances. If disputed invoices resist collection, repair documentation and tighten future terms instead of coercing customers or booking fictitious recoveries. The back-office integration play consolidates reporting and administration: eliminating $2,400 monthly duplicated costs while adding $900 monthly support creates $18,000 annual recurring savings. A $12,000 migration expense leaves $6,000 first-year benefit before other effects; retain parallel reporting until balances reconcile. The recurring-contract conversion play turns demonstrated repeat demand into properly scoped service agreements. Converting 200 willing customers to $40 monthly plans adds $96,000 annual contracted sales if every plan remains active; $36,000 direct fulfillment costs leave $60,000 contribution before other charges. Prepayments still represent future service obligations where performance remains outstanding. If attachment or renewal disappoints, keep profitable one-off work and stop subsidizing uneconomic plans. Do not add these examples together and call the sum the Base case: overlaps, implementation timing, staffing, and financing determine which improvements can coexist.

## 4. Reconcile debt, diligence, and the first 100 days before closing  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Finance the acquisition around verified downside cash flow, not the maximum loan someone will discuss. SBA-backed acquisition financing remains subject to eligibility, credit analysis, current program requirements, and lender underwriting; 13 CFR 120.110 identifies ineligible business categories and 13 CFR 120.150 addresses lending criteria. Obtain the lender's written treatment of equity injection, seller debt, standby arrangements, guarantees, and distributions before relying on a proposed structure. A seller note does not automatically satisfy equity requirements or permit immediate payments. Model interest within the scorecard margin and principal below it, avoiding double subtraction. With illustrative annual principal of $48,000 and maintenance capital spending of $20,000, the three canonical profits leave negative $8,000, positive $175,750, and positive $472,000 respectively before working-capital changes and any other cash items outside the scorecard boundary. Those amounts are cash sensitivity outputs, not revised canonical profits. Debt-service coverage uses the lender's defined cash-flow numerator and total required debt service; do not substitute owner profit without a reconciliation. Stress collection delays, a lost anchor customer, higher borrowing costs where applicable, and manager replacement. Maintain a funded liquidity reserve rather than treating an undrawn or uncommitted credit discussion as available cash.

Convert diligence findings into closing mechanics. Agree the working-capital definition and peg, excluding cash, debt, and other items exactly as the purchase agreement specifies; examine seasonality and stale inventory before using a trailing average. Trace customer deposits to remaining fulfillment costs so the buyer does not inherit service obligations without funding. In a qualifying asset acquisition, coordinate purchase-price allocation under 26 U.S.C. 1060 and the applicable Form 8594 reporting requirements; separately model amortization of qualifying acquired intangibles under 26 U.S.C. 197. Tax deductions do not eliminate the initial cash purchase. Obtain lien searches, payoff letters, release documentation, lease permissions, license continuity plans, and required customer consents. Inspect payroll tax filings and worker classifications, then reconcile accrued wages, vacation obligations, and benefits according to applicable law and the transaction documents. Negotiate representations, indemnity limits, escrow, and earnout definitions around identified risks, recognizing that contractual recourse is only as useful as its enforceability and collectibility. A seller's promise to fix accounting later should become a dated deliverable with a measurable closing consequence. Keep a single issues register linking every unresolved finding to cash exposure, responsible ownership, and the final agreement.

The first 100 days should preserve the revenue engine before attempting a turnaround. During days one through thirty, retain key employees, contact priority customers, validate bank permissions, reconcile opening balances, and run a rolling thirteen-week cash forecast. Confirm that payroll, insurance, licenses, supplier accounts, and emergency service coverage work under the new ownership. During days thirty-one through sixty, eliminate billing leakage, repair purchasing controls, and publish weekly operational metrics with named owners. During days sixty-one through one hundred, implement only improvements supported by measured capacity and customer feedback. Automate financial reporting and back-office workflows through controlled integrations, not shared administrator credentials or unreviewed payment instructions. Separate vendor creation, payment preparation, and payment release wherever staffing permits, with independent review where roles must overlap. Monitor retained revenue, direct contribution, overtime, receivable days, callbacks, and covenant headroom together. A revenue increase with slower collections and more rework may indicate deterioration rather than progress. Review the acquisition thesis monthly against the original downside case. Durable small-team ownership is visible in reliable reporting, repeatable delivery, and management depth; fragile beginner hype depends on aggressive add-backs, perpetual refinancing, and the founder quietly continuing to perform work nobody included in the budget.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Define the transferable operating perimeter
   - List purchased assets or equity, assumed obligations, customer contracts, licenses, people, and data rights.
   - Assign each item an evidence source, transfer requirement, responsible reviewer, and specific closing deliverable.
2. Rebuild earnings from underlying transactions
   - Tie delivered work, invoices, credits, payroll, processor settlements, and bank activity to the reported ledger.
   - Quantify replacement management, deferred service obligations, disputed receivables, and unsupported seller adjustments before setting valuation.
3. Underwrite financing and downside liquidity
   - Model interest, principal, maintenance capital, working capital, lender covenants, and seller-note payment restrictions separately.
   - Test customer loss and collection delays, then document committed funding for the resulting cash shortfall before closing.
4. Run the evidence-led 100-day transition
   - Preserve people and customers first, reconcile opening balances, and introduce measured improvements through weekly ownership reviews.
   - Track retained revenue, contribution, overdue balances, service failures, and covenant headroom against the acquisition thesis.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Verify four conditions before committing acquisition capital**
> 
> Proceed only when the documented operating case survives the actual transaction perimeter, staffing requirements, and cash obligations.
> - Reconcile the three canonical revenue and profit cases, then separately fund principal, maintenance capital, seasonal working capital, and transition expenses.
> - Verify title, lien releases, required assignments, licensing continuity, and the buyer's authority to access every critical operating system.
> - Replace seller labor with named coverage at fully burdened cost and obtain a practical retention plan for essential employees and customers.
> - Obtain written lender terms and a downside cash forecast showing covenant calculations, payment dates, committed liquidity, and seller-note restrictions.
