## 1. Rebuild acquired revenue into a reconciled cohort waterfall  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Start with acquired operating-company sales, not purchase price, enterprise value, customer transaction volume, or financing proceeds. For comparability, define one cohort unit as a completed job producing $1,000 of recognized revenue after customer credits. The Conservative, Base, and Scaled cases therefore contain 750, 1,875, and 3,000 annual job equivalents. Illustrative gross billings are $1,040 per equivalent, with $40 deducted for contractual credits, refunds, and expected revenue reversals. That deduction equals 3.846% of gross billings, not 4% of billings. Annual recognized sales reconcile to $750,000, $1,875,000, and $3,000,000 exactly. In an agency business, replace the job measure with earned commissions and keep customer principal outside revenue. Build the source bridge from QuickBooks Online or NetSuite invoices, credit memos, bank deposits, and Stripe settlement exports. Match customer identifiers and service dates before grouping cohorts. A deposit proves cash movement, not completed performance; an invoice proves a billing event, not collectibility. Separate sales-tax collections, customer deposits, and purchased receivables from current-period earned sales. Require the monthly revenue bridge to explain timing differences rather than forcing deposits to equal sales through miscellaneous journal entries. Preserve source exports so subsequent collections can validate the original acquisition underwriting.

The waterfall below supplies illustrative deductions because the canonical scorecard specifies endpoints, not a detailed chart of accounts. Materials and fulfillment inputs consume $225,000, $525,000, and $780,000. Loaded employee delivery labor consumes $280,000, $520,000, and $720,000; contractor delivery labor consumes $60,000, $150,000, and $240,000. Employee allocations include payroll taxes and benefits, while contractor allocations require completed deliverables and verified invoices. The resulting direct contribution is $185,000, $680,000, and $1,260,000 before customer acquisition and overhead. Budget customer acquisition at $20,000, $60,000, and $90,000 and operating overhead at $65,000, $210,000, and $390,000. These allocations produce EBITDA of $100,000, $410,000, and $780,000. Deduct depreciation and amortization of $10,000, $31,250, and $60,000, then interest of $30,000, $135,000, and $180,000. Annual owner profit lands exactly at $60,000, $243,750, and $540,000. Treat these as pass-through operating cases without an entity-level income-tax expense; owner personal taxes remain outside the model. A different tax structure requires a separate bridge. Do not describe this residual as spendable distributions: principal, capital investment, and working-capital changes still consume cash after the profit calculation. The allocation is an underwriting hypothesis to replace with verified target-company records, not evidence about an actual seller.

Use seven operating plays to organize execution. Six chapter-local labels describe QoE revenue scrubbing, acquisition pricing, delivery-labor control, receivables collection, debt structuring, and the 100-day turnaround; the supplied mapped identifier is chapter11_trade_claim_convenience_class_rollup. Acquisition pricing connects sustainable earnings to purchase consideration rather than treating borrowed money as income. Delivery control compares contractor cost of $80 per job across all three cases against rework and acceptance evidence. Receivables collection measures days to usable cash, including processor restrictions. Debt structuring separates interest expense from principal cash requirements. The turnaround play assigns owners to collection, retention, and procurement improvements before assuming margin expansion. The trade-claim play uses a separate investment ledger because a purchased receivable is not a sale by the acquired operating company. At Base, owner profit is $130 per job; at Conservative it is $80, and at Scaled it is $180. Any improvement proposal must identify which deduction changes, when cash moves, and what operating evidence supports the change. Reject plans that count the same payroll reduction both as a seller add-back and a future saving.

## 2. Price retention, delivery capacity, and debt before paying for growth  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Customer acquisition cost means the cost to win customers, not the price to acquire the company. The illustrative budgets purchase 100, 250, and 375 new customers, giving CAC of $200, $240, and $240. Assuming each new customer buys two $1,000 jobs within twelve months, recognized first-year customer revenue is $2,000. Before fixed employee payroll, materials and contractor deductions leave $620, $640, and $660 per job. Two jobs therefore generate $1,240, $1,280, and $1,320 of marginal contribution, or $1,040, $1,040, and $1,080 after CAC. These figures are not owner profit and are usable only when existing employee capacity can absorb the work. Including allocated employee delivery labor reduces two-job contribution after CAC to approximately $293, $485, and $600 before overhead, depreciation, and interest. Track both views so a busy schedule does not conceal a poor fully loaded return. HubSpot acquisition-source cohorts should reconcile to invoiced jobs, while payroll or timekeeping exports establish available capacity. If customers buy only once, cut projected lifetime contribution rather than borrowing an industry retention assumption. Pause channels with negative fully loaded cohort contribution and retain existing customers before expanding acquisition spending. Charge sales commissions consistently to CAC instead of also burying them in overhead.

Acquisition pricing needs its own sources-and-uses schedule. An illustrative Base transaction pays $1,200,000 for a cash-free, debt-free operating business with normal working capital delivered at closing. Fund that price with $900,000 of acquisition debt and $300,000 of buyer equity, then separately fund $75,000 of transaction expenses and $135,785 of opening liquidity. Buyer cash totals $510,785; the price already includes the negotiated working-capital peg, so adding the peg again would double-count funding. Average debt of $900,000 at an illustrative blended 15% annual rate produces the Base interest deduction of $135,000. This is a modeling assumption, not a quoted SBA rate or an assertion that a particular loan qualifies. Seller-note standby, amortization, permitted uses, and security priorities must match executed documents and lender requirements. Assuming $108,000 annual principal and $31,250 maintenance investment, Base cash after debt service and maintenance is $135,750 before working-capital changes and owner taxes. The bridge adds back $31,250 of noncash expense before subtracting equal maintenance spending. An asset acquisition also requires a supported purchase-price allocation under 26 U.S.C. 1060 where applicable; book expense, tax amortization, and cash investment need separate schedules. If lender proceeds shrink, reduce consideration or increase committed equity before signing.

Stress the same economic system rather than inventing a second optimistic model. A 10% sales loss, with only materials and contractors flexing proportionally, reduces annual profit to $13,500 Conservative, $123,750 Base, and $342,000 Scaled. Employee payroll, CAC, overhead, depreciation, and interest remain fixed in this test. An additional reversal expense equal to one percentage point of baseline recognized sales reduces profit to $52,500, $225,000, and $510,000, assuming no cost recovery. A two-percentage-point interest increase on average debt balances of $300,000, $900,000, and $1,200,000 removes another $6,000, $18,000, and $24,000 respectively. Run these separately first, then combine them without assuming every exposure is independent. For the 100-day turnaround, a one-percentage-point materials reduction at Base saves $18,750 annually only after supplier pricing and service quality support the change. With $5,000 implementation expense, first-year improvement becomes $13,750 before timing effects. If the cheaper input increases refunds, stop substitution and restore the approved specification. The Scaled case's eight employees and four contractors are twelve people, not twelve interchangeable full-time positions. Require actual shift capacity, skill coverage, and employment classification evidence before assigning growth to automation. Reporting automation saves reconciliation effort; it does not manufacture delivery capacity.

## 3. Fund the gap between earned sales and usable cash  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Model working capital from the balance sheet and a weekly cash forecast, not from the income statement alone. Base DSO of 45 days produces receivables of $231,164 on $1,875,000 annual recognized sales. Inventory of 20 days on $525,000 annual materials cost requires $28,767; supplier terms of 30 days provide $43,151 of payable funding. Net operating working capital is approximately $216,781 using unrounded calculations. This simplified peg includes receivables plus inventory less trade payables; the purchase agreement must address accrued payroll, customer deposits, prepaid expenses, and other operating balances explicitly. Conservative assumptions of 30 DSO, 15 inventory days, and 25 payable days produce approximately $55,479. Scaled assumptions of 35 DSO, 15 inventory days, and 30 payable days produce approximately $255,616. These are illustrative steady-state requirements, not additions to revenue or deductions from annual profit. A closing shortfall against the delivered peg changes the purchase adjustment and immediate funding requirement. Inspect aged receivables for disputes and inventory for obsolescence before accepting their book values. A seller can deliver the nominal peg with unusable assets unless eligibility rules, valuation policies, and the true-up mechanism are specified. Agree those definitions before headline price becomes emotionally or contractually difficult to change.

Receivables collection and processor availability are different clocks. Under a stated convention that DSO ends at customer payment, a seven-day settlement lock on 20% of Base sales adds $7,192 of restricted settlement funding. If the accounting DSO already runs through bank availability, do not add those days again. A 15-day DSO deterioration adds $30,822 Conservative, $77,055 Base, and $123,288 Scaled. Base opening liquidity of $135,785 combines the $77,055 delay buffer, $7,192 settlement lock, and $51,538 for two biweekly employee-and-contractor payment cycles. The payment-cycle calculation uses $670,000 annual delivery labor divided by 26 and multiplied by two; actual contractor payment terms should replace that simplifying convention. This reserve is additional to delivered normal working capital and excludes transaction fees. The quoted $6,000-$25,000 monthly operating expense range cannot stand in for total modeled cash outflow: Base materials, delivery labor, CAC, and overhead total approximately $122,083 monthly before interest and principal. Use the detailed waterfall for liquidity planning rather than silently capping expenses at the benchmark range. Negotiate supplier terms without assuming suppliers will finance a change of control. Suspend discretionary distributions whenever the forecast consumes the protected liquidity floor.

Maintain a thirteen-week direct cash forecast with weekly beginning cash, customer receipts, supplier payments, payroll, contractors, tax remittances, interest, principal, and ending unrestricted cash. Keep restricted processor balances in their own column. Use invoice-level expected payment dates for the largest accounts and historical curves for the long tail. A refund reserve recognized against sales is an accounting estimate; money reserved to fund actual refunds is a liquidity allocation, not a second expense. Maintain an opening reserve plus new provisions less settlements roll-forward and connect each settlement to the originating cohort. Bad-debt expense, contractual credits, and processor chargebacks need separate reason codes because they imply different operating fixes. Under the Base 15-day collection stress, the $77,055 incremental funding requirement would reduce the $135,750 annual cash bridge to $58,695 before other working-capital changes or owner taxes. Annual arithmetic still misses a payroll failure in week three, so inspect the minimum weekly balance. Assign collection ownership before closing, obtain customer remittance instructions through verified channels, and preserve access to historical dispute records. When timing slips, accelerate clean billing, negotiate documented payment plans, and draw only committed facilities permitted by their terms rather than postponing payroll or statutory remittances.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the invoice-to-cash spine
   - Export invoices, credits, receipts, settlement reports, and aging by customer and originating job.
   - Reconcile opening receivables plus sales less credits and receipts to closing receivables; isolate processor restrictions.
2. Reconstruct the closing capital peg
   - Calculate eligible receivables, inventory, payables, and other agreed operating balances using consistent policies.
   - Record exclusions for disputed invoices, obsolete stock, debt-like items, and balances outside the transaction perimeter.
3. Forecast thirteen weeks of usable cash
   - Schedule customer receipts and every material operating, financing, and tax payment by actual expected week.
   - Show baseline and delayed-collection cases with explicit restricted balances, committed facilities, and minimum liquidity.
4. Close the forecast-to-actual loop
   - Replace estimates with bank-cleared amounts weekly and assign every material variance to an accountable operator.
   - Update collection promises, supplier terms, reversal reserves, and distribution capacity without changing canonical scenario endpoints.

## 4. Underwrite trade-claim recoveries without contaminating operating revenue  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

The mapped play chapter11_trade_claim_convenience_class_rollup purchases documented small trade claims below risk-adjusted expected plan distributions. Treat it as an optional, separately funded investment sleeve, not an assumed revenue stream inside the three acquisition cases. For a $10,000 face claim, suppose a confirmed plan supports a 35% distribution if the claim is allowed, underwriting assigns a 90% probability of that outcome, and the downside outcome pays zero. Expected gross collection is $3,150. Paying $1,800 and reserving $300 for administration produces $1,050 expected economic surplus before financing and taxes. That is a 50% expected return on $2,100 total cash committed, not a 50% annual yield. An eighteen-month wait ties up the purchase price and servicing budget while the operating company still owes payroll. Claim face amount is neither acquired-business sales nor earned fees. Recoveries contain return of invested capital; track basis recovery, economic gain, cash timing, and applicable accounting treatment separately. Do not transfer SBA loan proceeds or restricted operating cash into the sleeve without establishing that the use is permitted under governing program rules and financing documents. If no permitted surplus capital exists, make no claim purchases.

Section 1122(b) of title 11 permits a separate class of sufficiently small unsecured claims when the court approves the amount as reasonable and necessary for administrative convenience. It does not promise a recovery premium to a buyer who splits claims or aggregates holders. Read the actual plan, disclosure statement, confirmation order, class definitions, election terms, and distribution procedures before pricing any convenience treatment. Schedules E/F are sourcing evidence, not a substitute for reconciling the claims register, proofs of claim, amendments, and objections. Inspect purchase orders, delivery evidence, invoices, credits, and payment history to establish the underlying obligation. Section 502 governs allowance and objections; setoff, prior payments, priority disputes, and avoidance exposure can change the economics. For qualifying post-proof transfers other than security transfers, Bankruptcy Rule 3001(e)(2) requires transfer evidence and notice, with a 21-day transferor objection period after mailing unless extended by the court. Follow the rule's applicable branch and local procedures rather than applying one workflow to every instrument. Use PACER and the court-authorized claims agent to verify docket events. Retain assignment authority, seller identity, and payment instructions through independent verification to prevent buying an unsupported or already-transferred claim.

Price the fallback before placing the order. If supported general-unsecured recovery is only 10% of face with the same 90% allowance probability, expected receipts fall to $900 and the $2,100 commitment loses $1,200 before financing costs. If timing extends from eighteen to thirty months, the additional year costs $252 at a simple 12% funding assumption on $2,100. These are separate sensitivities, not independent probabilities to multiply without evidence. Stop affected purchases when the legal or documentary premise fails, enforce documented seller recourse where available, and price only supported general-unsecured recoveries. Recourse is worth less when the seller lacks assets or the contract does not cover the defect. Set debtor-level concentration limits because buying many claims against one estate does not diversify plan risk. Return to the acquisition dashboard each week: collections, labor utilization, customer losses, reversal provision, covenant headroom, and minimum cash. Release a turnaround saving into the forecast only after invoices or payroll verify it. Keep claims distributions outside the canonical operating-sales bridge so an investment windfall cannot conceal customer attrition. Preserve the operating company's ability to serve customers before pursuing delayed recoveries from another company's insolvency.
