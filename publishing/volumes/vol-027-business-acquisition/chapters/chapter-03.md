## Executive Summary

Practitioners running and backing searches agree the job is a high-volume relationship grind: Pete Seligman's funnel ran 200 companies to 50 IMs to 20 conversations to 5 offers to 1 purchase, and Coley Andrews describes thousands of outreaches for one deal. Targets are healthy, cash-generative, recurring-revenue businesses priced around 4-6x cash flow, financed with roughly half bank debt plus seller notes and equity. Traditional funds raise about $400-700K of search capital for a two-year runway and hand the searcher 20-30% equity in thirds; self-funded searchers keep 60-73% but carry the runway themselves. The sharpest split is Robert Graham versus Greg Geronimus on whether traditional capital is a win-win or 'more expensive than a payday loan,' while Peter and Garrett reject mass broker spam in favor of authentic follow-ups every couple of weeks.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who has actually closed describes the search as a volume business that only converts through relationships. Pete Seligman and a partner each pitched the other five business ideas every Sunday night, then worked a funnel of roughly 200 companies down to 50 IMs read, 20 owners spoken to, five offers and one purchase: 'we probably spoke to 20, put in five offers, and bought one' [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:15:42]. Peter and Garrett, who closed an HVAC business after 13 months, put it bluntly: 'it's simple but it's a numbers game,' with Garrett grinding 15-20 broker calls a day [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:22:15]. Coley Andrews of Pacific Lake Partners scales the same math upward: thousands of outreaches, hundreds of conversations and tens of meetings to buy one company, with about 90% of each day spent absorbing rejection [_rMBWgqtfUI @ 00:05:47]. Brian O'Connor of NextGen Growth Partners lands in the same place after building trade-show target lists: 'at the end of the day, I think it is a numbers game in this path' [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:36:46]. The volume only matters because the close itself is relational; Peter called it 'not a finance game' but a trust game, with the seller still dropping in about three times a week after the contract ended.

The buy box is remarkably consistent across funded and self-funded operators: healthy, cash-generative, boring businesses that nobody needs to rescue. Tim Ludwig wants industry growth at least at GDP, recurring or 'actuarially recurring' revenue, gross margins of 50% or higher, and growth already underway, because 'it's hard enough as it is why not why try and make it any harder than you have to' [AWJaRrNXru8 @ 00:01:24]. Coley Andrews frames the same rule as risk control for inexperienced CEOs: buy companies that are 'unlikely to be broken' and sold by owners in their 60s or 70s with no successor child [_rMBWgqtfUI @ 00:01:17]. Brian O'Connor's preferred list reads like a checklist: recurring revenue, mission-critical product, low share of wallet, B2B over B2C, asset-light, and negative working capital [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:42:07]. Pete Seligman screened for no customer or product concentration, stable earnings and good cash conversion, and specifically wanted blue-chip customers issuing purchase orders directly rather than sitting 'five layers down' a supply chain [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:17:28]. Peter and Garrett's HVAC search ran almost the identical filter: recurring revenue, low customer concentration, stable margin, stable EBITDA and a non-cyclical industry.

The baseline prerequisite is runway. Greg Geronimus describes traditional search capital as money that covers salary and operating costs for two years before a second raise funds the deal, and Robert Graham states flatly that self-funded search does not fit anyone who cannot fund two years of searching [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:54:24]. Peter Harris prices that runway at a typical $400-700K raise with a median of $500-550K, of which $100-200K is searcher salary and the remaining $300K-plus goes to interns, legal, travel, bankers and diligence [i_Ownd9smbk @ 00:02:13]. Lenders demand the same staying power at the closing table: Jared Johnson, billed as the top SBA lender in the country, looks for liquidity well beyond the 10% down payment, experience mapped against the target, other income, and personal saving habits [pqmsev-3MPY @ 00:11:43]. The last shared conviction is how a searcher actually wins. Steve Divitikos, who closed in Toronto after just under two years, won over father-and-son founders who had developed 'a high distaste for private Equity' by presenting as 'a succession plan that's looking for the right business to succeed,' with a portfolio of one, a promise to keep the company name, and 99.9% of personal net worth riding on the deal [GYvpRW21sSY @ 00:00:59]. Price and structure only had to be good enough; the qualitative factors closed it.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Sourcing runs on two engines: brokers and proprietary lists. Peter and Garrett split the labor, with Garrett making 15-20 broker calls a day while Peter systematized a deal tracker that took six to seven months to refine [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:22:01]. Their broker list snowballed from about 10 to 500, and instead of blasting thousands of brokers they followed up with every broker every couple of weeks in emails that 'sounded authentic'; 40-50 brokers ended up knowing them by name, which matters because brokers filter buyers too [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:23:59]. The proprietary engine is cheap labor plus lists. Peter Harris describes intern armies at $10-15 an hour 'smiling and dialing,' or a sector thesis such as personal care or an HVAC roll-up [i_Ownd9smbk @ 00:03:55]. Brian O'Connor works trade-show exhibitor lists of about 1,000 companies, uses interns for research, and narrows to 200-400 targets after scoring roughly three industry spaces on a weighted attribute scorecard covering growth, fragmentation, cyclicality, a TAM of about $1B per niche, and low operational complexity [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:36:46]. Once deal flow is running, the cadence compounds: Peter's team read about five SIMs a week early, saw the first LOI die after about three months, and after six months always had one or two deals under contract.

The pricing and capital-stack math is standardized. Built to Sell's Harvard guest values targets at 'somewhere around four times' cash flow, maybe five for larger firms, so a $10M company carries roughly $2-2.5M of cash flow. On that $10M deal, the bank lends about 50%, or about $5M repaid over roughly seven years, usually without a personal guarantee at that size; a seller note of $1-2.5M sits subordinated and pays over about four years with interest; and investors supply about $3M of equity [004ptgWQ2TE @ 00:06:38]. Brian O'Connor benchmarks purchase prices at 4-6x TTM EBITDA with $5-10M of acquisition equity [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:45:37]. In the SBA lane, Jared Johnson requires 10% down on the total project, meaning price plus working capital, closing and diligence costs; a seller can carry half of that on full standby, typically no payments for 10 years, but the buyer still needs at least 5% [pqmsev-3MPY @ 00:06:14]. Adam of ProjectionHub models the coverage test: lenders want a DSCR of at least 1.25, and the template's example, built from a $5M SBA 7(a) loan, a $500K seller note and $5M of search-fund equity with $500K earmarked for working capital, reaches a $10M purchase price and shows 4.64 in year one [uXEbfHnDSes @ 00:01:53].

After the LOI, the clock and the paperwork take over. The Harvard guest on Built to Sell signs the LOI before financing is lined up, budgets 2-4 weeks of business diligence, 1-3 weeks to collect bank proposals and about six weeks for the bank's own process, and stresses that the no-shop clause, usually 90 days, is 'in fact the only binding part of the agreement' [004ptgWQ2TE @ 00:16:54]. Laura D. Franchesco of Dean Street Law adds that confidentiality and deposits bind too, so an LOI is 'not entirely non-binding' [ScOjsEfBwZQ @ 00:22:50]. Josh Dennis and Brian O'Connor close in 60-90 days, 'typically 75 days on average,' 30-45 when rushed and 120-plus when it drags, and they negotiate buffer into exclusivity, such as a 60-day close under 90-day exclusivity [kKwXdeUVNOw @ 00:45:15]. They issue the data-request list the moment the LOI is signed, run good cop/bad cop, insist on 'the power of the pen' by drafting the purchase agreement, and do business diligence in-house before hiring a QoE firm to keep dead-deal costs near zero. Robert Graham and Jordan Carter put real prices on that work: typical QoE runs $10-25K and legal $25-30K, 85% of surveyed self-funded acquirers spent under $30K on QoE, 68% spent under $50K on legal, and 75% ate under $25K in dead-deal costs [KT5T3j2jVQ4 @ 00:51:02].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the thesis and build the target list
   - Score industries on a weighted attribute scorecard, pick about three spaces, and turn trade-show exhibitor lists into 200-400 targets.
   - Filter for growing, fragmented, non-cyclical niches with no 800-pound gorilla and about $1B TAM, then use interns to research roughly 1,000 exhibitors down to the target list [The University of Chicago Booth School of Business — ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:15:59]
2. Run the daily broker and outreach cadence
   - Make 15-20 broker calls a day while a partner systematizes the deal tracker.
   - Peter and Garrett refined their tracker over six to seven months, reviewed about five SIMs a week early, and after six months always had one or two deals under contract [Before You Buy or Sell a Business Podcast — Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:22:01]
3. Model the deal before the LOI
   - Build a five-year monthly projection with the full capital stack and confirm DSCR clears 1.25.
   - Match the projection start year to the acquisition year because date handling is finicky, edit only the blue assumption cells, and hold expenses at a percentage of revenue for a conservative case [ProjectionHub — uXEbfHnDSes @ 00:06:02]
4. Sign the LOI with buffer and fire the data request
   - Negotiate exclusivity longer than the target close and send the data-request list the moment the LOI is signed.
   - Target a 60-day close under 90-day exclusivity, expect about 75 days on average, and set data expectations immediately to blunt deal fatigue [The University of Chicago Booth School of Business — kKwXdeUVNOw @ 00:45:15]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in search is who owns the equity. In the live Acquiring Minds debate, Greg Geronimus laid out the traditional deal: the searcher earns 25 percent in thirds, with one third at close, one vesting over about four years, and one tied to performance hurdles [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:10:12]. Robert Graham attacked that last tranche, arguing a searcher has to return 25 to 35 percent IRR to earn it, a hurdle 'over four times higher' than what LPs expect from a private equity firm, and adding that they have never been able to deposit a percentage of common equity in the bank [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:17:15]. Robert's counter-numbers: Sig searchers average 73 percent ownership of businesses with $2.6M average EBITDA, while traditional searchers hold 10 percent or less at close of a median $1.8M EBITDA business [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:12:55]. The wider self-funded survey shows 86 percent keeping more than 60 percent of the common equity [KT5T3j2jVQ4 @ 00:24:04]. The Harvard view lands in the same place: funded searchers carry up to 30 percent, more likely 20, while unfunded searchers may hold 60 to 70 percent [004ptgWQ2TE @ 00:11:26]. Greg's rebuttal is scale, with 46 percent of traditional deals above $12M TEV against a $3M to $8M self-funded range [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:08:43].

Sourcing splits into volume machines versus relationship plays. Pete Seligman tried wining and dining brokers and found it failed because brokers were not proactive [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:14:26], while noting that some current searchers source 2,000 businesses a month and make hundreds of calls a week [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:15:24]. Peter Harris describes the hired-volume version: intern armies at $10 to $15 an hour 'smiling and dialing,' or a sector thesis such as personal care or an HVAC roll-up [i_Ownd9smbk @ 00:03:55]. Brian O'Connor at Booth runs the thesis version, working trade-show exhibitor lists of about 1,000 companies down to 200 to 400 targets and calling it 'a numbers game in this path' [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:36:46]. Peter and Garrett, who closed an HVAC deal after 13 months, explicitly reject spamming thousands of brokers. Their list snowballed from 10 to 500, and every broker got a follow-up every couple of weeks in emails written to sound authentic [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:23:27], while Garrett made 15 to 20 broker calls a day and Peter systematized the tracker [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:22:01]. The payoff was 40 to 50 brokers who came to know them, and those brokers filter buyers too [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:25:20]. The real fork is not volume versus no volume; it is automated blast versus personal cadence.

Practitioners also split on what a buyer must bring before day one. Coley Andrews of Pacific Lake Partners says flatly, 'We believe in backing smarts and hustle, not experience' [_rMBWgqtfUI @ 00:00:54], and offsets the gap by buying healthy businesses that are unlikely to be broken [_rMBWgqtfUI @ 00:01:17]. Jared Johnson, billed as the top SBA lender in the country, wants the opposite profile: someone who has worked in consulting, PE, IB, or up through a company, managed people, saved money, and targets an adjacent industry [pqmsev-3MPY @ 00:05:02]. The Harvard buyer profile sits between them, with 7 to 15 years of management experience that is usually outside the target industry [004ptgWQ2TE @ 00:01:33]. The two debaters even agree on who each model excludes. Greg Geronimus concedes traditional search does not fit narrow-geography searchers or anyone weighing a startup [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:51:39], after earlier conceding a narrow Milwaukee search will not work in the traditional model [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:30:14]. Robert Graham concedes self-funded search does not fit anyone who cannot fund two years of searching [0lg8Gh_uAPU @ 00:54:24]. Coley's investor math frames the trade from the other side: $30K to $50K per searcher buys two years of runway, and even with some chance of losing that small amount, '75 out of a 100 is still pretty good' [_rMBWgqtfUI @ 00:05:15].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first economics check is attrition. One of the Sig study presenters, drawing on time at a PE firm, said an executed LOI there carried 'north of a 75 percent chance' of closing, and 'that's just not the case here' [KT5T3j2jVQ4 @ 00:40:09]. The self-funded survey shows the average acquirer submitted 6.9 LOIs to get 2.4 executed [KT5T3j2jVQ4 @ 00:45:01]. Pete Seligman's own funnel ran about 200 companies, 50 IMs read, 20 conversations, five offers, and one purchase [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:15:42]. Peter and Garrett's first LOI landed at about three months and died a week later; only after roughly six months did they hold one or two deals under contract at all times [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:20:17], and the partners tell searchers to expect to 'fail about 200 times' [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:27:36]. Jared Johnson watches searchers drift toward 'junk' deals eight or nine months in and refuses to finance them with a blunt 'I forbid you' [Hh83Lc7sWf4 @ 00:28:05]. Brian O'Connor names the two behavioral traps around that curve: sitting on your hands early in the search [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:28:59] and tunnel vision once a deal is under LOI [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:30:36]. Brian's reason: 'time when you're doing this, you'll find is your most scarce resource' [ccaGmVeLcto @ 00:25:54].

The second check is whether the numbers and the seller are real. Jared Johnson says the biggest deal-killer is usually that 'the cash flow doesn't work,' followed by red flags such as broker numbers that exceed tax returns or the QoE, low buyer cash, remote operators who show up 'once a month for a week,' and one or two customers dominating revenue [pqmsev-3MPY @ 00:09:02]. Jared's own first deal shows the cost of a prior owner's behavior: the bankrupt manufacturer had pre-collected customer payments, so the buying group put in $700,000 just to finish products for customers who had already paid [pqmsev-3MPY @ 00:25:33]. Robert Graham's first Pillar deal was the exception to a 78 percent positive seller-relationship rate, ending in an IRS fines letter, police involvement, and a lawsuit [KT5T3j2jVQ4 @ 01:04:14]. Tim Ludwig's rule: a gut red flag about the seller usually means problems elsewhere in the organization too [AWJaRrNXru8 @ 00:03:03]. Laura D. Franchesco adds the legal traps: an LOI is 'not entirely non-binding,' because exclusivity, confidentiality, and deposits bind [ScOjsEfBwZQ @ 00:22:50], and a waste-management acquisition must be a stock purchase with license transfer checked [ScOjsEfBwZQ @ 00:22:34]. Jared's adviser fix is to hire an acquisition specialist whose career this is, not a family-law or real-estate attorney [pqmsev-3MPY @ 00:19:28].

The third check is the first year after close, where practitioners report self-inflicted wounds. Pete Seligman ran an icebreaker at a strategy day that caused the owner to walk out, concluding 'application is everything' [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:29:30]. Pete's travel business lost to online wholesalers and exited around 2019 without a loss, summed up as 'can't fight a market' [_exEOi-Wxuk @ 00:42:22]. The Harvard list of failure modes covers external shocks, fixing broken businesses, mistiming cyclical bottoms, due diligence mistakes, and growth plays that amount to 'bolting a startup' onto the company [004ptgWQ2TE @ 00:48:45]; the counter is to avoid big, hard-to-reverse decisions in year one [004ptgWQ2TE @ 00:50:29]. Coley Andrews compresses the transition plan into 'do nothing,' holding off on a sales force, new segments, or senior hires until 12 to 18 months in [_rMBWgqtfUI @ 00:04:07]. Peak Frameworks puts the downside in aggregate: 31 percent of searchers never acquire and 29 percent of acquirers sustain a loss, which the speaker combines into roughly a 50 percent failure rate, alongside single-company idiosyncratic risk [0KhOZrLBMVQ @ 00:03:12]. The same speaker flags the timing collision with family plans in a searcher's late 20s or early 30s [0KhOZrLBMVQ @ 00:04:52].
