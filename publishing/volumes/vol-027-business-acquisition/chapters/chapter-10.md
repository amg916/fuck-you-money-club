# Chapter 10: Closing Price Mechanics and LOI Negotiation

*Working capital adjustments in asset sales, targets and true-up calculations, WC adjustments vs. the locked-box approach, negotiating NWC in lower-middle-market deals, how sellers capture value through the peg, buyer's cash vs. seller's cash, plus negotiating LOI terms such as exclusivity, shifting leverage, and seller-side lessons*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from David C Barnett to John Colley converge on one hard truth: the headline price is not the check at closing. Deals are priced cash-free/debt-free with a normal working capital peg, and every dollar delivered short of that peg comes off the seller's proceeds dollar for dollar, trued up 60 to 90 days post-close against escrow or seller notes. Street numbers: Anchor Partners' Westbrook owner lost a net $100K by pushing collections and stretching suppliers against a $900K peg; Colley's Summit case swung $900K on seasonal timing. On the LOI side, exclusivity is the seller's last real leverage, typically 30 to 120 days. The fights: whether the peg belongs in the LOI or after diligence, whether historical averages or zero is the right opening anchor, and closing-accounts true-ups versus locked box.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The headline number on the LOI is not the wire that hits the seller's account. Anchor Partners puts it bluntly: deliver less working capital than the peg, 'and the price comes down by exactly that amount,' and the damage usually happens in the last 30 days before close [ob7Q-yxAsNs @ 00:01:06]. RareBrain hammers the same trap from the seller's chair: a 'cash free debt free' purchase agreement still generally requires the owner to leave a normal amount of working capital in the business, so cash splits into buyer's cash and seller's cash, and an under-capitalized company lets the owner take nothing off the table [fPwi8HVhX3Q @ 00:00:44]. Mergers & Inquisitions frames the buyer's logic: in cash-free/debt-free private deals the adjustment keeps the buyer's effective price constant while the seller's proceeds float, which incentivizes the owner to run the business normally instead of delaying bills or under-ordering inventory [oa8dUICluLM @ 00:00:28]. FinanceKid writes it straight into the LOI template, pricing the deal cash-free/debt-free with normal working capital, and notes working capital can be priced separately with lower goodwill [JSIfjvnhOco @ 00:25:22]. Every one of these operators treats the peg as a second price negotiation running alongside the multiple, not an accounting footnote to clean up later.

What goes into the peg is itself a negotiation. Brett Cenkus starts from the textbook, current assets minus current liabilities, then warns the definition is negotiated, and in practice it often collapses to receivables minus payables because cash usually does not transfer [lPy47SFZqDw @ 00:00:17]. John Colley gives the cleanest definition sheet: strip out cash, debt and tax items, keep receivables, inventory, prepaids, payables, accruals and deferred revenue, and recognize that working capital causes more post-closing disputes than any other mechanism in the deal [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:02:55]. FinanceKid adds the edge cases: exclude related-party accounts and corporate income tax, but keep GST/HST inside the formula [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:06:20]. David C Barnett takes the same logic down to Main Street, where sellers keep cash and receivables, inventory transfers, and the seller retains payables, credit cards and the personally guaranteed loan, so the buyer walks away with $2,500 of $2,800 in assets and no liabilities. Barnett's core warning is that the reported balance sheet reflects the owner's whims, desires and fears, and 'what's missing here is normalization' [gFJZwK-3sq0 @ 00:14:36]. The shared baseline: agree the line items before anyone argues about the number, because the number follows the definition.

On the LOI itself, the consensus is that exclusivity is the one real chip the seller hands over, and it transfers leverage the moment the document is signed. A Simple Model shows the power curve flipping at execution because the LOI lets the buyer secure an exclusivity period that bars talks with other groups [mADDz83tbN0 @ 00:00:14]. Jeff Brooker at McGuireWoods works the sell side on that premise, holding the buyer's feet to the fire on every term that matters because once exclusivity is granted the seller has lost leverage, and Greg Hawver agrees seller power peaks before signing; only exclusivity, confidentiality and expenses are binding, and punting an issue usually favors the buyer [OzPzqbUSgPw @ 00:02:51]. PCE Investment Bankers lists the non-binding economics, price, structure, earnout or seller note, contingencies, diligence and working capital, against binding exclusivity, confidentiality and governing law [YJMOV57uFMk @ 00:01:40]. MySaasForSale reduces it to one line for small software sellers: exclusivity is the one thing a seller gives up at this stage, so its length matters, with 30 days common and an end date in writing [OOlka50xFck @ 00:01:41]. The operating rule across all four: negotiate hard before signing, because afterward the seller is bargaining from inside a locked room.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Setting the peg is arithmetic on monthly data, not a glance at year-end. Anchor Partners builds net working capital as receivables plus inventory minus payables minus accruals and pegs it at the trailing-12-month average; on Westbrook that ran $1.6M plus $150K minus $700K minus $150K for a $900K peg [ob7Q-yxAsNs @ 00:00:48]. John Colley's rule is 'use trailing 12 months, never a single month' [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:07:31]. FinanceKid goes deeper: pull 24 to 36 months of monthly statements and track net working capital as a percentage of TTM revenue, which typically lands at 5 to 6% and can run up to 15 to 20% [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:28:44]. For a declining business FinanceKid weights the last 6 to 12 months; in the worked example the ratio sat at 17.6% against 5.8%, and the target landed at 10 to 11%. For a flat business, unexplained low months get excluded, moving the range to 14 to 16% versus 12 to 14%, and one sample month computes as $900K minus $159K for $740K against a $701K average [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:32:15]. Normalizations show up in 2 to 3 of every 10 deals, so the monthly build is where the real money is argued.

The true-up is a two-step cash event: an estimate at close, then a recalculation 60 to 90 days later. Brett Cenkus runs the surplus case: a $5M price, a $500K target and a $600K seller estimate means the buyer pays $100K extra at closing, then recalculates post-close with every incentive to push the figure down; GAAP is not perfect science, so 'there's a lot of litigation around that' [ydfu0lpiDjc @ 00:03:34]. FinanceKid shows the deficit side: a $100K shortfall comes back out of a $200K escrow holdback, or gets cut from the vendor take-back note or earn-out [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:08:56]. ProjectionHub flags the closing-week drift buyers forget: payroll due two days after close, a $40,000 customer check landing the morning of closing, an undisclosed $2,000 payable the seller must reimburse, and both sides escrowing money, $50,000 each in their example, with a lawyer and CPA defining the cutoff [BvJfOb1y6x8 @ 00:04:05]. RareBrain's nasty-surprise case is $1M of inventory found half obsolete at the post-close count, producing a $500K shortfall, a seller paid $500K less, and a buyer claim against a $1M escrow [fPwi8HVhX3Q @ 00:01:58]. Escrow, holdback or note offset is the enforcement tool in every version.

On Main Street deals, David C Barnett shows how normalization rewrites the price. His sample balance sheet carries $2,000 of inventory, $500 of payables and a $300 credit card; normalized, inventory drops to $1,500 for old stock and ordering inefficiency, payables rise to $750 because bills were being paid early, and the credit card falls to $100 because the owner ran charges for airline points [gFJZwK-3sq0 @ 00:15:48]. Barnett then adds a $750 inventory and receivables line of credit, supportable because the inventory is fungible, leaving normalized working capital of $200, so a $2,000 price becomes $1,800 and the buyer lines up the credit line [gFJZwK-3sq0 @ 00:18:45]. Mergers & Inquisitions runs the leveraged-buyout version: a 12x multiple on $50 of EBITDA gives a 600 headline, a 50 deficit against a 100 target cuts enterprise value to 550 plus 50 of working capital funding, so the buyer's effective price stays 600, while a surplus pushes EV to 650 and seller equity from 500 to 600 [oa8dUICluLM @ 00:02:03]. John Colley draws the contrast that sets negotiating priorities: an EBITDA dollar moves through the multiple, $1 times 10 equals $10, while working capital moves dollar for dollar [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:06:53].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build the monthly working capital history
   - Pull 24 to 36 months of monthly statements and chart NWC as a percentage of TTM revenue.
   - FinanceKid benchmarks NWC at 5 to 6% of TTM revenue and up to 15 to 20%, weights the last 6 to 12 months for declining businesses, and excludes unexplained low months for flat ones [FinanceKid — 8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:25:15].
2. Normalize and strip distortions
   - Remove seasonal peaks, one-time receivables, accelerated collections and owner quirks.
   - Colley excluded August and September, a $400K insurance receivable and $600K of accelerated collections to land $8.3M against the seller's $9.2M [John Colley | The Investment Banking Practitioner — x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:06:16]; Barnett normalizes inventory, early-paid payables and points-driven card balances [David C Barnett Small Business and Deal Making SMB — gFJZwK-3sq0 @ 00:15:48].
3. Paper the structure into the LOI
   - Front-load share vs. asset structure, payment forms, exclusivity end date and personal sticking points.
   - The Built to Sell guest's attorney had them settle title, comp, earn-outs and legal-fee reimbursement in the LOI because parties a thousand feet apart blow up at the finish line [Built to Sell — 3FGfilDTCro @ 00:00:13]; an LOI that just says 'acquisition' for a C corp can hide millions between asset and stock deals [McGuireWoods LLP — OzPzqbUSgPw @ 00:12:20].
4. Run the closing estimate and true-up
   - Set the cutoff, fund escrow, close on an estimate and reconcile at 60 to 90 days.
   - ProjectionHub has a lawyer and CPA define the cutoff and both sides escrow money, $50,000 each in their example [ProjectionHub — BvJfOb1y6x8 @ 00:07:22]; Colley insists the purchase agreement carry dispute procedures for the 60-to-90-day final figure [John Colley | The Investment Banking Practitioner — x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:05:00].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Where the Money Moves Before and After Signing**
> 
> Four levers practitioners pull to shift closing dollars without touching the headline multiple, starting from the rule that every dollar less of working capital is a dollar in the seller's pocket [Brett Cenkus — lPy47SFZqDw @ 00:02:52].
> - Open the peg at zero: Brett Cenkus argues a profitable business needs zero working capital, answers PE diligence teams with 'who cares what it's been historically,' and falls back to forward-looking need, or historical only if historical is low [Brett Cenkus — lPy47SFZqDw @ 00:03:08].
> - Shrink the peg a year out: at $33K per day of billing, collecting one week faster takes $230K off the peg, so shorten the cash cycle a year before the LOI and track working capital monthly, because you cannot negotiate a number you only see once a year [Anchor Partners — ob7Q-yxAsNs @ 00:02:49].

> - Manufacture competition before signing: a seller with one real bidder flew to the buyer-universe cities listed in the CIM, let calls go to voicemail, called back from hotel phones, and answered 'some last minute work nothing big'; the buyer matched area codes, raised the price and rushed the LOI [A Simple Model — mADDz83tbN0 @ 00:01:11].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over when the peg gets locked. Anchor Partners defines NWC as receivables plus inventory minus payables and accruals. They set the peg at the trailing-twelve-month average and negotiate it inside the LOI itself, while the seller still holds leverage [ob7Q-yxAsNs @ 00:00:48]. FinanceKid runs the opposite play on lower-middle-market sell-side deals. There is no peg in the LOI, only a condition that the target is 'to be negotiated during due diligence.' The one exception is volatile working capital, where the number gets pinned before the seller grants 90-day exclusivity [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:16:54]. Brett Cenkus describes the US default as sitting between the two. Price is fixed at the LOI, the LOI binds only on exclusivity and confidentiality, and the target is set during the 30, 60 or 90 days of diligence that follow [ydfu0lpiDjc @ 00:02:07]. The McGuireWoods team prices the cost of waiting from the seller's chair. Punting an issue usually favors the buyer, because sometimes a buyer just wants to get the exclusivity and live to fight another day [OzPzqbUSgPw @ 00:03:27]. The operator choice is a straight trade-off. Lock a number while seller leverage peaks, or let monthly diligence data produce a figure both sides can defend when the true-up arrives.

The second fight is over the starting anchor for the target. Brett Cenkus attacks the private equity habit of presenting historical working capital as 'the number.' His answer is 'who cares what it's been historically,' because every dollar less of working capital is a dollar in the seller's pocket [lPy47SFZqDw @ 00:01:46]. His opening position is that a profitable business needs zero working capital. His fallbacks are a forward-looking need, or history only when history is low. He concedes that denying working capital to a business that genuinely needs it is 'swimming upstream' [lPy47SFZqDw @ 00:03:08]. John Colley takes the buyer-protective line: use trailing 12 months, never a single month. He then strips out seasonal peaks, one-time receivables and accelerated collections before the average counts [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:07:31]. FinanceKid builds the target from 24 to 36 months of monthly statements and tracks NWC as a percentage of trailing revenue. He reports that real normalizations show up in only 2 to 3 of every 10 deals [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:25:15]. The gap shapes the whole negotiation. Cenkus argues from what the business needs going forward, while Colley and FinanceKid argue from measured monthly history. A seller facing a history-based buyer should arrive with a forward-looking case already built, or concede the average early.

Closing mechanics and paperwork depth also split practitioners. Brett Cenkus works the $2 to $20 million emerging middle market. He rarely uses the European locked box but recommends it for complex or low-trust deals. Price is fixed on today's balance sheet, the seller avoids riding the trade, disputes shrink and permitted leakage is allowed. The cost is heavier upfront diligence [ydfu0lpiDjc @ 00:06:37]. FinanceKid offers two alternatives to a single fixed peg. High-growth businesses get a minimum target, and seasonal or commodity businesses get a collar, for example $4 to $6 million [8AOvKeCdAH0 @ 00:46:57]. On LOI length the camps diverge hard. FinanceKid says 1 to 3 page LOIs cause deal failure. His LOIs run 6 to 9 pages covering payment forms, covenants, vendor take-back terms and the working capital target [JSIfjvnhOco @ 00:02:18]. MySaasForSale went the other way and compressed the InvoicePing deal to one page, which was signed the next day. Its terms were €88,000 at closing through a jointly chosen escrow, and two weeks of email support capped at 10 hours a week. It also set 30-day auto-ending exclusivity, 21-day diligence and a two-year non-compete limited to invoice reminder tools [OOlka50xFck @ 00:04:11]. Both formats pin hard numbers on the terms that later get fought over.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive firsthand trap is a seller gaming the final 30 days. Anchor Partners walks through the Westbrook case. Receivables of $1.6 million plus $150,000 of inventory, minus $700,000 of payables and $150,000 of accruals, produced a $900,000 peg. The owner pushed collections and stretched suppliers by $100,000 and delivered $550,000 at close. The price dropped $350,000 against $250,000 of cash kept, a net loss of $100,000 plus the buyer's trust [ob7Q-yxAsNs @ 00:01:25]. John Colley's Summit Industrial Services case runs the same play at scale. NWC swung from $7.8 million in February to $11.0 million in August, and the seller chose an August close [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:05:38]. The buyer then excluded August and September, a $400,000 insurance receivable and $600,000 of accelerated collections. Its figure landed at $8.3 million against the seller's $9.2 million, a $900,000 gap [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:06:34]. Colley's economics check separates the two price levers. An EBITDA adjustment moves through the multiple, so one dollar at 10x becomes ten dollars. A working capital adjustment moves dollar for dollar, and working capital drives more post-closing disputes than any other mechanism [x_72u8PgHpc @ 00:06:53].

Post-close counts and cutoff errors are where the true-up bites. RareBrain reports the classic case. During the post-closing count, $1 million of inventory turned out to be half obsolete, creating a $500,000 shortfall. The seller was paid $500,000 less and the buyer claimed against a $1 million escrow, the kind of surprise that derails retirement plans [fPwi8HVhX3Q @ 00:01:58]. ProjectionHub flags the buyer-side misses. Buyers forget the payroll that falls due two days after close. Estimates also drift between calculation and signing, as when a $40,000 customer check lands the morning of close [BvJfOb1y6x8 @ 00:01:00]. Their fix starts with a lawyer and CPA defining the cutoff, followed by a 60 or 90 day true-up. Both parties also escrow money, for example $50,000 each, and an undisclosed $2,000 payable means the seller reimburses the buyer [BvJfOb1y6x8 @ 00:06:10]. Brett Cenkus warns that the closing-accounts model itself breeds conflict. The buyer recalculates after closing, GAAP is not a perfect science and the buyer is motivated to push the figure down. The result is litigation [ydfu0lpiDjc @ 00:04:02]. The closing estimate is a placeholder, and the escrow plus the dispute procedure decide what the seller actually keeps.

Upstream, the failures start with the balance sheet and the LOI process. David C Barnett normalizes a small listing line by line. Inventory is cut from $2,000 to $1,500 for old stock, and payables rise to $750 because bills were paid early. The credit card drops to $100 once airline-point charges are stripped out, and a $750 line of credit is added. That leaves normalized NWC of $200, and a $2,000 price resets to $1,800 [gFJZwK-3sq0 @ 00:18:45]. He calls paying 2.5x and then injecting another 1.5x of working capital 'nuts,' because the buyer is really paying 4x, and says such listings languish [gFJZwK-3sq0 @ 00:20:52]. On the LOI side, the MySaasForSale InvoicePing seller agreed €88,000 verbally and then waited two weeks for the buyer's draft while other buyers stopped writing. After three weeks nothing was signed, and the buyer wanted months more data [OOlka50xFck @ 00:00:00]. The Built to Sell guest assumed a non-binding LOI carried no risk and started with a personal lawyer who lacked SaaS knowledge of metrics like churn. The guest switched to a UK M&A tech firm after the acquirer side advised the upgrade [NBxRy2fAY-s @ 00:02:46]. Each failure traces back to terms or numbers left loose before exclusivity.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Gates Before Signing Exclusivity**
> 
> Every gate below comes from a loss or dispute a practitioner watched happen. Clear each one before the LOI goes out, because leverage shifts to the buyer the moment exclusivity is granted.
