# Chapter 01: Working to the Standard: IICRC S500 Water Damage and S520 Mold Remediation

*S500 2021 edition changes, S520 2015 and 2024 revisions, S520 core principles and homeowner guide, the Texas TMARR overlay, applied structural drying readings, and the MRS application and exam handbook*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Working practitioners boil the IICRC standards down to a hard consensus: remove mold physically, never fog or bleach it, and judge jobs by Conditions 1, 2 and 3 rather than visible square footage. “Should” is the standard of care, and any deviation needs documentation and written authorization, because courts measure remediators against the book. The street mechanics are specific: 6 mil fire-retardant poly, -5 pascals on a manometer, HEPA at 99.97% at 0.3 microns, a HEPA filter near $153, moisture readings every 5 ft, 12 to 16 air movers for 750 square feet, and the same meter used daily. Disagreements are real: Mark Cornelius says cleaning to zero prices remediation out of the market, Nicole Bijlsma ranks species order over spore counts, and speakers split on whether remediators may test their own work.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every serious voice on the mold side lands on the same rule: the job is physical removal, not chemistry. Phill McGurk calls removal “the big one” among five principles (safety, documentation before, during and after, containment, removal, and fixing the moisture) and says flatly that “proper remediation is about physical removal,” with spraying or fogging “totally inadequate” because dead mold is still an allergen [Dl8DpDk97_A @ 00:03:11]. S520 consensus body chair Jim Pearson pushes the same line for the 2024 edition: remove mold rather than kill it, and vice chair Scott Armor reduces the whole document to “remove don't kill” [JOiWfXdUwjM @ 00:27:50]. Time's Dark Captains reads it straight off the page: S520 4.4 requires physical removal, killing or encapsulating is inadequate, and 5.8.1.1 says chemicals are not a substitute [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:29:33]. The 2015 revision already told remediators not to mist disinfectants or sanitizers “to try to kill mold in lieu of source removal,” and ruled out temperature extremes, hot or cold, as an alternative to cleaning [6CI2NAsooLw @ 00:11:36]. The operator takeaway is blunt: a bid built around a fogger, bleach, or an ozone box is a bid built outside the standard of care, and these practitioners treat it as a red flag rather than a shortcut.

The second point of agreement is how a job gets sized. McGurk calls Condition 1 a normal, not sterile, environment and “the finish line” of every job, Condition 2 the settled or airborne spores and fragments that “amateurs totally missed,” and Condition 3 the “mold factory” that has to come out before the fallout gets cleaned [Dl8DpDk97_A @ 00:01:23]. Balanced Building Biology defines Condition 1 as indoor fungal levels similar to or lower than outdoors, and says Condition 3 includes active, dormant or dead growth [YdUmzaa5wO0 @ 00:00:57]. Pearson notes “air” was added to the Condition definitions in the 2024 edition, so the Conditions now reach airborne material rather than surfaces alone [JOiWfXdUwjM @ 00:26:38]. Time's Dark Captains drives the practical consequence home: Texas TMARR draws its line at 25 contiguous square feet including 2 ft past visible growth, OSHA tiers run under 10, 10 to 30, 30 to 100 and over 100 square feet, but the S520 foreword rejects visible-area thresholds and replaces them with Conditions 1, 2 and 3 [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:15:58]. For an operator, that means a small visible patch does not mean a small job; McGurk's dandelion analogy makes the same point, since the flower is small but the seeds have already spread.

The third consensus is the language and its legal teeth. Darren Floyd of APPALeadership lays out the vocabulary: “shall” is mandatory, “should” is the standard of care, and “recommend,” “may” and “can” are not; a deviation, in Floyd's words, “is not a license to do something wrong,” more like a detour [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:08:12]. Time's Dark Captains puts it in street terms (under “should” you need a “damn good reason” not to) and spells out the deviation drill from S520 9.3.4: document it, communicate with affected parties, and get written authorization [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:07:28]. Pearson is just as direct: the standard is voluntary, deviation needs written full disclosure to the client, and in front of a judge or jury, not following it means “you're probably going to lose” [JOiWfXdUwjM @ 00:14:53]. Learn To Restore explains where that weight comes from: ANSI accreditation, which arrived with the 2006 edition, does not validate the water-damage content but the drafting procedure, meaning balance of interest, no favoring of any company or person, and every one of thousands of public comments answered, so “it has a lot of legal weight behind it by the time you get there” [i4RGAC2VwaY @ 00:03:38]. None of these speakers treats voluntary as optional once a claim turns into a lawsuit.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Containment is where the numbers get specific. Balanced Building Biology specs 6 mil fire-retardant poly, zip walls and critical barriers over vents and doors, warns never to demolish or place air movers before the room is sealed, and names the crucial metric: hold -5 pascals of negative pressure, tracked with a manometer, using true HEPA at 99.97% filtration at 0.3 microns, while shop vacs and M-class extractors are “not allowed” [YdUmzaa5wO0 @ 00:02:47]. Andrew Melrose runs the same kit on mold jobs: 6 mil plastic, negative air, high-CFM HEPA scrubbers, a dehumidifier to stop humidity spikes, HEPA vacuums, P100 respirators and decon chambers, and names random chemicals, no containment and humidity spikes as the signature of bad work [iQ4PIKW3sEc @ 00:03:54]. Time's Dark Captains adds the Texas poly spec of 4 to 6 mil for walls and ceilings, 10 mil for floors, fire-retardant with flame spread of 25 or less, and reads the standard as not allowing scrubber mode without negative pressure [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:46:31]. Floyd puts the consumables on the P&L: AFD pre-filters run $8 to $12, secondaries $15 to $20, and the HEPA about $153, with carbon filters for odor, and Floyd warns never to draw negative air from a crawlspace because of furnace backdraft [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:29:36].

Floyd supplies the water-side sizing kit. Drying classes run Class 1 under 5% of surfaces wet, Class 2 at 5 to 40%, Class 3 over 40%, and Class 4 bound water. Refrigerant dehumidification is cubic feet divided by the class factor to get AHAM pints; desiccant is cubic feet times air changes per hour divided by 60 to get CFM. The 2015 air-mover rule is one per room, plus one per 50 to 70 square feet of floor, plus one per 100 to 150 square feet of wall and ceiling above 2 ft, plus one per offset over 18 inches, so a 750 square foot loss lands at 12 to 16 units, and water under 2 ft up the wall runs linear feet divided by 14 [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:42:22]. TheIICRC's 2021 review stresses that those dehumidification formulas only give a minimum, and that Class 4 needs at least an LGR [bcid0F9jRTI @ 01:21:22]. Destry Ransdell at Restoration Technical Institute maps moisture with linear readings every 5 ft and floor readings every 5 by 5 ft, and marks equipment and migration lines on the sketch [XMpZ-8ZsSW8 @ 00:00:00]. The RTI Rover ride-on extractor moves about 1 square foot per 10 seconds: fantastic extraction, but “probably not the greatest” for large areas [XMpZ-8ZsSW8 @ 00:01:22].

Verification and hard measurement close the loop on every job. Nicole Bijlsma strongly recommends one air and one surface sample in every room suspected of elevated fungal particulates, calls spore count “the least important” number next to rank order, notes outdoor Aspergillus and Penicillium typically sit at only 5 to 10%, and flags a field tell: under the 2024 Condition 1, a spore-free air sample suggests a scrubber was running, which voids the test [r1JeD2hb2jM @ 00:14:00]. Coach8 insists the restorer use the same meter throughout a project, since Delmhorst, Tramex, Protimeter, Flir and Trotec readings differ by brand, and that readings be taken at least daily [7gZTdFWoHgs @ 00:37:15]. Floyd adds that an infrared camera “does not say it's wet or dry” and only shows a cooler surface, so always validate with the meter, and that non-penetrating meters cannot read through an air gap, while foil backing and metal throw false readings [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:40:06]. On sequencing, the 2015 S520 rollout splits the roles: remediators may run post-remediation evaluation on their own jobs, IEPs perform post-remediation verification, a non-independent IEP must disclose that in writing, and PRV has to happen before any coating or sealant goes on [6CI2NAsooLw @ 00:12:57].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Stop the source and pick the standard
   - Get the water source stopped, then decide whether the loss is S500 drying work or S520 mold work.
   - Melrose has a plumber stop the source first, works the 24 to roughly 72 hour S500 window, and switches to the S520 once mold exists [Andrew Melrose — iQ4PIKW3sEc @ 00:00:39]. Time's Dark Captains points to S500 8.6: when mold turns up on a water job, stop and contain [Time's Dark Captains — y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:20:45].
2. Seal and pressurize before anyone swings a hammer
   - Build 6 mil fire-retardant poly containment with critical barriers and establish -5 Pa before demolition.
   - Never demolish or place air movers before sealing the room, then hold -5 Pa on a manometer [Balanced Building Biology — YdUmzaa5wO0 @ 00:02:30]. Floyd: decontaminate first rather than bringing in air movers that start blowing contamination around [APPALeadership — cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:23:50].
3. Physically remove, then clean to dust-free
   - Remove porous growth, HEPA vacuum, then damp wipe every surface.
   - Drywall and insulation “got to go,” mold is pulled manually from concrete and wood, then a HEPA vacuum pass is followed by a microfiber damp wipe with dish soap and water to reach dust-free [Andrew Melrose — iQ4PIKW3sEc @ 00:06:03]. Stain removal stays the last cosmetic step, done only after the space is confirmed clean [Phill McGurk — Dl8DpDk97_A @ 00:04:11].
4. Dry, monitor, and verify before sealing
   - Take daily readings with one meter, then run post-remediation evaluation or third-party PRV before any coatings.
   - Readings are taken at least daily, with the same meter used throughout the project [Coach8 — 7gZTdFWoHgs @ 00:35:55]. PRV happens before coatings or sealants, and a non-independent IEP must disclose that in writing [TheIICRC — 6CI2NAsooLw @ 00:12:57].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is over who grades the job. Phill McGurk frames the IEP as the independent referee who performs post-remediation verification so the contractor never grades its own homework, and calls that independence the homeowner's ultimate quality control [Dl8DpDk97_A @ 00:05:15]. Jim Pearson, chairing the S520 consensus body, starts from field reality instead: most jobs have no IEP at all, so the 2024 edition forces the remediator to perform a post-remediation evaluation on every job, clean, dry and odor-free, and pushes IEP verification over to the S530 [JOiWfXdUwjM @ 00:23:10]. The Texas trainer behind Time's Dark Captains reads it tighter, arguing an IEP is needed to tell Condition 2 from Condition 1 and that remediators should not act as their own IEP under R520 page 93 [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:37:12]. Nicole Bijlsma splits the difference by risk tier: the restorer or remediator evaluates recovery levels 0 to 1, while independent PRV covers levels 2 to 4, triggered by more than 10 square meters affected, occupant health concerns, or Category 2 or 3 water [r1JeD2hb2jM @ 00:30:41]. The operator playbook is to decide the recovery level at intake, because that single call determines whether your own sign-off closes the file or a third party has to.

Practitioners also fight over what counts as clean. Balanced Building Biology says the industry obsesses over total counts like under 1,000 spores, when rank order of species matters more, and indoor Aspergillus dominance is a problem even at lower totals [YdUmzaa5wO0 @ 00:01:44]. Bijlsma goes further, calling spore count the least important number and rank order far superior, and recommends one air and one surface sample in every suspect room [r1JeD2hb2jM @ 00:46:29]. Bijlsma anchors that comparison on the outdoor baseline, where Aspergillus and Penicillium typically make up only 5 to 10 percent of the count. Darren Floyd, with more than 30 years in the trade, offers the older playbook: there are no national thresholds, but some industrial hygienists use Penicillium and Aspergillus below about 3000 as a working line [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:51:22]. Mark Cornelius pushes back from the commercial side, warning that cleaning to zero is pricing remediation out of the market because mold is so often uninsured, and telling the industry to stop trying to turn it into rocket science [jFMj8u8OsU4 @ 00:25:17]. The working split is clear: lab-driven operators sell species ranking, while count-driven shops anchor on a number the client and payer can follow, and both camps need the clearance yardstick agreed before the first sample is pulled.

On chemistry, the line between alternative playbook and violation runs through written disclosure. Andrew Melrose teaches a peroxide protocol they call the safest, paired with manually pulling mold from concrete and wood, while drywall and insulation simply have to go [iQ4PIKW3sEc @ 00:05:47]. Floyd gives the classic deviation case: mold was encapsulated instead of the drywall being removed because a computer and phone bank sat on the other side of the wall, and they insist a deviation is not a license to do something wrong but a detour to the same destination [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:09:16]. The 2024 S520 panel now defines fog, mist and knockdown and permits knockdown of particulates, without loosening the remove-rather-than-kill rule [JOiWfXdUwjM @ 00:45:30]. The Texas trainer is the hard-liner, citing S520 section 5.8.1.1 that chemicals are not a substitute for removal and reading the standard to forbid scrubber mode without negative pressure [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:32:28]. The split for an operator is practical, not philosophical. Melrose and Floyd show workable variations when a material or a site constraint forces it, while the Texas reading treats any chemical shortcut as indefensible. Whichever side you run, the variation has to be documented, communicated to affected parties, and authorized in writing before a single sprayer or encapsulant comes off the truck.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive failures start with a job someone called dry. Melrose describes a house that had been dried that still had saturated materials and fresh mold, and warns that three or four days of fans proves nothing; only monitoring proves dryness [iQ4PIKW3sEc @ 00:02:41]. Balanced Building Biology names the concrete version the 2 cm trap: scanning meters miss moisture at the bottom of the slab, and the floor fails about three weeks later, which is why they call proper deep probing absolutely essential [YdUmzaa5wO0 @ 00:06:15]. Bijlsma turns that into a liability move, telling assessors to state in their disclaimer that they are unable to determine whether the concrete is dried beyond 2 cm, and notes Don was paid $5,000 for three hours of lectures on AS 2180/2170 sleeve testing [r1JeD2hb2jM @ 00:41:14]. Floyd adds the instrument traps: an infrared camera only says a surface is cooler and could be evaporation, so validate with a meter, and non-penetrating meters cannot read through an air gap while foil backing and metal throw false readings [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:40:06]. Treat any reading you have not personally validated with a probe or a pin as unverified, and write the limitation into the file before the floor goes down.

The second cluster of failures is the crew spreading the problem it was hired to remove. Floyd warns that decontamination comes first, because rolling in a bunch of air movers before it just blows contamination around, and that a non-HEPA shop vac aerosolizes waterborne pathogens [cPAFOZbMKQE @ 00:23:50]. Balanced Building Biology sets the sequence as a hard rule: never demolish or place air movers before the room is sealed with 6 mil fire-retardant poly, zip walls and critical barriers, and shop vacs and M-class extractors are not allowed [YdUmzaa5wO0 @ 00:02:30]. TheIICRC's S520 2015 briefing rejects misting disinfectants in lieu of source removal and warns that the rejected approach accomplishes a nice diffusion of the mold throughout the property, so it is not recommended at all [6CI2NAsooLw @ 00:12:38]. Bijlsma flags a quieter trap on the testing side: a spore-free air sample suggests a scrubber was running, which voids the test, so a too-perfect clearance result can sink the job rather than close it [r1JeD2hb2jM @ 00:12:14]. Every shortcut in this cluster converts a contained source into structure-wide fallout, so the operator rule is simple: seal, decontaminate, filter through true HEPA, and only then move air or pull clearance samples.

The money traps hit before and after the job. McGurk warns that general insurance almost always excludes mold and tells clients to demand a separate contractor's pollution liability policy, the mark of a true professional, and puts mold-specific insurance on their four-point hiring checklist [Dl8DpDk97_A @ 00:04:27]. Pearson adds that the standard is voluntary, but before a judge or jury a contractor who did not follow it is probably going to lose, and that deviation requires written full disclosure to the client; they relay Dave Dell's view that most firms lack adequate coverage [JOiWfXdUwjM @ 00:14:53]. The Texas trainer attacks the Harvey-era habit of whole-house flood cuts and says claiming compliance without doing the work is a shield made out of cardboard, adding that only the owner or owner's agent can impose limitations, never the adjuster working the claim [y00Yi4bnLx4 @ 00:27:34]. Cornelius supplies the backstory: the mold-is-gold era turned $2,000 water jobs into $40,000 mold jobs, and when bad actors surface the whole industry looks bad [jFMj8u8OsU4 @ 00:03:08]. Coverage, written deviation sign-off and an owner-signed limitations list are the three documents that decide whether a disputed job gets paid, gets reopened, or gets litigated in front of a jury.
