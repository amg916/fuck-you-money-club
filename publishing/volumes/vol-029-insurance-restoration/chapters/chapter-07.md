## Executive Summary

Fire and contents operators converge on one rule: you get paid for what you document. Encircle's Paul Donald, Octagon's Brendy Riley, and bootcamp instructor Chris all tie payment to defensible photos, scans, and narratives built from the first site visit, while FirehouseEducation and Omegasonics treat a digital photo inventory as the floor for every packout. The splits are sharp: ozone versus hydroxyl, Matterport accuracy versus Leica RTC point clouds, Encircle's own 2-5% versus 5-10% revenue-lift claims, and outsourced estimating versus doing it yourself.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator who talks money ties it back to the file. Encircle CEO Paul Donald frames the whole business in one line: restoration contractors "only get paid for the work they document," and without defensible data, AI is just "a creative tool generating a plausible story" [uy09gaqf4AU @ 00:03:46]. Octagon project manager Brendy Riley lived that on a 7,000 sq ft ski-resort retirement home where a stain rag combusted over a weekend; the smoke path stayed invisible until drywall came off, engineers reviewed the estimates, and the team still got paid for the correct scope because they had "the photo documentation to justify it and the knowledge on how to convey that" [WTz841eMFkk @ 00:11:32]. Bootcamp instructor Chris pushes the same logic upstream: set the restoration objective at the start, the way you would a drying plan, because "if you establish the objective at the beginning then it makes it easier to get paid at the end" [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 00:05:10]. On the mitigation side, magicplan sells floor-plan sketches exported straight to Xactimate specifically to "expedite your payment process and ensure full compensation," replacing pen-and-paper sketches with a mobile app that carries photos, moisture readings and signatures in one project [irpHqZ46CG8 @ 00:05:00].

On contents, the FirehouseEducation crew gives the cleanest decision rule: medium to heavy fire, or anything needing ultrasonic work, gets packed out to the warehouse, while light smoke is cleaned in place to avoid expense and hassle [w85_KBcX690 @ 00:00:49]. Even a light job flips to a partial packout when the structure needs paint or carpet, and the deciding factors are degree of damage, need for ultrasonic, intensity of cleaning and structural damage. Omegasonics' Frank Pedu sets the floor for every packout: a digital photo inventory is the minimum because it "protects you from fraudulent claims and misplaced contents," and each item's original condition and final storage location must be recorded [3UmvqUcOFDc @ 00:01:09]. Annissa Coy is blunter: inventory everything on insurance claims [MkCNqNRsk04 @ 00:12:04]. Chris adds the money angle on pre-cleaning: HEPA-vacuuming contents before they ever hit a box raises the save percentage, and since there is no industry labeling standard, Chris's convention is white boxes for clean and brown boxes for dirty [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 03:47:03]. The shared baseline is that contents are not loaded and forgotten; every piece leaves the house photographed, categorized, pre-cleaned where soot is heavy, and assigned to a known storage spot.

The cleaning sequence is where the fire specialists line up hardest. Chris teaches four steps of fire remediation, "Source removal cleaning deodorization and then applying surface sealant," with the effort front-loaded, and warns that most disputes come from attacking odor before contaminants are gone [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 02:10:13]. Brendy Riley's field workflow at Octagon mirrors it: environmental controls first to stop soot and carcinogens resettling, then demo, media blasting depending on char level and the clean-versus-replace call, then HEPA vacuum, chem sponge and damp wipe, odor mitigation throughout, and finally a fire and smoke sealer, where "usually it takes one to two applications to mitigate any lingering odors" [WTz841eMFkk @ 00:04:03]. Travis Chauncey of Abbotts explains why the HEPA step is non-negotiable: smoke particulates run under 10 microns, so a regular shop vac will not capture them [iiCSRcut-WA @ 00:01:33]. Annissa Coy carries that into contents, HEPA-vacuuming before boxing, especially under heavy soot, and unpacking only inside the processing center under a HEPA scrubber [MkCNqNRsk04 @ 00:01:37]. Chris puts it flatly elsewhere: the most effective removal is physical, not hydroxy. The consensus baseline: physical removal first, deodorize second, seal last, on a clean, dry surface as the sealer label itself requires.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Adjuster TV's walkthrough with Kylee Kempton of Titan Restoration in Mesa gives the tightest scan protocol. Water technicians carry a Matterport in every truck, shoot a pre-scan before mitigation and a post-scan after demolition, so the adjuster sees removed versus to-repair work, with Mattertags carrying loss descriptions and quantities [86mEe0yHwiw @ 00:06:43]. The capture rules: place scans 5 to 8 ft apart, hold the camera at about 4 to 4.5 ft for restoration rather than eye level, hide behind it to avoid "no alignment" errors, scan just before and after doorways, close blinds so sunlight does not confuse the infrared, mark mirrors early and often, open doors to 45 degrees, and add 2 to 3 extra scans in tight bathrooms [86mEe0yHwiw @ 00:13:42]. For under-sink losses, open the cabinets and drop the tripod to its lowest height. The magicplan workflow layers moisture data on the sketch: insert a restoration humidity and temperature object, drag it onto the annotated humid zone, and log surface and air humidity, date and photos as custom attributes [irpHqZ46CG8 @ 00:04:10]. On paper, Adjusterpedia's Mark runs a coded scope sheet because photos alone led to mislabeling and confusion over what was actually damaged [4EdiIO_RCX0 @ 00:00:55].

The estimating pipeline is where the minutes get won. In Encircle's demo, the technician dictates a damage description by voice, adds photos, 360s, video, moisture and equipment data, and the platform outputs a narrative, room tasks and IICRC-backed equipment calculations in 5 to 10 minutes [uy09gaqf4AU @ 00:12:32]. In Xactimate, the operator files a vendor data request, matches the claim ID, and sends; AI converts the sketch and maps tasks to line items priced from the price list chosen when the project was created. Joe's live run, a 15x12x8 Category 1 bedroom with carpet saved, pad pulled and a 9x3 hallway of wet hardwood, took about 30 to 60 seconds to describe and produced a priced estimate in under 20 minutes [uy09gaqf4AU @ 00:37:55]. David Herring's field number backs the voice-note method: 998 windows inspected in 3 days versus about 7 the old way, and Herring calls line items produced in the field "a disservice" [uy09gaqf4AU @ 00:22:42]. For firms that would rather hand off the Xactimate build entirely, American Roof Supplements publishes flat pricing: first estimate or supplement free, $50 pre-adjuster estimate, $100 follow-up supplement on a job they estimated and got approved, $150 standalone supplement, no monthly fee or minimum [GK_Was-iK98 @ 00:09:42].

Packout and cleaning carry their own unit economics. FirehouseEducation treats materials as billable, charged hourly or built into the per-box price, with bubble wrap running about "twenty dollars a box" and the instruction "don't skimp"; specialty TV, lamp, dish-pack and glass-pack boxes come out, plates go on end, dish packs bill higher, and wood gets paper-wrapped before shrink wrap in hot areas [rAAR3Zmm80c @ 00:01:53]. That crew credits the method with very little damage over 16 to 17 years. In the processing center, Annissa Coy runs dishes and dippable items through an ultrasonic with a pre-soak because it is faster "and thus profitable," handles dry-method items with HEPA plus a dry sponge or art gum eraser, and deodorizes with hydroxyl inside the drying chamber [MkCNqNRsk04 @ 00:06:42]. Chris quantifies the structure side: verify cleaning with a 1 ft by 1 ft sponge test using horizontal and vertical passes, size air filtration for 6 to 8 air changes per hour then drop to 4, targeting 2,000 particles at 3 microns, with particle counts like 13,000 down to 500 as proof [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 01:36:28]. Heat-calcined drywall cannot be rehydrated, so replace it at $8 to $16 a sheet, which Chris frames as an extra $4 [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 02:31:11].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Odor control is the sharpest split. Annissa Coy at FirehouseEducation bans ozone outright on contents with an "absolutely not," because it bleaches anything damp and takes years off leather. Coy runs hydroxyl inside the drying chamber instead, since it is safe for occupants and works on wet goods [MkCNqNRsk04 @ 00:06:05]. Chris, the umpire-appraiser teaching Encircle's bootcamp, reverses that ranking. On protein fires Chris rates ozone above hydroxy and calls hydroxy three to four times weaker. Chris still holds that the most effective removal is physical, not hydroxy, and warns that whoever over-oxidizes a house owns the result [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 02:59:48]. The lever Chris prefers was used by only 3 to 4 percent of attendees: fresh-air dilution. The reasoning is that bringing fresh air in carries less risk than applying another product [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 03:02:49]. Travis Chauncey at Abbotts Cleanup & Restoration runs a homeowner-grade version. Open doors and windows, put positive air pressure in some areas and negative air machines in the affected ones, and move to demolition only if the odor persists [iiCSRcut-WA @ 00:00:43]. The working read is that contents crews lean toward hydroxyl, while structure-side dispute experts lean toward physical removal plus dilution. None of these operators treats an oxidizer as a substitute for actual cleaning.

The packout call is driven by damage load, not habit. Annissa Coy of FirehouseEducation sends medium to heavy fire jobs, and anything that needs ultrasonic work, to the warehouse. Light smoke gets cleaned in place to avoid the expense and hassle. Coy still runs a partial packout on light jobs when the structure needs paint or carpet work [w85_KBcX690 @ 00:00:49]. Coy's four decision factors are degree of damage, need for ultrasonic, intensity of cleaning, and structural damage. Coy states flatly that structural damage often forces the crew to pack contents up and get them out of the way so it can handle the structure [w85_KBcX690 @ 00:01:56]. Brendy Riley, a project manager with 11 years at Octagon, treats the packout as one line in a PM-run logistics stack. That stack also covers textile pickup, art restoration, and storage pods dropped on site. Riley keeps homeowners physically involved in moving items rather than icing them out [WTz841eMFkk @ 00:04:35]. Chris on the Encircle bootcamp argues that the save rate is decided before the truck is loaded, because HEPA pre-cleaning ahead of packout raises the percentage of items saved [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 03:47:03]. So the real disagreement is where the deep cleaning happens. Both camps HEPA the soot off before anything goes into a box.

The sharpest arguments over documentation tooling are about accuracy and about who structures the data. Kylee Kempton, the Matterport technician at Titan Restoration, pitches scanning as the cure for digging through 700 photos and making return trips to the site. Kempton claims correct scans are far more accurate than photos and that floor plans land within 1 to 2 percent [86mEe0yHwiw @ 00:04:07]. FirstLook Services runs Matterport and Leica RTC side by side and says accuracy is where Matterport falls off a cliff. In that firm's view Matterport is fine for quick-and-dirty floor plans and visuals but unfit for millimeter or centimeter construction documents, and mirrors throw phantom walls into Revit [C_iLoKezwu4 @ 00:01:16]. On the Encircle webinar, David Herring calls line items produced in the field a disservice. Herring argues for narrative damage descriptions instead and says they get about 90 percent more items cleared [uy09gaqf4AU @ 00:23:21]. Mark at Adjusterpedia still swears by a coded paper scope sheet printed on the back of the Xactanalysis claim info. Mark rejects separate roof, interior, and shed sheets as way too much, and insists that anyone skipping a scope sheet is probably not doing as good a job as possible [4EdiIO_RCX0 @ 00:00:19]. The playbooks diverge on capture medium but agree that structured input beats loose photos.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest failures come from letting someone else own the scope. Chris describes a loss of more than $600,000 in which an untrained adjuster changed the scope and the contractor followed it. The contractor ultimately was responsible, so the rule taught is never to take work instructions from the adjuster [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 00:09:57]. The same instructor saw a contractor seal odor into a senior home's roof without offering options, on a roughly $800,000 invoice, and the firm was left holding the bag [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 00:59:47]. Brendy Riley of Octagon answers with scope discipline set early. Build a hard scope with the adjuster upfront, raise new items the moment they appear so there are no surprises, and meet adjusters on site, since there may be several. On a 7,000-square-foot ski-resort home, a stain rag combusted over a weekend. The smoke path stayed invisible until the drywall came off, and engineers pushed back on the estimates. The crew still got paid for the correct scope, which Riley credits to having the photo documentation to justify it and the knowledge to convey it [WTz841eMFkk @ 00:11:32]. Chris's economics check matches that experience: establishing the objective at the beginning makes it easier to get paid at the end [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 00:05:10].

Chemistry mistakes cannot be undone, which is why practitioners treat them as economic traps rather than technique notes. Annissa Coy warns that you cannot ozone an item that is damp or wet, because it bleaches the goods and takes years off leather. Soft goods get no ozone at all [MkCNqNRsk04 @ 00:06:22]. Chris goes further on structures. Over-oxidation cannot be reversed, houses have ended up transferred to insurers because of it, and many cleaning-chemical labels already read "Do not expose to oxidizers" [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 02:59:48]. Protein fires are the other money pit, accounting for about 60 percent of Chris's disputes. The residue is invisible and tacky, it hardens like shellac within 24 to 72 hours, and oxidizers do little against it. Chris's warning is that protein fires are expensive, with no physical damage and simply a ton of cleaning [33iGNdZ6fyE @ 00:36:30]. Coy also forbids homeowner laundry on fire jobs, because it obscures the source of any remaining odor. Textiles go in-house or to a certified vendor rather than Suzy's Wash and Fold [MkCNqNRsk04 @ 00:09:05]. The common thread is that once a residue has been sealed, oxidized, or washed by the wrong hands, the restorer can no longer prove what is causing the lingering smell.

Packout and paperwork failures drain margin quietly. Annissa Coy and Jeremy of Faithful Restoration in Billings name the two classic packout losses: damage in transit, and cleaned items going dusty in storage and needing a second cleaning. Jeremy draws on 15 years at a furniture store plus moving labor. The fix is full shrink-wrap, moving blankets on corners, and taking pieces apart before loading, such as a floor mirror's three screw-in legs, because the pod tilts high once it is loaded [Appti0ctcGA @ 00:00:53]. Coy adds a heat trap: in hot areas, paper-wrap wood before the shrink wrap goes on. Coy also says never to skimp on billable materials, citing bubble wrap at about twenty dollars a box [rAAR3Zmm80c @ 00:03:46]. Frank Pedu at Omegasonics treats a digital photo inventory as the minimum, not the goal, because it protects the company from fraudulent claims and misplaced contents. Each item's original condition and final storage location should be logged [3UmvqUcOFDc @ 00:01:09]. On the estimating side, American Roof Supplements puts the public adjuster bottleneck at estimate number six. The firm treats late-night estimating, delayed follow-up, and turned-away files as signs of a capacity problem, and rejects handoffs that amount to forty photos and "figure it out" [GK_Was-iK98 @ 00:00:15].
