## 1. Ring-fence working cash before buying production capacity  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Insurance restoration fails when an attractive equipment yard hides an empty operating account. Treat the $18,000–$75,000 startup range as cash available for deployment, not permission to purchase that much machinery. The three budgets below reserve $40,000, $95,000, and $190,000 respectively: 53.3%, 54.3%, and 54.3% of total capital. Equipment, vehicles, deposits, training, and initial insurance payments consume the remainder. These are illustrative capital packages, not replacements for the canonical revenue scenarios. The chapter-local reserve-first mobilization play starts with separate operating, tax, and reserve ledger buckets, backed by bank accounts where useful. Give dispatch visibility into available mobilization cash without granting authority to move reserves. Build a thirteen-week cash forecast from signed work, actual payment milestones, payroll dates, rental commitments, and supplier terms. Exclude unsigned supplements, prospective catastrophe volume, and assumed insurer releases from cash available today. A disputed invoice is an asset requiring collection effort, not payroll funding. Review the forecast every Monday and after every material claim dispute. The fallback for an unfunded mobilization is a smaller authorized scope, a lawful customer milestone payment, or a documented referral—not silent borrowing from payroll taxes or another customer's restricted funds.

Distinguish readiness overhead from the full cost of delivering restoration. The $4,000–$20,000 monthly operating range is a planning envelope for the lean fixed stack; job labor, subcontractors, materials, disposal, and surge rentals still enter the complete job-cost ledger. It cannot credibly represent every monthly expense at all three canonical revenue levels. Conservative revenue of $500,000 less $40,000 owner profit implies $460,000 annual total expense, or approximately $38,333 monthly. Base implies $1,305,000 annual expense, or $108,750 monthly; Scaled implies $2,050,000, or approximately $170,833 monthly. Subcontractor and material spending already sits inside those totals. Do not deduct it twice or present insurance claim value as incremental operator revenue. A $25,000 monthly fixed-overhead plan uses $300,000 annually, leaving $1,005,000 within the Base expense envelope for remaining delivery costs. That arithmetic is a constraint, not proof of feasibility. Quote actual wages, payroll burdens, insurance, and production utilization before hiring. The Conservative team remains five full-time staff plus six contractors; the low overhead endpoint does not make those employees free. A phased prelaunch crew is a readiness tactic, not a revised scorecard team.

Make the chapter-local rental-to-owned drying capacity play pass a utilization test. Suppose a dehumidifier costs $3,000 delivered, equivalent rental costs $75 per deployed day, and owned maintenance plus handling adds $10 per deployed day. Simple purchase recovery requires $3,000 divided by $65, approximately 47 deployed days, before storage, repairs, capital cost, and resale value. These are explicit planning assumptions, not vendor quotes or insurer reimbursement rates. Two sporadic losses do not justify a warehouse of units. Start with calibrated meters, essential extraction tools, a small compatible drying fleet, and a reserved rental relationship with Sunbelt Rentals or United Rentals. The fallback is rented surge capacity with written availability, delivery terms, and cleaning expectations. Stress-test the middle capital package separately: keep a $25,000 emergency floor inside its $95,000 reserve, leaving $70,000 deployable. Two months of $25,000 fixed overhead consume $50,000, leaving only $20,000 for unreimbursed job cash. That is the real mobilization limit without additional committed funding. Never call the entire reserve runway while also counting it as equipment capital. Release purchases only when the remaining reserve still covers the forecast's deepest cash trough.

## 2. Configure twelve layers without creating twelve administrative jobs  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Use the twelve-layer matrix as a procurement worksheet, not a claim that twelve paid subscriptions are necessary. Each exact dollar amount is an illustrative budget input for the named tier and billing basis; it is not a verified current vendor quotation. Confirm regional prices, minimum users, annual commitments, taxes, storage limits, and export rights before purchase. The modeled stack totals $597 monthly equivalent: $35 email, $6 identity, $35 accounting, $119 dispatch, $200 estimating, $79 photographs, $18 signatures, $60 voice, $20 credential storage, $15 backup, $10 endpoint protection, and zero incremental dashboard cost. Annual equivalent is $7,164. Annual-billed subscriptions can require cash upfront, so monthly equivalents belong in overhead while actual invoice dates belong in the cash forecast. The lean capital package therefore cannot prepay every modeled subscription and still fund training from its $4,000 allowance. Activate fewer seats, choose monthly billing where available, and defer overlapping products. The core production chain is dispatch, evidence, estimate, invoice, and collection. Buy a paid layer only when it closes a measured failure in that chain or satisfies a specific contractual requirement.

Define one job identifier before configuring integrations. Dispatch creates the identifier; photographs, psychrometric readings, estimates, change orders, rental tickets, subcontractor invoices, and customer receipts inherit it. CompanyCam records scene evidence, while Xactimate holds priced scope; neither substitutes for a controlled drying log. Record affected material, room, instrument identifier, moisture readings, temperature, relative humidity, equipment placement, and visit time using a consistent IICRC-informed procedure appropriate to the loss. Compare affected materials against documented dry standards rather than declaring completion from elapsed days alone. Technicians capture measurements and access limitations; the estimator determines supported quantities and explains exceptions. The chapter-local evidence-to-cash collections play targets preventable rework rather than aggressive policy advocacy. If a $12,000 invoice requires $8,000 direct cash and collection arrives thirty days late, that $8,000 remains tied up for another month; four similar jobs tie up $32,000. The fallback for disputed scope is an itemized factual supplement with photographs, dimensions, equipment logs, and signed work changes. Do not manufacture readings, extend drying time to fill a rental allowance, or represent a customer in coverage negotiations without the required separate authority.

Automate reminders and reconciliation before automating judgment. Dispatch can warn when a daily reading is missing, a rental pickup is overdue, or an invoice has passed its contractual milestone. It should not automatically declare a room dry, accept a contaminated water assignment, or convert every photographed surface into demolition. Restrict technicians to their assigned jobs, require multifactor authentication, and keep carrier portals out of shared browser profiles. A credential vault stores individual access, not a universal adjuster login. Export a complete test job before committing to annual subscriptions: original images, timestamps, readings, estimate versions, signed authorizations, and payment history must remain usable outside the vendor interface. Calculate automation value conservatively. Twenty jobs monthly with fifteen minutes saved each release five hours; at an assumed $35 loaded hourly cost, that is $175 of capacity, not automatically $175 of cash savings. A $250 integration cannot justify itself from that task alone. First use native exports and a controlled spreadsheet; add integrations only when field completeness or collection performance improves measurably. The fallback for an outage is a numbered offline capture pack, followed by same-day reconciliation when service returns.

## 3. Establish entity, insurance, and contracting boundaries before selling  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Form the operating entity through the relevant state filing office, obtain its IRS employer identification number, and open bank accounts under the same legal identity used on contracts and insurance. Confirm contractor licensing, local registration, and any separate mold assessment or remediation requirements for each service territory. An IICRC credential supports professional competence; it does not replace a state license. Match the actual operations described to the insurer with water extraction, demolition, drying, smoke cleaning, and any microbial work you intend to perform. Obtain written explanations of pollution exclusions, microbial limitations, professional services exclusions, subcontractor conditions, commercial auto coverage, equipment coverage, and workers' compensation obligations. A certificate summarizes coverage; it does not amend an exclusion or establish additional-insured status by itself. Require relevant endorsements where the contract demands them. Keep customer money, tax liabilities, and owner distributions distinguishable in the ledger. Verify any state trust-fund rules before treating a deposit as unrestricted cash. Confirm changed supplier bank details through a known telephone number, not the number in the change email. One fraudulent $15,000 transfer would consume 37.5% of the lean package's $40,000 working reserve.

Build collections around construction facts and the customer's payment obligation, not a promise of insurer reimbursement. The authorization identifies the property, permitted emergency work, rate basis, equipment charges, access, payment milestones, and change-order method. Explain who owes payment when coverage is disputed. Texas Insurance Code §4102.051 requires licensing for public adjusting, and §4102.163 restricts acting as a public adjuster for property on which the person provides contracting services. Keep estimating your own work distinct from representing the insured in settlement negotiations; other states draw their own boundaries. Texas Insurance Code §707.002 requires payment of applicable property-insurance deductibles, with related contractor restrictions in Business & Commerce Code §27.02. Do not advertise deductible waivers or disguise them as rebates. Separately, screen pre-1978 housing and covered child-occupied facilities for EPA renovation requirements before disturbing painted surfaces; evaluate applicable exceptions rather than assuming emergency work erases every duty. The fallback for work outside licensing, training, or insurance scope is a documented referral to a qualified provider. A signed customer request cannot cure an unlicensed activity, an excluded exposure, or an unsafe production method.

The supplied mapped play, gsa_municipal_set_aside_facility_ghost_brokering, means disclosed coordination between eligible facility-service primes and qualified subcontractors, not a shell prime collecting a spread. For a hypothetical $100,000 federal services award, assume $40,000 goes to a nonsimilarly situated qualified subcontractor and $60,000 remains for genuine prime performance, administration, and margin. A documented $3,000 coordination fee must fit within that economics, reflect real services, and satisfy solicitation, disclosure, and compensation rules. It is not another $100,000 of restoration revenue for the coordinator. Where applicable, 13 CFR §125.6 limits payments to nonsimilarly situated subcontractors on covered service contracts to 50% of the relevant government-paid amount; exclusions and similarly situated entities require careful classification. Construction contracts have different thresholds. Check FAR 52.219-14 when included and FAR 52.215-23 where applicable to excessive pass-through charges. Municipal eligibility and subcontracting rules come from the actual local solicitation, not automatically from federal clauses. Document capability, supervision, licenses, pricing, and performance responsibility before bidding. Exclude opaque pass-throughs and false eligibility claims. The explicit fallback is to withdraw noncompliant bids and operate as a disclosed subcontractor on unrestricted work.

## 4. Prove readiness with a funded dry run before taking emergency calls  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

The chapter-local staged crew activation play turns staffing into a capacity decision rather than a ceremonial opening date. Use the canonical five full-time staff and six contractors for Conservative and Base planning, and six full-time staff plus seven contractors for Scaled planning. Contractor count means an available qualified network, not evidence that thirteen people fit a five-person payroll. Confirm worker classification under applicable tests rather than issuing a contractor form to someone managed as an employee. Before activating another deployment lane, model its direct cash requirement. An illustrative two-person crew working forty hours each at $35 loaded hourly cost consumes $2,800 weekly before vehicles, materials, rentals, or supervision. Four weeks consumes $11,200; this is a subset of operating costs, not an addition to an already fully burdened payroll forecast. Maintain documented on-call arrangements and realistic response promises. The fallback when demand exceeds funded capacity is a prequalified referral or subcontracted scope with clear supervision and payment terms. Never dispatch a tired crew to an electrical, structural, or contaminated-water hazard simply because the answering service promised immediate entry.

Run one simulated loss through the complete production system before publishing emergency response claims. The exercise begins with a caller reporting water intrusion and ends with a reconciled mock receipt. Verify that intake captures location, caller authority, access restrictions, apparent hazards, and the source-control status without promising coverage. The crew records initial conditions, safe access decisions, equipment serials, room dimensions, and moisture observations. The estimator produces a versioned scope, and a second person checks quantities against field evidence. Accounting then links the authorization, vendor charges, invoice, and receipt to the same job identifier. Test equipment pickup because forgotten rentals silently destroy contribution. A five-day avoidable rental at $75 daily costs $375; across eight jobs it consumes $3,000, equal to the illustrative dehumidifier purchase price. Test respiratory-protection readiness wherever required: under 29 CFR §1926.103, construction respiratory protection follows §1910.134 requirements, including applicable medical evaluation, fit testing, training, and program administration. PPE purchased but never fitted is not operational capacity. Record every failed handoff with an owner and a correction date before assigning real work.

Measure readiness through evidence, not a completed shopping list. The minimum dashboard shows unrestricted cash, thirteen-week low cash point, authorized backlog, deployed equipment days, missing daily readings, unbilled completed work, disputed receivables, and collection age by customer. Separate invoice age from insurer correspondence age because the contract determines who owes payment. Use a weekly owner review to stop new unfunded mobilizations when cash crosses the agreed floor. Do not use a blanket credit line as evidence of liquidity unless availability, covenants, draw conditions, and repayment terms are confirmed. A first deal may arrive in one to six months, so a two-month reserve plan needs a documented response to a six-month sales cycle. That response can be reduced prelaunch overhead, staged hiring, unrestricted service work within competence, or additional committed capital. It cannot be fictional revenue. Finally, tie the operating review back to the scorecard: $40,000, $195,000, and $450,000 remain the annual owner-profit targets at the stated volumes. Startup purchases, working-capital timing, debt principal, and owner withdrawals affect cash differently from recognized operating expense; reconcile those differences instead of equating bank balance with profit.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Lock the capital and cash model
   - Select the capital package and segregate its working-cash reserve.
   - Replace every modeled price with a written quote, schedule actual payment dates, and retain a protected emergency floor in the forecast.
2. Configure only the critical production chain
   - Connect dispatch, evidence capture, estimating, invoicing, and collections.
   - Use one job identifier, individual access, tested exports, and documented offline procedures before adding optional integrations.
3. Run and inspect the simulated loss
   - Complete intake through equipment pickup and receipt reconciliation.
   - Assign independent reviewers to cash, safety, scope, and records; correct failed handoffs before accepting production exposure.
4. Release a funded, bounded first assignment
   - Dispatch only within documented authority, competence, and cash capacity.
   - Review daily evidence and commitments, then reconcile actual job costs and collection timing before expanding the next deployment.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four verifications before the first production dispatch**
> 
> A passing result requires retained evidence and a named owner; a verbal assurance does not release spending or field work.
> - Verify that at least 50% of startup capital remains allocated to working cash and that the first job fits the thirteen-week forecast without breaching the emergency floor.
> - Verify entity identity, territory-specific licensing, actual policy coverage, and subcontractor qualifications against the precise work being sold.
> - Verify a complete test job containing authorization, room-level evidence, drying records, supported estimate, invoice, payment record, and usable exports.
> - Verify task-specific safety readiness, functioning equipment, rental backup, on-call staffing, and a referral path for work outside competence or funded capacity.
