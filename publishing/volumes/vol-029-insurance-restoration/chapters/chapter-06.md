## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The restoration operators agree on the first fact of this market: the carrier adjuster is no longer the gatekeeper. Jon Schoen puts it flatly: 'very few adjusters now work for the insurance companies and most of their work goes through third- party administrators.' He traces the shift to TPAs offering to run claims for free while carriers cut adjuster headcount [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:04:23]. On The DYOJO panel, the host put the TPA share at upwards of 80% of claims. He quoted growth from 1.21B to 271B across 2010 to 2020 [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:19:10]. Schoen stresses that a program is not one door but dozens. Contractor Connection alone holds 30 to 40 carriers, including USAA, Nationwide, State Farm and Allstate, and a contractor must win approval from each carrier inside each program [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:01:00]. Sylvia Browy draws the same line from the adjuster side. She describes Sedgwick as a TPA that handles claims for others and sells no policies, in contrast to State Farm as a direct carrier [u947iwIhI7A @ 00:00:41]. The operating consequence is simple. Anyone running mitigation or repairs is negotiating with an administrator whose client is the carrier. Every approval, rotation slot and price list is set at that administrative layer, not at the homeowner's kitchen table.

Every restoration voice agrees that the volume carries a toll. Schoen states the TPA will 'charge the contractor 5 10%' to do the work. On top of that sits a preset, discounted price list with line items removed: 'their price list is is preset you can't change it.' Outside programs, he marks the standard list up 20% [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:07:43]. Ty Penzone, a Washington repair contractor with 20-plus years in the trade, says the same thing in fewer words: 'the line items are what the line items are' [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:16:04]. Brian Close of American Restoration runs recon only and stays off programs. He frames program work as guaranteed work that is 'not always guaranteed to be profitable,' the fallback an owner reaches for before bankruptcy [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:02:58]. Schoen adds the sharpest edge. Even inside a program, 'you're still not guaranteed to get paid,' and a homeowner can walk off with the money [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:04:45]. The baseline prerequisite is margin discipline. A shop entering programs must price its overhead against a 5 to 10 percent fee, a frozen list with no markup, and collection risk the program does not absorb. Operators who cannot run lean on those terms are buying volume with their own balance sheet.

The third consensus point is documentation. It comes from the mitigation side as hard as from the repair side. David Smith, an Oregon mitigation owner with 11 years in, says reviewer pushback means you lack documentation, and that he never lost a dispute he had documented [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:37:35]. His rule is 'documentation, documentation, documentation, regardless of where your work is coming from.' That holds whether the end user is a carrier, a reviewer or a homeowner, because you will have to explain the reason for your repair process [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:48:01]. Schoen gets specific about what program reviewers want to see: moisture readings must show specific humidity dropping [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:08:21]. Ty Penzone treats the relationship as a partnership rather than a vendor transaction, and the panel credits TPAs with partnering with contractors straight through COVID [onSDIprTeAU @ 01:03:47]. The prerequisites the operators agree on stack up in order. First comes a local foundation before chasing programs. Next is a willingness to learn both the program's rules and each carrier's rules, plus admin capacity for duplicate uploads and estimating. Last is the habit of logging every reading and photo as if the file will be argued.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Getting on a list is a sales campaign, not an application. Schoen's playbook starts with applying to every program, expecting to pay a fee, and accepting there is no guarantee of placement. The opening comes when an incumbent underperforms in a territory and the 'switch flips' [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:10:19]. The next move is relationship work. Get the regional manager's name and number, introduce yourself, and invite them to walk your facility and inspect your equipment. In his words, it is 'very important to have the regional manager's name and number so you can introduce yourself' [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:11:42]. He also works the field side by having adjusters and agents push for the company. That push lands hardest when claim volume is high and the program needs capacity [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:12:01]. David Smith's addition is commitment. Once inside, learn both the program requirements and the carrier requirements, because both rule sets apply to the same file [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:12:23]. The sequence matters. Applications sit dormant until a slot opens. The regional manager decides who fills that slot, and adjuster and agent advocacy tips the decision. Whether a contractor stays on the list once the switch flips depends on mastering two rulebooks.

Inside the program, the drying math is dictated rather than negotiated. Schoen describes the 'micia' report, timestamped day by day, which sets dehumidifier and air-mover counts that can fall below S500. If the room calls for a small dehu, you are paid for a small even if you placed a large [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:06:02]. David Smith sees contractors overshoot in the other direction. They set '45 D HUs and 75 fans in two rooms' and then do not get paid for it [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:44:40]. The paperwork load is a fixed cost to plan for. Documentation must be uploaded twice, once to your own system and once to the program, though some CRMs auto-upload [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:05:23]. Xactimate is mandatory and a second software platform may be required. Schoen tells owners to either train admin staff or sub the paperwork out [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:06:42]. The practical read is direct. Set equipment to match what the program's report will pay. Log readings daily to show specific humidity dropping, and keep estimating in Xactimate from the first visit forward. Shops that set equipment by feel end up arguing with a reviewer over gear they already paid to haul in, run and pull back out.

The economics reward speed and volume rather than per-job margin. Schoen describes somewhat regular job flow through rotation, scored on arrival speed and direct customer reviews. He is blunt that you are 'going to pay 5 10% a fee to be on the program' [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:08:41]. He adds that mitigation pays well while construction is harder [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:09:17]. The program wars show what TPAs actually sell to carriers. Alacrity won Nationwide by promising $5M a year in savings, with no roughly $100 setup fee and no $150 service-call fee. Contractor Connection took the account back with a 3-day drying cap it pitched as $20M in savings [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:03:50]. Each of those savings is a line the contractor no longer bills. Ty Penzone explains how repair shops still make the channel work: subs accept lower rates in exchange for year-round volume [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:55:23]. Put the numbers together and the model is clear. Arrival speed and reviews win the rotation, and mitigation carries the margin while construction fights the list. Every dollar a TPA promises a carrier comes out of fees and drying days the contractor used to control. The model only works when crews and subs stay busy enough to spread the fee across many files.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Program work versus going direct is the sharpest split on the restoration side. Jon Schoen sits on Contractor Connection, Alacrity, and Sedgwick and still frames the deal as a toll: the TPA charges the contractor 5 to 10% to do the work, the price list is preset and discounted, and outside the programs Schoen marks the standard list up 20% instead [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:02:35]. Brian Close of American Restoration runs recon only and stays off the programs, calling program volume guaranteed work that is 'not always guaranteed to be profitable' and the fallback a shop reaches for right before bankruptcy [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:02:58]. David Smith, eleven years into Oregon mitigation, pushes back hard: a program can hand you a $250,000 fire you never chased, and the payoff only comes if you commit and learn both the program rules and each carrier's requirements [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:11:17]. Ty Penzone, twenty-plus years in Washington repairs, reports that going direct demanded heavy marketing spend, so the real choice is sweat equity plus some financial outlay, or more financial outlay and less sweat [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:33:09]. David's own sequencing is the practical middle path: build a local direct foundation first, then run program and direct channels side by side.

The second fault line is whether a carrier's preferred vendor is a partner or a liability. Mike of The Claim Squad Public Adjusters calls preferred vendors beginners grinding volume on thin margins who get dropped if they treat policyholders fairly, and tells homeowners to answer the intake question 'Do you need water mitigation?' by declining and naming their own vendor [h_W9kUbadMM @ 00:03:07]. On-Site Adjusting goes further, warning that the insurer's contractor removes and dumps evidence before coverage is even decided, so an attorney, public adjuster, or engineer should be on site within days [oEc6cQdcK6I @ 00:00:32]. The DYOJO panel tells the opposite story from inside the network: when Ty Penzone's own house burned in 2008, a network vendor did the repair [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:34:09]. Chris Zimmerman adds a contractor's warning about the public adjuster route itself: once a letter of representation lands, the insurer stops talking to the contractor, the homeowner risks getting ghosted, cancelling the letter is difficult, and the result may cost more and turn out worse [esg8I0BJFLM @ 00:05:18]. Zimmerman also stresses that bringing in a public adjuster is the homeowner's decision and belongs on the table only once a claim has stalled.

The third split is how much leverage anyone has against the administrator's paper. On the repair side, one DYOJO voice shrugs that 'the line items are what the line items are' [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:16:04], while David Smith insists reviewer pushback only means the file lacks documentation and reports never losing a documented dispute [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:37:35]. The benefits-plan practitioners fight the same war over contracts. The independent-TPA voice on Chris Hamilton's channel argues that shifting administrators 'shouldn't be at all that difficult' and that brokers who renew a bundled solution for 20-plus years are hitting the easy button [LEzohuG5ykg @ 00:02:28]. Claire Brockbank of 32BJ, running about 200,000 lives, paints a harder picture: bad contracts block program exits, force upside-down claims, and cede data ownership, and even after a disciplined RFP a buyer should expect to win roughly a third of the asks [pNIYu-t990E @ 00:31:59]. The 32BJ alternative playbook is to send the RFP with the buyer's own contract, demand a redline in every response, plant parallel 'Easter egg' questions to catch sales and legal contradictions, and play Aetna, United, and Anthem against each other whenever one calls an ask 'not industry practice.' Both camps land on the same lever: owning the data and the documentation.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap is placing equipment the program will not pay for. Jon Schoen explains that the micia report is timestamped day by day and dictates dehumidifier and air-mover counts, possibly below S500, so if the room calls for a small dehu you are paid for a small even when you placed a large [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:06:02]. The caps come straight from TPA sales pitches to carriers: Alacrity won Nationwide by promising $5M a year in savings by dropping the roughly $100 setup fee and $150 service-call fee, and Contractor Connection won the account back with a three-day drying cap claiming $20M in savings [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:03:50]. David Smith sees contractors set '45 D HUs and 75 fans in two rooms' and then get nothing for it [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:44:40]. Schoen's readings rule closes the loop: the logs must show specific humidity dropping day over day [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:08:21]. Even when the file is clean, Schoen warns payment is still not guaranteed, because a homeowner can walk away with the money. The economics check is simple: run the job to the program's equipment math and drying window, not to your own habits, and log readings that prove every day billed.

The second trap is building a company whose survival depends on someone else's dispatch queue. Ty Penzone's flooring company ran 125 homes a year from 2002 to 2006 while charging retail clients 1.5 times, then fell to one job in a month in 2008 [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:56:46]. One DYOJO panelist puts it bluntly: 'I had a business that its entire solvency was built around doing 150 jobs a year plus' [onSDIprTeAU @ 00:59:09], which is why Ty argues for running diversified models to survive a 'model interrupt.' Back-office load is the quieter drain. Jon Schoen reports that documentation must be uploaded twice unless a CRM auto-uploads, that Xactimate is mandatory and a second software may be required, and that owners should plan to train admin staff or sub the paperwork out [-0hAYhkhJaw @ 00:06:42]. Joe Boyle of ClearFile sees the same scheduling trap from the administrator's own licensing desk: approvals take one week to three months, renewals cluster January through March, and financials must be current within 30 to 60 days, so step one is 'a very modest timeline' [kR0zavFV0HU @ 00:02:38]. Underestimating paperwork time, on either side of the TPA relationship, quietly kills margin and deadlines.

The third trap is signing or working under paper nobody read. On Relentless Health Value, 32BJ's Claire Brockbank and host Stacey Richter peg about 95% of employers as deferring to the TPA's paper and losing rights in fine print like a 'footnote on page 78,' because 'very few of us have contracting expertise, so we accept the language' [pNIYu-t990E @ 00:06:09]. The cost was concrete: upside-down claims ran 32BJ about $10M in one year, with a $100 bill paid at $200, and the typical failure has sales overpromising, lawyers retracting, and contracting still open a year into implementation [pNIYu-t990E @ 00:04:19]. Their rule after signing is 'You have to police this.' On the claims side, Leah Small of Chisholm Chisholm & Kilpatrick reports that Sedgwick recommends approval or denial without paying benefits, often aims to minimize payouts, and leans on file reviews instead of exams so treating physicians get dismissed [lnhw4pYCOFU @ 00:01:29]. The fatal mistake is a thin appeal: in most cases no new evidence is allowed after the appeal closes, so every denial basis has to be rebutted with vocational reports, symptom journals, and physician reports up front [lnhw4pYCOFU @ 00:02:54]. Whether the counterparty is a plan buyer or a claimant, losing the record early is losing the fight.
