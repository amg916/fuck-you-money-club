## 1. Mine five local demand lanes before buying emergency leads  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with five mapped plays, each carrying a different buying trigger and operational burden: plumber-led water mitigation, multifamily response agreements, fire stabilization referrals, properly licensed mold remediation, and storm-envelope restoration. These are sourcing lanes, not five departments. For the canonical 5 FT + 6 C pod, assign one owner-estimator, one dispatcher-documentation coordinator, one field supervisor, and two technicians; prequalify six contractors across the trades actually offered. Contractors are scheduled capacity, not six people automatically available overnight. Begin within a drive-time polygon that preserves monitoring visits, equipment retrieval, and a second emergency response. High-margin opportunities usually hide in avoidable friction: a plumber has stopped the leak but cannot dry concealed assemblies, or a property manager has recurring losses but no vendor who closes documentation promptly. The opportunity is the service gap, not the customer's distress or the size of their policy. Prioritize safe, accessible losses with a known decision-maker, an isolated source, and a realistic payment path. Reject the assumption that dramatic damage implies attractive economics. A modest water loss near the warehouse can outperform a distant fire project with uncertain access, specialist dependencies, and months of disputed scope. Record these constraints before building any prospect list.

Build the signal feed from county assessor ownership records, municipal permit portals, public fire incident releases, NOAA Storm Events records, and National Weather Service alerts. Use assessor data to identify the owner or management company, then verify current authority directly; tax ownership alone does not identify who can sign a work order. Permit applications for roof repair, demolition, or major plumbing can reveal active property work, but they do not establish an insured loss or an invitation to solicit residents. Historical NOAA data guides territory selection; current weather alerts only indicate where field verification may become useful. Public fire releases support outreach to established property contacts after incident command permits access, never entry into an active scene. In Airtable or HubSpot, store source URL, observation date, property address, signal category, contact basis, assigned owner, and next permitted action. Deduplicate by normalized address and property organization before assigning outreach. Refresh active signals daily and archive stale ones when the underlying need disappears. Do not scrape restricted claim portals, purchase leaked policyholder files, or treat occupant names in incident records as a ready-made sales list. Ask existing plumbers and managers to introduce the service directly, sharing occupant details only with appropriate permission.

Convert each signal into a narrow offer that matches the play. For plumbers, offer documented extraction and drying after source control, with a clear handoff rather than a kickback-dependent arrangement. For multifamily managers, offer a site readiness walk, shutoff map, after-hours contact tree, and written response pricing; an unsigned vendor introduction is not a deal. For fire stabilization, offer permitted board-up and weather protection only after release and hazard review, using qualified specialists when structural or electrical conditions demand them. For mold, establish the local licensing, assessment, remediation, and independence requirements before describing a service; Florida Statutes section 468.8419 restricts conflicting assessment and remediation activity. For storms, sell verified temporary protection and repair scopes, not speculative full replacements based on weather maps. Build inbound pages for these five services with genuine service-area coverage, actual operating hours, and an intake number the pod can answer. A Google Business Profile and documented local case studies can reinforce trust without invented offices or coverage claims. Ask customers for permission before publishing property photographs, and remove personal documents and claim identifiers. Measure inquiries by service lane and source, then keep only the channels producing work the existing crew can execute.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Win referrals by removing the partner's operational headache**
> 
> Make handoffs easier while keeping technical judgment, customer choice, and billing evidence intact.
> - Give plumbers a three-field handoff: source isolated, customer introduction authorized, and access contact confirmed; gather technical detail directly during intake.
> - Photograph equipment serial numbers beside room identifiers at installation and removal so equipment-day disputes can be traced to the actual deployment.
> - Send property managers a concise daily update covering access, drying trend, next visit, and decisions needed rather than an unexplained photo dump.
> - Ask every referral partner which previous vendor failure caused the most friction, then demonstrate the specific corrective workflow before requesting another introduction.

## 2. Qualify authority, scope, and collectible economics before dispatch  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Use a 100-point qualification scorecard with five weighted dimensions: authority and access, 25 points; technical fit and source control, 25; payment clarity, 20; dispatch fit, 15; and evidence readiness, 15. Score each dimension at zero, half, or full weight against recorded facts rather than intuition. Authority earns full points only when the signer and access contact are verified; payment clarity requires a named contractual payer and written terms. A claim number does not establish coverage, available proceeds, or customer creditworthiness. Technical fit requires a controllable source and personnel qualified for the actual hazards. Dispatch fit measures travel, monitoring load, equipment inventory, and contractor confirmation. Evidence readiness measures whether baseline readings, photographs, and scope facts can be collected lawfully and safely. Treat 80 points as a scheduling priority and 60–79 as a request for missing facts; below 60, decline or refer to a better-equipped provider. These are internal operating thresholds, not observed market conversion statistics. Regardless of score, unresolved structural danger, absent authorization, prohibited licensing activity, or a promised deductible waiver stops the affected work. A high score never cancels a mandatory restriction. Record the reason for every decline so marketing learns which signals create unusable demand.

Price the job from physical quantities and execution requirements before discussing what an insurer may reimburse. Capture affected rooms, material layers, water source history, elapsed time, access restrictions, electrical conditions, and possible lead or asbestos disturbance. A moisture meter reading by itself does not establish water category, contamination, or demolition necessity. Use IICRC S500-informed procedures for water work and the applicable mold framework for remediation, recognizing that standards do not replace licensing or site-specific judgment. The field supervisor owns a consistent psychrometric record: indoor and outdoor temperature, relative humidity, material moisture, equipment placement, and drying goals tied to appropriate references. Use CompanyCam or Encircle for organized evidence and Xactimate for a reproducible estimate, checking quantities, local pricing assumptions, equipment duration, and line-item applicability. Prepare supplements as explanations of additional contractor work, with photographs and measurements supporting each change. Do not convert a supplement into negotiation of policy rights. Texas Insurance Code section 4102.163 prohibits a contractor from acting as a public adjuster on property where they provide contracting services, even if otherwise licensed. Keep customer communications focused on observed damage, proposed work, price, and payment terms, referring coverage disputes to the customer's insurer or an appropriately authorized professional.

Separate job contribution from annual owner profit when deciding which inquiries deserve scarce crew time. As an illustrative screening case, a $12,000 restoration scope with $3,200 subcontract cost, $1,800 materials, $2,400 allocated field labor, and $600 equipment and disposal cost leaves $4,000 before remaining overhead, collection losses, and taxes. That 33.3% contribution does not replace the canonical annual net margins. Conservative remains $500,000 gross billings at 8%, producing $40,000 owner profit; Base remains $1,500,000 at 13.0%, producing $195,000; Scaled remains $2,500,000 at 18%, producing $450,000. These revenues include subcontractor and material charges, rather than counting the same costs again as separate revenue. Claim limits, property values, and insurer reserves are not operator revenue. Use the $75,000–$350,000 startup range and $10,000–$45,000 monthly operating-expense range as planning boundaries, then build a weekly cash forecast from the actual pod. Check deposits and progress billing against applicable state contract rules before offering them. Describe deductible responsibility accurately; Texas Insurance Code section 707.002 prohibits specified deductible-waiver arrangements. If a customer asks whether insurance will pay everything, answer with the documented scope and contractual payment terms, not a coverage promise. Decline profitable-looking jobs whose procurement and payroll timing exceed available working capital.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four checks before accepting the first live loss**
> 
> Release a dispatch slot only when the work is authorized, executable, documented, and funded on lawful terms.
> - Verify the contracting entity, authorized signer, property access, cancellation requirements, and any required contractor or remediation license.
> - Confirm source control, incident release where relevant, utility hazards, and the need for lead, asbestos, structural, or other qualified specialists.
> - Confirm written scope, contractual payer, permitted deposit terms, deductible responsibility, and a cash forecast that supports payroll before collection.
> - Reserve technicians, monitoring visits, available equipment, and confirmed contractors; reject response promises that depend on uncommitted capacity.

## 3. Run a measured outreach cadence that produces paid work  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Reserve a weekly prospecting block for the owner-estimator and a shorter daily list-maintenance block for the coordinator; neither activity should interrupt urgent field safety decisions. Start with 120 researched partner or property-management accounts across the five plays, emphasizing nearby plumbers and multifamily operators before specialized fire or mold work. These are business accounts, not 120 disaster victims. Within the first month, aim to validate a decision-maker and contact route for 90 accounts, hold substantive conversations with 30, secure 12 readiness visits or referral introductions, and produce eight qualified live opportunities. If six receive authorized written scopes and three sign paid work orders, conversion is 2.5% from raw accounts and 50% from scoped opportunities. Those figures are planning hypotheses, not industry averages or promised results. A readiness agreement without a paid incident does not count among the first three wins. Track accounts and incidents separately because one manager can produce multiple losses while one storm can generate duplicate inquiries for the same property. Review the cohort weekly using first-touch dates and actual stage transitions. If conversations are scarce, fix targeting or relevance; if scopes fail to close, investigate authority, price clarity, response timing, and customer trust before increasing volume.

Use a restrained 15-business-day sequence for researched business contacts: a personalized email on day one, a manual call to the published business number on day three, a useful checklist on day seven, and a final follow-up on day fifteen. Stop when the recipient declines, requests removal, or explains that the service is irrelevant. Identify the sender accurately, use truthful subjects, include a valid postal address and working opt-out mechanism, and honor commercial-email obligations under 15 U.S.C. section 7704. Do not assume that a publicly visible mobile number permits automated calls or texts; evaluate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act and applicable state restrictions before using those channels. For partner outreach, say: We handle documented drying after your repair, keep your customer updated, and return plumbing questions to your team. Who owns your after-hours restoration handoffs? For managers, ask which properties lack shutoff maps or written after-hours response instructions. Offer a short readiness walk, not an unsolicited coverage review. Inbound calls receive immediate triage when staffed; outside staffed hours, describe the actual callback process rather than claiming continuous dispatch. Send the customer a written recap of access, requested service, known hazards, and next contact so an urgent conversation becomes an auditable handoff.

Handle objections by identifying the decision underneath them. We already have a vendor means ask where overflow or documentation failures occur, then offer a backup role with no exclusivity demand. Your price is higher means compare quantities, monitoring visits, disposal, specialist work, and exclusions line by line; do not inflate a later supplement to recover an artificially cheap opening quote. The insurer has not approved it means distinguish optional work from genuinely urgent protective work and present a lawful authorization path without predicting reimbursement. Can you absorb my deductible receives a clear explanation of lawful pricing and customer obligations, with no disguised gift card or false invoice. Our adjuster wants fewer drying days means supply actual moisture trends, equipment logs, and the technical rationale, correcting unsupported charges when the evidence does not support them. After the first three paid authorizations, extend the cohort through months two to six using referral requests from completed jobs and verified partner feedback. An illustrative 240-account cumulative cohort converting 5% to qualified incidents creates 12 incidents; eight scoped incidents closing at 75% produce six signed jobs, not twelve. Reach ten only when observed conversion and delivery capacity support additional volume. Keep purchased-lead experiments small until attribution and collection quality are visible.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Build and verify the first territory cohort
   - Research 120 business accounts and assign each to one of the five mapped plays.
   - Store source, observation date, territory fit, contact basis, and decision-maker; remove duplicates before outreach.
2. Run the restrained business-contact sequence
   - Use the day-one email, day-three call, day-seven resource, and day-fifteen closeout.
   - Honor removal requests immediately and record conversations separately from sent messages; use lawful contact methods.
3. Qualify each incident before estimating
   - Complete the weighted scorecard and the four pre-flight checks before reserving field capacity.
   - Identify signer, access, source control, hazards, payment responsibility, and evidence needs; mandatory restrictions override scores.
4. Close, execute, and reconcile the first cohort
   - Count signed paid work orders, then track completion, collections, and realized job contribution.
   - Ask for an authorized referral after a successful handoff and update channel spending from collected results.

## 4. Convert the first wins into documented cash and repeatable referrals  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Treat the first signed work order as the beginning of conversion, not its end. The dispatcher creates one job identifier linking the contract, estimate version, photographs, drying records, subcontract orders, customer communications, and invoices. The field supervisor records baseline conditions before disturbance, labels rooms consistently, and assigns daily documentation ownership. Automate reminders and photo routing, not technical conclusions. A missing moisture reading cannot be reconstructed from a calendar entry, and equipment left onsite does not automatically justify every billed day. Use job-cost tracking to compare committed labor, purchased materials, subcontract changes, and equipment against the authorized scope before the next expenditure. When concealed damage changes the work, explain the finding and obtain the required written authorization rather than silently expanding demolition. Screen renovation activities for applicable EPA lead requirements under 40 CFR part 745, and evaluate OSHA asbestos obligations under 29 CFR 1926.1101 before disturbing suspect material. An emergency does not erase worker-protection duties. For storm work, qualified personnel must address fall hazards before tarping or roof access. Keep customer updates short and factual: completed work, measured conditions, next action, access requirements, and pending decisions. This reduces repeated calls while preserving an accurate record of what the contractor actually did.

Build collections around the customer contract and substantiated work, not around a promise to secure an insurance settlement. Issue invoices at lawful contractual milestones, reconcile deposits, and separate authorized changes from disputed items. Where a check includes a lender or other payee, ask the customer to confirm endorsement and release procedures early; never assume a contractor can bypass those controls. For a carrier's scope question, provide the estimate, photographs, readings, dates, and quantity calculations. For a coverage or policy-rights dispute, stay within the contractor role and direct the customer to the appropriate authorized party. Track days from service to invoice separately from days from invoice to cash so administrative delay does not masquerade as insurer delay. Illustratively, a 30-day collection delay on $60,000 of completed monthly billings can leave roughly $60,000 more cash tied up if the same volume continues and other terms stay constant. The cash effect depends on deposits, payables, and actual costs; it is not another revenue stream or a change to the scorecard. Review receivables every week with named follow-up owners, documented disputes, and promised payment dates. Never manufacture liens, threaten remedies unavailable under local law, or withhold truthful completion records to pressure payment.

At each monthly review, rank the five plays by collected contribution, crew hours, travel burden, dispute frequency, and repeat introductions, not by signed estimate totals alone. The canonical Conservative and Base cases both use 5 FT + 6 C; reaching Base therefore requires better throughput, scope mix, utilization, or realized pricing rather than pretending labor is unlimited. Scaled uses 6 FT + 7 C, totaling 13 people, and its $450,000 annual owner profit requires the stated 18% margin on $2,500,000 gross billings. Do not annualize the first three emergency jobs into that result. Track leading constraints instead: technician hours already committed, next-day monitoring slots, equipment ready for deployment, contractor acceptance, and cash due before the next payroll. Pause a channel when it fills the calendar with distant, poorly authorized, or heavily disputed work. Expand the strongest lane only after completed jobs show stable documentation and collectible economics. Ask satisfied customers and partners for specific introductions, without exposing loss details or paying prohibited referral compensation. By month six, the useful outcome is three to ten paid, well-documented wins and a repeatable source of suitable work, not a database of distressed properties. Preserve the ability to decline a bad loss quickly; that discipline protects the entire pod.
