## The Practitioner Consensus

Every launch playbook starts narrow. Albi of Restoration Success Strategies tells new owners to run emergency water mitigation only, a line carrying 70%+ gross margins, and to refer rebuilds to a general contractor for a 10% kickback until revenue clears $3-5M, summed up as "Simple scales and complex fails" [3PlUwHvZkxE @ 00:02:56]. PushLeads' Jeremy draws the same line from the customer side: mitigation and reconstruction are sequential phases, not rival bids, and "The restoration company always comes first," because mold can take hold within 24-48 hours and the carrier may then deny the mold claim [wnVxHv3JM28 @ 00:01:01]. The money sits in that first phase. Albi's second guide puts the average restoration ticket at $8-12K against $2-3K for a plumbing call, with drying equipment running and billed on site for five to seven days [MDZLPIkMaI8 @ 00:03:58]. Capital requirements are modest by contracting standards. Albi wants $50-200K of purchasing power counting loans, plus one referral source, while Restoration Made Simple, built on 18 years and two from-scratch companies, quotes $50-100K typical and $10-30K for bootstrappers, with the first $1-2K going to WRT and ASD certification taken together at a flood house [aBQA1Wn_LNQ @ 00:01:42]. Both treat the 24/7 phone as the real job description, and Restoration Made Simple warns the business never closes.

The second consensus point: own the phone. Albi credits half of Mason Dixon Restoration's $2.6M first nine months to online marketing and names Google LSA, Google PPC and a lead aggregator as the stack that takes a shop from $0 to $1M, with plumbers preferred over insurance agents as the second source [3PlUwHvZkxE @ 00:07:50]. Vinnie Mac, who runs dozens of LSA campaigns, explains why Google wins: roughly 90-95% of search goes there, the LSA unit sits at the top of the page, review stars trigger an "Amazon effect," and only qualified phone calls count, not clicks or impressions [IDqwheOOgb0 @ 00:09:52]. In a later breakdown, the agency reports "about 80% of the phone calls come from either Google Guaranteed or Google Maps," and that lead-gen outfits such as 33 Mile Radius and Inquirly cannot qualify for the placement [O0STLVhCL2c @ 00:08:54]. The 2026 update adds a warning that LSA does not stand alone: results track Maps, SEO and PPC visibility, and "You can't just turn on LSA" [2akNGTmJry8 @ 00:03:48]. The ad operators all land on the same rule for what happens after the ring: pick up live, around the clock, with no voicemail. Restoration Made Simple puts the conversion side bluntly: "Doesn't do us any good to get the phone to ring if we can't convert them."

On paper, contract specialists who otherwise fight over details agree on one sequence: signature before work. Shane O'Dazier of RestorationProfits wants a reconstruction "agreement," named that way to kill the "not a contract" defense, signed before any estimate is written, with no free estimates ever, plus a separate work authorization for tarping, mitigation, mold, asbestos and bio, and both signed when in doubt [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:01:57]. C&R Magazine's guest Ed, "the Restoration Lawyer," states the same priority: "We want to make sure we have a signature first from somebody who is financially responsible in agreeing to pay," preferring fixed-price over time-and-materials [t7tJ735gl4s @ 00:03:31]. Florida counsel Hans Kennan, on Contractor Talk Podcast with Richie Kidwell, recommends a service authorization with direction to pay on 90-day net terms and rejects contingency contracts [Tg526FvHOmY @ 00:27:01]. Margin is the final shared prerequisite. George Gabriel warns that on insurance work the carrier sets the price, so cost is the only lever: "So you can't fix a thin job by putting more markup on it" [KnWhJDOPsTc @ 00:04:04]. Albi's homeowner rule closes the loop: the homeowner, not the carrier, owes the bill, so collect the $2-3K deductible up front and let the insurance check on the back end become the profit.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Albi's launch budget runs about $200K, half cash and half financed. A new Sprinter van costs roughly $60K plus a $6,500 wrap, about $800 a month financed; $45K of equipment financed at 8-10% buys 8 dehus, 80 air movers, one extractor and four scrubbers; then $5K a year in insurance, $1K legal, $12K for office and warehouse, $10-20K for web and marketing, and a 60-90 day cash cushion [3PlUwHvZkxE @ 00:05:22]. Ram Exterior proved the floor is lower, starting on $30K of Menards and Chase Ink credit plus a truck. Staffing stays at three roles, crew chief, technician and executive assistant, and growth comes from adding trucks rather than middle managers: "a two-person unit can produce a million dollars of revenue per year, $83,000 per month" [3PlUwHvZkxE @ 00:07:09]. Restoration Made Simple adds the closing kit: an infrared camera at about $500-800 or a phone plug-in, a moisture meter and a hydro sensor, worked as a "double visual close" on the claim that 65% of people react to visuals; one extra closed job pays for all three [aBQA1Wn_LNQ @ 00:03:20]. On the phone, the script asks "what city do you live in?" and holds the full address until the job is sold [aBQA1Wn_LNQ @ 00:06:44].

The paper carries specific numbers. Shane O'Dazier sells "5 000 worth of agreements for under 50 bucks," built around a Clause 6 that bills any portion of a day as a full 24 hours with a three-day minimum on equipment rental, which strips the adjuster's main argument even though the check still has to be collected [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:06:15]. The termination clause charges the customer 20% of the gross job if they fire the contractor after the estimate and negotiation; O'Dazier dismisses public-adjuster and legality objections, keeps an AOB-style right to endorse and deposit the check even where a state voids it, adds an attorney-fee provision, and calls California's roughly 10% deposit limit "stupid" and something to violate [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:10:02]. Ed's CAT-work method phases emergency work into a fixed-price contract: price the first 24-48 hours, such as "half a dozen air movers and D U and and 10 labor hours," promise nothing about pre-loss condition, then follow up with a change order [t7tJ735gl4s @ 00:03:48]. Against mortgage companies, Ed sends a photocopy of the check plus a contract obligating them to endorse and return it, and pairs an assignment with a direction to pay so the contractor's name lands on the check [t7tJ735gl4s @ 00:07:14].

Paid-lead setup is equally specific. Vinnie Mac sets the LSA budget above intended spend, up to "99,999" a week, because franchises outbid smaller shops, notes that overnight calls cost more but are more profitable, and recommends $1,500-$2,000 a week: "I would not start out at $250 per week" [IDqwheOOgb0 @ 00:25:33]. Launch on automated bidding, move to target cost per lead after 30-60 days, ignore Google's leads-per-week projection ("This is false"), answer message leads inside 30-60 minutes, and qualify callers within 60-90 seconds [O0STLVhCL2c @ 00:16:02]. On PPC, Restoration Growth Co shows the leak: four clicks at $34 each for one booked job is $136 per job, and the fix is shutting off broad match, search partners, display expansion and "presence or interest" location targeting, then adding campaign-level phrase negatives like salary, jobs, DIY, how to, certification and rental [zkR_04H9hBQ @ 00:06:07]. PushLeads prices emergency clicks at $50-100 against $3-15K jobs, so the account runs exact match, phrase at most, never broad, with urgency modifiers like emergency, 24-7, near me, same day and after hours, CallRail counting calls over 1-2 minutes as conversions, and bids raised on nights and weekends [9FnufX3pXj4 @ 00:03:23].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Paper the job before the first air mover
   - Get the work authorization and reconstruction agreement signed, then price the first 24-48 hours as a fixed-price phase.
   - Have the customer sign the work authorization for emergency work and the reconstruction agreement before any estimate, signing both when in doubt [Shane O'Dazier | RestorationProfits — b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:03:50]; scope the first 24-48 hours at a fixed price and handle the rest by change order [C&R Magazine — t7tJ735gl4s @ 00:03:48].
2. Clear LSA verification and launch the budget
   - Load the $1M liability COI, the IICRC firm certification with an exact name match, and at least 3 Google reviews.
   - Verification checks the owner's background, a certificate of insurance showing $1M liability, an IICRC firm certification costing a few hundred dollars with the name matching exactly, and a profile with at least 3 reviews [Water Damage Restoration Marketing - Vinnie Mac — O0STLVhCL2c @ 00:11:50].
3. Lock down PPC intent every Monday
   - Switch location targeting to presence, kill broad match, and read the search terms report weekly.
   - Every Monday spend 20 minutes on the last 90 days of search terms sorted by cost, reading the top 50 rows and adding phrase-match negatives at campaign level [Restoration Growth Co — zkR_04H9hBQ @ 00:08:37].
4. Price off margin and write the phase budget
   - Convert the target margin into markup, then write a phase-by-phase budget that sums to the contract price.
   - A 30% margin requires a 43% markup, or $3,440 on an $8,000 job [Roofing Insights — ppn4akh1gPo @ 00:02:55]; at signing, write a phase-by-phase budget that sums exactly to the contract price and classify every overrun as your miss or a change order [George Gabriel — KnWhJDOPsTc @ 00:02:53].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Contract philosophy splits between an aggressive paper camp and a cautious lawyer camp. Shane O'Dazier tells operators to title the document an 'agreement' to kill the 'not a contract' defense, sign it before writing any estimate, and keep the AOB clause even where a state voids it, because it works like a limited power of attorney to endorse and deposit the check [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:08:02]. He stacks a termination clause billing 20% of the gross job if the homeowner fires the contractor after estimate and negotiation, dismisses PA and legality objections, and says he would violate California's roughly 10% deposit limit because it is 'stupid' [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:10:02]. Ed, the Restoration Lawyer on C&R Magazine, runs the opposite play: phase emergency work into a fixed-price contract covering the first 24 to 48 hours, commit to nothing about pre-loss condition, follow up with a change order, and 'think twice' before using assignments in Florida after the insurer lobby passed its counter-law [t7tJ735gl4s @ 00:10:25]. Hans Kennan on Contractor Talk Podcast lands in the middle: with the Florida AOB ban hitting policies issued or renewed from 2023 onward, he pushes a service authorization with direction to pay on 90-day net terms and rejects contingency contracts [Tg526FvHOmY @ 00:27:01].

The billing stack splits just as hard. Albi of Restoration Success Strategies calls mandatory Xactimate a myth, pointing to Rhino Restoration reaching $3M on 95% retail work, and reports that 300+ attendees at the RAA conference said documentation did not speed payment, with his own data suggesting about 70% of dry logs are 'not real.' He adds that TPAs control only 10 to 15% of dollar volume, demand 2 years of history, and route work to top contractors by algorithm [3PlUwHvZkxE @ 00:09:35]. Restoration Made Simple bets the other way on paper: room-by-room Excel sheets catch missed line items on every job, and a dedicated self-pay sheet helped crews in Colorado and Utah close 3 to 5 more self-pay jobs a month [aBQA1Wn_LNQ @ 00:05:33]. Payment timing is the sharpest contrast. Albi insists the homeowner, not the carrier, owes the bill, so the $2,000 to $3,000 deductible gets collected up front: 'that cash covers the payroll for the job, and the insurance check on the back end is just the profit' [3PlUwHvZkxE @ 00:14:12]. Kennan, speaking to homeowners on Contractor Talk Podcast, gives the opposite instruction: never pay up front, verify license and insurance, and lean toward big names such as SERVPRO [Tg526FvHOmY @ 00:36:52]. Albi treats the deductible as the payroll engine; Kennan treats an up-front ask as the red flag.

Even inside one agency, the Google playbook shifts year to year. Vinnie Mac's 2024 LSA session says 'go wider than narrow' on service areas, pushing a south Chicago account into Indiana for fringe opportunities [IDqwheOOgb0 @ 00:27:59]. The 2026 version narrows that advice: use cities and zip codes instead of counties when results are weak, and stop pushing outward because the account loses to competitors closer to the searcher [2akNGTmJry8 @ 00:25:28]. That same 2026 session flips credentials and budgets too, moving from a firm-level IICRC certification to a state-by-state IICRC or contractor license requirement, and from weekly to daily budgets under the Performance Max migration. On paid search, Restoration Growth Co tells operators to keep 'cost' and 'cheap' live, calling that 'the best term in your whole account,' and to route technician searches to a careers page through a separate $5/day campaign rather than only blocking them [zkR_04H9hBQ @ 00:07:41]. PushLeads preloads 'cheap' and 'free' as negatives alongside job, salary, DIY, and out-of-area cities, runs exact match with phrase at most, never broad, and reviews the negative list weekly because every click in this vertical costs $50 to $100 [9FnufX3pXj4 @ 00:04:03].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Lead spend bleeds before a truck ever rolls. Restoration Growth Co breaks down one booked job from four $34 clicks, a true $136 per job, where the other three clicks were a technician hunting 'jobs,' a DIY 'how to' searcher, and a landlord 200 miles away; the fix is not cheaper CPC but shutting off broad match, search partners, display expansion, and 'presence or interest' location targeting [zkR_04H9hBQ @ 00:00:26]. PushLeads raises the stakes with $50 to $100 clicks and warns that 'no one is filling out a form,' so the phone has to be staffed live 24/7 with bids raised on nights and weekends [9FnufX3pXj4 @ 00:04:23]. On LSA, Vinnie Mac calls Google's weekly lead projections 'false' peak numbers and notes dispute credits land 30 to 45 days later as next-month offsets, not card refunds [IDqwheOOgb0 @ 00:16:40]. The 2026 session lists the self-inflicted wounds: starting and stopping a campaign acts as a reset, one or two missed calls kill a profile, expired documents knock the account offline, unresolved Google Business Profile suspensions linger, and Google still charges when the operator refers a caller to another company [2akNGTmJry8 @ 00:36:16]. The cure he teaches is lowering hours, bid, or territory instead of pausing outright.

Margin dies twice, once in the pricing math and again in the field. Roofing Insights shows a $10,000 price on $8,000 cost is a 20% margin but a 25% markup, and blames contractors 'thinking in dollars and not percentages'; hitting 30% on that $8,000 job takes a 43% markup, or $3,440 [ppn4akh1gPo @ 00:01:58]. George Gabriel walks one job where a second dumpster haul ($450), two bundles short ($180), and a second shingle layer at 25 squares times $25 ($625) stacked into a $1,255 overrun that dropped margin from 33% to 25%, against a net profit band of only 6 to 12% [KnWhJDOPsTc @ 00:02:03]. His insurance-work warning is blunt: the carrier sets the price, so 'you can't fix a thin job by putting more markup on it,' and cost is the only lever; his fix is a phase-by-phase budget written at signing that sums exactly to the contract price, with every overrun classed as either the contractor's miss or a change order [KnWhJDOPsTc @ 00:04:04]. On the Roofing Insights growth panel, Alex describes a 7pm 'money in/money out' report that flags every job under 35% margin in red, replacing the 4 to 6 month margin lag of after-the-fact accounting [evwV66xRf4I @ 00:47:08].

Collections is where unsigned paper and slow timelines cost real money. Shane O'Dazier warns that without a signed agreement before the estimate, the homeowner gets the check and stops returning calls, a trap he ties directly to re-roofs, and he refuses to give free estimates at all [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:03:24]. His 3-day minimum rental clause, counting any portion of a day as a full 24 hours, strips the adjuster's argument but still does not guarantee payment [b1-Dcqk4F9g @ 00:07:36]. In Florida, Kennan lays out the squeeze: insurers now pay or deny within 60 days instead of 90, contractors hold a 90-day lien window, and that leaves roughly 30 days to appraise, mediate, or litigate [Tg526FvHOmY @ 00:41:11]. Ed adds the mortgage-company trap, protecting against it by sending a photocopy of the check plus a contract obligating the lender to endorse and return it, and recounts forcing carriers to pay a second time 'because the policyholder ran off to Hawaii with the money or used the money to pay off the mortgage' [t7tJ735gl4s @ 00:16:05]. His escalation rule is not too fast and not too slow, because older debts are harder to collect, and never experiment with mechanics liens because the deadlines are strict.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Gates Before Spending on Leads or Rolling Equipment**
>
