## 1. The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who has sat across from a carrier agrees on the opening truth: the insurer's first number is a starting position it wrote for itself, not a verdict. claimhelpme calls it the baseline problem, warning that “A weak estimate creates a weak starting point” and that when the low figure goes unchallenged, every later conversation negotiates down from it rather than up from the real scope [2WVlG5yPBTU @ 00:03:46]. Builder's Perspective Public Adjusting puts dollars on the gap, walking through a file where Frontline paid about $40,000 against a contractor bid of almost $75,000 on a scope he describes as relatively similar, which is why he tells owners to wait for the carrier's paperwork before deciding whether to bring in a PA [kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:00:33]. Chad Michael takes the hardest line: “you should never agree to settle ever with an insurance company ever no matter where you are in the process,” even when the adjuster climbs from $20K to about $30K against a $40K contractor number, because an estimate is only “a prediction of the future” and the contractor holds the power [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:00:59]. On Roofing Insights, a State Farm direct payment of $5,000 became $17,000 once a stormchaser worked the same claim [uykTx34p0Js @ 00:11:02].

The second point of agreement is where the legal lines sit, and contractors who blur them get burned. Vince Perri, citing Florida statute, says a contractor may suggest filing a claim and may discuss its bid for usual and customary fees, but may not negotiate the claim, which is the PA's lane [RRn3mzn9xCw @ 00:09:43]. Builder's Perspective Public Adjusting draws the same boundary from the other side: a contractor cannot act as the PA on the same claim, though it can still talk through its own pricing with the carrier [kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:01:10]. John Black of Daly & Black lists the Texas functions that trigger unauthorized practice of public adjusting, including investigating, appraising, evaluating or advocating, preparing or negotiating the claim, advising on policy terms, and advertising as an adjuster, and he shuts down the permission or power-of-attorney workaround under Texas Insurance Code section 4102.163, leaving a contractor with a void contract and a policyholder who can refuse to pay [jaGktNyKvE4 @ 00:01:55]. Dimmitri Lipinski reduces it to one rule on Roofing Insights: roofers may discuss scope only, not coverage, interior damage, or hotel costs, and anything past that line is UPPA exposure [uykTx34p0Js @ 00:20:28].

The third consensus point is that the file wins, not the volume of the argument. Chad Michael's standard is a photo, a code citation, and a footnote for every line item, built with Xactimate's item image and add item info note functions so the adjuster sees exactly what each code includes [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:06:44]. Chris Zimmerman tells contractors to walk into the first meeting carrying the estimate, homeowner-facing evidence, damage photos, technical bulletins, building codes, and a detailed scope, because “If you want to be taken seriously, you need to be someone who deserves to be taken seriously” [LwNOcenSP_c @ 00:01:13]. American Roof Supplements adds the photo discipline: shoot the overall slope before the detail, include a measurement, label everything, and remember that “The contractor's job is to make the technical facts clear, not to promise what the policy will pay” [zDKvQs0viSI @ 00:02:50]. Daniel Kern of The Adjuster's Roundtable shows where that discipline leads on a hard file: a Hurricane Helene loss on two buildings, denied as flood on one and pre-existing damage on the other, was taken to policy limits after he brought in vetted experts, prepared rebuild estimates, and presented a full documentation package [_wmRAji2ZmI @ 00:02:48].

## 2. Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Reading the carrier's Xactimate estimate starts with simple math. Builder's Perspective Public Adjusting divides the roof sketch area by 100, so 2,901.94 SF becomes 29.02 squares, and then compares tear-off quantity to roof area: when they match, the carrier is agreeing to full replacement, while something like 0.33 squares means “one bundle of shingles to repair your roof basically” [kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:04:42]. The same review caught water barrier membrane entered as 24.48 SF when it should have been 2,448 SF, which he fixes by multiplying the 21.61 unit price by 100 and calls likely an honest mistake [kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:06:15]. Chad Michael works the trade codes on the DFW price list, where drywall runs 0.46 to remove, 3.12 to replace, and 3.58 to R&R. His move is switching removal lines from DMO to the actual trade: drip edge removal goes from 0.36 to 0.96, drywall removal from 0.46 to 1.05, and shingle removal from 61.73 to about 150 [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:18:23]. His first 20 attempts were all denied, and he now lands roughly 40 percent, 4 of 10, with the switch working on ESX submissions and with Nationwide while State Farm will not revise its own estimate [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:13:50].

The adjuster meeting is run like a job site. Chris Zimmerman's truck kit is a ladder, tool bag, chalk, tape measure, shingle gauge, pitch gauge, and something to write on, and anything missing once stays in the truck permanently. On the roof he asks “What do you need from me?”, conversationally checks whether the adjuster has authority, is deciding or only documenting, and has seen the claim file, then raises unmarked damage before they finish [LwNOcenSP_c @ 00:02:07]. He closes with a recap in front of the homeowner, ideally on the Ring camera, pinning down peril causation and agreed counts such as eight wind damage shingles on the front and two on the left, because desk adjusters often contradict the field adjuster [LwNOcenSP_c @ 00:04:38]. Chad Michael's inspection standard is every room, closet, and hallway, at least 2 hours, both owners present, and 2 to 3 appointments a day instead of 8, with 200 to 300 photos plus drone footage handed to the client in a Google Drive folder [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:34:49]. When an adjuster piles on paperwork, Zimmerman's script is “is this a recommendation or is this a requirement?”, and if it is a requirement, ask what it is based on [9A5QEe9xxXk @ 00:01:21].

The negotiation itself is short and the money math is blunt. Shane O'Dazier, who starts from the premise that adjusters are obligated “to not overpay,” abandoned the hour of phone-tag line-by-line argument that used to cut a 25-line estimate in half, $6K to $3K or $8K to $4K, and now collects every adjuster question first without arguing, totals them, and finishes in 3 to 4 minutes [3xfQ7PdL-AA @ 00:06:10]. His rule is to accept up to 10 percent off, refuse anything deeper, collect the undisputed amount, and back it with an attorney-fee provision clause, with his front desk holding authority to accept reductions [3xfQ7PdL-AA @ 00:07:49]. On the PA side, Vince Perri quotes fees of 10, 15, or 20 percent, says it is no issue in 9 of 10 cases, and otherwise the contractor absorbs the loss or adjusts commission to keep the deal alive [RRn3mzn9xCw @ 00:15:09]. American Roof Supplements prices its outsourced estimating work for contractors at a free first estimate or supplement, $50 for a pre-adjuster estimate, $100 for a follow-up supplement on the same approved job, and $150 standalone, with no monthly fee or minimum and PAs paying the same [zDKvQs0viSI @ 00:10:20].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Decode the carrier estimate
   - Convert sketch area to squares, test tear-off against roof area, and hunt unit errors.
   - Divide square footage by 100, treat a tear-off matching roof area as full replacement, and flag entries like a 24.48-square tear-off that is likely the entire roof or membrane entered at 24.48 SF instead of 2,448 SF [Builder's Perspective Public Adjusting — kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:05:53]
2. Rebuild the line items with trade codes and proof
   - Switch DMO removal codes to the actual trade and attach a photo, code, and footnote to each item.
   - Move drip edge removal from 0.36 to 0.96 and drywall removal from 0.46 to 1.05, document each with item image and add item info note, and submit as ESX [Chad Michael - The Practitioner — vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:11:46]
3. Run the adjuster meeting and recorded recap
   - Arrive with the full kit, ask what the adjuster needs, raise unmarked damage, then recap with the homeowner.
   - Confirm peril causation, substantial damage, and agreed counts such as eight wind damage shingles on the front and two on the left, ideally on the Ring camera [Chris Zimmerman — LwNOcenSP_c @ 00:05:20]
4. Close on the question list
   - Collect every adjuster question without arguing, total the cuts, and apply the 10 percent rule.
   - List all questions first, total them up, finish in 3 to 4 minutes, accept up to 10 percent off, and refuse deeper cuts while collecting the undisputed amount [Shane O'Dazier | RestorationProfits — 3xfQ7PdL-AA @ 00:05:41]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split at the negotiation table is whether to settle at all. Chad Michael draws a hard line: never agree to settle with an insurance company, no matter where the claim sits in the process, and when an adjuster at $20K revises toward $30K against a $40K contractor number, the script is 'I can't agree to anything... send the money,' because an estimate is only a prediction of the future and the contractor holds the power [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:00:59]. claimhelpme also rejects bargaining, stating 'I don't believe in negotiations' and pointing to the New York DFS definition of a public adjuster, yet still calls an informed settlement acceptable after watching $90K of repairs plus $20K of pack-out resolve for about $40K with a homeowner who was happy [2WVlG5yPBTU @ 00:02:01]. Shane O'Dazier runs a pragmatic middle playbook: the shop accepts reductions up to 10 percent, refuses anything deeper, collects the undisputed amount, and gives the front desk authority to approve cuts inside that band [3xfQ7PdL-AA @ 00:06:23]. The Roofing Insights panel supplies the counterweight, with one panelist stating flatly that insurance companies do make reasonable settlements, so walling off every carrier number is not universal doctrine [uykTx34p0Js @ 00:09:53].

The second fault line is who sits across from the adjuster and who gets cut out. Vince Perri, running Elite Resolutions across Florida, Texas, North Carolina and California, lays out the Florida split: a contractor may suggest filing a claim and discuss a bid for usual and customary fees but may not negotiate, while a public adjuster puts more money in the client's pocket '10 times out of 10' [RRn3mzn9xCw @ 00:09:10]. Avner Gat goes further against restorers, warning that restoration companies offering to negotiate are not equipped, that clients call once those talks get stuck, and that 'You need a license in order to do that' [8x3Gfr6Uyr8 @ 00:02:41]. Anissa Coy of FirehouseEducation flips the whole premise: the restorer's real client is the insurer, bringing in a PA risks losing insurer work, and the restorer should never deal with the PA, only with the homeowner and the claims adjuster [GhZSitNz6aQ @ 00:05:11]. Mike of Repair King on Roofing Insights runs the reverse order of operations, finding the contractor first as the 'mechanic' and letting the contractor pick the PA, after watching a PA take 10 percent and simply go to appraisal, something Mike could have done without the fee [uykTx34p0Js @ 00:03:50].

Fees split the room just as hard. In the Roofing Insights debate, the insurer-side lawyer argues a 10 percent PA fee leaves the insured unable to fully fix the damage, running a $20K roof with a $1K deductible and a $2K fee, though the same lawyer prefers a PA at 10 percent over a lawyer at 45 percent and proposes no PA fee on uncontested dollars, which the PA refuses at that pay level [Qv99F8pvYNU @ 00:02:31]. Host Dimmitri Lipinski calls 10 percent reasonable and 25 to 35 percent a ripoff, arguing that getting 10 percent more while paying 25 percent is not a gain, and holds up a $25,000 claim where a PA charged a $1,500 flat fee as a must-hire case [uykTx34p0Js @ 00:06:28]. Builder's Perspective Public Adjusting times the hire instead of fighting the rate, waiting for the insurer's paperwork so the homeowner avoids paying 10 percent on that initial check [kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:00:00]. On tooling, Shane O'Dazier walked away from Xactimate after more than 1,000 claims, calling it the devil's tools owned by an insurance company, while the Roofing Insights lawyer concedes Xactimate is not the be-all and points toward retail-price contracts [3xfQ7PdL-AA @ 00:04:10].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Estimate-level failures show up first. Shane O'Dazier reports that Xactimate plus detailed notes was garbage, with adjusters cutting at random; the old phone-tag process meant an hour of line-by-line argument, a typical 12 questions on a 25-line estimate, and totals sliced in half, $6K to $3K, $8K to $4K, $4K to $2K [3xfQ7PdL-AA @ 00:01:20]. Chad Michael's trade-code switch, moving items off DMO to the actual trade so drip edge removal jumps from 0.36 to 0.96 and drywall removal from 0.46 to 1.05, went 0 for 20 at first before climbing to roughly 4 approvals in 10, and State Farm still will not revise its own estimate [vkrXTVQcVQQ @ 00:13:23]. Builder's Perspective Public Adjusting caught a carrier entering water barrier membrane at 24.48 SF instead of 2,448 SF, a gap exposed by multiplying the 21.61 price by 100, and calls it likely an honest mistake, but one that still shorts the homeowner until someone reads the estimate line by line [kWxJ6PIkM-M @ 00:06:15]. claimhelpme adds that holding a license does not mean a PA understands construction or estimating, and that an unchallenged low carrier number becomes the baseline everyone negotiates from [2WVlG5yPBTU @ 00:00:57].

The legal tripwires land on the contractor's paper. John Black of Daly & Black lists the Texas UPPA functions, investigating, appraising, evaluating or advocating, preparing or negotiating the claim, advising on policy terms, and advertising as an adjuster, and says the permission or power-of-attorney workaround is barred by Texas Insurance Code section 4102.163; the result is a void contract, a policyholder who can refuse to pay, and a contractor left holding the bag [jaGktNyKvE4 @ 00:02:12]. Building Experts Institute targets the classic contingency line, 'We will do the work for the amount the insurance company approves,' as negotiating the claim outcome, likely illegal anyway for lacking a set dollar consideration, and functioning as an assignment of benefits in states without one; the fix is a stated scope and price, a cancellation clause if the insurer does not approve in full, and a change order on partial approval [RmjqTZJ3II0 @ 00:00:20]. American Roof Supplements reminds contractors that a roofing contract does not authorize representing the insured and that Texas regulators warn contractors against acting as PAs [zDKvQs0viSI @ 00:01:55]. Grossman PA LLC reviews invoices for attorneys where homeowners are pursued for unpaid restoration bills years later, because the homeowner's name sits on the contract [diiBgujlZF0 @ 00:01:02].

Adjuster behavior and the money math create the rest of the losses. Chris Zimmerman reads an Auto-Owners demand for a city-inspector form across 64 townhouse units, over crickets and chimney flashing after a 5-day adjustment, as a claim-fatigue tactic, since getting the city to fill it out could take 3 to 6 months; even a city-page screenshot showing 2021 IRC enforcement was refused [9A5QEe9xxXk @ 00:04:03]. Zimmerman also warns that field adjusters' statements often get contradicted by the desk adjuster, which is why the recap happens with the homeowner, preferably on the Ring camera, locking in agreed counts like eight wind-damaged shingles on the front and two on the left [LwNOcenSP_c @ 00:04:38]. Grossman PA LLC flags the inflation trap from the carrier-review side: a $7,500 job paid directly by a homeowner gets billed to the insurer at $10,000 to $20,000, and reviews surface duplicate charges, unsupported labor, unnecessary equipment and outsourced estimates the contractor cannot explain [diiBgujlZF0 @ 00:01:31]. On Roofing Insights, Mike warns depreciation is released only after the work is complete, owners who claim 100 percent but do 20 percent get caught on later claims, and 15 of 18 properties handed to a big-name PA produced zero results [uykTx34p0Js @ 00:17:15].
