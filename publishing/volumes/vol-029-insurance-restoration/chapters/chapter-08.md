## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every operator here treats the plumber as the front door to water work. Jon Schoen built a 47-truck company and sold it. He calls plumbers the number one referral source because they see the job before the adjuster or agent ever does [HFcU3Nls6fM @ 00:00:01]. Restorex in Indianapolis says most of its business comes from referrals, and it frames plumbers as first on the scene [nFvUl2Kjwjs @ 00:00:21]. Shane O'Dazier goes further. Water and fire are the only gateway jobs, and the top restoration companies in any market are the ones that dominate plumbers [sUMKhFYRGSQ @ 00:09:01]. The proof case is Rowdy Legs of Homeway in Memphis. Rowdy learned the system from Shane and averaged six to ten jobs a week almost entirely from plumbers. Homeway went from zero to eleven million dollars in three years and only used internet marketing in the last six to eight months [Fj9S8rNJ5d4 @ 00:05:41]. The shared baseline is blunt: a restorer who cannot reliably pull jobs out of plumbing trucks is renting volume from storms and ad platforms. Every number in this chapter, from referral trees to close rates, sits downstream of that one acquisition skill. Practitioners treat the plumber channel as the base layer of a water company, not a side campaign run when the phones go quiet.

The second point of agreement is that you diagnose before you pitch. Shane's core rule is to find out what the plumber wants and give it to them. The options run from referral fees to leads to free tryouts, a combination, or none of those [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:01:28]. Marketing Ninjas warns that walking in with a flyer or gift card attached to a pitch blows the visit. They use discovery questions instead, including asking about bad experiences the plumber has had with other restorers [n7ECptsaJTQ @ 00:00:26]. Shane learned this the hard way. Free dry-outs, swag, referral fees, breakfasts and a motorcycle giveaway all failed with the top plumber Shane chased. Every plumber is different, not all of them want free dry-outs, and some refuse referral fees outright [Rcmd8gVFzB8 @ 00:03:18]. Shane's phrase for the whole discipline is to use the right bait for the right fish [sUMKhFYRGSQ @ 00:01:13]. Guessing wrong is expensive. Shane says a wrong assumption flips a positive relationship negative fast. Unsolicited cash to a plumber who never asked for it tells them you are not different anymore. The consensus prerequisite is a documented answer, plumber by plumber, to one question: what actually moves this shop?

Practitioners also agree on who to target and on running the phone as a measured system. Shane tells operators not to judge a plumbing company by truck count. A one-truck service plumber sees leaks every day, while a twenty-truck construction outfit only sends warranty jobs. Target service over construction and residential over commercial. On commercial jobs a property manager controls the referral, and the contract has to be with the owner [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:07:00]. Shane's criteria also include a minimum job volume per plumber and a less-is-more stance on how many relationships to run, all kept inside a tight county radius [xXe9Z763dDc @ 00:10:40]. Once referrals start ringing, Restoration Made Simple records every call through CallRail. Missed calls get caught, called back and used to train staff [8nQyv3vEI6A @ 00:02:50]. Albi's benchmark sets the bar for closing that call. A close rate under seventy percent means the company is missing a system, and the framework aims for seventy-five to eighty percent [mq8qI2hag0I @ 00:00:00]. Shane also advises staying out of multi-family and commercial policies for the first few years. The operator setup is a narrow list of residential service plumbers feeding a recorded, scripted phone line.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The prospecting engine starts with who you call and how you sound. Shane hunts what they call shadow plumbers, meaning shops absent from Google's front page. Plumbers who advertise want leads, while Google-Maps-only plumbers may be construction outfits [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:16:59]. The call is made posing as a customer, not as a solicitor. Ask about relocating a water heater and a failed pop-off valve, then ask 'do y'all dry them out,' and follow a branching flowchart from whatever the plumber says [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:19:05]. Every answer lands in a CRM plumber profile. The profile logs incentive preference and current fee in bands of under $300, $300, $500 and over $500. Call tracking is attached, and triangulating questions expose lies such as 'we don't refer anybody but want a referral fee' [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:21:22]. When the plumber has no restorer, Shane runs the free leads play. Ask if they do this work, cover the area and want leads. Get a contact name, phone and email, and book an onboarding meeting, or simply send leads first. The non-negotiable is a backdoor contact, not the 1-800 number [ufmHlZ6tfKg @ 00:08:06]. Shane names the challenges the script has to solve: separating what a plumber wants from what they will take, differentiating, and landing the first job through servant marketing.

Incentives are built as specific instruments, not vague goodwill. Shane's slow leak play is a rebrand of the fake leak. It puts a real service call in front of the plumber's technician. In the example, the plumber was called to Joe's lake house for a sump pump and crawl space. Joe asked who the plumber calls for water damage and heard that the office calls somebody. Joe then handed over a card pre-loaded with $100, plus a $100 weekly drip [CV0x51fC7pw @ 00:02:34]. Joe replaces that sump pump every three to four years at $400 anyway and runs five consistent plumbers. Shane offers the drip only when the plumber already takes a fee elsewhere [MLuoMdYemWU @ 00:03:15]. Marketing Ninjas uses a fast-start bonus of double commission for thirty days [n7ECptsaJTQ @ 00:01:55]. Marketing Ninjas also runs a tier model in place of cash. After one referral the plumber gets priority dispatch, free dry-outs, dumpsters and shut-off tags with tracking numbers. After three, they get plumbing leads, co-branded materials and seasonal swag. After five, they get quarterly raffles, VIP access and training perks. After ten, they get team dinners, job-site banners, welcome boxes and a legacy wall [5Ibyq8oHR7Y @ 00:01:35]. Shane's timeline is about four weeks of incentivizing, which yields enough data to chase ideal fits instead of half-committed plumbers.

Closing the call is the back half of the engine. Restoration Made Simple sets a separate tracking number per site. A call whisper tells the answerer the source before they say hello, such as 'Denver North website lead' or 'plumbing referral 800 number' [8nQyv3vEI6A @ 00:04:29]. Their strategy four asks what city the caller is in, then relates with a shop fifteen minutes away or a tech in Littleton. Strategy eleven promises arrival in less than sixty minutes and a callback in three to five minutes, because a caller waiting on you is less likely to dial someone else. Their clients close ten to thirty percent more leads with these strategies [8nQyv3vEI6A @ 00:11:00]. Albi's phone rules are a scripted greeting, three qualifiers, no price ranges on the phone and no outsourced answering. On arrival that means a clean truck, a tucked shirt and street parking. On site, the ask is a crew within the next thirty minutes. The stronger ask is homeowner financing, or at least a deposit. An insurance objection gets a capped out-of-pocket cost with 0% APR for thirty-six months [mq8qI2hag0I @ 00:09:48]. Follow-up runs every twenty-four hours, and Albi reports that two of five leads lost at first close later with a good sequence [mq8qI2hag0I @ 00:13:51].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The loudest split in the plumber channel is cash. Restorex Disaster Restoration runs a money-first program out of Indianapolis, advertising the highest referral fees in its market, paid as a percentage of the dry-out that ranges from a couple hundred to a couple thousand dollars, with payment landing the next day or when the job starts [nFvUl2Kjwjs @ 00:00:37]. Jon Schoen lists cash as the first lever, either a flat dollar amount or a percentage of the job, with the market deciding which [HFcU3Nls6fM @ 00:00:20]. Marketing Ninjas takes the opposite position: a $250 fee gets beaten by a $300 fee, kickbacks stay transactional and sketchy, and plumbers never brag about a $150 check, so the playbook replaces cash with a four-tier ladder of priority dispatch, free dry-outs, dumpsters, co-branded materials, raffles and team dinners [5Ibyq8oHR7Y @ 00:00:17]. Shane O'Dazier sits in the middle. Shane tells operators never to pay unless the plumber asks, because unsolicited money means you are no longer different, and warns that a rival will always pay ten or twenty more; when money is on the table, Shane incentivizes the tech and gives more to the GM or owner, while noting some plumbing companies ban tech fees outright [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:10:16].

Free services split the field just as hard. Shane frames free dry-outs as the path to 500k of plumber referrals, labeling them "water - free dryout" on the job board, running different protocols such as top-down drying instead of hammers, and never charging a plumber for containment [ufmHlZ6tfKg @ 00:05:54]. Yet Shane also reports that free dry-outs, swag, referral fees, breakfasts and motorcycle giveaways all failed with the top plumber, and that the door only opened after roughly four months of servant marketing, when Shane ate a $300 mortar patch an hour away rather than marking it up to $600 [Rcmd8gVFzB8 @ 00:10:43]. Shane's guardrail is that free dry-outs need a 4:1 ratio of converted claims behind them [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:12:00]. Jon Schoen runs a cheaper, event-driven playbook: a yearly bucket giveaway with one entry per referral for a used WaveRunner, four-wheeler or boat bought inexpensively, plus hot dog, burrito or chili lunches at Home Depot and Lowe's distributor counters, while warning those counters already rotate two or three restoration companies [HFcU3Nls6fM @ 00:02:43]. Shane's version is a slow, personal favor bank built one plumber at a time; Schoen's version is a cheap, repeatable circuit built around raffle entries and distributor-counter food that reaches many plumbers at once.

On the close, the phone camps diverge on what to say first. Restoration Made Simple teaches reading the caller, calm versus panicking, regular versus after-hours, and warns that pitching insurance to a self-pay caller loses them; its Strategy 11 promises arrival in under 60 minutes and a callback in three to five minutes so the homeowner waits instead of dialing a competitor [8nQyv3vEI6A @ 00:11:00]. Albi's framework is stricter: a scripted greeting, three qualifiers, no price ranges on the phone, no outsourced answering, and a target close rate of 75 to 80 percent, with anything under 70 percent signaling a missing system [mq8qI2hag0I @ 00:03:22]. Where Restoration Made Simple leans on local proof such as a shop fifteen minutes away and a tech in Littleton, Albi pushes a harder money ask on site: homeowner financing that makes the job cost incurred, or at minimum a deposit, plus 0% APR for 36 months when the insurance objection surfaces [mq8qI2hag0I @ 00:10:38]. claimhelpme rejects the common habit of taking the undisputed amount, starting, and supplementing later, telling contractors to review the carrier estimate immediately and ask the carrier to stop the check, because otherwise they are selling from weakness instead of knowledge [eLQHV7yIGx8 @ 00:00:39].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Shane's targeting trap is judging plumbers by truck count. A one-truck service plumber sees leaks, while a twenty-truck construction outfit only sends warranty jobs, so the target is service over construction and residential over commercial, where the property manager controls the referral and the contract has to be with the owner [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:06:47]. Shane also says to avoid multi-family and commercial policies in the first few years. Guessing what a plumber wants flips a positive relationship negative fast, and triangulating questions catch liars, such as the plumber who claims to refer nobody but still wants a referral fee [D4BavFC6d1E @ 00:22:40]. The relationship can also turn on you after it lands. A referring plumber may fire you if you use another plumber, so Shane offers them the work with an "adjuster's only given me this amount" framing, while warning that retail plumbers who quoted $5,500 for half a day can charge $4,000 for a $600 task, which makes them the wrong hire for recon [MLuoMdYemWU @ 00:09:34]. Marketing Ninjas adds that plumbers see twelve rivals a month; in the extreme, one client brings a monkey into the plumbing offices, and the playbook adds branded shutoff tags and photo books [n7ECptsaJTQ @ 00:02:20].

The recon and big-loss traps hit cash flow. Shane warns that a $25K reconstruction job is tempting but ties you up six to fourteen weeks and puts the review at risk, and that on the internet side one "Yahoo" spending $25K a month wrecks the metrics that once delivered six jobs a week inside a 33-mile radius [xXe9Z763dDc @ 00:07:36]. Rowdy Legs of Homeway in Memphis reached $6.7M doing recon-only for one partner, and the split cost Homeway five to six jobs a week [Fj9S8rNJ5d4 @ 00:05:01]. Vinnie Mac's client Jim of Pride Restoration in St. Louis turned a Google Guarantee lead that cost a couple hundred dollars into a 70-unit, three-floor pipe burst worth more than $2M, but the payer moved from Farmers to the plumber's insurer to the owner's insurer with a PA involved [KYZjCfsaWhs @ 00:04:29]. Vinnie Mac's checks: hold cash reserves, file a preliminary notice of lien, partner with or refer to a larger firm when the job is too big, and remember a large loss can end in bankruptcy if you don't get paid [KYZjCfsaWhs @ 00:10:18]. Answering only 30 percent of calls, Vinnie Mac adds, burns the ad spend that produced the lead in the first place.

The closing failures are mostly unmeasured leaks. Restoration Made Simple sees clients spending $5k, $10k or $30k a month on marketing without tracking it, against lead costs of $300, $500 or $800 and as high as $1,500 to $1,600 in some regions; the fix is CallRail recording every call, a separate tracking number per site, call whispers like "plumbing referral 800 number," and source-level job averages that expose $3,000 versus $8,000 gaps [8nQyv3vEI6A @ 00:05:36]. Albi treats a close rate under 70 percent as a missing system and counts follow-up as recoverable revenue: follow up every 24 hours, and two of five leads that don't close initially later close with a good sequence; silence is part of the close [mq8qI2hag0I @ 00:13:51]. Shane frames the deeper failure as ownership: 99 percent of owners are weak at sales and marketing, an operator has about five years to figure it out, and franchises sell pipe dreams [sUMKhFYRGSQ @ 00:02:20]. Delegating too early backfires as well, because marketing hires fail 80 percent of the time, so Shane puts founder-first marketing ahead of hiring until the referral tree reaches at least three jobs within 90 days [xXe9Z763dDc @ 00:19:11].
