## 1. Days 1-7: Fund the Pod and Define Exactly What It May Sell  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start with five explicit operating plays rather than an unrestricted promise to restore every insured loss. For this chapter, the mapping is water extraction and structural drying; fire and soot cleaning; mold containment and remediation; storm stabilization and reconstruction; and evidence-led estimating, supplements, and collections. The fifth play supports the other four and must never double-count their invoices as additional revenue. Assign the five full-time seats to an owner/general manager, estimator/project manager, dispatch/accounts-receivable coordinator, lead mitigation technician, and second mitigation technician. Designate six individual contract resources for roofing, carpentry, electrical work, plumbing, specialized remediation, and overflow field labor. These are six people, not six unlimited crews. Record each person's actual weekly availability, credentials, response radius, and substitution limits. Use qualified external specialists where the work requires independence, but charge their services to the job rather than pretending they expand internal capacity. Initially permit one active mitigation site per two-person field crew and one separately supervised reconstruction job. The owner handles overflow coordination, not simultaneous unqualified technical work. Reserve protected estimating and collection blocks daily; losing those blocks converts completed production into disputed receivables instead of usable cash. Treat classification and overtime obligations as staffing inputs, not optional paperwork.

Fund a bounded experiment before buying a warehouse of drying equipment. An illustrative $125,000 launch authorization, within the stated $75,000-$350,000 startup range, allocates $30,000 to vehicles and equipment deposits or purchases, $10,000 to initial insurance, credentials, training, and setup, $45,000 to the first month's operating cash envelope, and $40,000 to protected reserve. Inside that operating envelope, budget $27,000 for loaded payroll across five full-time seats, $8,000 for recurring overhead, and at most $10,000 for incremental pilot-job outlays. Replace these illustrative allowances with actual local quotes before committing. The $35,000 recurring overhead fits the canonical $10,000-$45,000 monthly operating range; subcontractor and material spending still requires a separate job-level cash schedule. Track gross disbursements and receipts separately: collections do not silently increase the authorized spending ceiling. Release reserve only against a documented revised cash plan. At $35,000 monthly fixed overhead and a 45% contribution margin after variable job costs, operating break-even requires approximately $77,778 monthly billings. That arithmetic exposes why the Conservative case cannot carry this particular launch overhead indefinitely. Use rentals and staged purchasing to preserve cash, not unpaid labor or unsupported reimbursement assumptions.

Days 1-3 establish the service territory, licensing matrix, customer agreement, and role boundaries before outreach generates emergencies. Contractor and remediation requirements vary by state and locality; record the authority, credential holder, expiration date, and permitted scope for each offered service. Florida provides a concrete boundary example: Fla. Stat. § 626.854 distinguishes public adjusting from a contractor explaining their own construction or repair bid. Train staff to describe observed damage, performed work, quantities, and prices without negotiating policy coverage for the insured. Days 4-5 establish safety procedures and validate equipment through a controlled exercise. OSHA requirements apply according to the task and employment setting; construction respiratory protection under 29 CFR 1926.103 incorporates 29 CFR 1910.134. Required respirator use needs the applicable written program, medical evaluation, training, and fit testing before exposure. Days 6-7 prove dispatch and accounting with a simulated loss, including an after-hours escalation. Treat IICRC S500 and S520 as technical standards, not statutes or automatic insurer payment rules. A qualified technical lead must select procedures appropriate to the actual loss. Open sales only for scopes that passed the exercise; keep unsupported specialties off the service menu.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four Checks Before the First Occupied-Property Dispatch**
> 
> Every check needs recorded evidence and an accountable person; a verbal assurance does not release a crew.
> - Verify the actual jurisdiction's contractor and remediation requirements against the named credential holder and the proposed work scope.
> - Confirm customer authority, written work authorization, payment terms, and a script that does not promise coverage or negotiate the insured's claim.
> - Complete the task-specific hazard assessment, required respiratory protections, electrical checks, and emergency contact briefing before exposure.
> - Reserve the job's expected cash outlay, assign two available field technicians, and confirm who will review drying records before invoicing.

## 2. Days 8-14: Generate Qualified Demand and Build Defensible Job Files  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Week two tests whether a narrow service promise produces qualified demand without exhausting the pod. Build a list of thirty local referral accounts across plumbers, property managers, roofers, and facilities operators. Seek ten substantive conversations by Day 14 and twenty by Day 30; count a conversation only when someone discusses an actual service need, territory, and response expectations. Ask for inclusion on an emergency contact list rather than implying insurer endorsement. Record referral source, loss date, property location, customer authority, apparent hazard, and requested service in a simple CRM. A qualified opportunity requires a reachable authorized buyer, an in-territory loss, a permitted scope, and an agreed next action. An unanswered contact form does not qualify. Test phone coverage within five minutes during advertised staffed hours, but offer arrival times only after checking crew location and hazards. Because the canonical time to first deal is one to six months, zero completed jobs during this week does not independently invalidate the business. It does prohibit claims that unit economics have been demonstrated. Track outreach effort and conversion by source before purchasing more leads; expensive calls outside the licensed service scope merely accelerate cash consumption.

Create one job identifier across CompanyCam or an equivalent photo system, Xactimate, dispatch, and QuickBooks Online job costing. For water mitigation, capture the source condition, affected materials, room dimensions, moisture map, and a suitable unaffected reference where available. Record temperature and relative humidity with location, timestamp, instrument identification, and relevant psychrometric calculations; distinguish ambient conditions from material moisture readings. Equipment placement and changes need a stated drying objective, not just a photograph of running machines. Track daily readings while active drying continues and investigate stalled progress before extending rental days. Fire files require material-specific cleaning tests, soot observations, and decisions about salvage versus replacement. Mold files need the assessed scope, containment plan, exposure controls, and any jurisdiction-required assessment or clearance arrangements. Storm files need safe-access decisions, temporary protection details, and separation of emergency work from reconstruction. Photos support facts; they do not establish policy coverage. Keep original files and edit history, restrict access to assigned staff, and exclude unnecessary occupant documents from images. Have the estimator audit the file before the crew demobilizes, while missing measurements can still be recovered without another disruptive visit to the property.

Use the estimator's first Xactimate exercise to expose unsupported line items before a customer sees an invoice. Select the relevant price list and date, measure quantities, distinguish removal from replacement, and attach evidence for equipment duration, labor exceptions, and material selection. A supplement should explain a factual difference between the documented scope and the earlier estimate; it is not a device for adding unperformed work. Separate contractor pricing communication from activity requiring a public-adjuster license. The accounts-receivable coordinator can send the contractor's invoice, provide records, explain payment terms, and request status without making coverage arguments on the customer's behalf. For residential work disturbing painted surfaces in covered pre-1978 housing, evaluate EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting requirements under 40 CFR Part 745 before demolition. Evaluate asbestos and other suspect materials through qualified procedures rather than assuming every wet wall is safe to remove. On Day 14, score the complete simulated file and any live files against the same checklist. Require every critical authorization and safety item, plus at least 95% of all applicable documentation fields. Record legitimate inapplicability with a specific reason instead of blank cells or invented measurements.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Make the File Collectible While the Crew Is Still There**
> 
> Experienced restoration operators prevent collection friction at the property instead of trying to repair the record weeks later.
> - Photograph meter placement and record the material being measured; a detached screen reading cannot establish where moisture remained.
> - Save the Xactimate price-list identifier and estimate revision history so a supplement can explain changed quantities without obscuring the original scope.
> - Record why each dehumidifier or air mover remains deployed, then compare drying progress before extending equipment days.
> - Call the customer before the final crew visit to identify access, punch-list, and invoice-delivery problems while correction still costs one trip.

## 3. Days 15-21: Run Controlled Production and Close the Cash Loop  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Week three converts preparation into a controlled production cycle when authorized demand exists. Dispatch only after confirming the signed scope, site contact, hazard review, equipment availability, and cash reservation. Hold a fifteen-minute morning huddle covering the two field technicians, contractor commitments, pending estimates, and collection blockers. Count travel, setup, cleaning, equipment handling, and documentation as scheduled work rather than hiding them outside utilization. Cap committed field hours at 80% of paid available hours during the pilot, leaving the balance for emergency response, training, and corrections. Do not imply around-the-clock coverage from two technicians without a funded lawful rota. If a storm creates multiple calls, triage by safety, authorized scope, and capacity; refer excess work to vetted alternatives with the customer's permission instead of accepting impossible arrival commitments. Contractor mobilization must identify the individual performing the work and the employee reviewing it. The estimator remains accountable for scope changes even when a trade contractor proposes them. On a quiet day, run a timed tabletop exercise and label it as such. A rehearsal can prove routing and recordkeeping, but it cannot prove customer conversion, realized margin, or payment speed.

Close daily job costs before discussing growth. Code technician hours, payroll burden, contractor invoices, consumed materials, disposal, equipment rental, and attributable transport to the job. Reconcile how employee labor appears in the contribution calculation: if regular payroll remains in fixed overhead, do not deduct it again as variable cost. Also calculate a fully burdened job margin that allocates regular labor, so apparently attractive jobs cannot conceal inefficient field production. For the gate model, contribution means collectible billed revenue less incremental job costs, with regular payroll held in fixed overhead. Use a 45% minimum pilot contribution target and show disputed amounts separately from collectible revenue. For example, an authorized $10,000 job with $5,500 incremental costs contributes $4,500 before fixed payroll and overhead; it does not produce $4,500 owner profit. A further $1,000 unsupported supplement stays outside the gate calculation until the amount has a defensible contractual basis and collection assessment. Review purchase commitments as well as paid invoices, because an unpaid subcontractor bill still consumes runway. Flag any job whose revised direct-cost forecast rises more than 10% above its approved budget, and investigate quantities, crew productivity, and scope before accepting another similar loss.

Use Days 19-21 to exercise the entire collection chain, not merely produce an attractive estimate. Deliver the completed work packet within two business days after the relevant milestone, subject to actual contract billing terms. Confirm receipt with the responsible payer and customer, record objections verbatim, and assign each objection to scope evidence, work quality, billing error, or coverage discussion requiring a separately authorized professional. Do not tell the customer that insurer delay erases contractual obligations, and do not promise that the insurer will pay. Handle deductibles according to the contract and applicable state restrictions; Texas Insurance Code § 707.002 prohibits specified deductible assistance by providers of insurance-funded services. Never manufacture invoice credits to conceal a prohibited waiver. Track receivables from invoice date and also track days past contractual due date, because those measures answer different questions. Prepare a thirteen-week cash forecast with weekly receipts, payroll, taxes, trade payments, rentals, and committed purchases. Stress it by delaying expected claim-related receipts thirty days. The owner reviews the lowest projected cash balance, not just the ending balance, and reduces commitments before an otherwise profitable job causes a payroll shortfall. Collections activity must remain inside the contractor's permitted role throughout.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Release a bounded assignment
   - Verify scope, customer authority, hazard controls, and the job cash reservation.
   - The dispatcher records the assigned field pair, available equipment, escalation contact, and promised response window before mobilization.
2. Capture evidence during production
   - Record measurements, quantities, crew time, equipment decisions, and approved scope changes.
   - The lead technician uploads original records daily; the estimator checks critical omissions before the next site visit.
3. Reconcile scope against actual cost
   - Match completed quantities to cost codes and the supported invoice.
   - Show incremental contribution and fully burdened job margin separately, with disputed charges excluded from the gate calculation.
4. Confirm receipt and forecast cash
   - Send the billing packet, confirm delivery, and update expected receipts.
   - The coordinator assigns objections without negotiating coverage; the owner checks the stressed weekly cash trough before accepting more work.

## 4. Days 22-30 and Reviews at 60/90: Kill, Fix, or Scale on Evidence  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Finish the month with a deliberate closing sequence. Day 22 reconciles every open authorization and purchase commitment. Day 23 audits all pilot files, including failed or declined assignments, so the review does not cherry-pick easy losses. Day 24 interviews referral partners and customers about response, clarity, and unfinished work. Day 25 ranks lead sources by qualified opportunities rather than call volume. Day 26 reprices the least attractive scope using observed costs. Day 27 runs the thirty-day receipt-delay stress test again. Day 28 checks actual employee hours and contractor availability against the next fortnight's commitments. Day 29 prepares a decision packet containing cash, aging, documentation, safety, contribution, and conversion measures. Day 30 records a signed Kill, Fix, or Scale decision with the owner, due date, and next cash ceiling. These thresholds are proposed management rules, not scorecard forecasts or insurer standards. Apply the strictest failed condition first. A critical safety or licensing defect stops the affected service immediately, regardless of sales. Insufficient evidence normally means Fix with a bounded test, not a fabricated passing score. Kill means stopping new commitments in the failed play and arranging an orderly completion or transfer of existing obligations.

At Day 30, permit only limited scale within the existing pod when at least three completed, cost-reconciled customer jobs support a weighted contribution margin of 45% or better, every critical record is present, overall documentation reaches 95%, and post-commitment cash covers sixty days of fixed overhead plus forecast incremental outlays. Without that sample, use Fix even if the rehearsal was excellent. Twenty referral conversations producing fewer than three qualified opportunities trigger a fourteen-day channel repair experiment; they do not prove that a one-to-six-month sales cycle has failed. At Day 60, require at least six completed jobs, 90% on-time billing, no more than 15% of receivables over sixty days old, and the same cash and contribution tests before adding volume. At Day 90, require ten completed jobs and two consecutive months with realized contribution covering actual fixed overhead. Receivable tests use dollars, not invoice counts; disputed amounts remain visible in the denominator. Fewer observations keep the decision in Fix with an explicit cash limit. If funded runway falls below thirty days and no committed replenishment or executable cost reduction exists, stop acquisition immediately rather than waiting for the next calendar review.

Scale the bottleneck that evidence identifies, not the owner's enthusiasm. A Day 30 pass permits at most a 20% increase in accepted weekly job hours while preserving the 80% scheduling ceiling; it does not authorize another full-time hire. A Day 60 pass may expand a proven referral source within existing capacity. Moving toward the Scaled configuration requires the Day 90 tests, a funded role-specific hiring plan, and explicit conversion from five employees plus six contractors to six employees plus seven contractors. That is thirteen people, not an automated eleven-person pod. Keep canonical annual economics intact: $500,000 gross restoration billings at 8% net produces $40,000 owner profit; $1,500,000 at 13.0% produces $195,000; $2,500,000 at 18% produces $450,000. The corresponding total annual cost envelopes are $460,000, $1,305,000, and $2,050,000. Materials and subcontractors belong inside those costs. Customer claim totals, property values, and insurer reserves are not operator revenue. The launch budget and contribution tests do not derive these annual net margins; actual overhead must be reconciled separately. In particular, the illustrative launch cost structure needs repair before it could support the Conservative outcome. Dispatch automation, photo capture, and job-cost tracking improve control, but cannot replace licensed judgment, field labor, or collectible customer demand.
