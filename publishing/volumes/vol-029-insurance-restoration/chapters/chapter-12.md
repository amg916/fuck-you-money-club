## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner who touches a hail roof agrees that documentation, not opinion, moves money. Adjuster TV's Matt, a CAT adjuster since 1999, walks every house front, right, back, left on every file [6084H_VeZTA @ 00:40:37], then shoots roughly 30 to 50 roof photos and 90 to 120 photos total; that photo set later refuted a roofer who ripped shingles off after the inspection. Matt also says no-damage findings demand even more proof than paid ones. Chris Zimmerman frames the same principle from the sales side: the linchpin of restoration roofing is evidence that replacement, not repair, is necessary, because insurers 'generally speaking don't want to pay' [1quXRyfkTgo @ 00:04:12]. Homeowner Explained pushes owners to build the file themselves: photograph soft metals and AC fins, check downspouts for granules, use a coin for scale, turn on date stamps, and save the weather report [MPSOkm3YFak @ 00:03:35]. On commercial work, RestoreMasters' Kevin reports that the formal walkthrough and claim reports at West Georgia Technical College uncovered more damage than either the crew or the client expected, including a damaged pipe, dinged metal siding, windows, doors, and skylight-driven interior damage, all of which had to be returned to pre-loss condition [lkJlJQH7bVw @ 00:01:58]. The working rule across all four: if it is not photographed, measured, and written down, it does not get paid.

The practitioners also agree on what real hail damage looks like, and what it does not. Claim Daddy teaches that genuine hail bruises the shingle mat, while pulverized granules, quarter-shaped gouges, and non-random field hits point to mechanical damage, and that a contractor damaging a roof leaves a non-random pattern that gives the game away [yytgxtBGSF8 @ 00:30:46]. Matt draws a hard line on masonry: 'ice is not harder than rock,' so brick is never paid as hail no matter how big the ice balls were [6084H_VeZTA @ 00:43:04]. On TPO, the damage hides. RestoreMasters' John McDonald reads hail splatter for volume, density, and direction, feels for indentations at large-splatter spots, then cuts into and peels back the membrane; impact dents in the ISO board mean it is compromised and must be replaced [rw8dEal_Ppk @ 00:00:42]. Kevin's crew at LaGrange found hail holes so numerous and small that 'that's why we have to get up on personal with these things,' because the dirty, weathered membrane hides them from a casual walk [lkJlJQH7bVw @ 00:00:40]. The shared standard is that the inspector's eyes get within inches of the surface, whether on a residential slope or a commercial deck, and that splatter, soft metals, and AC fins establish that a storm actually hit the property before anyone argues scope or price.

The third point of agreement is how storm money actually moves, and it is never one check. Matt explains ACV to homeowners as 'used car value,' holding back roughly 10% on a three-year-old 30-year shingle, and his F9 script tells the insured that withheld depreciation, such as 40% on a 10-year-old roof with a 25-year lifespan, is released once they send the final contractor bill [ccWMQpIzp_w @ 00:04:49]. Chris Zimmerman lays out the same chain from the contractor seat: a certificate of completion releases withheld depreciation, the lender may have to sign the check, and payment lands 30 to 60 days after completion [1quXRyfkTgo @ 00:02:47]. Homeowner Explained adds the deductible reality: wind/hail deductibles run 1 to 5%, so 2% on a $300,000 home is $6,000, and 'if your damage is less than that, filing won't pay you anything' [MPSOkm3YFak @ 00:05:04]. Claim Daddy's training file applies 1% of $450,000 for a $4,500 deductible and notes southern wind/hail deductibles commonly sit at 1 to 2% [yytgxtBGSF8 @ 00:11:49]. Anyone selling, building, or adjusting storm roofs has to know the deductible, the ACV/RCV split, and the recoverable depreciation path before quoting a homeowner a single number, or the deal collapses at the kitchen table.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Matt's full-replacement build in Xactimate is the clearest line-by-line playbook. Tear-off of comp shingles goes in at 24 squares, all labor, and already includes vents, ridge cap, starter, and valley; never add dumpsters, pickups, or dump trucks because haul-off is built in [ccWMQpIzp_w @ 00:07:03]. The 25-year shingle goes in replace-only at 24*1.1 for 10% waste, while felt stays at 24 squares because 'there's no waste on felt.' Depreciation runs age over lifespan, 10/25 = 40%, applied by selecting first through last line, then Global Changes, then Depreciation; Ctrl+M opens macros like the '240' built on SQ*1.15 with edge, ridge, and valley variables [ccWMQpIzp_w @ 00:20:22]. On the hail walkthrough Matt imports a Hover ESX into the sketch with Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V, enters waste as *1.15, and catches that ridge alone reads 43.47 LF when ridge plus hip is 250 LF [6084H_VeZTA @ 01:30:29]. Claim Daddy layers in detail: 15% hip waste, ridge vent covering 80% of the ridge entered as 0.8R, ridge cap held out of waste and handled as remove-and-replace with caution, and storm direction read from splatter marks [yytgxtBGSF8 @ 00:43:38]. Every file then gets resequenced, print-previewed, and checked for a missing deductible before it leaves the desk.

Repair-versus-replace math gets numeric fast. Claim Daddy runs a Haag-based Roof Dura worksheet at $24.80 per shingle against $368.78 per square, applies a repair difficulty factor of 1, 1.5, or 2, and lands on replacing the rear and left slopes while repairing the right with 22 shingles, warning 'this does not include vents' [yytgxtBGSF8 @ 00:26:11]. Matt's wind-repair estimate prices RFG 300E per shingle, noting 'that's actually 33 dollars a single,' writes 30 shingles with no difficulty factor on a new roof, removes and replaces 13 LF of ridge cap, and adds 1 SQ steep at 7/12 to 9/12 because 'there's 67 shingles in a square' [lIyYVIMZa0s @ 00:05:19]. On commercial roofs, Chad Michael's El Paso play is to 'take that coverage cuz it opens the door' when the carrier offers coating only, then show that recoating over water-soaked layers violates code, forcing tear-off to the deck and R25 insulation, which means adding ISO to the estimate [Op9Qkl-qW58 @ 00:05:05]. He reports winning big many times by getting carriers to pay ACV on every layer present, then amending to an invoice for incurred work [Op9Qkl-qW58 @ 00:10:03]. For tile, shake, slate, and metal he skips walking and inspects by 4K drone video, pulling screen grabs.

Crew and pricing economics are where storm money is made or bled. Adam Bensman targets $250 to $450 per square on the scope of loss and calls $190 per square unprofitable, collects a build deposit of 50% or the ACV check, whichever avoids homeowner out-of-pocket, and canvasses neighbors on build day [-aZHNsY5qIY @ 00:04:06]. RoofCoach prices on margin: overhead runs 20 to 35% of every job, net profit must hit at least 10%, and a $10,000 cost job priced at 1.35 markup lands at $13,500, 'short almost $2,000,' while $10,000 divided by 0.65 gives the correct $15,384 [gLFs8l5O0j4 @ 00:02:17]. ProLine's Scott runs about 400 jobs a year at 15% overhead, expects at least 20% per job, and warns that 54% markup is only about 35% margin, after one price sheet left out 7% sales tax entirely [g3I-8o2x140 @ 00:46:37]. On paying the crew, Digital Roofing Innovations splits a $3,000 sub cost on a $10,000 roof into $1,500 at start and $1,500 at completion, uses 20/40/60/80% progress payments on large jobs, verifies workers' comp and GL before every project, and holds final payment until the homeowner signs off [tVMI8UScmmE @ 00:03:11]. Callbacks, which Scott pegs at about $1,000 each, eat whatever margin the pricing math failed to protect.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pre-Inspection Policy and Deductible Check
   - Pull prior claims, endorsements, and deductible math before the first call.
   - Check prior claims first, such as a $27,000 roof payment two years back, watch for an ACV roof endorsement left on a new roof, and run the deductible: $1,000 plus 1% wind/hail on $350,000 equals $3,500 [Adjuster TV — 6084H_VeZTA @ 00:32:35].
2. Elevations, Roof Checks, and Photo Set
   - Walk front, right, back, left, then measure the roof before shooting photos.
   - Take pitch across the shingle edges plus layers, gauge, and valley metal before photos, then build the 30-50 roof photo set that later defends the file [Adjuster TV — 6084H_VeZTA @ 00:50:07].
3. Write, Resequence, and Self-Check the Estimate
   - Build from the estimate items area, apply depreciation, then preview and fix errors.
   - Resequence line numbers, print preview, check spelling and placeholder x's, confirm depreciation shows, and add the $1,000 deductible before sending [Adjuster TV — ccWMQpIzp_w @ 00:16:08].
4. Pay the Crew Against Verified Work
   - Verify insurance, contract every job, and release pay on milestones.
   - Use 20/40/60/80% progress payments on large jobs, agree per-square or per-job pay up front, and enforce 4-6 nails per shingle after a crew that shot 2 had to tear off and got dropped [Digital Roofing Innovations — tVMI8UScmmE @ 00:07:03].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in storm roofing is how hard to push across the table. Matt at Adjuster TV, a CAT property adjuster since 1999, refuses to get pulled into philosophical ridge and starter debates with contractors and tells adjusters flatly not to fight with people, because there is nothing else to do once the scope is set [6084H_VeZTA @ 00:08:31]. Adam Bensman of The STRONG Roofer runs the opposite posture from the contractor seat: sign a contingency agreement where the insurer sets the price, the rep promises to go to bat, and a price lock guarantee backs the deal, then attend the adjuster meeting wherever local law allows [-aZHNsY5qIY @ 00:02:36]. Chris Zimmerman frames the middle path, calling scope-and-price agreement on the supplement the biggest breakdown in restoration and pointing operators toward a sweet spot in the middle rather than total victory [1quXRyfkTgo @ 00:03:02]. Chad Michael plays a longer game on commercial roofs: when a carrier offers coating-only coverage, take it, because it opens the door, then use the code fact that recoating over water-soaked layers is not allowed to force a full tear-off to the deck [Op9Qkl-qW58 @ 00:03:23]. The contrast is clean: adjusters de-escalate and document, while contractors stack leverage step by step.

Even inside one channel the line-item math moves. Matt enters 10% waste by hand on a 24-square three-tab tear-off, yet the saved 240 macro Matt swears by runs SQ*1.15, and Matt still ranks macros as the fastest way to write estimates bar none [ccWMQpIzp_w @ 00:20:22]. Claim Daddy applies 15% waste on a hip roof, excludes ridge cap from the waste calculation, and pays remove-and-replace ridge only with caution [yytgxtBGSF8 @ 00:44:59]. Matt draws a harder line on ridge: special ridge cap is not paid when the original was cut from 3-tab, and the scope gets drawn on graph paper from a physical walk, never skipped from photos [6084H_VeZTA @ 02:08:20]. Chad Michael argues the reverse for tile, shake, slate and metal, flying them with a 4K drone and pulling screen grabs, because walking forces you to be careful and you rarely cover the whole roof [Op9Qkl-qW58 @ 00:14:49]. Two operating philosophies sit underneath. The adjuster camp wants a defensible scope that survives QA review and proves the loss. The contractor camp wants coverage of every square foot and every layer, and Chad Michael reports winning on a big level many times by getting carriers to pay the ACV value of every layer present. Pick the camp before picking the tool.

Pricing and labor models split operators into volume players and margin purists. RoofCoach teaches margin, not markup: overhead runs 20 to 35% of every job, net profit must clear 10%, and a $10K cost marked up by 1.35 to $13,500 lands short almost $2,000 against the correct $10K divided by 0.65, about $15,384 [gLFs8l5O0j4 @ 00:01:59]. On ProLine, Scott runs roughly 400 jobs a year at 15% overhead, expects at least 20% per job, and admits about 5 jobs land at cost, while Joel in Niagara Falls clears $300K to $500K solo carrying 40% overhead [g3I-8o2x140 @ 00:29:39]. Adam Bensman reads the insurance scope itself as the price gate, targeting $250 to $450 per square and calling $190 per square unprofitable [-aZHNsY5qIY @ 00:04:06]. Labor splits too. Scott partners with a labor LLC carrying workers comp, while competitors pay 1099 labor $15 an hour, a wage that costs Scott $20 on W2, and AJ warns the FLSA change effective March 11 eliminates 1099 sales reps. Roofing Insights with Kareem Hunter estimates 20% of installers nationally and 70 to 80% in Texas are illegal, notes firms doing 1,000 roofs a year do not run all in-house crews, and pushes a funded trade high school so roofers earn $100K to $200K by age 20 [FqdbOCycivo @ 00:09:12].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive adjuster mistakes are promises and sloppy files, not missed hail. Matt watched a claim go sideways after promising replacement cost on a 40x60 outbuilding holding more than $100K in contents, only to learn the insured had declined the PPRC endorsement to save $91 a year, leaving contents at ACV under the HO3 [6084H_VeZTA @ 00:19:58]. On a simple wind repair, Matt's own self-check catches a missing deductible on the claim summary, depreciation showing despite a no-depreciation note, labor minimums, and a stray copper-gutter note on a house with no gutters, all of which trigger kickbacks [lIyYVIMZa0s @ 00:08:03]. Claim Daddy reports QA rejecting a seal line written at 35.2 square feet when only the replaced 32 square feet should be sealed, and caps labor minimums at three per estimate [yytgxtBGSF8 @ 01:18:33]. Matt also warns that dragging line items onto the sketch can drop them outside the building in the grouping tree, so estimates get written from the estimate items area as the simplest and safest route [ccWMQpIzp_w @ 00:18:19]. Photos are the backstop: 30 to 50 roof shots and 90 to 120 overall let Matt refute a roofer who ripped shingles off after the inspection, and test squares stay off the contractor's square.

Crew economics break on two fronts: bad installs and unpaid invoices. Digital Roofing Innovations reports a crew shooting 2 nails per shingle on an Alabama job where code called for 4, against a manufacturer spec of 4 to 6; the shingles had to be torn off, money was deducted from the crew, and the crew was dropped. The same channel says to verify workers comp and GL before every project or risk being fined [tVMI8UScmmE @ 00:05:13]. Kareem Hunter, subcontracting out of Tulsa in 2004 with a 6-man crew doing 20 squares a day, got paid up front on a 100-square job and then never got paid for the 300-square job that followed, calling it 'my fault' for moving too fast [FqdbOCycivo @ 00:01:46]. Hunter estimates 10 to 20% of inexperienced subs risk not getting paid, and bigger companies shortchange with unreasonable reasons such as a $1,000 cleanup fine on a $2,000 bill [FqdbOCycivo @ 00:02:51]. The rule that follows: settle documentation and the agreement before the first nail, and never carry 3 unpaid jobs into more work. On the contractor side, Scott at ProLine treats a sub certificate covering 20 employees on only $100K of payroll as a red flag and refreshes certificates regularly [g3I-8o2x140 @ 00:01:17].

Pricing and evidence traps quietly drain jobs that look won. RoofCoach tells of Mark, who lost $7K on an $85K job after pricing with a competitor's markup, the same error that leaves a $10K cost marked up 1.35 short almost $2,000 [gLFs8l5O0j4 @ 00:04:33]. ProLine traces profit loss to the sale itself and to callbacks running about $1,000 each, warns that a 54% markup is only about a 35% margin, and caught 7% sales tax missing from the price sheet entirely [g3I-8o2x140 @ 00:47:13]. On the evidence side, MaprBot's Mid-Atlantic recap logged 34 preliminary SBC reports with a largest stone of 1.75 inches at Burlington Township, NJ, yet stresses that reflectivity is not hail diameter, model radar is a forecast rather than proof of hail at a given property, reports may arrive late or be revised, and a list of reported locations is not a complete damage survey and offers no neighborhood all clear [wQbjMlmo1Bg @ 00:01:55]. RestoreMasters shows the flip side on a TPO campus roof at West Georgia Technical College: small round impacts hide on dirty, weathered membrane, and the claim walkthrough turned up more damage than either the contractor or the client expected, including a damaged pipe, dinged metal siding, windows, doors and skylight-driven interior damage [lkJlJQH7bVw @ 00:01:58].
