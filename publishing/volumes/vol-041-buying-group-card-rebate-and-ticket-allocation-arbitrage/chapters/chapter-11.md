## 1. Assign eleven play responsibilities without building eleven jobs  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Treat the eleven mapped plays as responsibilities inside one cash-conversion system. This chapter uses working labels rather than assuming an unpublished catalog of play names: P01 buying-group qualification, P02 committed purchase-order sourcing, P03 retail procurement, P04 insured shipment and acceptance, P05 buying-group settlement recovery, P06 card-cycle scheduling, P07 rebate capture, P08 lawful ticket acquisition, P09 multi-exchange ticket distribution, P10 seat-license and corporate-suite allocation brokerage, and P11 repricing and exit liquidation. In the three-person pod, the full-time owner controls P01, P02, P06, and P08 purchase authorization. The first contractor owns evidence assembly across P03 through P05 and P07; the second executes P09 through P11 within documented price and inventory limits. Execution ownership does not grant payment authority. Every purchase requires a recorded commitment identifier, basis ceiling, settlement expectation, and authorized card before money moves. The owner reviews exceptions rather than retyping receipts. A ticket specialist may propose an allocation but cannot both create its inventory record and certify its delivery. This division covers all eleven plays while keeping cash release, source evidence, and performance reporting separate. Give each responsibility one primary operator and one named backup; the backup uses individual access and the same evidence checklist.

Design contractor engagements around accepted deliverables, service windows, and demonstrable independence, not employee-like supervision disguised by an invoice. A starting commercial model is $600 monthly for the reconciliation package plus $3 for each accepted buying-group order packet, and $500 monthly for ticket inventory maintenance plus $4 for each completed ticket-order evidence packet. These are illustrative negotiated prices, not embedded scorecard assumptions. At 120 buying-group packets and 80 ticket packets, combined monthly fees are $1,780 before any separately agreed specialist work. Require complete order packets within one business day after supporting records become available, inventory exceptions acknowledged within two contracted coverage hours, and unresolved cash variances escalated that day. Pay for accepted work, with a documented dispute process; do not reward contractors for increasing card spend or hiding losses. Classification turns on the actual relationship under applicable federal and state tests. Where the business needs continuous direction, fixed shifts, or exclusive service, price an employee arrangement instead. Keep agreements specific about deliverables, correction windows, confidentiality, customer records, and permitted tools. A small team cannot absorb ambiguous responsibility when two exchanges sell the same allocation or a buying group disputes a carton.

Expand to five people by adding an employed operations controller and a third contractor for shipment inspection and allocation verification. The founder remains the commercial and treasury lead; the controller owns the weekly close, exception routing, and access administration. The original contractors retain their specialty deliverables rather than inheriting unbounded managerial duties. Add capacity only after four consecutive weeks of measured overload, such as more than twenty unresolved evidence packets at close or repeated breaches of the agreed ticket coverage window. First remove duplicate entry through the buying-group deal-commit reconciler and SkyBox or Automatiq integrations. Confirm supported exchange functions in the contracted product before relying on synchronization. Every operator uses an individual account, multifactor authentication where supported, and minimum necessary permissions. No contractor receives the founder's banking credentials or unrestricted issuer access. Conduct a quarterly absence exercise in which the backup completes settlement matching and an event delivery cycle without founder intervention. Log every question requiring the founder's memory, then convert the answer into a controlled procedure. Transferability begins with that exercise, because a buyer purchases a repeatable operation rather than a collection of personal relationships and undocumented judgment calls.

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Buy back founder time without buying hidden settlement risk**
> 
> Contract around evidence quality and exception closure. Cheap task completion becomes expensive when nobody can prove where the cash or allocation went.
> - Batch buying-group packets by commitment identifier and receiving location; carton-level proof resolves partial acceptance disputes faster than a single shipment screenshot.
> - Give the ticket contractor a pre-event markdown ladder and minimum contribution authority, then reserve exceptional overrides for the treasury lead.
> - Export supported SkyBox or Automatiq inventory and settlement reports before changing integrations; reconcile the first complete event cohort after every configuration change.
> - Run the founder-absence exercise during an ordinary event week, not a quiet holiday, and measure unanswered questions as operating debt.

## 2. Run a weekly cash-and-quality scorecard with enforceable exposure limits  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Close the weekly scorecard on Monday using the prior Sunday's cutoff, with separate columns for accrued contribution, settled cash, and unresolved exceptions. The founder signs a short decision record covering new purchase capacity, buying-group exposure, card maturities, and upcoming event liquidations. Measure buying-group settlement against the actual Net-3 or Net-5 agreement, including its business-day convention and acceptance trigger. Report median settlement lag alongside the oldest unpaid accepted order; averages alone conceal the receivable most likely to consume liquidity. Measure first-pass packet acceptance as complete accepted packets divided by submitted packets, targeting at least 98% over a rolling four weeks. Separate contractor defects from missing upstream evidence, but keep both visible in the unresolved queue. For tickets, report accepted sales with confirmed delivery, duplicate listing incidents, cancellation cost, and unsold basis by event date. Use zero duplicate sales as an operating target, not an assumption baked into profit. Each metric needs an owner, a reproducible query or export, and a stated response when it breaches its threshold. A dashboard without a corresponding purchase restriction or correction assignment merely documents deterioration after capital has already been committed.

Enforce a maximum 25% share for each economically related customer or buying-group counterparty, measured separately against trailing ninety-day retained contribution and current unsecured receivables. Group affiliated entities together rather than counting different trading names as diversification. Also cap any single event at 25% of open ticket basis, and any ticket settlement platform at 25% of outstanding ticket receivables; use a tighter limit where one outage could prevent card payment. Test concentration before each proposed purchase, including already committed but undelivered orders. During startup, a first counterparty mathematically represents all exposure. Do not describe that book as diversified: assemble diversified pilot commitments, use prepaid or simultaneously settled terms where practical, or postpone unsecured scale until the cap is achievable. A breach freezes incremental exposure to the affected counterparty while collections and permitted sales continue. Reopening requires a documented calculation below the limit, not the founder's confidence in a familiar contact. Evaluate concentration at the economic obligor level for corporate suites and seat-license brokerage too. Multiple attendees funded by one corporate account remain one customer for credit-risk purposes, even when the ticket platform issues separate order numbers.

Replace the phrase sixty-day float with a dated maturity ladder. A purchase near statement closing can have far less available time, and issuer review can eliminate expected capacity immediately. For every card, track statement close, payment due date, posted balance, pending charges, rebate eligibility, and linked settlement cohorts. Maintain a cash-only coverage ratio of at least 1.0 for statement payments due within fourteen days after reserving taxes and customer refunds. Show expected receipts separately; receivables are not cash coverage. Stress the ladder with a seven-business-day buying-group delay, a temporary issuer spending freeze, and a 20% reduction in expected proceeds from unsold tickets. These are operating stress assumptions, not changes to the canonical annual scenarios. The owner reduces purchases or commits additional available capital before the projected balance becomes negative. Contractor incentives should reward timely, accurate exception closure and loss documentation, not optimistic forecast revisions. Maintain pre-event markdown curves with checkpoints at twenty-one, fourteen, seven, and two days before the event, adjusted for actual transfer deadlines. Never retain speculative inventory merely to protect a weekly margin percentage while the recoverable cash value continues to decay.

> **OPERATOR GATE — Four checks before releasing the next purchasing batch**
> 
> The treasury lead records these checks against the proposed batch, including existing commitments. A failed check blocks additional exposure, not collections.
> - Verify that related-counterparty contribution and unsecured receivable shares remain at or below 25%, and that the proposed event inventory also passes its exposure cap.
> - Verify cash-only coverage of statements due within fourteen days after reserving taxes, refunds, and already authorized payments.
> - Verify buying-group commitment terms, insured shipment requirements, actual card eligibility, and dated expected settlement against the purchase identifier.
> - Verify ticket acquisition provenance, transfer restrictions, total-price disclosure, and listing synchronization; reject queue or CAPTCHA circumvention under 15 U.S.C. section 45c.

## 3. Reconcile accrual cohorts to the canonical owner-profit bridge  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Keep the operating scorecard's gross activity basis separate from the retained revenue bridge. Formula v2.0 uses a 35% retained share for Candidate #41: Conservative gross activity of $250,000 produces $87,500 retained revenue; Base gross activity of $1,025,000 produces $358,750; Scaled gross activity of $1,800,000 produces $630,000. The corresponding 65% merchandise and ticket basis amounts are $162,500, $666,250, and $1,170,000. Remaining deductions reconcile to $67,500, $215,250, and $270,000, leaving exactly $20,000, $143,500, and $360,000 of annual owner profit. Those profits equal 8%, 14.0%, and 20% of gross activity, or 22.86%, 40.0%, and 57.14% of retained revenue, with displayed percentages rounded. Do not deduct the merchandise or ticket basis a second time from retained revenue. The scorecard's retained-share presentation is a management bridge; actual ASC 606 gross-versus-net presentation requires a contract-specific principal-versus-agent analysis. A merchant controlling inventory before transfer can require different presentation from a broker arranging a sale. Preserve both the transaction ledger and this management bridge so a buyer can reconcile results without confusing throughput with recognized operator revenue.

Build cohorts around buying-group purchase commitments, ticket events, and individual allocation contracts. Each record carries acquisition date, supplier invoice, accepted quantity, landed basis, card transaction, customer promise, delivery evidence, expected settlement date, and actual cash receipt. Unsold merchandise and tickets remain identified inventory or other appropriate assets until the applicable accounting treatment requires expense recognition or impairment; cash payment alone does not establish a current-period operating loss. Buying-group receivables follow the contractual transfer and acceptance evidence. Ticket collections received before the promised performance is satisfied may represent liabilities rather than earned revenue. Review exchange terms and the operator's actual performance obligation instead of mechanically recognizing every ticket sale on event day. Reconcile pending payouts, refund obligations, and disputed orders separately. Track card rebates by eligible settled purchase and issuer statement, recognizing amounts under a consistent documented policy and reversing clawbacks promptly. Purchase-linked rebates may reduce acquisition cost rather than constitute separate revenue; map their treatment into the scorecard bridge without counting the benefit twice. Tax timing can differ from book timing under 26 U.S.C. section 461 and related inventory rules. Maintain a separate tax reconciliation rather than forcing the operating ledger to imitate the bank feed.

Use the monthly operating-expense range of $1,500 to $9,000 as a planning envelope, not a substitute for the full annual profit bridge. The residual deductions above can contain variable selling fees, freight, labor, losses, and other modeled costs; their exact composition must be supported before presenting historical earnings. Classify each cost once, because landed freight included in inventory basis cannot also remain in operating expense. Contractor packet fees belong to their serviced cohorts or a consistently allocated overhead pool. Separate owner distributions, debt principal payments, and inventory purchases from operating expense, then reconcile cash movement to accrual profit monthly. Preserve statements, settlement exports, receipts, and adjustment approvals in a controlled evidence archive with transaction identifiers. The controller prepares the close; the founder reviews unusual entries and any change in policy. At three people, the founder performs that review directly rather than pretending the pod has a separate finance department. Maintain a twelve-month rolling cash forecast alongside the ledger, including issuer freezes and delayed buying-group settlement. Profitable cohorts can still create a funding crisis when statement dates arrive before cash collections, especially during rapid inventory growth or concentrated event seasons.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the reporting cutoff
   - Export bank, issuer, buying-group, and ticket-platform records for the same weekly cutoff.
   - Retain original exports and record timezone, extraction date, source account, and responsible operator.
2. Match economic cohorts
   - Attach purchases, shipments, allocations, rebates, and settlements to their commitment or event identifiers.
   - Route unmatched transactions to a named exception owner instead of posting unsupported balancing entries.
3. Reconcile contribution and liquidity
   - Produce the gross-to-retained-to-owner-profit bridge and the fourteen-day cash-only coverage calculation.
   - Reconcile inventory basis once, identify refund liabilities, and keep expected receipts separate from available cash.
4. Approve corrections and purchasing capacity
   - Review exceptions, concentration, cohort losses, and statement maturities before authorizing additional commitments.
   - Archive the founder's decision record and assign each correction a responsible person and completion date.

## 4. Package transferable earnings and price the business without fantasy multiples  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Build the sale package while operating, starting with a clean entity ledger, counterparty agreements, access register, and ownership schedule for software and procedures. Contractor-created automation does not automatically belong to the business merely because an invoice was paid. Obtain a signed written copyright assignment covering identified deliverables under 17 U.S.C. section 204(a), plus appropriate rights to modify, maintain, and transfer the work. Identify pre-existing contractor components and third-party license restrictions separately. Do not rely solely on a work-made-for-hire label for commissioned software. Include repositories, deployment instructions for the business's own systems, reconciliation rules, and data dictionaries in the asset schedule. Confirm whether buying-group contracts, ticket exchange accounts, suite allocations, and seat licenses permit assignment or require counterparty consent. Personal card accounts and founder guarantees are not transferable operating assets simply because the business used them. A buyer needs independently approved funding capacity and a workable settlement transition. Acquisition provenance should demonstrate adherence to the BOTS Act, while ticket offers must follow applicable total-price disclosure rules, including 16 CFR Part 464. A valuable book contains reproducible rights and evidence, not screenshots of access that disappears when the founder leaves.

Price the business from normalized earnings, not gross merchandise movement or the canonical owner-profit figure alone. For planning, use an illustrative 1.5 to 3.0 times verified seller's discretionary earnings range for a small owner-operated book; this is an underwriting sensitivity, not a claim about observed transactions. If Base owner profit of $143,500 were independently reconciled to that earnings definition, the range would be $215,250 to $430,500 before debt, cash, working-capital, and transaction adjustments. If replacing unpaid founder duties costs $70,000 annually, the resulting cash-flow proxy is $73,500 before further normalization. At the same illustrative multiples, that sensitivity becomes $110,250 to $220,500; it is not automatically an EBITDA valuation. For a genuinely manager-run Scaled book, a separate 2.5 to 4.0 times normalized EBITDA sensitivity may be useful. Applying it to $360,000 would imply $900,000 to $1,440,000 only if that entire amount actually reconciles to EBITDA after market replacement compensation. The scorecard does not establish that condition. Buyers discount volatile ticket cohorts, nonassignable relationships, concentrated receivables, and issuer dependence. Demonstrated independence and durable collections support negotiation more effectively than an asserted premium multiple.

Separate the operating-business price from the working capital required to keep commitments funded after closing. Agree on a normalized working-capital schedule that identifies collectible receivables, saleable inventory, supplier obligations, customer refunds, and deferred obligations. Determine explicitly whether card balances are debt-like items or included operating liabilities, and prevent double deduction. Event inventory near expiry deserves cohort-level review rather than a blanket valuation at acquisition cost. Any earnout should define collected contribution, returns, rebate clawbacks, accounting consistency, and buyer-controlled operating changes. A holdback tied to proven pre-closing obligations is different from paying twice for ordinary working capital. Offer a bounded transition with named deliverables: counterparty introductions, reconciler handover, one complete statement cycle, and one representative ticket settlement cohort. If sale proceeds fail to compensate for reliable distributions and transition risk, retain the operation as a cash cow with quarterly capital limits and documented founder coverage. The Conservative case yields only $20,000 annually, so transaction costs and replacement labor can overwhelm its sale economics. Revisit the decision using actual trailing earnings, required reinvestment, and owner hours. A premium outcome comes from reducing the buyer's avoidable uncertainty while preserving the business's ability to operate without a sale.
