## Executive Summary

Landmen and mineral owners working these numbers agree on one spine: before a lease the mineral owner holds 100% working interest and 100% net revenue interest; after the lease the lessee takes 100% of the WI and the lessor keeps only the royalty fraction. From there every division-order decimal is built by multiplication: royalty times mineral interest times tract acres over unit acres. Matt Sands turns 16 net acres in a 1,280-acre DSU at a 15% royalty into .001875 and a roughly $937 three-month check estimate. Alyce Hoge and the Land Training drills show 1/8 leaving the operator 0.875 and 3/16 leaving 0.8125, with overrides and NPRIs carved out further. UHD's instructor insists on a lease-level check-and-balance, while Land Training leans on Excel fill-down and summing back to the tract share.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The cleanest consensus across the Land Training sessions and Alyce Hoge's lease breakdown is the grand bargain. Before any oil and gas lease, the mineral owner holds 100% of the working interest and 100% of the net revenue interest, meaning they pay every drilling bill and keep every barrel, while the oil company owns 0% of both. Sign the lease and the lessor hands 100% of the working interest to the lessee, keeping only a royalty fraction in return [eZU5hvGcngg @ 00:03:47]. The Land Training instructor runs the same trade through the Harry Truman fact pattern: Hiroshima Drilling leases all 160 acres for a 1/8 royalty, leaving Truman with 0% working interest and a 0.125 net revenue interest, while the company holds 100% working interest and a 0.875 net revenue interest [PjGnR-YfLy4 @ 00:07:07]. Why would an owner accept that split? Every speaker answers with risk. Hoge prices a well at about $6 million and stresses that a dry hole is the company's loss alone; the royalty owner pays nothing [eZU5hvGcngg @ 00:05:34]. A second Land Training walkthrough puts the cost at a million dollars and up, climbing with every rework, and calls drilling cost prohibitive for most landowners [yWZaJ-ItXKQ @ 00:02:51]. The lessor trades cost exposure for a clean, cost-free slice of production.

The second point of agreement is that pooling shrinks the royalty by acreage, and everyone uses the same multiplication. The Land Training instructor explains that state conservation agencies, the Texas Railroad Commission, Louisiana's Department of Conservation and the Oklahoma Corporation Commission, cap wells per acreage, so owners without a wellbore on their tract still share production in proportion to the acres they contribute [WPeA0rs7SLA @ 00:04:17]. The formula: royalty interest times tract acres divided by unit acres. Hoge runs a lessor with 160 acres in a 640-acre gas unit at 3/16 and lands on 0.046875, warning that these decimals usually run out to eight decimal places [eZU5hvGcngg @ 00:13:51]. Matt Sands, coming at it from the mineral owner's chair, builds the identical equation from three inputs that take under five minutes once gathered: net mineral acres, drilling spacing unit size and lease royalty rate [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:01:35]. Another Land Training walkthrough lands on 1/32, or 0.03125, for 160 of 640 acres at 1/8 [yWZaJ-ItXKQ @ 00:09:51]. Practitioners also agree on where the numbers live: working interest on the joint interest billing deck, net revenue interest on the division order pay sheet. If the operator's decimal disagrees with the owner's math, the owner digs in.

Third, the operator's net revenue interest is always gross working interest minus burdens, and overrides come off the company's side, never the lessor's. The UHD instructor calls net revenue interest the most important number an oil company has, the take-home pay after deductions, and lays out the order: calculate gross working interest first, calculate every burden second, then subtract [oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:00:36]. The Land Training instructor applies it to Adam's 1/8 lease with a 3% override assigned to the geologist: the override is carved out of the working interest, so the company's 0.875 drops to 0.845 and the geologist holds a 0.03 decimal that pays nothing if the well is dry [B5fBIyi1-hw @ 00:04:40]. Non-participating royalties hit the other side of the ledger. When Alpha conveys 2.5% of the oil and gas produced and saved to Bravo and then leases to Charlie at 1/8, Alpha's 12.5% shrinks to 10%, Bravo keeps 2.5%, and Charlie still holds 87.5% [JVbDAxk_YTU @ 00:04:49]. The instructor flags 'produced and saved' as the buzzwords that separate a royalty conveyance from a mineral conveyance, and notes that nobody takes a lease from the NPRI holder [HkEK5QVZAJ0 @ 00:01:14]. Whoever granted the burden absorbs it: the lessee for the override, the mineral owner for the NPRI.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Matt Sands teaches the owner-side workflow as three inputs and one equation. Net mineral acres come from the legal description: gross acres times decimal ownership, so a 1/40 interest, 2.5%, in a 640-acre section yields 16 net mineral acres; if the decimal is unknown, a landman or lawyer's title search running the chain of transfers in county records supplies it [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:06:19]. Drilling spacing unit size comes off the state oil and gas commission's map; in North Dakota, Sands uses the Industrial Commission's GIS map server, turns on the spacing-unit layer, finds the section and reads a 1,280-acre, two-section DSU for a horizontal Bakken well. The royalty rate sits on page one of the lease, in the clause delivering the lessor's share free of cost into the tanks or pipeline, here 15%, pulled from the county clerk and recorder if the owner has no copy [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:10:11]. Then 16 divided by 1,280 times 0.15 equals .001875, the decimal that must match the division order [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:12:22]. Alyce Hoge confirms operators build the same figure, net acres over unit acres times the royalty fraction, which is how those crazy eight-place decimals appear on the pay deck [eZU5hvGcngg @ 00:12:21].

Once ownership splits, the formula gains a factor. The Land Training instructor's spacing-unit formula for co-tenants is royalty interest times mineral interest times tract acres divided by unit acres, and they keep mineral interest as its own term so errors stay traceable [wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:04:21]. The lease itself only shows 160 acres; the actual mineral interest comes from the lease purchase report built off the landman's chain of title, so assuming 100% from the lease face is wrong. In the Brady heirs problem, four children each hold 25% undivided in 160 acres inside a 320-acre unit: Greg at 1/4 royalty nets 0.03125, Peter at 3/16 nets 0.0234375, and Bobby and Cindy at 1/8 net 0.015625 each. The instructor builds it in Excel, typing the formula once with an equals sign, then dragging the fill handle when the skinny plus sign appears to populate every row [wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:10:21]. The single-owner drills stay fast: Franklin Roosevelt's 20 acres in an 80-acre unit at 3/16 come to 0.046875 [kw0rYmoBLXU @ 00:01:34], while Budro's 160 acres in a 160-acre unit keep the full 0.1875 because the tract fills the unit. Ten Marcellus acres at 1/5 in an 80-acre unit work out to 0.20 times 10 over 80, or 0.025 [kC2dYFP0U0g @ 00:01:04].

Burdens get the same discipline. The UHD instructor's Sunrise Oil and Gas example: 100% gross working interest in Homer Murray's 80-acre lease, 12.5% royalty, 5% override to the geologist, so Sunrise pockets 82.5 cents on every dollar [oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:05:22]. Pool the 80 acres into a 320-acre unit and Sunrise's unit gross becomes 0.25, Homer's unit royalty 0.03125, the override 0.0125, because it is tied to Homer's acres, leaving a 0.20625 net. The check: 0.825 times 0.25 must equal 0.20625, and it does [oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:09:36]. The UHD instructor's caution: if Sunrise brings five leases with different burdens, run the check lease by lease, never commingled, or the totals will not reconcile. Always run it, the instructor says, even when you think you aced the math. Land Training checks the co-tenant split the same way, summing royalty and lessee shares back to each owner's mineral interest times the unit factor, as with Greg's 0.5 times 0.25 equaling 0.125 [n2pg2-Q06P8 @ 00:10:23]. On paper, the landman writes the full fraction, 1/8, 3/16 or 1/6, into each co-tenant's lease, never the reduced decimal; the proportionate reduction clause then lets the division order drop Greg's 1/8 to 0.0625 based on what Greg actually owns [PNLVi6Ee0KM @ 00:09:16].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pin down net mineral acres and true mineral interest
   - Multiply gross tract acres by the decimal ownership from the legal description or title work.
   - Sands gets 16 net mineral acres from 2.5% of 640 acres and falls back on a landman's title search when the decimal is unknown [Matt Sands — Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:06:19]; lease administration confirms the actual mineral interest on the lease purchase report, not the lease face [Land Training www.landtraining.net — wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:02:40].
2. Pull unit size and the lease royalty rate
   - Read the DSU from the state commission GIS layer and the royalty from page one of the lease.
   - Sands turns on the spacing-unit layer on the North Dakota map server, reads a 1,280-acre DSU, and takes the 15% royalty from the delivery clause, pulling the lease from the county clerk and recorder if needed [Matt Sands — Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:10:11].
3. Run the formula, then subtract operator burdens
   - Multiply royalty x mineral interest x tract acres / unit acres; for the lessee, subtract every burden from gross WI.
   - The co-tenant formula stacks royalty, mineral interest and tract-over-unit acres [Land Training www.landtraining.net — wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:00:42]; the operator's 0.875 less a 0.03 override leaves 0.845 [Land Training www.landtraining.net — B5fBIyi1-hw @ 00:05:21].
4. Check, reconcile and challenge
   - Run a check-and-balance per lease, compare to the division order, and challenge mismatches.
   - UHD checks 0.825 x 0.25 against the 0.20625 net and runs each lease separately when burdens differ [UHD Continuing and Professional Education — oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:10:00]; Sands goes back to the operator who sent the division order when a verified calculation does not match [Matt Sands — Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:12:07].

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

Matt Sands and the Land Training instructors reach the same decimal through different keystrokes, and the order matters when you hunt for an error. Sands front-loads the ownership fraction: multiply gross acreage by your decimal interest to get net mineral acres, so 2.5% of a 640-acre section yields 16 NMA, then divide by the 1280-acre DSU and multiply by the 15% royalty to land on .001875 [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:06:19]. The Land Training co-tenant playbook refuses to pre-blend: royalty interest times mineral interest times tract acres divided by unit acres. The instructor explains that keeping the mineral interest as its own factor 'helps you know if there's a mistake where you can go back and look at it' [wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:04:43]. For a 100% owner, the pooled-unit drills strip the formula down to royalty times tract acres over unit acres. Problem 2 shows that a 160-acre tract in a 160-acre spacing unit leaves Boudreaux's 3/16 royalty untouched at .1875 [VPrc0ax0-_o @ 00:01:26]. The operator takeaway is a split by use case. Sands's collapsed NMA method is faster for a single mineral owner reading one division order. The separated-factor method scales better when four heirs, three royalty rates, and one spacing unit all hit the same pay deck and you need to isolate which input broke the number.

Sands pitches self-service. With net mineral acres, DSU size, and royalty rate in hand, the math 'should take you less than five minutes,' and the county clerk and recorder website is 'worth giving it a shot yourself first' before paying a landman to pull the lease [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:09:40]. Sands flips that advice for bigger positions. When multiple horizontal wells are being drilled, Sands recommends a petroleum engineer for reserves and a proper production forecast, plus a landman, because you 'could actually own more or less' than you think [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:18:14]. The Land Training instructor routes the grind into Excel instead. After fumbling a hand calculation on Bobby's interest, the instructor says 'this is why we want to use a spreadsheet,' builds royalty, mineral, tract, and unit columns, and drags the formula down to fill every heir's row [wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:08:19]. The UHD instructor adds a verification layer that the other two playbooks do not mandate: 'always do a check and a balance even if it feels cumbersome.' The check multiplies the lease-level take of 82.5 cents on the dollar by the unit gross working interest of 0.25 and confirms it matches the gross-minus-burdens figure of .20625 [oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:08:28]. That gives an operator three playbooks at three cost levels: free solo math, spreadsheet automation, or hired professionals.

The practitioners also split on whose pocket a burden comes out of, and the answer changes every decimal downstream. An overriding royalty rides on the operator. In Situation #3, the oil company assigns a 3% override to its geologist, and that override is 'carved out of the working interest.' The company's 0.875 drops to a 0.845 net revenue interest, while Adam's .125 royalty stays whole [B5fBIyi1-hw @ 00:02:56]. A non-participating royalty rides on the mineral owner instead. In Situation #4, Alpha conveys 2.5% of the oil and gas produced and saved to Bravo, then leases to Charlie at 1/8. Alpha's 12.5% shrinks to a 10% net revenue interest, while Charlie the lessee keeps the full 87.5% [JVbDAxk_YTU @ 00:03:42]. On the company side, the royalty fraction itself is the lever, and Alyce Hoge walks it down the board. A 1/8 royalty leaves the operator 87.5%, 3/16 leaves 81.25%, and a quarter royalty leaves 75%, because the company's net revenue interest 'decreases the higher the royalty goes' [eZU5hvGcngg @ 00:10:32]. Read the conveyance language before touching a calculator. Situation #4 teaches that 'produced and saved' signals a royalty conveyance rather than minerals, and that one phrase decides which party's decimal absorbs the hit [JVbDAxk_YTU @ 00:01:03].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first failure mode is feeding the formula a bad input. The Land Training instructor warns that a lease reading 160 acres never states the lessor's fractional ownership. In the instructor's words, 'if you look at just the lease and it says 160 Acres you might assume that Greg owns 100, that would be incorrect,' so lease administration has to pull the lease purchase report [wUqopw4hh2o @ 00:02:40]. Sands flags a timing trap on production data. State commission sites update monthly and 'may lag a couple of months,' so a May lookup may only surface February or March volumes, and a brand-new well may not show at all [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:13:41]. Price is the next leak. Sands notes that Bakken barrels can clear at 50 dollars when WTI prints 60, because of a price differential tied to transport and processing costs, and that operator hedging blurs the average price on the check stub further [Yy7az5T7_vs @ 00:16:47]. The AAPL study-guide trap is premature math. In Situation #5, a 1/8 lease minus Charlie's 3% NPRI would net David 9.5%, but no lease exists yet. The instructor says 'don't be thinking the answer to this is 9.5%,' because the correct answer is 100% minerals subject to the 3% NPRI [HkEK5QVZAJ0 @ 00:04:30]. Each of these errors produces a clean-looking decimal that is simply wrong.

The second failure mode is fat-fingering decimals, and the instructors commit it live on camera. While checking the four Brady heirs' joint-ownership total, the Land Training instructor stalls mid-multiplication with 'oh I did that wrong sorry.' It takes a third pass before 0.03125 times four reconciles back to the original 1/8 royalty [pjP04Ns4pww @ 00:06:07]. In Pooled Unit Problem 4, a one-fifth royalty on 10 acres inside an 80-acre Marcellus drilling and spacing unit correctly computes to 0.025. It then gets restated aloud as 'a 0.25 is the net revenue interest,' a tenfold slip that would wreck a pay deck if it reached a division order [kC2dYFP0U0g @ 00:01:26]. In the unit co-tenant lesson, the calculator returns 014063 for Peter's royalty share in the 640-acre unit, and the whiteboard receives 01463, a dropped zero that silently shifts the figure [n2pg2-Q06P8 @ 00:06:59]. Alyce Hoge explains why slips like these hide so well. Division order decimals 'usually go out to eight decimal places,' which is how owners end up staring at 'crazy decimals' they cannot eyeball [eZU5hvGcngg @ 00:13:51]. The practitioner fix is structural: separate factor columns, Excel formulas copied down the sheet, and a closing sum that must reconcile.

The third failure mode is running a check that cannot catch the error. The UHD instructor's paycheck analogy is blunt: if the payroll person botches a deduction, take-home pay is wrong. Likewise, 'if we make a mistake anywhere before we get to the net it will affect the net revenue interest' [oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:04:15]. The same instructor caps the method with a warning. When an operator brings five leases with different burdens into one unit, 'you can't co-mingle them.' The check has to run lease by lease, or it will not return the gross-minus-burdens figure [oHKF06pFkh0 @ 00:10:21]. The unit co-tenant lesson supplies a per-owner reconciliation. Greg's royalty share plus the lessee's share must sum to 0.125, which equals his 0.5 mineral interest times the 0.25 unit factor, and the same test holds for Peter and Bobby [n2pg2-Q06P8 @ 00:10:23]. On the paper side, the Land Training instructor warns lease drafters not to write 0.0625 into a lease for a 50% owner. Write 1/8 instead and let the proportionate reduction clause cut the royalty on the division order, because that clause is what gives the operator the right to reduce it [PNLVi6Ee0KM @ 00:09:16]. Skip the clause logic, and the lease and the pay deck stop agreeing.
