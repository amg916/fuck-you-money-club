# Chapter 10: Taxes, Working Interests and Passing Minerals to Heirs

*Percentage and cost depletion, intangible drilling cost deductions, working vs. royalty interest risk and tax treatment, property taxes on minerals, tax strategies for royalty owners, inheritance issues, affidavits of heirship, and family wealth transfer*

## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree on the split that drives every tax and inheritance decision. Royalty owners pay no drilling costs and shelter 15% of gross royalties through percentage depletion. Working interest owners fund wells and write off 65-85% of the ticket as intangible drilling costs against ordinary income. The street mechanics run through Schedule E depletion lines, a tax ID on file with every operator to stop roughly 30% withholding, cost-depletion appraisals costing a couple thousand dollars, and Texas affidavits of heirship recorded in every county to release suspended royalties. The disagreements are sharp. IDC percentages range from 65-80% up to 100%, royalty 1031 eligibility is contested, and working interest reporting splits between K-1 and Schedule C. Troy Eckard calls drilling mandatory above $300-500K of income, while Tracy Lenz says non-op owners are stuck in the middle.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner draws the same first line: a royalty owner carries no drilling bill, while a working interest owner pays the freight. Tracy Lenz, a petroleum engineer and independent appraiser, explains that a royalty owner pays only when paid. Ad valorem tax, severance tax and marketing fees come off the check, and if a well goes negative, the shortfall is netted against future royalties instead of billed out [mHolW0RfGQ0 @ 00:01:03]. On a standard lease the operator takes 80% of revenue while paying 100% of costs. Lenz describes it as the operator 'paying a dollar in order to make 80 cents,' with the royalty share running 20%, 25%, or 18% down to 12% [mHolW0RfGQ0 @ 00:02:42]. Mike of Sovereign Energy Partners reduces the decision to two variables: who pays the costs and who gets what share. He models a 10% working interest in a $1M well that costs $100K, against a royalty position with no capital calls that he calls 'a much more passive form of participation' [J9jZGFyolV0 @ 00:03:26]. Tim Pawul goes further, calling minerals a 'triple net lease of oil and gas' with no ongoing CapEx once they are purchased or inherited [mcQ5KsrbKzc @ 00:05:11]. Matt Sands confirms that royalty owners bear only severance and property taxes plus post-production costs [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:07:58].

The second point of agreement is percentage depletion. Austin at ADKF says the rate is 'typically... 15% of your gross income.' The deduction is unavailable in a net-loss year and is capped at 65% of net income. The IRS lets you take the higher of cost or percentage depletion, never both [GPDm8NftR5U @ 00:00:51]. Lenz walks through the same 15%: $10,000 in 1099-MISC Box 2 yields a $1,500 deduction, leaving 85% taxable. Royalty, mineral, ORRI and working interest owners all qualify [NYvxAlVlAmQ @ 00:23:24]. Lenz describes depletion as a tax write-off 'for you owning less things.' Matt Sands and Justin Williams add the ceiling: the deduction is limited to the smaller of 100% of taxable income from the property or 65% of taxable income from all sources. It also runs against royalties only, never against a lease bonus payment [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:11:58]. On the working interest side, Scott, a Colorado tax lawyer at Protect Wealth Academy, names the big three: tangible drilling costs, intangible drilling costs and depletion. He classes working interest as active income that offsets W-2 wages, while royalties stay passive [J21M9DVmwY8 @ 00:22:26]. The shared operator read is that royalties shelter 15% of the check, while working interests shelter most of the drilling budget itself in year one.

On passing minerals to heirs, the consensus is that paperwork, not tax, is where families lose money. Sands and Williams treat minerals as real property conveyed by deed, 'very similar to conveying a house.' They warn that operators park royalties in suspense until heirship is settled, which they call 'a headache' costing 'several thousand dollar usually in each state' [cO3OBCezwjw @ 00:10:28]. Royalty Clerk's Texas walkthrough opens on exactly that freeze. Walt died three years ago without a will, the checks stopped, and Texas law lets the operator hold royalties without interest while ownership is in reasonable doubt [q5XqULtWaUQ @ 00:00:57]. Pawul lists funds in suspense among the costliest owner mistakes and cites one $10M recovery [mcQ5KsrbKzc @ 00:44:56]. On tax, the speakers agree that inheritance itself triggers no tax that year. The value at the date of death or the alternate valuation date becomes the basis for any later sale, and Sands reports fielding one to two inheritance questions per week [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:04:12]. The same baseline prerequisites recur across channels: know every county where the interest sits, keep a tax ID on file with each operator, and leave heirs the deeds, leases, well lists, check stubs and division orders they will need.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Working interest math starts with the intangible drilling cost bucket. Scott puts IDC at 70-85% of well costs, with small producers reaching 100% and large ones limited to 70%. On a $50K ticket, 85% IDC saves about $16K and bonus depreciation on the $7,500 remainder saves about $3K, for roughly $18,500, or 37%, back [J21M9DVmwY8 @ 00:24:11]. Neil Jesani Tax Advisors runs the same split at 65-80% IDC with the remainder depreciable. The firm quotes bonus depreciation at 40% for 2025 and uses an example of $80K IDC plus $20K depreciable [FagQvbKX71g @ 00:01:05]. Toby Mathis prices the year-one deduction at 70-75%, up to 80%, which turns a $100K investment into about a $70K deduction worth '20-something thousand.' Mathis also flags a 90-day rule: drilling must start within 90 days of year-end to deduct in the year invested [TfG5auc0OXc @ 00:06:17]. Troy Eckard pitches the most aggressive version. Eckard claims that under the 'OBBB,' 100% of the amount invested comes off gross income, so a $2M earner who puts in $1M shows $1M taxable, and that the write-offs are identical for a dry hole and a producer [41VgsNOdrto @ 00:04:57]. Scott's counterweight is that IDC is recaptured as ordinary income on sale, though the time value of money still favors taking the deduction.

For royalty owners, the filing mechanics are specific. Lenz places percentage depletion on Schedule E Part 1: property code 6 on line 1b, income on line 4 and depletion on line 18. 'Gross' means before severance and post-production costs such as gathering, marketing, transportation, processing and fuel. Lease bonuses, advance royalties and easements are excluded [NYvxAlVlAmQ @ 00:32:53]. Lenz's cost depletion formula is basis divided by barrels remaining, times barrels sold, with a switch point around $9-10 per barrel. The 15% rate applies up to 1,000 bbl/day, using a 6:1 BOE conversion [NYvxAlVlAmQ @ 00:39:29]. Sands passes along a listener-CPA tip: an inherited step-up can accelerate cost depletion. The appraisal and EUR it requires cost 'a couple thousand dollars,' though, so it only pays on a substantial interest [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:14:19]. Cash management is the other lever. Without a W-9 or tax ID on file, operators withhold about 30% by law. Once the tax ID is in, nothing is withheld, so owners have to save cash and make estimated payments to avoid penalties [_MHogbd2xzU @ 00:16:53]. Ad valorem bills vary by state: Colorado taxes severed non-producing minerals at about $3-$5 a year, while Texas sends a bill only once a well starts producing.

The heir-side execution is a paperwork sprint. Royalty Clerk lays out the Texas affidavit of heirship, a sworn, notarized statement on the Estates Code §203.002 form. It lists the date of death, every marriage and how it ended, every child living or dead, and whether a will exists. It should ideally be signed by a disinterested neighbor, friend or pastor (operators often want two) and recorded with the county clerk in every county holding minerals [q5XqULtWaUQ @ 00:03:05]. The packet to each company is the recorded affidavit, a certified death certificate, and each heir's address and W-9. It goes with two written questions: what else is needed, and how much has been held [q5XqULtWaUQ @ 00:04:03]. Sands and Williams push structure upstream. A properly funded trust avoids probate, and a family LLC changes ownership without recording a deed. Keeping interests whole in a trust or LLC beats fractionalizing from 100% to 50%, 25%, 12.5% and 6.25% [cO3OBCezwjw @ 00:08:09]. On sale, Sands sets the hold at a year and a day for long-term treatment. An appraisal pays when the inheritance was within 10 years and proceeds reach multiple six figures. Sands cites the 2023 estate exemption at $13.61M per individual, scheduled to sunset at the end of 2025 [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:27:40].

1. Inventory every interest and county

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether an outsider should ever hold a non-operated working interest. Tracy Lenz, a petroleum engineer and independent appraiser, argues that the two best seats are royalty owner or operator. In Lenz's view the non-op owner is stuck in the middle, with no control over spending or timing, and working interest is only worth chasing for industry insiders such as part-time landmen [mHolW0RfGQ0 @ 00:08:06]. Troy Eckard flips that completely. Eckard tells physicians that anyone making over $300,000 to $500,000 a year who is not drilling wells is making a massive mistake, and pitches nearly 100 wells in six years with zero dry holes and roughly 93% of capital back in under two years [41VgsNOdrto @ 00:24:04]. Mike at Sovereign Energy Partners refuses to crown a winner. Neither structure is inherently better, Mike says, and the playbook is to pair working interest for upside with royalty interest as the lower-risk income base, matched to risk tolerance and investment mindset [J9jZGFyolV0 @ 00:05:35]. Scott, a Colorado tax lawyer, cuts against the deduction-driven pitch entirely with a blunt rule: don't invest for the tax benefits. The operator and the deal still have to stand on their own [J21M9DVmwY8 @ 00:40:42].

The 1031 exchange is the most direct factual collision in this chapter. Scott states that royalty and leasehold interests do not qualify, that eligibility turns on state law treating the interest as real property, and that royalty holders should not plan on a 1031 exchange on the back end [J21M9DVmwY8 @ 00:33:58]. Chris Bentley of Bellator, who runs $28 million of Permian royalties, says the opposite. Exchanges run both directions between mineral rights and commercial real estate through a qualified intermediary, and Bentley asks why an investor would not 1031 into a portfolio of mineral interests that pays every month [Ij8eFGyDOC4 @ 00:09:19]. Matt Sands sides with Bentley on the sell side, naming a 1031 into a rental property as a way to defer gains after selling inherited minerals [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:30:35]. Tim Pawul threads the needle with four routes. The first is a minerals DST such as Montego Minerals or Resource Royalty. The second is the 'lazy man's 1031': the seller eats the capital gain and buys into a non-op IDC fund with a GP flip, for roughly a 90% year-one write-off. The other two are public royalty trusts and a direct asset. Pawul warns that you typically cannot 1031 into a fund, and when Pawul's own father sold, the IDC fund won [mcQ5KsrbKzc @ 00:16:14].

Depletion method is the next fork. Austin at ADKF says royalty holders and other passive investors typically default to percentage depletion because it is easier. The IRS lets you take the higher of percentage or cost depletion, never both [GPDm8NftR5U @ 00:03:25]. Tracy Lenz pushes owners off autopilot. After a recent inheritance or purchase, such as an asset with 18 newly drilled wells, cost depletion can win, and methods can be mixed property by property. Lenz's formula is basis divided by barrels remaining, times barrels sold, with the switch point at roughly $9 to $10 per barrel [NYvxAlVlAmQ @ 00:39:29]. Matt Sands relays a listener-CPA tip that an inherited step-up can accelerate cost depletion. Sands limits the play to substantial interests, because the appraisal and EUR work costs a couple thousand dollars [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:13:42]. The allowance itself has long been a political target. In a historical debate clip, Senator Kennedy was pressed on whether the 27.5% allowance was a conspicuous loophole. The senator answered that about 104 commodities carry depletion allowances and that smaller producers were having hard times, and noted a past vote to reduce the allowance only for the largest producers [NAcWqXJVAcs @ 00:01:18]. Lenz tracks two pending bills today, one raising the marginal-well limit from 1,000 to 2,000 barrels, but expects neither to move.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Non-op working interest bleeds cash in ways royalty owners never see. Tracy Lenz walks through the math: $800 of monthly revenue against $1,000 of operator charges, including JOA overhead with COPAS escalation, nets the owner negative $200 every month. The owner cannot force a shut-in and can only audit the operator. Declining to participate triggers roughly a 300% non-consent penalty, and plugging and abandonment at $30,000 to $40,000 per well can make the interest unsellable [mHolW0RfGQ0 @ 00:06:58]. Matt Sands sees that liability land on clients who inherited non-op working interest from an uncle or grandfather, bringing environmental, financial, and plugging exposure they never chose [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:18:02]. On the buy side, Troy Eckard warns that new promoters with no track record are flooding in and marking interests up four to five times, so $100,000 may buy only $20,000 of real interest [41VgsNOdrto @ 00:07:09]. Toby Mathis adds the drilling and liquidity risk. Exploration outfits can see two or three of ten wells come up dry, the money is illiquid, and payback can stretch five to seven years, or about eleven [TfG5auc0OXc @ 00:11:25].

Inheritance is where royalty checks die quietly. Royalty Clerk's Walt scenario starts with a Texas owner who died three years ago without a will. The checks stopped because Texas law lets the operator hold royalties without interest while ownership is in reasonable doubt, and the most common affidavit-of-heirship failure is a missing child [q5XqULtWaUQ @ 00:03:25]. Matt Sands and Justin Williams put a price on that freeze: operators hold royalties in suspense until heirship is decided, and clearing it usually costs several thousand dollars in each state where the minerals sit [cO3OBCezwjw @ 00:10:28]. Williams lived the worst version. Family lore contradicted the will and trust, a marital share split the interests, and title disputes locked up nearly all payments [cO3OBCezwjw @ 00:27:04]. The same hosts warn that recorded deeds trigger unsolicited offers, and the first offer can leave money on the table. Fractionalization from 100% to 50%, 25%, 12.5%, and 6.25% makes buyers discount, because one net mineral acre takes the same work as a hundred. Tim Pawul's list of owner mistakes includes not knowing what you own, decimal errors as small as 0.00000786% versus 0.00000797%, and money left in suspense, citing one recovery of $10 million [mcQ5KsrbKzc @ 00:44:56].

The tax traps hit cash flow before they hit the return. Matt Sands and Justin Williams report that without a W-9 and tax ID on file, operators withhold roughly 30% by law. A lease with no post-production deductions does not exempt you from severance, ad valorem, or production taxes. In their own acquisition, the prior owner kept receiving the county bill while penalties accrued, though penalties may be negotiable if you show you were never notified [_MHogbd2xzU @ 00:16:53]. The same hosts flag the opposite problem: once the tax ID is in, nothing is withheld, so owners must set aside cash and make estimated payments. A zero basis at sale means the entire proceeds are taxed [z9gHz7FYvRk @ 00:06:35]. Scott warns that IDC is recaptured as ordinary income on sale, and that K-1s arrive in June or July, so you should file an extension. Working and royalty interests should never share one entity, though the time value of money still favors taking the deduction [J21M9DVmwY8 @ 00:27:58]. Neil Jesani Tax Advisors adds that royalty income is ordinary income and is also exposed to the net investment income tax [FagQvbKX71g @ 00:04:01]. Each of these hits lands even when the wells perform exactly as planned.
