# Chapter 09: Buying and Selling Mineral Rights

*Reasons to sell, three ways to sell, types of mineral buyers and who to avoid, the history of mineral buying, selling at peak value, buying producing cash-flowing minerals, brokering and flipping vs. holding royalties, and building monthly income*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Mineral buyers and sell-side advisors converge on one rule: follow the drill bit. Steven Hatcher, Troy Eckard and Matt Sands all buy proven, multi-zone acreage near active permits, chain title from patent to present because no title company insures minerals, and treat a sale as a liquidity trade where the seller hands price and volume risk to the buyer. The mechanics are numeric: Sands discounts producing minerals at about 10%, rig-on-site acreage at 15-20% and five-year-plan acreage at 20-30%, and pays 36-60x monthly royalty for old flat wells; Hatcher reports a $95,000 PDP buy recouping 40% in nine months and a $30,000-plus contract flip with no capital. Disagreements center on never-sell advice, dry gas, auction quality, and whether flipping minerals is a legitimate play or something to avoid.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

The buyers agree on one thing first: follow the drill bit. Steven Hatcher calls West Texas 'Mecca,' adds North Louisiana, North Dakota and Northeastern Colorado, and tells buyers to stay near active permitting and drilling [I-uVEypiL9w @ 00:02:32]. He backs that with his own allocation: 95% of drilling is horizontal shale, he spends 95% of his time there, and buying in the wrong area is the first mistake on his list [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:04:32]. Matt Sands gives the same instruction in distance terms: buy one to two miles from active drilling that is moving your way, because even undrilled acreage in that path may get leased, and a rig running 16 horizontal wells means royalties inside 12 months [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:27:56]. Troy Eckard tightens the screen further. He refuses speculative minerals, wants well-drilled direct offsets that are proven and derisked, and wants primary, secondary and tertiary zones stacked underneath, because a single-zone tract depletes to no residual value [S-yENwSQhi0 @ 00:01:11]. The shared logic is that operators spend millions per well, so the mineral owner's only job is to sit where a well-capitalized company is already committing capital. Sands calls anything outside that zone a goat pasture and warns that minerals are not a lottery ticket.

The second point of agreement is that the asset is invisible until you dig it out of the courthouse. Hatcher notes the surface owner is usually not the mineral owner, because minerals get reserved when surface sells, so he chains title through county deed records and grantor/grantee indexes, mostly online, using mineral tax rolls in Texas and pooling and unitization records in Oklahoma and Ohio [I-uVEypiL9w @ 00:03:48]. Sands states flatly that no title company will underwrite title insurance on minerals, so the buyer must run title from patent to present, and he warns that 640 gross acres can shrink to a quarter of a net acre once fractions are traced [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:16:39]. In his Cashflow Ninja walkthrough he pulls the oil-and-gas commission's list of interested parties for each drilling unit, verifies deeds at the county clerk, and corroborates ownership with operator title opinions and royalty checks because fraudulent deeds circulate [uhffuS4DlLQ @ 00:33:33]. Hatcher reduces the whole discipline to one line: you have to learn how to run title and review revenue statements to understand exactly what they own, and 'that is the entire game right there' [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:05:42]. Finding hidden ownership and underwriting are precisely the two gaps he tells wholesalers and land flippers they must close.

Buyers and sell-side advisors also agree on what the transaction actually is: a liquidity trade. Hatcher frames his company's role as providing a valuable service, liquidity, to owners who want to buy a home, start a business, retire or move into more stable assets, and he likens selling to taking the lower Powerball lump sum over the bigger 30-year stream [WvlIfeyW4-Q @ 00:02:52]. In his seller-side video he spells out what changes hands: the seller shifts price risk and volume risk onto the buyer in exchange for certainty in a fixed sum, and selling half or a quarter is allowed [Sz-1-E8zTqU @ 00:05:01]. Sands rejects blanket never-sell advice on the same grounds, telling concentrated owners they can sell any fraction, such as half, and diversify by tract, basin or oil-versus-gas mix [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:13:51]. Daniel Lewis pitches Oklahoma owners the identical flexibility, buying anything from 5 to 50,000 net mineral acres with partial sales welcome and no pressure [qZGXdB7E1lE @ 00:01:45]. For an acquirer, that consensus becomes the opening line of every call: you are not asking for the whole estate, only the slice that pays off a mortgage, a medical bill or a wedding.

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Valuation starts from Hatcher's warning that there is no Zillow or Redfin for minerals, so he prices off three methods: a multiple of the existing royalty check, price per acre from comps, and discounted cash flow [I-uVEypiL9w @ 00:04:36]. Sands supplies the discount rates that drive the DCF: about 10% for producing minerals, 15-20% with a rig on site, and 20-30% for acreage inside an operator's SEC five-year plan within one to two miles of drilling. His shortcut for wells older than five years with no future development is 36 to 60 times the monthly royalty [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:33:41]. On the sell side he reports how buyers haircut the price deck: at $100 oil they underwrite at $75-80, at roughly $60 oil they underwrite near $50, and they apply a 10-15% discount rate, which makes commodity price timing and development timing the two biggest levers on value [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:23:04]. In his tips episode he explains why wellbore-only interests are in hot demand: PDP gets discounted at 8-10% against 30-50% for PUD, because producing wells demand a lower discount rate, which means higher value [RR2BlQf14H8 @ 00:20:22]. Offers are typically quoted per net mineral acre, such as three thousand dollars per acre.

Deal flow is a records-and-outreach machine. Sands points to statutory pooling filings, such as those at the Oklahoma Corporation Commission, which list mineral owners a year or more before permits appear, and he runs the same outreach land investors use: letters, calls, email and texts, timed to catch owners facing something like medical bills [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:29:35]. Hatcher's sourcing system is four moves: track operator permits, pull tax, regulatory and courthouse records, contact owners systematically, and buy only where operators are spending [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:05:42]. Blue Mesa Minerals warns that starting an acquisitions company is a lot of work built on two numbers games, because many owners must be contacted to find one seller [08zWLxyGaag @ 00:02:20]. Sands sequences the paper so cash stays protected: target permitted or drilling acreage, pull the commission's interested-parties list, verify deeds, send offers, run DCF at your target return, and only after a signed purchase and sale agreement hire a landman, then adjust price to the net acres found, close, and notify the operator promptly [uhffuS4DlLQ @ 00:34:38]. He also notes that one winner pays for three or four duds, which is why he calls the whole business a numbers game.

Hatcher puts hard numbers on three ways to make money. For PDP cash flow, he paid $95,000 for 16 Louisiana acres with two existing and two new Haynesville wells, recouped 40% in nine months, and projects about $155,000 total, a 1.63 MOIC and 20% IRR [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:01:54]. For white-space appreciation, 60 acres in Benville Parish cost $325,000; nine months later the operator permitted and drilled four wells, and he projects over $1 million, a 3x return. For brokering, he contracted an Ohio interest at $220,000 after courthouse title revealed more acreage than the 5.5 acres on the tax records, then assigned it for $250,000, netting over $30,000 with no capital used [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:03:32]. Sands calibrates entry pricing: owning 1/160 of a 160-acre tract equals one net mineral acre worth a few thousand dollars, beginners can start with quarter-acre deals costing a few hundred, but in the Permian owners get unsolicited $25,000-per-acre offers and a $5,000 bid gets laughed out [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:13:06]. On the sell side he quotes non-producing Permian at $5,000-10,000 per net royalty acre, possibly two to three times that once permitted, versus a couple hundred per acre for an Oklahoma goat pasture [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:30:45].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether to sell at all. Steven Hatcher rejects the never-sell mindset outright, telling owners to treat minerals as a balance-sheet asset and weigh the opportunity cost of holding against the opportunity cost of selling [Sz-1-E8zTqU @ 00:00:35]. Hatcher frames a sale through the Powerball choice: $500M paid over 30 years versus a $300M lump sum, where selling means choosing the lower lump sum in exchange for certainty [WvlIfeyW4-Q @ 00:03:22]. Matt Sands lands on the same side, disagreeing with blanket never-sell advice and stressing that any fraction, such as half, can go to market while the rest keeps paying [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:13:51]. Yet in the 2020 outlook episode with guest Justin Williams, the counterweight surfaces: buyers were calling that moment a good buying opportunity, so an owner selling into the window risks handing future upside to the buyer [RR2BlQf14H8 @ 00:33:07]. Both camps treat the asset as a tradeable position rather than a family heirloom; they diverge on timing. Hatcher sells certainty and redeployment into a home, 1031 real estate, a farm tract, or a small business, while Sands asks a harder question first: is the buyer bidding into a trough that will reverse?

Commodity and basin selection split the room just as hard. Troy Eckard avoids dry gas, naming the Marcellus and Haynesville, and only breaks that rule for a pure rate-of-return play under a solid operator targeting 1.5 to 3x [S-yENwSQhi0 @ 00:03:35]. Steven Hatcher walked straight into Haynesville gas anyway: 16 acres in Bojer Parish, Louisiana with two existing and two new wells, bought for $95,000, 40% recouped in nine months, projected at roughly $155,000 total for a 1.63 MOIC and 20% IRR, which lands inside Eckard's own exception band [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:01:54]. Matt Sands goes further on gas, arguing that US gas at $2.50 to $3 per Mcf reflects a glut and that LNG export capacity coming online points to buying depressed gas assets now [uhffuS4DlLQ @ 00:26:17]. On geography, Hatcher calls West Texas 'Mecca' and says to buy near permitting and drilling [I-uVEypiL9w @ 00:02:32], while Sands steers toward overlooked areas under regulatory pressure because the Permian costs multiples per acre and draws more competition. Pick the lane deliberately: Eckard's legacy-hold filter demands multiple stacked formations, Hatcher's filter is the right neighborhood, right operator and multiple wells, and Sands insists on a mix of oil and gas across basins.

Auctions and flipping draw the most direct contradictions. On the buy side, Blue Mesa Minerals reports seeing decent deals at auction but never really good ones, since lots have usually been passed over by everybody first [08zWLxyGaag @ 00:01:17]. From the sell side, Matt Sands flips the read: auction commissions run 6 to 10%, but less sophisticated auction buyers may overpay, and Sands positions a fixed, lower advisory percentage as the alternative route to competing bids [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:34:41]. Steven Hatcher teaches flipping as a core income stream: a Harrison County, Ohio deal started at 5.5 acres off tax records, courthouse title turned up more acreage, and the contract at $220,000 was assigned for $250,000, netting over 30K with no capital deployed [zV7XrUzBPJQ @ 00:03:32]. Blue Mesa Minerals rejects that model on its own book, describing flippers who buy, wait for a new well, then list on EnergyNet, and stating it keeps everything it buys [vogFrTHuYiY @ 00:09:12]. The two flips are different animals: Hatcher's is a contract assignment with zero capital at risk, while the Blue Mesa complaint targets buy-and-hold-for-a-well arbitrage aimed at auction buyers who miss the coming decline. Sellers should know which flipper is on the other side of the table.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Title is where practitioners have already watched real money disappear. The Modern Land Investor host spent roughly $3,000 to $4,000 on a mineral-rights attorney to learn severance, parked the severed minerals in a separate entity without researching them, and never earned a dime from them [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:02:33]. Matt Sands spells out why diligence is non-negotiable on that same episode: no title company will insure minerals, title runs from patent to present, 640 gross acres can shrink to a quarter net acre, and a $6,000 quiet-title suit on a quarter NMA means pass or pay very little [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:57:36]. On Cashflow Ninja, Sands adds fraudulent deeds to the threat list and corroborates every claimed ownership interest against operator title opinions and actual royalty checks [uhffuS4DlLQ @ 00:30:04]. For sellers, the same defects cut the other way: in MRP 310 Sands reports that defects lower offers, cures such as a determination of heirship or reopened probate sit on the critical path to closing, and sellers can negotiate a split of the curative cost [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:20:51]. Run title before money moves, or price every defect directly into the offer.

The second failure cluster is development that never arrives or decays faster than modeled. Troy Eckard warns that a single-zone position leaves no residual value once it depletes, and that an undercapitalized 'Johnny come lately' operator may wait about two years for each well to pay out, stretching four to twelve analog wells across 10 to 20 years [S-yENwSQhi0 @ 00:02:19]. Matt Sands flags that operators may never drill permitted wells and that new wells can underperform the analogs used to price them, so the fix is diversification across states, basins and commodities [uhffuS4DlLQ @ 00:27:16]. On Modern Land Investor, Sands adds that an oil-price crash can cancel drilling plans outright, that a property where nothing will happen is a 'goat pasture,' and that one winner has to pay for three or four duds [1c2UBDuXhrk @ 00:31:45]. Blue Mesa Minerals quantifies the decline trap from the auction desk: a new well's revenue can fall to maybe 10% within a year, and $1,000 a month can become $200 a year later [vogFrTHuYiY @ 00:07:40]. Any buyer underwriting off a first-month check without a decline curve is paying peak price for a fading stream.

The final cluster is the paper itself. Steven Hatcher tells owners to throw away unsolicited mail resembling a check, legal agreement or deed, notes that sending deed-like mailers is illegal in several jurisdictions, and lists evasive identity, no online presence and high-pressure tactics as red flags [WIqkczvqoz0 @ 00:06:12]. The MRP 70 episode with Justin Williams warns that buyers forced to make assumptions will make them in their own favor, which is why an independent consultant or engineer at $600 to $800 per property is pitched as the way to level the playing field [RR2BlQf14H8 @ 00:06:55]. MRP 310 puts the PSA risk bluntly, 'it takes one sentence, Matt, to sign away far more than what you were anticipating,' with royalties in suspense the named target, and Sands adds that leasing at 12% instead of 25% in Reeves, Lea or Eddy counties halves the property's value before any sale [y5etlsUEcO0 @ 00:21:04]. Blue Mesa Minerals closes the loop: no escrow companies will go near mineral rights, so the sale is a signed deed against a wire in a 'Wild Wild West' market, and Sands separately insists on getting paid before handing over the deed [vogFrTHuYiY @ 00:02:21].
