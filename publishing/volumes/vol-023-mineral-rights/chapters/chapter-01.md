# Chapter 01: What You Actually Own: Mineral Estates, Royalties and Their Carve-Outs

*Severance from the surface, mineral vs. royalty ownership, net mineral acres vs. net royalty acres, gross vs. net acreage, fixed vs. floating NPRIs, overriding royalty interests, and conveying royalties vs. mineral interests*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from Matt Sands to Steven Hatcher, LMJ Land Services, PetroPeak Investments, and the Energy Law Professor agree on the core map: minerals are real property severed by deed, the mineral owner holds the executive right to lease and collect bonus, and every carve-out below that strips rights. Royalty and NPRI holders get production but no lease, bonus, or rentals; the ORRI is carved from the working interest and dies with the lease. Street math runs on three numbers: net mineral acres, royalty decimal, and net royalty acres, with Sands showing a $6,000 per NMA offer is only $3,000 per NRA at 25%. The fights are over wording: fixed versus floating NPRIs, where Van Dyke put about $44 million on one reading, and whether a small royalty beats a mineral interest with future-development upside.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner starts from the same foundation: minerals are real property, and what you actually own depends on which sticks of the bundle your deed carried. Matt Sands explains that US minerals were originally granted to surface owners in fee simple, pass by will or deed, and need a legal description that is 'sufficient' [3AnqzZSRGtg @ 00:01:54]. Steven Hatcher defines severance as the moment the mineral estate is created separate and apart from the surface, triggered when it is granted or reserved in a conveyance instrument, which he calls basically a deed. A mineral deed hands over the entire estate, royalties plus the executive rights to grant leases and receive bonuses, while the royalty deed is the second type he names [b13q6ndzbZM @ 00:01:02]. Hatcher adds that mineral rights are themselves a subdividable stick. Sands sorts owners into four positions: fee simple owners who can lease, severed mineral owners who keep necessary surface use, surface owners who cannot lease, and royalty owners who get no lease, no bonus, and no rentals [3AnqzZSRGtg @ 00:04:23]. The Venergy Momentum speaker draws the street conclusion from the dominant mineral estate: do not buy surface alone, and when you sell surface, reserve the minerals in the sale [175xk8ZQHpI @ 00:01:45].

On the cash side, the speakers agree a royalty is a cost-free cut of gross revenue whose percentage gets set in the lease. Steven Hatcher puts the range at 12.5% to 25% of gross well revenue, with checks arriving monthly for as long as the well produces, anywhere from 10 to 50-plus years [WZxmCcTgeWk @ 00:00:49]. LandApp by LandGate says royalties bear no operating costs and are usually stated as an eighth or a sixth, while the lease bonus is a one-time payment [r7MMN5ueVCs @ 00:00:55]. John Jordan of PetroPeak Investments lines up mineral, royalty, and ORRI owners as three positions that all get paid without funding drilling, though he flags that taxes and post-production deductions may still apply [zHSS1bvXiNk @ 00:02:58]. The consensus on the shape of that money is blunt: it declines. Hatcher compares royalties to rents or annuities, only more volatile because they ride oil and gas prices, and calls it a declining stream unlike investments that stay flat or appreciate. The Venergy Momentum speaker agrees wells can produce 20 to 50 years, but most revenue lands in years one through five and re-stimulation bumps are short-lived [175xk8ZQHpI @ 00:03:56]. Venergy adds that most companies don't pay until your account hits $100 or more.

The carve-outs are where the consensus gets sharpest. Matt Sands, John Jordan, and Blue Mesa Minerals all place the overriding royalty inside the working interest rather than the minerals, and all three say it dies with the lease; Sands names geologists, landmen, engineers, and brokers as the usual holders [pjmqehPUgL8 @ 00:01:31]. Blue Mesa calls an ORRI an interest in the proceeds of the sale of oil and gas, free from the cost of production, and once the wells go uneconomic and the lease is released, both the royalty and the ORRI are gone [PR8LyKmo8S4 @ 00:00:37]. The non-participating royalty interest sits on the other side of the line: Sands says it is carved from the mineral estate and perpetual, while the ORRI expires with the lease [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:10:28]. Sands notes owners create NPRIs to raise cash or as gifts to grandkids. Jordan, Sands, and the Energy Law Professor agree the NPRI holder takes a share of production with no leasing rights, no bonus, and no delay rental. LMJ Land Services makes it concrete with Carl, who holds an NPRI under Bob's half: Carl can't sign the lease, gets no bonus, and no delay rentals, and his net mineral acres are zero [IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:00:40].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The first number to nail is net mineral acres, and every speaker computes it the same way: gross acres times your fractional interest. Tracy Lenz warns that the 320 gross acres in a description like Dawson County, Section 39, Block 35, Township 4 is just the fence, not what you own, and that the '$10' consideration on a deed is not the actual sale price [9zqL93Kdy1I @ 00:01:06]. To find your real share, Lenz either traces title back to patent or backs into it from the payment: 50% of 320 gross acres is 160 NMA [9zqL93Kdy1I @ 00:03:39]. Matt Sands defines one NMA as 100% of the minerals under one acre and runs a 160-acre quarter at a 1/10 interest to 16 NMA [iVFjOPF8JW0 @ 00:03:23]. LMJ Land Services applies the same formula to a 160-acre tract split between Ann and Bob, giving each 80 NMA and Carl, the NPRI holder, zero, which is why the bonus goes to Ann and Bob only [IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:00:40]. Lenz compares undivided interest to owning shares of Google: you own a slice of the whole tract, not a fenced corner, so 10% of a 100-acre ranch is 10 NMA.

Next comes the royalty decimal, which LMJ Land Services computes as mineral interest times lease royalty, so Ann's half under a 1/4 lease is .125. Carl's NPRI is where the money moves. Read as a fixed 1/16, Carl gets .03125 carved out of Bob's share, leaving Bob .09375; read as a floating 'one half of the royalty,' Carl and Bob each get .0625, with Hysaw and Van Dyke cited [IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:01:41]. 'Same tract, two readings, and Carl's decimal doubles.' LMJ's control check: Ann plus Bob plus Carl has to equal the lease royalty of .25, and if it doesn't, go back to the deeds [IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:02:02]. Matt Sands runs the same split on a 1/5 NPRI under a 25% lease: fixed gives the NPRI owner 20% and the mineral owner 5%, while floating flips it to 5% and 20% [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:14:25]. His drafting tell is that 'of all the oil and gas' reads fixed and 'of the royalty' reads floating, with 'usual 1/8' language as the twist. Oliva Gibbs shows the stakes: a fixed 1/16 stays 1/16 whether the lease is 1/8 or 25%, and Van Dyke put about $44 million on the reading [QPk4GTvt5YU @ 00:12:22].

Net royalty acres is the normalizing tool buyers and owners use to compare offers apples to apples. Matt Sands defines one NRA as 1/8 of 8/8, or 12.5%, under one acre, and gives the formula: NRA equals NMA times royalty rate times eight [iVFjOPF8JW0 @ 00:05:59]. His worked offer: 16 NMA at a 25% royalty is 32 NRA, so a $6,000-per-NMA offer, $96,000 total, is really only $3,000 per NRA, while the neighbor's $5,000 per NMA at 1/8 is $5,000 per NRA [iVFjOPF8JW0 @ 00:09:06]. Burdened tracts take one more step: subtract the NPRI from your royalty, then divide by 12.5%. Tracy Lenz runs a sibling case: same NMA, but the sister leased at 25% and the brother at 20%, so her acres are worth more, and normalized to the old 1/8 standard she holds 20 NRA [37dcxhS1a6E @ 00:04:32]. Sands also flags 'assuming 25%' offers: $10,000 per acre at 25% is about $5,000 at 12.5%. On rate versus bonus, Sands compares $500 per NMA at 3/16 to $250 at 20 to 25% and says almost always take zero bonus and the highest royalty [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:09:06].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Trace the Deed Chain to Severance
   - Pull every conveyance back to patent and mark where the minerals were severed or reserved.
   - Hatcher pins severance to the grant or reservation of the mineral estate in a conveyance instrument, and Lenz traces title back to patent while ignoring the '$10' consideration line [Steven Hatcher | Minerals Guy — b13q6ndzbZM @ 00:00:17] [Tracy Lenz — 9zqL93Kdy1I @ 00:02:42]
2. Compute NMA and the Royalty Decimal
   - Multiply gross acres by your fractional interest, then multiply your interest by the lease royalty.
   - LMJ's 160-acre tract gives Ann and Bob 80 NMA each, Ann's decimal comes out at .125, and every owner's decimal must sum to the .25 lease royalty [LMJ Land Services — IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:02:02]
3. Classify Every Carve-Out by Its Wording
   - Read each reservation for fixed versus floating language and mineral versus royalty language.
   - Sands reads 'of all the oil and gas' as fixed and 'of the royalty' as floating, and the Energy Law Professor treats 'in and under' as a mineral interest and 'produced and saved' as a royalty [Matt Sands — qru8bh4mMms @ 00:17:44] [Energy Law Professor — OYaKeqiMHWc @ 00:29:01]
4. Verify Payout and Normalize Every Offer
   - Check wells on the state commission site, chase suspense funds, and convert offers to dollars per NRA.
   - Sands checks state commission wells and calls the operator's owner relations about suspense funds, then runs NRA equals NMA times royalty rate times eight before comparing bids [Matt Sands — pjmqehPUgL8 @ 00:23:49] [Matt Sands — iVFjOPF8JW0 @ 00:06:20]

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether a small mineral interest or a straight royalty is the better asset to hold. Energy Law Professor walks through McSwain v. Musselshell, a 1981 Montana fight where 1933 and 1943 instruments reserved 2.5% of the minerals but a 1944 deed reserved a 2.5% royalty. The appellate court reversed the district court because a 2.5% mineral interest under a 1/8 lease yields far less than a 2.5% royalty, and concluded the royalty 'usually is' better in reality [OYaKeqiMHWc @ 00:11:16]. John Jordan of PetroPeak Investments argues the other side of the ledger. Minerals carry future leasing and development rights, so they can hold value even with little current production, such as additional horizontal wells targeting other formations [zHSS1bvXiNk @ 00:02:07]. Jordan then hedges his own pitch. He warns that future drilling is not guaranteed because plans change, prices fluctuate, and owners may wait years, so minerals in an active horizontal play do not automatically beat declining wells under an ORRI [zHSS1bvXiNk @ 00:09:28]. Matt Sands settles the leasing version of the fight with a cash-flow rule. Offered $500/NMA at 3/16 versus $250 at 20 to 25%, he says it is almost always better to take zero bonus and the highest royalty rate, because the rate directly drives the value of the minerals going forward [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:09:06].

Whether an NPRI is fixed or floating is where the money swings hardest, and the outcome depends on the state and the exact words in the deed. LMJ Land Services runs one 160-acre tract with a 1/4 lease royalty. Read as fixed, Carl's 1/16 NPRI yields .03125, which comes out of Bob's share. Read as floating ('one half of the royalty'), Carl and Bob each take .0625, so 'Carl's decimal doubles' on the same tract [IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:01:41]. Matt Sands gives the working rule of thumb: 'of all the oil and gas' means fixed and 'of the royalty' means floating. He adds that the 'usual 1/8' language is a twist and that interpretation varies by state [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:17:44]. Oliva Gibbs shows how far Texas courts will push toward floating. In Van Dyke, with roughly $44M at stake, the lower courts said 1/16, the Texas Supreme Court said 1/2, and the presumption can be rebutted only from the document itself [QPk4GTvt5YU @ 00:13:17]. Ohio runs the other way in Gibbs's account. In Crum, the word 'his' made the interest a fixed 1/16. Mineral Development v. SWN held the 1/32 and 1/16 fixed and not reduced by the lease royalty. The Seventh District rejects any 1/8 presumption, and the Ohio Supreme Court has not ruled [QPk4GTvt5YU @ 00:39:11].

On overrides and offers, the playbooks split between sellers and buyers. Blue Mesa Minerals reports that a lot of ORRIs are sold in the first two or three months of production, at peak output and before anyone knows how the well will settle, and that selling early usually generates more cash for the holder [PR8LyKmo8S4 @ 00:00:54]. Matt Sands applies a buyer's discipline to an old single well: value it on existing production only, and add protective language to the assignment against washout risk [pjmqehPUgL8 @ 00:05:20]. John Jordan frames the same tension as identical checks hiding different rights. $100,000 gross at a 20% royalty creates a $20,000 burden, and a 10% holder of that burden gets $2,000 a month. A 2% ORRI on the same gross also gets $2,000, yet the override usually dies with the lease while the minerals persist [zHSS1bvXiNk @ 00:07:53]. On pricing, Sands buys in dollars per NMA with reduction terms for burdens, but he coaches sellers to convert everything to net royalty acres. At 16 NMA under a 25% royalty, the tract becomes 32 NRA. A $6,000/NMA offer, $96,000 total, then shrinks to $3,000 per NRA, even though that royalty pays twice the neighbor's [iVFjOPF8JW0 @ 00:09:06].

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most common trap is trusting a number nobody has reconciled. LMJ Land Services runs a hard check on every tract: Ann, plus Bob, plus Carl must equal the lease royalty of .25. If the decimals do not sum, the analyst goes back to the deeds rather than forcing the math, and the report shows the volume, page, and quoted NPRI language [IBxLIQjDNto @ 00:02:02]. Tracy Lenz warns that the deed itself misleads. The '$10' consideration is not the actual sale price, and 320 gross acres in a Dawson County section is the fence, not ownership. She finds net mineral acres by tracing back to patent or by backing into the figure from payment, so 50% of 320 equals 160 NMA [9zqL93Kdy1I @ 00:03:39]. Matt Sands reports that 'attorneys aren't perfect' and tells owners to keep their own documents, because an operator change can trigger a fresh title review. He also notes that Texas alone requires a signed division order, with suspense acceptable while title is resolved [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:22:33]. Steven Hatcher adds that the conveyance instrument controls everything downstream. Severance happens at the moment a deed grants or reserves the mineral estate, and a mineral deed carrying executive rights differs from a royalty deed [b13q6ndzbZM @ 00:00:17].

The second trap is pricing a royalty like a flat annuity. Steven Hatcher calls royalties more volatile than rents because they ride oil and gas prices. His own deal on 35.5 NMA at a 25% royalty, with six new wells planned, projected roughly $600,000 in Year 1, about $250,000 in Year 2, and about $20,000 by Year 10, which he contrasts with real estate's 10-to-30-year payback [WZxmCcTgeWk @ 00:02:58]. Venergy Momentum Oil & Gas confirms the curve from the owner side. Wells can produce 20 to 50 years, but most revenue lands in years one through five, re-stimulation bumps are short-lived, and most companies do not pay until the account hits $100 or greater [175xk8ZQHpI @ 00:03:56]. Blue Mesa Minerals spells out the terminal event for overrides: once the wells are uneconomic, the lease is released, which kills both the royalty and the ORRI under it [PR8LyKmo8S4 @ 00:00:37]. John Jordan's due-diligence answer at PetroPeak is a full review of existing wells, declines, remaining reserves, operators, ownership rights, lease terms, and development potential. He then matches the purchase price to the remaining economic life rather than to the current monthly check [zHSS1bvXiNk @ 00:10:57].

The third trap is the counterparty, or the paperwork, quietly reshaping the deal. Matt Sands warns that buyers may exploit owners' unfamiliarity with these metrics. An offer of '$10,000/acre assuming 25%' is only about $5,000 at 12.5%, so with unleased minerals he tells owners to ask which royalty rate the buyer assumed. A going rate of 15 to 20% against the buyer's 12.5% is negotiation room [iVFjOPF8JW0 @ 00:11:18]. On NPRIs, Sands flags dependency risk: the NPRI owner rides on whatever lease terms the executive negotiates and cannot sign the lease [qru8bh4mMms @ 00:04:54]. For overrides, his checklist starts with proportionate reduction: 1/2 times 1/8 equals 1/16, while a '1/8 of 8/8' override is not reduced. It continues with literal interpretation, depth limits, deductions, pooling consent, state commission well records, and calls to the operator's owner relations about suspense funds [pjmqehPUgL8 @ 00:24:59]. Even polished pitches contain slips. John Jordan states that royalty owners bear no drilling or operating cost, then says they 'generally are responsible for paying their share of the drilling and completion costs.' That line contradicts his own earlier point and Blue Mesa's cost-free description of overrides [zHSS1bvXiNk @ 00:03:30].
