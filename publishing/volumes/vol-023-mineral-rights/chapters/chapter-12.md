## Executive Summary

Across Matt Sands, Justin Williams, guest CEO Tim Rynott, Minerals & Royalties Authority LLC, The Byte-Size Brief, ComboCurve and Bison Interests, the operating reality is clear: price spikes reach royalty checks about two months late, and operators route windfalls to buybacks and dividends before drilling, so rig response lags one to two quarters. Street mechanics center on pricing royalties off the futures strip minus basis while buyers anchor on $50 to $70 oil, attacking post-production deductions running 20% to 35%, and negotiating pipeline easements where one ranch turned a $21,000 offer into a roughly $7M verdict. Capital is consolidating into public buyers, continuation vehicles and platforms that rarely resell. Disagreement is sharp: Sands sees long-term oil at $60 to $70 or lower, while Bison's Josh Young puts fair value near $105 and warns of a 1970s replay.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Price spikes do not hit royalty owners on headline day, and they rarely move rigs fast. Matt Sands reminds owners that royalties are paid about two months in arrears, so the war-driven price run only now reaches checks [oHhvtjxLX9c @ 00:03:00]. On the capital side, Sands lays out the spending order operators follow under shareholder discipline after the COVID-era bankruptcies: buybacks first, then dividends, then drilling, and Sands calls the White House sit-down with Chevron and others a “meaningless meeting” for supply [IZpkZEGcVjk @ 00:12:52]. Sands treats rig response as a lagging indicator, with capital taking one to two quarters to react, so drilling cuts in a depressed 2026 surface as weaker 2027 production [XdquVDjbEAk @ 00:19:02]. The split that matters for owners: public companies keep favoring buybacks while private operators add rigs, with the October count at 595, up 4 week over week and 58 year over year [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:16:46]. Sands also warns that rig commitments are risky if cash flow stops, and that 4-mile laterals mean operators do not have to drill as much as they did five or ten years ago. The baseline: operators want prolonged, stable high prices before they commit, so owners should not budget on a rig showing up because oil jumped.

On valuation, Matt Sands builds royalty values on the futures strip minus the basis differential, and noted the strip sat in the upper $60s at the start of the May episode even as spot ran far higher [IZpkZEGcVjk @ 00:07:04]. Buyers do the same: Sands told a seller that buyers are banking on $50 to $70 oil over the life of the asset, and that the real question is the average price of oil or gas over the life of the holding, not the earlier $100 print before oil slid 5% to $76 on August 4 [n2MrqKJ_h80 @ 00:13:57]. Sands also flags that higher royalty checks cut both ways: royalty income is a small share of most owners' total income, so above some price point the effect turns “net negative” [oHhvtjxLX9c @ 00:16:15]. On the September episode, the hosts note higher royalties are offset by inflation, with the 30-year Treasury near 5.18% and the average 30-year fixed mortgage at 6.74% [dwr0eYVQHFo @ 00:10:09]. Colorado gasoline above $4.30 eats into the royalty bump, and Sands paid $5.99 a gallon in rural Wyoming. The shared operator rule: take headlines with a grain of salt, price assets on long-term averages, and treat spikes as temporary windfalls rather than the new baseline for any hold-or-sell decision on a royalty package.

The capital map has shifted, and three separate channels describe a buyer base that is deeper and stickier than before. Minerals & Royalties Authority LLC describes a funnel: grassroots buyers at the top, and at the bottom, where Blackstone, KRP and Viper used to stand, there are now about 10 public companies buying, including Franco-Nevada, Alliance and Cresset, plus CVs, insurance-company capital such as an Apollo offshoot, and Six Street [9p4KYSjq9r0 @ 00:00:39]. The same channel tracks private equity morphing into investment platforms rather than portcos: NGP's Elk Range, Post Oak, KKR, Bandera under Apollo and Pegasus under EnCap, with MAP running a $1B continuation vehicle [xL8qooqAeq0 @ 00:00:29]. On the operator side, ComboCurve's guest says capital providers told the team the old model rarely works; most now require a production base, so teams must buy some production first and then expand [5KNOGVVmCuw @ 00:00:00]. Public operators are running the same consolidation play at the acreage level: The Byte-Size Brief reports Permian Resources spent about $1.05B year to date across about 190 micro-deals, adding 54k net leasehold acres and 20k royalty acres while holding leverage near 0.5x [fqGTqf-VpBc @ 00:06:14]. Net effect for sellers: the biggest portfolios may never trade again, because owners intend to hold and add.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

Deductions are where royalty owners bleed quietly. Matt Sands reports North Dakota owners seeing 20% to 35% post-production deductions after a 2009 change moved royalty valuation “at the well,” with deductions surging by 2015 because gas must be compressed, processed and stripped of NGLs before sale [IrkmOVOU8E8 @ 00:01:04]. One owner, Diana Scarpole, lost about 35% of the expected payment. Sands' remedy ladder: pay an attorney for one to two hours to review the lease, claim reimbursement plus interest, or join a class action such as Iverson v. Hess, which sought $10B; at signing, negotiate a cost-free lease even if that means a slightly lower royalty rate [IrkmOVOU8E8 @ 00:06:07]. The Arkansas appeals court backed lease language: Flywheel had deducted post-production costs since 2019 under the net-proceeds statute despite gross-proceeds leases, and the court held the operator cannot use a competing statute as a backdoor to take deductions the lease does not authorize, while silent leases stay unaffected [n2MrqKJ_h80 @ 00:08:33]. Sands adds that even in states with more owner protection, such as Oklahoma, Sands' own overrides are still heavily deducted. When checking statements, remember they reflect a monthly weighted average price, not intraday spikes like Henry Hub's $30.565 spot record [M3eFdVkW_mY @ 00:03:56].

Pipeline easements are a one-shot negotiation. Sands lays out the standard terms: a right-of-way about 40 feet wide, paid once by the foot, with the owner able to demand burial below plow depth, and common-carrier lines able to use eminent domain [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:06:13]. The outlier case: Artemis Ranch, a 4,000-acre Lampasas County property crossed for half a mile by Matterhorn Express, turned down a final offer of about $21,000, litigated about four years, and won a jury award near $7M, roughly 330 times the offer; Matterhorn had offered $3M plus a second line to settle, was refused, and is appealing [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:08:21]. Sands is blunt that the typical protester wins only 10% to 20% above the final offer and says to hire a landman or attorney; easements are perpetual, and on corridors like one McCulloch County ranch now carrying four lines, each added line pays less [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:10:24]. Midstream bottlenecks hit the royalty side too. The Byte-Size Brief notes producers literally paying midstream operators to take gas away at Waha, which went as low as negative $9.52 per MCF, while Sands counts six pipeline projects due by 2029 to relieve West Texas gas [fqGTqf-VpBc @ 00:01:18].

The emerging plays come with specific screens. Tim Rynott, the helium CEO on Sands' show, says the market is in its fifth shortage in 22 years, with shortages lasting two to two and a half years, after shocks like the 2022 Amur fire that cut 12% of global supply [ptrz57qzl54 @ 00:06:42]. Prices ran from $80 to $90 per MCF in 2018-19 to $500 to $700 on contract, with spot at $1,000 to $2,000. Rynott's prospect target is 0.5% to 1.5% helium, 3 to 6 MMcf/d and 10 to 20 locations, against big fields running 0.4% to 0.65% [ptrz57qzl54 @ 00:20:57]. For owners, Rynott's leasing playbook is to insist on “all gases” royalty language, secure 3D seismic options, and start with a low bonus paired with an escalating royalty [ptrz57qzl54 @ 00:46:59]. CO2 is the parallel bet. Sands explains that after primary production and waterflood, CO2 injection lowers oil viscosity and restores reservoir pressure, producing a flat production curve that benefits royalty owners on mature wells, especially in West Texas, and cites a University of Houston study finding 137B barrels technically recoverable via EOR [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:03:08]. Rynott also points out that CO2 found alongside helium can earn through EOR or 45Q credits.

## Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is the price deck. Josh Young of Bison Interests pegs WTI near $101 against a fair value of about $105, and says that if inventories drop below 1 billion barrels the outcome is "150, sorry, or higher" [5sPOq_WPRHQ @ 00:06:28]. Matt Sands runs a far colder deck for valuing minerals: a long-term view of $60 to $70 oil or lower, and a blunt message to one seller that buyers are "banking on, you know, 50 to $70 oil" when they price a package [n2MrqKJ_h80 @ 00:13:57]. Sands is not static, though. In May the valuation method was the futures strip minus the basis differential, with no floor expected below roughly $75 even after Hormuz reopens [IZpkZEGcVjk @ 00:08:42]. Back in December 2025 the bank desks Sands relayed were bearish to the extreme: JPMorgan saw Brent in the $30s by 2027 and Goldman saw WTI averaging $53 in 2026 [XdquVDjbEAk @ 00:07:20]. The operator takeaway is that the same asset can be valued on a $50 deck by the buyer and a $105 deck by an equity bull in the same quarter. Sellers who anchor on headline spikes are negotiating against a buyer underwriting the life-of-asset average, not this week's print.

The second fight is whether high prices actually pull rigs. Young argues the industry needs about 200 more rigs, close to a 50% increase, just to get back to 2023 levels, and warns of a 1970s replay [5sPOq_WPRHQ @ 00:10:33]. Sands keeps reporting the opposite reflex from public producers: free cash flow goes to buybacks first, then dividends, then drilling, a discipline tied to the COVID-era bankruptcies, and Sands dismissed Trump's meeting with Chevron and others as a "meaningless meeting" [IZpkZEGcVjk @ 00:12:52]. The August numbers back that up: land rigs climbed from 551 to 572, just under 4%, while prices had run up about 50% [n2MrqKJ_h80 @ 00:15:30]. The two camps partly reconcile on private capital. By October Sands counted 595 rigs, up 58 year over year, with public companies still favoring buybacks while private operators added rigs [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:16:46]. Sands also stresses that 4-mile laterals mean operators "don't have to drill as much as we did say 5 or 10 years ago," so a flat rig count does not mean flat volumes [yFLZSA3QGGI @ 00:18:36]. For a royalty owner, the alternative playbook is to identify who operates your acreage, because a private operator sitting on unbudgeted cash is the one most likely to spud next.

Exit strategy is where the camps diverge hardest. Minerals & Royalties Authority argues the biggest portfolios may never be sold, because owners want to hold forever and keep adding, and calls grassroots "the best part of the funnel" to buy as much as possible, as fast as possible, while the top layer's cost of capital falls: "now is the perfect time for harvest" [9p4KYSjq9r0 @ 00:01:20]. The same channel frames the institutional loop as scale, recap with yield investors for 10 to 15 years, then "Scale, CV, do it again," pointing to MAP's $1B continuation vehicle [xL8qooqAeq0 @ 00:01:16]. Young is the counterweight on timing: a board Young chaired sold about $70 to $80M of assets and then the whole company for about $150M, yet Young calls the 2024 Bison sale of Tamarack at fair value a flat mistake: "And selling there was a mistake" [5sPOq_WPRHQ @ 00:38:32]. On the funding side, ComboCurve's practitioner says the old model does not "work very often"; capital providers now require a production base first, so that team self-funded and raised from "mostly Midland people," some New Mexico people and family offices, explicitly "It won't be private equity" [5KNOGVVmCuw @ 00:00:29]. That cuts against the Authority's read that private equity firms remain the best institutional fundraisers.

## Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The most expensive trap reported firsthand is post-production deductions. Sands reports North Dakota owners seeing 20% to 35% deductions after a 2009 change moved royalty valuation "at the well," with one owner, Diana Scarpole, losing about 35% of the expected payment; Sands admits to personally holding Oklahoma overrides that are still heavily deducted even in a state with more owner protection [IrkmOVOU8E8 @ 00:04:10]. In Arkansas, Flywheel had deducted post-production costs since 2019 under the net-proceeds statute despite "gross proceeds" leases, until an appeals court ruled the operator "can't use maybe a competing statute as a backdoor to take deductions that the lease doesn't authorize" [n2MrqKJ_h80 @ 00:08:33]. The second trap is mistaking a spike for a paycheck. During Winter Storm Fern, Henry Hub spot hit an all-time high of $30.565 with an intraday $53.75, but Sands warns royalty statements reflect a monthly weighted average, not the spike [M3eFdVkW_mY @ 00:03:56]. Royalties also land about 2 months in arrears, and above some price point royalty income becomes "net negative" for owners because it is a small share of total income while fuel and utility bills climb; Sands paid $5.99 at the pump in rural Wyoming [oHhvtjxLX9c @ 00:16:15]. Run the household math before celebrating a headline.

Surface deals are the second failure zone. On pipeline easements, the Artemis Ranch in Lampasas County got a final Matterhorn Express offer of about $21,000 for a half-mile crossing; after roughly 4 years in court the jury awarded about $7M, roughly 330 times the offer, and Matterhorn is appealing after the ranch refused a $3M settlement that came bundled with a second line [FDXztudBSFk @ 00:08:21]. Sands is explicit that this is the outlier: protesters typically win only 10% to 20% above the final offer, so hire a landman or attorney, and the corridor effect bites because easements are perpetual and each added line pays less, as one McCulloch County ranch now carrying 4 lines shows. Midstream is the same bottleneck at the commodity level. Waha gas went as low as minus $9.52 per MCF, and The Byte-Size Brief put it plainly: "Producers literally have to pay midstream operators to take their gas away" [fqGTqf-VpBc @ 00:01:18]. Tim Rynott calls midstream lag the "biggest downfall" for the 50 to 60 helium pure plays, with facilities taking 12 to 18 months [ptrz57qzl54 @ 00:24:15], and Sands and Williams expect new pipelines to lift West Texas prices only over the next couple of years [dwr0eYVQHFo @ 00:13:11].

The last cluster is about who you trust and when you act. Rynott warns that "royalty owners in general need to be careful of re-promoters," and tells owners to demand drillers or a proven track record with "skin in the game"; blown seals already cause 40% of dry holes in conventional plays, and Rynott says to double that for helium [ptrz57qzl54 @ 01:04:23]. Sands flags the timing trap: drilling cuts are "kind of a lagging indicator," so 2027 production will feel a depressed 2026, and capital takes 1 to 2 quarters to respond [XdquVDjbEAk @ 00:19:02]. Theft is a live field risk too: a Texas and Oklahoma ring produced 6 arrests, 17 warrants and more than $12M in seized assets, and Sands tells owners to report anomalies by calling the number that operators "usually have" on the sign at the well pad [yFLZSA3QGGI @ 00:15:21]. Sands also warns that rig commitments get risky the moment cash flow stops. Even a disciplined consolidator carries execution risk. The Byte-Size Brief lists the integration of 190 titles, $25M set aside for Ward County integration, diesel and casing inflation, Waha bottlenecks and reliance on high oil prices, and leaves performance below $75 oil as the open question for Permian Resources [fqGTqf-VpBc @ 00:07:48].
