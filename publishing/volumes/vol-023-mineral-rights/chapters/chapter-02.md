## Executive Summary

Practitioners agree the first job is knowing exactly what you own: pull the legal description from the source deed, plot it on a free state GIS, and log every tract in one spreadsheet with interest type, net acres, lease status, and provenance. Mineral title runs on a separate chain from the surface, and a royalty check never cures a recorded gap. Street mechanics are state-specific: the Texas RRC viewer with 8-digit API entry, Oklahoma's OCC map plus OkTAP PUN severance records, and North Dakota's $50-a-year Basic paywall, which Matt Sands sidesteps with WellDatabase's free tier. Paid help runs about $600 a day for a landman and $3,000 for a limited title search. Sands and Justin Williams push AI for run sheets but demand spot-checks, while Dave of titlesearch.com insists only a dedicated mineral search exposes split-off rights.

## The Practitioner Consensus

Every practitioner here starts in the same place: before leasing, selling, or auditing a check, pin down exactly what you own. Blue Mesa Minerals calls knowing what you own 'the most important thing to managing your minerals' and says the best opening move is to pull the legal description and look at it on a map [PAfHHmByY2I @ 00:06:23]. Steven Hatcher, an oil and gas attorney with more than a decade in the business, labels it part one of minerals management and warns that owners who skip it are 'Flying Blind' and open themselves up to 'being on the bad end of a deal' [5hXDw_OFeQc @ 00:01:01]. Matt Sands and Justin Williams run the whole workflow in strict order: know what you own, learn the interest types, map the activity, and only then put a value on it [u4xA0UNrxQs @ 00:03:12]. NARO frames the same discipline as a pre-signature check: verify which rights a document actually touches and who the person contacting you represents, because leasing and selling are different transactions [o8FqhVu6MNM @ 00:00:33]. The trigger in the field is almost always external, a landman's leasing offer or a cold call to an heir who never knew the interest existed, which is exactly why the inventory has to be built before that phone rings rather than after it.

The second point of agreement is that mineral ownership lives on its own paper trail. Dave of titlesearch.com, speaking on AFX, says mineral rights are subsurface, 'do not run with the land,' and 'run on a separate chain of title,' so a standard search shows only the surface chain and a special mineral rights search is needed to surface them [JpHijIsWFL4 @ 00:01:50]. Rights can split off in any transfer, and probate and heirship gaps are common. NARO hits the same gap from the payment side: 'A royalty statement is a starting point, not a complete ownership record,' and 'A check in your name doesn't resolve a gap in the recorded chain of ownership' [o8FqhVu6MNM @ 00:04:48]. The prescribed move is to search county records by grantor and grantee and to ask the paying company which records and calculations support its decimal. Hatcher sends owners straight back to the source deed to extract the legal description rather than trusting secondhand summaries or a buyer's letter [5hXDw_OFeQc @ 00:03:43]. Sands and Williams add that deeds and leases are the documents that matter most when an heir reconstructs an inheritance, and that a missing deed into a trust or an unrecorded probate step leaves the family exposed to the same chain-of-title hole the title searchers keep finding [yfXV7bIliEk @ 00:09:25].

Practitioners also converge on one consolidated inventory. Sands and Williams want a single spreadsheet logging state, county, legal description, interest type, decimal or net acres, lease status, and provenance, and they point to a NARO member who needed 18 months to untangle an inheritance from boxes stacked to the ceiling, alongside their figure that executors average 16 months settling estates [yfXV7bIliEk @ 00:09:57]. Their timing play is the holidays, when families are together, the pace is slower, and people are reflective enough to walk through that sheet. Blue Mesa keeps a parallel physical file: one folder with leases, division orders, and a single sample statement, plus a free mineral spreadsheet the firm hands out [PAfHHmByY2I @ 00:06:41]. Sands admits that, looking back, they 'would absolutely keep everything that might be related to it' [u4xA0UNrxQs @ 00:11:17]. The math that fills the net-acre column is agreed and simple: NARO's worked example takes the NE/4 at 160 gross acres times a 1/8 mineral share to land at 20 net mineral acres held undivided across the whole tract, and it stresses that a 1/8 mineral share is not the same thing as a 1/8 royalty, so the spreadsheet needs separate columns for mineral fraction and lease royalty rate [o8FqhVu6MNM @ 00:03:04].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

In Texas the workhorse is the Railroad Commission GIS viewer. Sands says to Google 'Texas rrc GIS' or open it from rc.texas.gov, ignore the district regions, then either zoom down to block and section or hit the magnifying-glass search and choose Surveys [gFgW6n9nb-c @ 00:03:38]. Inputs are county plus survey name, block and section, or the abstract number; most deeds omit the abstract, so once you find it, write it down (A112 in the demo) for faster repeat searches. The legend reads: a line through the symbol is a plugged oil well, a solid green dot is producing, a red starburst is gas, and a hollow blue circle is a permit. For API lookups, drop the leading '42' and enter the 3-digit county code plus the 5-digit well ID, 8 digits total [gFgW6n9nb-c @ 00:08:29]. Blue Mesa runs the same viewer on Glascock County abstract 792 with no block or section needed, flags an 'H' suffix as a horizontal well, and warns production data runs a couple of months behind [PAfHHmByY2I @ 00:02:09]. Tracy Lenz, a petroleum engineer and certified mineral appraiser, stresses Texas is not a PLSS state, so tracts read survey, block, section; quarter calls like SW/4 of SE/4 are read backwards, and appraisal-district maps confirm which way is north [yEKUUQ-zkaU @ 00:10:43].

Oklahoma and North Dakota take more clicks. Williams calls Oklahoma 'trickier'; the path is OCC, Divisions, Oil & Gas Conservation, then the OCC Well Data Finder map, searched by section in STR format such as '01 11N 02W' [GS_N0TTrBPY @ 00:05:27]. On the demo tract the NW/4 of Sec 1-11N-2W has no surface location, yet the Roller 1-36H bottom hole sits in Sec 1, and the 480-acre horizontal unit yields a decimal of net mineral acres divided by 480 times the lease royalty rate. Official production lives in the Oklahoma Tax Commission OkTAP PUN search, the severance-tax record: drop state code '35,' use county 109, oil product code 1 and gas code 5; the demo pulls 1,343.43 bbl for September 2023 [GS_N0TTrBPY @ 00:13:44]. North Dakota paywalls its data through dmr.nd.gov: the GIS viewer is free, Basic adds permits, plats, well files, and production for $50 a year, and Advanced adds pooling and spacing orders, downloads, and hearing audio [1HDBzjGoDdc @ 00:16:47]. Sands' free workaround is a WellDatabase account, which shows production view-only a couple of days behind, and co-founder John Farrell says the free Lite plan carries well headers, production, permits, completions, frac, construction, and formations [EDD6HaFdEzE @ 00:20:59].

Paid research gets rationed hard. Sands and Williams say county services charge per download or per page, so search for free and buy only chain-of-title instruments: mineral, quitclaim and warranty deeds, leases, assignments, ratifications, and memoranda, skipping mortgages and refinances as noise [u4xA0UNrxQs @ 00:12:41]. When the work goes to a professional, a landman starts around $600 a day and a limited title search runs about a week and roughly $3,000. Sands' WellDatabase play is to pay one month of Essential at $250 for decline curve analysis, then downgrade, replacing a $600 to $800 consultant; Farrell adds that Essential exports up to 10,000 wells and that an owner facing a buyer should upgrade for just that month [EDD6HaFdEzE @ 00:03:52]. For digitizing old paper, Sands converted a 50-year-old Exhibit A into Excel with AI, computing fractions like 1/12 times 1/160, but insists on spot-checking because early ChatGPT invented case law that 'didn't exist' [NkT15qxxGYM @ 00:14:34]. Hatcher's map recipe on the free Louisiana GIS stacks townships, sections, roads, Haynesville units, wells without busy serial labels, then bottom holes and bores, which on the 120-acre FJ tract exposed 6 wells in Sec 1 and 4 in Sec 2 [5hXDw_OFeQc @ 00:08:12].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Extract the legal description
   - Pull the section, township and range, survey and block, or metes and bounds call from the source deed.
   - Hatcher identifies three description types, PLSS, subdivision, and metes and bounds, and says to find the description in your source deed before touching a map [Steven Hatcher | Minerals Guy — 5hXDw_OFeQc @ 00:03:43].
2. Plot the tract on the state GIS
   - Search the state viewer by survey, block, section, or abstract and record the abstract number once found.
   - On the Texas RRC viewer, enter county plus survey name, block and section, or abstract number, then note the abstract (A112 in the demo) because most deeds omit it [Matt Sands — gFgW6n9nb-c @ 00:05:18].
3. Read the unit and compute your decimal
   - Open the spacing order and plat, confirm unit size, and divide net mineral acres by gross unit acres.
   - For the Oklahoma demo, the 480-acre horizontal unit gives a decimal of net mineral acres divided by 480 times the lease royalty rate [Matt Sands — GS_N0TTrBPY @ 00:08:36].
4. Audit production against checks
   - Compare official production to statements and treat a production drop as the trigger to pull the well file.
   - In North Dakota a rod pump installed in November 2018 with only 15 producing days shows why a production drop is your trigger to open the well file [Matt Sands — 1HDBzjGoDdc @ 00:12:42].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — Reading the public data like an auditor**
> 
> The state sites hand you raw numbers that do not match a royalty statement line for line. These are the adjustments and checks the practitioners run before trusting any figure.
> - RRC gas volumes are wet gas while royalty statements show dry gas plus NGLs after shrinkage, so treat the commission figure as an approximation and expect paid volume to be lower [Matt Sands — gFgW6n9nb-c @ 00:27:03].
> - Texas reports production at the lease level, so add up the wells yourself and read the disposition column for sold volume; April 2012 showed 1,990 bbl produced but 1,905 sold [Matt Sands — gFgW6n9nb-c @ 00:25:22].
> - A Mineral IQ alert for a new permit or rig within a 2-mile radius does not prove you hold an interest; verify it against the permit and the plat before acting [Matt Sands — GS_N0TTrBPY @ 00:02:49].
> - When feeding division orders to ChatGPT or Claude, redact your Social Security number first and pay for a tier that lets you opt out of training on your data [Matt Sands — NkT15qxxGYM @ 00:34:48].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The first fork is who does the digging. Matt Sands and Justin Williams push owners to run their own county research and buy only chain-of-title instruments, but they price the human alternative bluntly: a landman starts around $600 a day, and a limited title search runs about a week and roughly $3,000 [u4xA0UNrxQs @ 00:19:39]. Their newer playbook bolts AI onto the DIY route, feeding deeds into Claude or ChatGPT to build Excel run sheets and compute fractions like 1/12 x 1/160 off a 50-year-old Exhibit A, while warning that early ChatGPT invented case law outright: “Like these cases didn't exist” [NkT15qxxGYM @ 00:10:08]. Dave of titlesearch.com argues from the vendor side that a standard search only walks the surface chain, and that you need a special mineral rights search because the minerals run on a separate chain of title [JpHijIsWFL4 @ 00:01:50]. Money Moves With K sits on the buyer side of the same problem and tells anyone sourcing deals off energynet.com, energydomain.com or county clerk records to run due diligence and a title search to dodge scams [8JwnQSZGjU0 @ 00:04:56]. The operator read: DIY plus AI for the inventory, a paid human or specialty search when the chain itself is in doubt.

Data access splits the camp hardest. Justin Williams calls Oklahoma trickier because it takes more interfaces, and Matt Sands rates the Texas RRC GIS map as the better tool even though the OCC site has improved [GS_N0TTrBPY @ 00:17:04]. North Dakota goes further and walls permits, plats, well files and production behind a Basic subscription at $50 a year, with pooling and spacing orders and hearing audio pushed into an Advanced tier; Matt calls the paywall out directly, saying it is not empowering people to access data that is public information [1HDBzjGoDdc @ 00:19:26]. John Farrell of WellDatabase pitches the counter-model: a free Lite plan with well headers, production, permits, completions and formations, plus paid Essential at $250 a month per user for declines and forecasting, which he says is the only system you could turn on for a month, run the analysis, and turn back off [EDD6HaFdEzE @ 00:30:26]. Farrell's own split: small owners validating checks stay free, while acquirers and anyone facing a buyer upgrade. Steven Hatcher argues the free route is nearly universal, saying most if not all producing states run a similar free GIS system like the Louisiana viewer he demos [5hXDw_OFeQc @ 00:01:49]. Matt himself pays for WellDatabase's paid tier even while sending owners to the free account for North Dakota production.

On the endgame, practitioners do not hand owners one answer. Justin Williams rejects the reflexive family line, “it's not enough to just tell your heirs never sell the minerals,” because heirs may need to sell for medical bills, and he and Matt weigh a trust or LLC against probate while noting a NARO listener named David is running probate himself on small interests [yfXV7bIliEk @ 00:29:00]. Steven Hatcher, who buys minerals as well as practicing oil and gas law, frames his whole channel around teaching valuation so owners can decide to sell or hold in a market he calls incredibly opaque [t1R3KQsH3MQ @ 00:00:52]. The Mineral IQ sponsor read pushes the hold side with an heir who kept the minerals instead of selling at the wrong price [S680WOmo72o @ 00:00:19]. The valuation shortcuts conflict even inside one episode: Matt and Justin give 50 to 60 times average monthly cash flow for wells older than four to five years, then say the rule of thumb is not usable for the IRS, and add lease bonus times 2.5 to 3 only as a floor for undeveloped acreage [u4xA0UNrxQs @ 00:35:33]. Unsolicited offers, both hosts say, land below fair market value. On the Mineral Rights Forum, they warn, you may get four different answers.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest failures are paper failures. Matt Sands and Justin Williams open with heirs who first learn of an inheritance from a landman's phone call, Justin among them, and a NARO member who needed 18 months to untangle an estate from boxes stacked to the ceiling [yfXV7bIliEk @ 00:05:48]. The same episode flags that an unfunded trust still means probate, newly discovered properties must also be deeded into the trust, splitting small interests among four heirs can leave shares worth less than probate, and ancillary probate in another state can be very expensive with attorneys. Once personal representative deeds hit the record, buyers they call vultures start mailing offers. One host's hindsight: “I would absolutely keep everything that might be related to it” [u4xA0UNrxQs @ 00:11:17]. In that same tools episode they recall an attorney who said minerals weren't part of an estate. NARO hammers the point that a royalty statement is a starting point, not a complete ownership record, and that “A check in your name doesn't resolve a gap in the recorded chain of ownership” [o8FqhVu6MNM @ 00:04:48]. Dave of titlesearch.com adds that probate and heirship gaps are common, and that rights can revert to the surface owner if lease terms were breached [JpHijIsWFL4 @ 00:02:29].

Map-reading traps cost real decimals. Justin Williams warns on the Texas RRC walkthrough that a unit can touch your boundary while the first take point sits outside it, so you have to dive into the plat; on horizontal wells only the FTP-to-LTP lateral produces, your share depends on net acres and lateral footage inside your tract, and an allocation-well permit splits production by lateral length [gFgW6n9nb-c @ 00:13:20]. In Oklahoma the trap runs the other way: the demo NW/4 of Sec 1-11N-2W has no surface location, yet the Roller 1-36H bottom hole sits in Section 1, an interest an owner scanning only for pads would miss [GS_N0TTrBPY @ 00:04:35]. Matt also warns that a Mineral IQ alert inside a two-mile radius does not prove you own an interest; verify it against the permit and plat. North Dakota adds a dormancy trap: after 20 years unused you must file a statement of claim or lose the minerals to the surface owner [1HDBzjGoDdc @ 00:03:43]. Enverus flags that default spacing settings produce chaotic results and that large coefficient values are often associated with problematic distance results for child wellbores [hYa6oTD7seU @ 00:12:31].

The economics checks carry their own blind spots. On Texas gas, the RRC figure is wet gas while royalty statements show dry gas plus NGLs after shrinkage: “the volume of gas would be less than what's shown on the Railroad Commission website so just keep that in mind” [S680WOmo72o @ 00:28:58]. Texas also reports at the lease level, so you may have to add up wells and read the disposition column, as in April 2012 when 1,990 barrels were produced but 1,905 sold. On AI, Matt and Justin report that it cannot query RRC production data, that websites block AI tools, that free tiers train on your data, and they put it plainly: “don't upload a division order with your social security number on it” [NkT15qxxGYM @ 00:34:48]. John Farrell concedes WellDatabase in-product results are a ballpark, not a full economic analysis, and that large fields need expertise [EDD6HaFdEzE @ 00:29:37]. North Dakota's Basic tier brings friction of its own: a PDF form, email, card payment by phone, and several business days before access [1HDBzjGoDdc @ 00:20:25]. Budget that wait before a production drop forces you to pull the well file.
