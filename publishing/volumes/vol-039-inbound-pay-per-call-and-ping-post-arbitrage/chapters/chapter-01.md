# Chapter 01: Pay-Per-Call Fundamentals: The Model, the Vocabulary, and Choosing Your Lane

*Inbound vs. outbound calls, core PPC terminology, pay-per-call vs. pay-per-lead, affiliate vs. local client lead gen, SEO and rank-and-rent call sites, niche selection, startup requirements, and the industry's biggest obstacles*

> THE BIG MONEY PLAYBOOKS · THE FUCK YOU MONEY CLUB

## Executive Summary

Practitioners from Ringba, TrackDrive, Matt Wacek, John Ventura, ceohec, John Casto, Leadbase and WordsAtScale converge on one model: a buyer pays a fixed price for a live inbound call that clears pre-agreed rules on duration, geography and hours, and the publisher or broker pockets the spread between what traffic costs and what the call sells for. The street numbers are concrete: TrackDrive's $50 revenue, $35 payout, $15 margin split; ceohec's 40% minimum markup that turns a $150 roofing call into $210; Ringba's $25 call costing $2 to generate; Ventura's $150-$400 personal-injury and $70-$1,000 water-damage payouts. The fight is over the starting lane: Leadbase argues pay-per-lead is two and a half times likelier to succeed for beginners, Wacek weighs easy network approval against fatter direct-client checks, and Casto would rather sell 100 calls than 1,000 leads.

## Section 1: The Practitioner Consensus

Pay-per-call is a performance deal in which a business pays only for a live inbound phone call. Ringba frames it as a risk transfer: acquisition risk moves off the advertiser and onto the network or the affiliates promoting the offer, so the buyer can predict cost per customer, and a $10 call closing at 25% lands a customer at roughly $40 every time [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:01:48]. TrackDrive's masterclass states the trigger plainly: the advertiser pays because a real consumer had a conversation with the business, and that call must clear rules fixed in advance, usually a minimum length, a specific location and the hours someone is there to answer [_h1_mcKPGjY @ 00:01:28]. The premium rests on intent. A click can be curiosity or an accident, while a call means someone stopped what they were doing to talk to a person. Start & Grow Your Business gives the common floor condition: the caller stays on the line a minute or two so the buyer knows nobody was tricked into dialing, and such calls can pay around $20 each [8qGNYXEyrqI @ 00:01:24]. Matt Wacek strips it to the bone: the marketer bridges the gap between a homeowner with a flooded basement and the water damage contractor equipped to fix it [t7-UhvFv7r4 @ 00:00:15].

Speakers agree on the cast. TrackDrive lists the consumer, the publisher (also called an affiliate or seller) who creates the reason to call, the advertiser or buyer who answers, and an optional network that tracks, routes, bills the buyer, pays the publisher and keeps the difference [_h1_mcKPGjY @ 00:06:39]. Its vocabulary episode puts numbers on the split: a buyer pays $50 revenue, the publisher earns a $35 payout, and the $15 gap is margin; in a direct deal there is no margin to split, and a cap, concurrency limit or billable duration can kill a call's earnings no matter how good the traffic was [V1FTIO7peWA @ 00:04:15]. ceohec runs the same chain as a broker play: one party is the media buyer generating calls who does not know how to acquire buyers, the broker in the middle connects them and, in that speaker's words, makes all the money, and the buyer is the roofer, insurance agent or lawyer who sells [Rcc1hu6P28g @ 00:00:30]. Ringba warns that the middle is not a straight pipe: depending on caller information, concurrency, hours and capacity, the same network may route your calls to many different buyers, load balancing them all over the place [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:11:21]. Everyone gets paid only when the agreed rules are met.

The baseline prerequisites repeat across channels. Ringba's first rule for anyone building a real business is to run their own call tracking, because without it an affiliate cannot verify network statistics, cannot tell if a call was legitimate or even happened, and leaves partners free to steal from them [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:07:37]. Adam Young, who co-founded Ringba, says the industry is an incredible opportunity but never easy, and lists the skills that matter: digital marketing to make the phone ring, plus business development, because in pay-per-call you are selling consumers and have to pound the pavement in Skype, Telegram, Facebook and LinkedIn groups and at trade shows [J58YYa45MF4 @ 00:05:24]. Matt Wacek wants the paperwork done before the first offer: a parent company, an EIN, and a W-9 on file, since networks ask for it so they can issue a 1099 and expense the commissions paid [m-08JHhJeok @ 00:05:02]. John Casto adds the operating prerequisite that keeps a campaign alive past its shelf life: a disposition feedback loop, with some buyers sending dispositions daily and others weekly, which let Casto's team scale one solar client to roughly $10 million in revenue since 2016 [XQvPekJ-K8M @ 00:00:24].

## Section 2: Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The network-affiliate route runs on borrowed numbers. Ringba walks a debt consolidation offer paying $30 per call for callers carrying more than $10,000 in unsecured card debt: get approved, take the network's tracking number, route your own tracking number into theirs, put yours on a mobile ad, and collect when the buyer qualifies the caller [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:13:58]. Calls that fail the rules, such as Massachusetts callers sent to an insurance buyer licensed only in California, simply go unpaid. The same speaker recommends separate numbers for each site and marketing channel so you can see which placements work. Getting approved is light. Matt Wacek says networks mainly want a registered business, a look at your creatives, whether a website, videos, Craigslist listings or call-only ads, and a quick phone conversation, after which you can apply to campaigns and potentially run traffic the same day [uxYMcIUwpLA @ 00:00:27]. In Wacek's longer build guide, the cheapest proof is three to five YouTube livestream videos on page one for low-competition city-plus-service terms, each taking under ten minutes, which is generally what it takes for approval [m-08JHhJeok @ 00:09:06]. Before building city pages, Wacek cross-checks the network's zip code spreadsheet, because not every city or state is approved for every offer [m-08JHhJeok @ 00:14:41].

The broker lane is spread trading. ceohec's worked example buys a roofing call from a publisher at $150 on a 120-second buffer, marks it up a 40% minimum to $210, and closes a roofer on a minimum of 10 calls, collecting $2,100 up front while the publisher invoices only $1,500 on net-three to net-ten terms at the end of the week, leaving $600 gross and roughly $450 after a $150 cost to acquire that buyer [Rcc1hu6P28g @ 00:06:32]. The plumbing is a ping: the publisher's routing system asks the broker's platform whether a funded buyer is online, sends the call only when one is, and the buyer's prepaid wallet is debited $210 once the call crosses the buffer [Rcc1hu6P28g @ 00:08:11]. TrackDrive's vocabulary governs the rest of the deal: a cap is the most calls a buyer accepts per hour, day or month, like a law office that handles 35 a day, while concurrency is how many it can take at once, so five agents means the sixth caller waits or goes elsewhere [V1FTIO7peWA @ 00:03:10]. Global Call Forwarding lists the inbound stack behind all of this: IVR, call flow builders, forwarding, routing and call tracking [PWSdRBsFa5Y @ 00:04:09].

Payout benchmarks vary by an order of magnitude, which is why niche selection drives everything. John Ventura quotes $150 to $400 per qualified personal injury call and $200 to $600 or more for active mass tort campaigns, $70 to $1,000 in water damage, $50 to $70 for debt relief plus $150 to $250 enrollment bonuses, and $20 to $40 for auto against $30 to $100 or more for Medicare and health [KAXRt_1Gm_4 @ 00:06:09]. Matt Wacek's floor-to-ceiling range runs from $8 to $12 on low-ticket services up to $400 to $800 per qualified call in addiction recovery, where a center earns $30,000 to $40,000 per customer [t7-UhvFv7r4 @ 00:03:08]. The same speaker pins water damage at $50 to $100 per call through a network while metro clicks cost $200 to $450, arguing a direct client could pay $250 to $500 per qualified lead [uxYMcIUwpLA @ 00:05:07]. WordsAtScale shows how much the receiving category matters by pulling eLocal's public rate card: biohazard cleanup at a $125 to $265 top bid against $10 to $35 for junk removal and cleanouts, so the category you send the call into decides what you earn, and you should search for the category a buyer already pays for rather than your exact niche [DJFaYJ3O7aU @ 00:01:22].

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Pick the Paying Category, Then Pull the Rate Card
   - Choose a niche, map it to the broader category buyers already pay for, and pull the rate card for your city before building anything.
   - Search your niche plus pay per call and buy your niche calls, check Offervault, and treat the published rate card as a best-case top bid rather than your price [WordsAtScale — DJFaYJ3O7aU @ 00:05:27]
2. Stand Up the Entity and Approval Assets
   - Register the parent company, get an EIN and W-9 ready, and publish three to five page-one livestream videos or a clean site with service-area pages.

3. Layer Your Own Tracking Over the Network's Number
   - Buy your own tracking numbers, one per site or channel, and forward them into the network's assigned tracking number.

4. Lock the Qualification Terms in Writing
   - Define minimum call length, service area, hours and duplicate rules, and bill weekly or collect up front for a block of calls.
   - Put qualified in writing: minimum call length, inside the service area, and not the same caller twice in 30 days, because most fights are about what counts [WordsAtScale — DJFaYJ3O7aU @ 00:05:10]

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split is whether a newcomer should sell calls at all. Leadbase runs a business that is 100% pay-per-lead and argues a beginner's success rate in pay-per-call is multiple times lower, because calls demand more investment and creative urgent enough to make someone stop scrolling and dial right now [ewNftdwX784 @ 00:01:50]. Their profitability math sets a $20 insurance or mortgage form lead resold at $40 to $45 against a call costing $50 or more, with a starting risk multiplier closer to five times and a $100 call for someone still learning [ewNftdwX784 @ 00:09:14]. On John Casto's channel the pitch runs the other way: they would rather sell 100 calls than 1,000 leads, because a quality call should net more, scale better, get paused less, and earn higher caps [b2k9lFS6EWI @ 00:02:16]. The same conversation frames getting a Facebook scroller onto the phone as the hardest and most expensive step in the funnel, which is exactly why the affiliate who owns that step holds more power [b2k9lFS6EWI @ 00:01:44]. TrackDrive refuses to crown either model, saying clicks, leads, and calls each win depending on the product, though calls are often the highest-value action [_h1_mcKPGjY @ 00:03:35]. Leadbase's compromise is a ladder: leads first, then appointments, live transfers, and calls [ewNftdwX784 @ 00:14:33].

The second fault line is who stands between you and the business answering the phone. Matt Wacek describes the affiliate-network lane as low friction: apply with a registered business, show your creatives, take one call with an affiliate manager, and you can start running traffic the same day [uxYMcIUwpLA @ 00:00:27]. The price of that comfort is margin. In Wacek's water-damage example a network pays $50 to $100 per qualified call, while the same lead could command $250 to $500 sold direct to a contractor whose clicks cost $200 to $450 [uxYMcIUwpLA @ 00:05:07], and in his whiteboard breakdown a $50 commission usually means the network bills the business about $100 [t7-UhvFv7r4 @ 00:03:53]. WordsAtScale refuses to choose: a marketplace pays less but needs no outreach and has a buyer from day one, while a direct deal pays more and lets you own the relationship, so the marketplace proves your calls are worth something and catches overflow [DJFaYJ3O7aU @ 00:04:22]. TrackDrive adds the structural catch for going direct: there is no margin to split, but no network handles billing either, so each side tracks the numbers, invoices, and pays the other itself [_h1_mcKPGjY @ 00:07:13]. Every version trades convenience against money per call.

Niche advice diverges sharply depending on who is selling it. John Ventura ranks legal first, quoting $150 to $400 per qualified personal-injury call and $200 to $600 or more for active mass-tort campaigns such as Roundup, Camp Lejeune, and PFAS [KAXRt_1Gm_4 @ 00:01:00]. ceohec agrees motor-vehicle-accident work is lucrative, recalling law firms wiring around 30 grand on a test batch, yet calls it a hard niche and steers students toward final expense, ACA, and auto insurance as the easiest entry points [Rcc1hu6P28g @ 00:02:20]. Home services draws the loudest split. Ventura pitches water-damage restoration at $70 to $1,000 per qualified call with an 85% booking rate on exclusive calls [KAXRt_1Gm_4 @ 00:03:28], while Leonidas at PPCBasic tells a beginner flatly that home services is extremely competitive in the US and that auto insurance is the simpler starting lane [mpyPMRohxDQ @ 00:00:43]. Matt Wacek declines to rank at all, insisting every niche is profitable even when big-city plumbing or water-damage clicks cost more than the commission; his workaround is ranking organic pages across thousands of smaller cities instead of buying those clicks [m-08JHhJeok @ 00:05:58]. Payout size and entry difficulty come from different operators running different traffic engines.

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The costliest trap is running another company's numbers blind. Ringba says that without your own call tracking you cannot tell whether network statistics are accurate, whether a call was legitimate, or whether it happened at all, which means partners can steal from you plain and simple [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:08:13]. Networks running home-built proprietary trackers get extra suspicion because nobody can audit the code, so your own tracking number has to sit in front of theirs [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:10:31]. Adam Young frames the same failure from both sides of the trade: operators not tracking calls and return on investment are likely just going to lose a lot of money, the equivalent of flying an airplane blind [J58YYa45MF4 @ 00:08:46]. The second silent killer is the qualification rulebook. TrackDrive sets the billable-duration example at a 60-second minimum where a 40-second caller pays nothing, and warns that a call arriving after a cap fills or when the buyer has no free line may go unpaid no matter how good the traffic was [V1FTIO7peWA @ 00:04:35]. Ringba shows the IVR version: a debt caller pressing one for under $10,000 in unsecured debt, or Massachusetts calls sent to a California-only insurance buyer, probably earns nothing [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:12:50].

Getting paid is its own minefield. Leadbase recalls a debt campaign about seven years ago, sold per lead, where the buyer agreed to pay for three of ten sent, turning a $50 cost per lead into $150, and expects the same pattern plus common call-quality disputes in pay-per-call [ewNftdwX784 @ 00:06:55]. They also flag the affiliate-offer illusion: a solar offer paying $30 to $40 a call looks easy until real costs land at $150 to $250 per call and not every call gets paid [ewNftdwX784 @ 00:07:55]. WordsAtScale lists the fine print that burns people: minimum call lengths of roughly 60 to 120 seconds, buyer hours that leave a Sunday-night call unpaid, a seven-day hold before approval, rate cards that move, and a network that owns the buyer instead of you [DJFaYJ3O7aU @ 00:02:20]. Their scam screen is blunt: one network bidding over $900 for biohazard calls had a domain registered that February, no team, no reviews, and WhatsApp contact, which is bait rather than a better buyer [DJFaYJ3O7aU @ 00:03:12]. Direct deals carry a different risk. Matt Wacek says networks rarely skip a bill, but businesses sometimes stiff you, and per-lead terms mean fronting operating cash, with June calls potentially unpaid until mid-July [uxYMcIUwpLA @ 00:08:06]. His leverage: shut off the ads and the faucet of customers closes [uxYMcIUwpLA @ 00:07:02].

Volume also carries legal and reputational exposure. John Casto says the TCPA demands attention, recalls running a call center on a Duke Energy contract where the client expected complaints as proof of volume, and warns that once a litigator sues your client, you are on the hook too [XQvPekJ-K8M @ 00:03:23]. Casto adds that misleading solar ads built on Tesla get clicks but get you shut off by the client and marked in a small industry, admitting their own operation paid what it owed after past compliance issues [XQvPekJ-K8M @ 00:04:41]. Leonidas reports auto-insurance operators running fake AI videos on Facebook to push people into calling, and calls SMS a big but really dangerous channel in the US [mpyPMRohxDQ @ 00:01:31]. The economics check is plain arithmetic. Ringba's buyer math: $10 a call at a 25% close ratio means roughly $40 to acquire each customer [4kaOQgGnpJo @ 00:01:48]. ceohec's broker math: buy a roofing call at $150 on a 120-second buffer, mark it up 40% to $210, sell a ten-call block, and keep $600 gross, closer to $450 after the roughly $150 spent acquiring that buyer [Rcc1hu6P28g @ 00:06:49]. Run both sides of that equation before routing a single call at scale.
