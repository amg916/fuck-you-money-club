## 1. Assign twelve plays to accountable seats, not twelve miniature businesses  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Treat the twelve mapped operator plays as one controlled inventory business with three operating lanes. For this chapter's staffing map, label them P01 multi-exchange broadcasting, P02 exchange spread capture, P03 event drop-curve repricing, P04 NFL playoff strips, P05 NBA playoff strips, P06 SSL-linked playoff options, P07 suite seat-splitting, P08 suite group placements, P09 Super Bowl hospitality, P10 World Cup hospitality, P11 F1 hospitality, and P12 permissioned sponsor-allotment bundles for Masters or Final Four demand. These labels allocate work; they do not assert that every rights holder permits resale or every ticket includes hospitality access. The owner underwrites acquisition, signs supplier contracts, approves event exposure, and owns cash forecasting. Contractor C1 operates listings, pricing rules, and transfer exceptions. Contractor C2 handles corporate proposals, suite guest manifests, supplier confirmations, and invoice matching. Both contractors work against documented deliverables rather than sharing an unrestricted administrator account. The owner remains the escalation seat when either specialist is unavailable. Maintain one inventory identifier across the purchase document, POS record, exchange order, barcode transfer, and accounting cohort. Every play must inherit these controls before its first listing, including a backup contact who can halt sales without needing the owner's personal device.

Separate the capital and settlement profiles before assigning workload. Multi-exchange resale uses SkyBox, Automatiq, or Logitix as the selected system of record, with verified integrations to StubHub, Vivid Seats, SeatGeek, and TickPick; integration coverage and synchronization latency must be tested rather than assumed. Inventory is commonly purchased before sale, and an exchange may release proceeds only after the event under its seller agreement. Suite splitting adds a fixed suite commitment, per-seat occupancy risk, catering minimums, parking entitlements, and a hard guest-manifest deadline. A partially sold suite can generate cash receipts while remaining economically underwater. Tentpole hospitality adds supplier deposits, staged corporate collections, hotel or transport deliverables, and refund mismatches across multiple providers. Super Bowl, World Cup, and F1 packages therefore need component-level obligations, not merely ticket quantities. Never treat a sponsor allocation as freely transferable because a contact offered it informally. Confirm the supplier's authority and each component's resale restrictions before taking customer money. For all three lanes, measure time from acquisition cash leaving the bank until unrestricted settlement arrives. A profitable event with a delayed payout can exhaust the same working capital needed to fulfill the following week's contracted orders.

Expand to the five-person pod only when an eight-week workload record shows sustained exceptions or corporate fulfillment exceeding existing capacity, and settled contribution funds the added seat. The second full-time employee becomes operations lead, owning daily exception triage, event readiness, and contractor quality assurance. C1 remains the exchange execution specialist; C2 remains hospitality fulfillment; C3 supplies reconciliation and reporting deliverables. The owner retains capital allocation and senior corporate relationships but should no longer be the only person who can explain an event's margin. Write a responsibility matrix separating purchase authority, listing authority, refund authority, and bank-payment release. Require two-person evidence review for supplier bank-detail changes without giving every worker banking access. Contractor status follows actual working conditions, not the letter C in a staffing model: evaluate federal employment standards, tax classification, and applicable state tests before imposing employee-like schedules or control. Use contracted event coverage windows and service outcomes where genuine independence exists. If classification requires payroll employment, reprice the operating plan rather than hiding payroll costs inside a nominal contractor budget. The scorecard's team counts describe capacity assumptions, not permission to misclassify the people who perform the work.

## 2. Pay for settled quality and review a scorecard that exposes hidden losses  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Contractor compensation should purchase reliable execution, not encourage inventory churn. For the Base pod, an illustrative monthly contract could pay C1 a $1,200 service retainer and C2 a $1,000 service retainer for explicitly bounded deliverables. Add a combined variable pool of up to 3% of positive event contribution after acquisition, exchange fees, markdowns, customer remedies, and event-specific fulfillment costs, but before the pool itself. Specify this ordering in the contract so the calculation cannot become circular. Pay the variable component after settlement reconciliation and a documented claims review, with a final true-up under agreed terms. These figures illustrate contract design, not additional profit on top of the canonical case; retainers, incentives, replacement coverage, and employer costs where applicable must fit inside its expense allowance. Do not impose arbitrary wage deductions or pass every exchange penalty directly to a worker. Assign errors by evidence, distinguish platform outages from controllable mistakes, and define commercially reasonable remediation. A contractor who flags a cancellation risk early should not lose the same quality score as one who conceals it. Separate speed, accuracy, and economic outcomes so a fast transfer cannot disguise an unprofitable purchase or an unresolved customer complaint.

Hold a thirty-minute weekly operating review using the same event-cohort export every time. Start with unrestricted cash, committed acquisitions, customer refund obligations, and expected post-event exchange receipts; then examine contribution and service quality. Set an internal target of zero duplicate fills and 100% evidence-backed transfer completion by contractual deadlines. For active event windows, contract an exception acknowledgment target such as fifteen minutes, with named backup coverage and an earlier internal transfer deadline than the exchange requires. These are negotiated service targets, not claims about vendor uptime. Track synchronization acknowledgment age and stop affected listings whenever their state cannot be verified. A POS auto-hold must demonstrably propagate across enabled exchanges; a local screen showing sold is insufficient. Model the cited 200% replacement-penalty exposure as a stress case wherever relevant contractual terms support it, rather than assuming every marketplace uses an identical penalty. Review unsold units by days to event, realized versus underwritten spread, postponement reserve coverage, and payout days past contractual expectation. Give each breach an owner, a cash impact, and a dated correction. Never accept ticket count or gross billings as a substitute for these operating measurements.

Concentration discipline belongs in the weekly scorecard, not only the eventual sale presentation. Cap each corporate customer and each acquisition counterparty at 25% of trailing-twelve-month retained contribution and at 25% of forward committed acquisition exposure, using separate denominators for the two tests. Aggregate related entities and commonly controlled purchasing accounts. Also cap each exchange at 25% of unsettled receivables when the available channel mix permits that policy; if it does not, reduce commitments rather than labeling the risk diversified. Launch cohorts with tiny denominators will naturally fail percentage tests, so identify them explicitly as concentrated and stop increasing the dominant exposure until the book broadens. A buyer should see that history, not a retrospective rewrite. All-in pricing checks belong beside transfer accuracy: 16 CFR Part 464 addresses live-event ticket price presentation, while New York Article 25 adds jurisdiction-specific reseller requirements. The BOTS Act, 15 U.S.C. 45c, prohibits covered security-measure circumvention and certain sales involving tickets obtained that way. Contractors must use permitted inventory sources and approved integrations, never queue-bypass automation. Preserve acquisition provenance and listing price evidence so operational quality remains auditable after a contractor leaves or an exchange disputes fulfillment.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Freeze the weekly evidence set
   - Export orders, inventory, transfers, supplier commitments, and bank activity.
   - Use a common cutoff timestamp and preserve the source exports before modifying classifications.
2. Reconcile cash and event exposure
   - Tie exchange receivables and customer deposits to individual event cohorts.
   - Separate available bank cash from amounts economically required for refunds, fulfillment, and unpaid suppliers.
3. Score service and contribution
   - Review the seven scorecard measures and calculate contractor variable compensation.
   - Apply documented expense ordering, resolve attribution disputes, and preserve signed review notes.
4. Close exceptions with evidence
   - Assign each breach a responsible seat, deadline, and measurable correction.
   - Carry unresolved items into the next review and restrict new commitments when liquidity or fulfillment remains impaired.

## 3. Reconcile gross billings, retained contribution and event-cohort cash  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Use the Formula v2.0 retained-share bridge without confusing ticket transaction value with operator economics. Conservative gross operating billings of $220,000 less $132,000 of ticket and suite acquisition produce $88,000 retained contribution at 40%. Subtract $61,600 of remaining costs to reach $26,400 owner profit: 12% of gross billings and 30% of retained contribution. Base billings of $1,010,000 less $606,000 acquisition produce $404,000 retained contribution; $212,100 of remaining costs leave $191,900, or 19.0% gross and 47.5% retained margin. Scaled billings of $1,800,000 less $1,080,000 acquisition produce $720,000 retained contribution; $252,000 remaining costs leave $468,000, or 26% gross and 65% retained margin. Remaining costs must include exchange sell fees, unsold inventory and markdown slippage, expected cancellation or postponement clawback losses, labor, software, fulfillment, and financing charges where incurred. Do not deduct acquisition twice. Do not count hotel or venue transaction values outside the operator's contracted billings. These are exact annual planning reconciliations; the expense residuals are constraints to substantiate with detailed budgets, not evidence that any particular staffing contract automatically achieves the stated margin. A cost overrun changes forecast profit rather than disappearing inside the fixed 40% retained-share assumption.

Build an event subledger that separates recognition from collection. Record customer deposits against unperformed obligations, supplier advances against undelivered components, ticket inventory where appropriate, and exchange receivables according to the actual contractual rights. Recognize revenue when the relevant performance obligation is satisfied under the adopted accounting policy, not simply when a buyer pays or an event date passes. Ticket-only delivery and a hosted hospitality package may have different fulfillment patterns. Assess principal-versus-agent presentation under ASC 606 for each arrangement: control before transfer, responsibility for fulfillment, inventory risk, and pricing discretion require documented judgment. The scorecard's 40% retained contribution is an analytical net basis, not a universal instruction to report exactly 40% as financial-statement revenue. A principal arrangement may report gross revenue and acquisition cost separately; an agent arrangement reports its fee or commission. Maintain a bridge from either presentation to the canonical scorecard. When an exchange remits cash net of sell fees, reconstruct the gross settlement and record the fee once. Otherwise a bookkeeper can both understate billings and deduct the same fee again, creating a false explanation for an event's apparently weak retained margin.

Close cohorts by event, purchase batch, channel, and package component, retaining both underwriting and actual outcomes. Treat a markdown as reduced realized proceeds or an explicitly bridged planning variance, never both. Unsold expired inventory requires the appropriate write-down; recoverable postponed inventory needs a supported recoverability assessment rather than an automatic full loss. Distinguish expected clawback expense and related obligations from a management cash reserve: earmarking cash does not itself create another expense. Post-event payout delay affects liquidity and any actual financing cost, while a doubtful receivable requires a separate collection assessment. Maintain a rolling thirteen-week cash forecast and stress a thirty-day settlement delay alongside simultaneous event postponements. The stated $10,000-$50,000 startup capital range does not automatically finance the Scaled case's acquisition pipeline. Fund timing gaps through retained cash, genuine supplier terms, or appropriately priced facilities and include their charges in forecasts. Monthly operating overhead within the $1,800-$11,000 planning range excludes the separate inventory funding requirement. A buyer should be able to trace a single Super Bowl package from signed scope through supplier payment, customer delivery, final settlement, and the remaining refund exposure without asking the owner to reconstruct it orally.

> 
> Complete these four checks before expanding a tentpole allocation, adding permanent payroll, or presenting earnings to a buyer.

## 4. Price transferable earnings, document ownership and preserve the cash-cow option  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Build the transfer file before asking anyone to pay a premium. Obtain signed confidentiality terms, a present assignment of commissioned copyright interests, and delivery of editable source materials from each contractor. Under 17 U.S.C. 204(a), a copyright ownership transfer generally requires a signed writing from the owner or an authorized agent. Do not rely exclusively on a work-for-hire label for contractor-created software, pricing scripts, or operating manuals. Identify pre-existing contractor tools and secure adequate licenses for anything embedded in the deliverable. Copyright ownership does not itself transfer exchange accounts, customer permissions, supplier contracts, or venue entitlements. List assignment and change-of-control provisions separately, then obtain required counterparties' written permissions. Use business-controlled email, role-based access, documented recovery procedures, and individual credentials; transfer administration through permitted platform procedures rather than sharing passwords. Retain corporate purchasing histories only within the applicable contractual and privacy permissions. A clean data room includes event-cohort statements, bank reconciliations, tax filings, rights provenance, contractor agreements, incident history, and customer renewal evidence. It also records what cannot transfer. A buyer discounts unassignable access and personal favors even when those relationships generated excellent historical ticket spreads.

Value the operation on normalized transferable earnings rather than gross ticket billings. For preliminary small-business underwriting, use an explicitly illustrative 2.0-3.0 times normalized seller's discretionary earnings for an owner-operated book and 3.0-4.0 times normalized EBITDA for a genuinely manager-run business. These are negotiation sensitivities, not observed transaction comparables or an automatic premium for using a POS. Before applying them, reconcile owner profit to the selected earnings definition, adding back only evidenced eligible items and preserving recurring labor, claims, and replacement costs. If Base owner profit of $191,900 is treated as an SDE proxy with no further adjustments, the illustrative enterprise-value range is $383,800-$575,700. If an absentee buyer must spend an additional $90,000 annually on fully loaded replacement management, the simplified earnings proxy becomes $101,900; at 3.0-4.0 times, the range is $305,700-$407,600. This proxy is not audited EBITDA without the full reconciliation. Apply replacement expense only for duties not already funded in the pod. Strong margins do not justify counting the same manager twice or pretending the founder's sales work has no economic cost.

Negotiate price, working capital, and contingent risk as separate schedules. Enterprise value is not closing cash: debt, agreed cash treatment, normal working capital, transaction expenses, escrows, and contingent consideration change proceeds. Define whether ticket inventory, supplier advances, exchange receivables, customer deposits, and refund obligations enter the working-capital calculation. Prevent deposits from appearing as free seller cash while the buyer inherits all fulfillment costs. Set any earnout against precisely defined settled cohort contribution, with rules for cancellation losses, channel migration, related-party sales, and buyer-controlled marketing spend. A headline multiple inflated by an uncertain earnout may deliver less value than a lower cash offer. The premium case rests on diversified repeat corporate demand, transferable supplier rights, low founder dependence, clean claims history, and reproducible settlement records. If those conditions do not support an attractive offer, retain the cash-cow structure: distribute only cash above event commitments, tax obligations, expected remedies, and the stress liquidity buffer. Compare holding the business with sale proceeds after taxes and transition workload, not with a promotional multiple. Revisit the decision after each full event season using actual owner hours and cash distributions.
