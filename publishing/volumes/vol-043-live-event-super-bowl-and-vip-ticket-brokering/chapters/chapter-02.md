## Executive Summary

Super Bowl seats come from a tightly carved allocation. Each competing team gets 17.5 percent, the host about 5 percent, and the other 29 clubs 1.2 percent each. The NFL routes the remaining 25.2 percent to hospitality partners such as NFL On Location and Prime Sport, which sell at market value. Face value starts near $950, but buyers can only reach it through season-holder raffles, roster allotments, sponsors, or contests. Brokers fill their boards from players, coaches, agents and team front-door deals, sometimes peeling seats off with $2,500 cash at pre-game parties. Reported resale floors ran from $3,500 to $4,500, with Ticketmaster listings up to $16,000 and other sites up to $30,000. Practitioners agree on two rules: stay on digital rails and distrust below-face offers. They split on whether prices sag toward kickoff, and on whether packages or a home couch deliver better value.

## The Practitioner Consensus: Face Value Is Locked Up, Resale Is the Real Market, Digital Is the Fraud Shield

Shiraz of thumbsuprun lays out the allocation math every Super Bowl sourcer works from. The NFL hands each qualifying team 17.5 percent of the seats. On a 65,000-ticket assumption for Raymond James Stadium, that comes to 11,375 per club, and the pile gets carved up before any season seat holder sees it. Players and coaches each get two tickets plus the option to buy up to another 13 at face value, so almost a thousand seats go to roster and staff [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:00:59]. The host team gets about 5 percent, roughly 3,250 seats, with around 2,000 left after staff take theirs. The other 29 clubs get 1.2 percent each, about 780 seats apiece [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:02:29]. The 24H SPORT rundown of USA Today Sports broker reporting puts the non-playing teams' share at about one third of all Super Bowl tickets. League rules bar selling those seats for profit on the secondary market, yet that is precisely where some of them surface [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:00:02]. Shiraz closes the loop: the remaining 25.2 percent stays under NFL control. The league dumps it on official hospitality sponsors such as NFL On Location and Prime Sport, which move it at market value, not face [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:03:21].

Every practitioner who touches pricing agrees that face value is a lottery ticket, not a sourcing channel. Shiraz says there is no real way to land face-value seats unless you are a season seat holder, a player, an NFL sponsor, or a lottery winner. Even the season-holder draw is a small chance nobody should rely on [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:04:02]. ABC7 Bay Area shows the draw in action. 49ers season ticket holders get thrown into a raffle for face-value seats, and after the NFC Championship Ray Pena received the email saying the bid was not chosen. So did everyone in Pena's circle [YZ3TuDNRcvw @ 00:01:46]. The spread between face and street is the entire business. The 24H SPORT report pegs face value starting at $950, with players often holding $1,250 seats, while seats in that band were listed on StubHub and TickPick for about $4,000 [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:01:17]. Chicoblanco, bartering up from a single penny, worked a different inside line. A friend who works for the NFL could get seats for $2,000 to $3,000, which meant the trade value had to climb to near $3,000 before that door would open [v-LGKb8taFw @ 00:01:08].

On fraud, the consensus is blunt: stay inside verified digital rails and treat private sellers as the threat. Gary Harper of Arizona's Family reports that the league moved to strictly digital tickets. The expert interviewed tells buyers and sellers to stay away from private parties advertising on the internet, because there is no way to verify the authenticity of what you are purchasing [3kV_14yzGfk @ 00:01:27]. The recommended rail is NFL Ticket Exchange operated by Ticketmaster. There the ticket cannot be copied or screenshotted, a setup credited with eliminating the fraud that frequently happens through private transactions. Going outside it, the expert says, is asking for trouble [3kV_14yzGfk @ 00:01:53]. Shiraz backs the mobile-ticket logic: once seats are transferred into your account you should be good to go. Hard-stock paper, though, demands due diligence so you do not pay a thousand bucks for a piece of paper that does nothing [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:07:18]. Shiraz and the Glendale police source on Yea Baby land on the same tripwire: a price too good to be true. Shiraz flags listings a thousand or two thousand dollars under market. Glendale cites a Super Bowl ticket below face value as the giveaway and closes with buyer beware [0a9pLnuCUAk @ 00:01:13].

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers: Roster Pipelines, Cash Buys, Price Alerts and Packages

Broker sourcing runs through the roster and the front office. One anonymous broker told USA Today Sports, as relayed by 24H SPORT, that about 80 percent of the seats on the broker's board that week, priced from $3,500 apiece, came directly from players, coaches and their agents [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:00:40]. TickPick co-CEO Bret Goldberg describes the street play. Brokers work the parties in the days before kickoff carrying wads of cash and tell anyone holding a seat that they have $2,500 cash right now. Goldberg contrasts that pitch with listing on TickPick and maybe making $2,500 or $3,000 later [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:03:42]. The bulk play is the front-door deal. Goldberg says it is not unusual for teams to sell their allotment straight to brokers, and some brokers source almost exclusively from teams. A broker who buys 500 season tickets during the regular season can reasonably expect Super Bowl seats from that club [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:05:35]. On the retail end, TickPick's own Super Bowl 50 walkthrough is candid that it is not an official reseller of the game. It is a marketplace where ticket brokers and individuals list inventory, and it pitched itself as the best route with only a couple of weeks to go [tY7hYkxsK3k @ 00:00:01].

For buyers, the tooling is mostly price discovery and patience. TickPick's walkthrough shows all-in prices with three sort options: by price, by best deal (which weighs seat location and price together), or by location. It also offers a price alert. Set two tickets at $3,000, below the $3,100 price showing at the time, pick the level you will sit in, and get notified when listings drop under that number [tY7hYkxsK3k @ 00:00:41]. Arizona's Family checked Ticketmaster and found a $4,500 minimum running to $16,000, with other sites going as high as $30,000 [3kV_14yzGfk @ 00:02:28]. Shiraz sets the resale floor at a minimum of $2,000 to $3,000. Prices can reach $4,000 to $6,000, and in some years top $8,000 just to get into the stadium, depending on the matchup, the location and what else is happening in the world [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:05:59]. The sharpest number play came from Ray Pena on ABC7. Pena made an early-season gamble through Sports Illustrated, putting up nearly $4,300 for the rights to two seats that would be lost if the 49ers missed the Super Bowl. Pena then paid $11,300 total for two lower end zone tickets trading above $12,000 apiece in the resale marketplace, after carrying that stress all year [YZ3TuDNRcvw @ 00:00:54].

Packages are the other sourcing lane, and the numbers are steep. For the Patriots-Eagles game in Minneapolis, the NFL's own On Location service sold packages bundled with concerts and experiences for $6,299 to $13,499, and those sold out. Prime Sport, which the NFL bought that December, still had more than 100 seats listed about 24 hours before kickoff, the cheapest at $2,600 [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:04:43]. For the Eagles' New Orleans trip, Jessica Parker, founder of The Travel Whisperer travel agency, told 6abc to buy an all-inclusive package from a reputable source rather than piece together ticket, airfare and hotel separately. Hotels started at $5,000 a night. On Location's ticket plus pregame party started above $6,600 per person, rising to $9,475 with hotel and $11,850 with airfare [w_3Yfr30xIo @ 00:01:16]. Those quotes covered only terrace-level seats and the cheapest airfare available, so any upgrade adds hundreds or even thousands more [w_3Yfr30xIo @ 00:02:27]. The rush is real. For the Eagles' Minneapolis run, M&J Travel in Newtown Square sold an airfare, hotel and game-ticket package starting at $7,600 per person. Phones rang nonstop, a party of four booked at midnight, and the owners expected the packages to sell out by the end of the day [cd508uGHJxU @ 00:01:06].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

Fraud starts with the counterparty. Gary Harper of Arizona's Family warns buyers to stay away from private parties advertising on the internet, because there is no way to verify the authenticity of what you are purchasing, and private transactions are flagged as asking for trouble [3kV_14yzGfk @ 00:01:27]. The league's all-digital format is the countermeasure: a ticket that cannot be copied or screenshotted, sold through NFL Ticket Exchange operated by Ticketmaster to eliminate the fraud that frequently happens in private deals [3kV_14yzGfk @ 00:01:53]. Shiraz adds a pricing tripwire. There is generally a market price for Super Bowl seats, so a listing $1,000 or $2,000 below resale deserves real suspicion. Anyone handed hard-stock paper should inspect it rather than pay a thousand bucks for a piece of paper that does nothing [O-Sq4DDGKAY @ 00:07:18]. Glendale police echo the rule from the street: if it seems too good to be true it probably is, and a Super Bowl ticket below face value should raise concern [0a9pLnuCUAk @ 00:01:13]. Mobile delivery helps, Shiraz says, because once tickets transfer into your account you should be good to go. Until that transfer lands, verification sits with the buyer at every handoff.

The player pipeline is real and runs against league rules. One broker told USA Today Sports that about 80 percent of the tickets on the broker's board that week, starting at $3,500 apiece, came directly from players, coaches and their agents. The broker spoke only under anonymity because the NFL bans the practice [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:00:40]. Goldberg says brokers also work pregame parties with wads of cash, offering $2,500 on the spot, because cash right now beats waiting on a $2,500 to $3,000 TickPick listing [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:03:42]. Enforcement bites rarely but hard: the one major scalping scandal cost a Vikings coach a $100,000 fine and two assistant coaches $10,000 each in 2005 [pAU7eEVgp1A @ 00:04:23]. Any broker buying from rosters is carrying inventory the league explicitly prohibits. The street has its own lines. In Glendale, scalpers can charge whatever they want but must stay inside a designated resale corral on the north side of Maryland Avenue at Sunrise Boulevard, and selling anywhere outside it is illegal. With a lot of cash changing hands, police tell victims to find an officer immediately, while reminding everyone it is always buyer beware [0a9pLnuCUAk @ 00:00:59].

Economics checks come from people who actually paid. Ray Pena's early rights purchase worked, but had the Niners missed, the nearly $4,300 was gone, and Pena admits being pretty stressed out all year [YZ3TuDNRcvw @ 00:01:13]. Chicoblanco's penny chain hit three walls. The first was a five-day trading clock. The second was a voicemail saying the discounted tickets needed an offer within two days, while the chain sat at only $500 in trade value. The third was a final rejection: a ring appraised around $2,300 at a pawn shop was judged not enough without something like a Brady jersey added [v-LGKb8taFw @ 00:18:37]. The football-to-tickets run nearly died on a counterparty who refused filming, left in a hurry and handed over a record player that turned out broken. Only a family member's old record player from the attic kept that run alive [we9NjqAzpU0 @ 00:04:31]. Package math also stretches. 6abc notes the On Location quotes assume terrace level seats and the cheapest airfare, so upgrades add hundreds or even thousands more, and individual item prices swing up or down over the final ten days [w_3Yfr30xIo @ 00:02:27].
