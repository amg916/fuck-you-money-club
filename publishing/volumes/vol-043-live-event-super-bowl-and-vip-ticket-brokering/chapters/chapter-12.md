## 1. Days 1-7: Define the Twelve Plays, Lock Rights, and Ring-Fence Cash  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Day 1 starts with a bounded operating map, not twelve simultaneous launches. For this chapter, the twelve mapped plays are P01 authorized multi-exchange ticket resale; P02 contractual sponsor-allotment resale; P03 NFL playoff-option strips; P04 NBA playoff-option strips; P05 full-suite seat-splitting; P06 weekday suite corporate seat blocks; P07 Super Bowl hospitality bundles; P08 World Cup hospitality bundles; P09 F1 hospitality bundles; P10 Masters hospitality bundles; P11 Final Four hospitality bundles; and P12 repeat corporate hospitality procurement. These are operating workstreams within Candidate #43, not twelve independent profit forecasts. P01-P04 carry ticket acquisition and exchange settlement exposure; P05-P06 concentrate capital in a suite whose unsold seats expire together; P07-P12 add supplier deposits, package obligations, and corporate collection timing. Score each against transferable inventory, verified buyer demand, cash duration, and replacement exposure. Choose one funded pilot and one unfunded backup. Favor nearby events with inspectable supply and published payout terms rather than a distant tentpole that consumes the entire launch reserve. The owner handles purchasing, contracts, and exceptions; contractor A handles listings and transfer evidence; contractor B handles buyer outreach and reconciliation. Reserve forty owner hours and ten hours per contractor weekly, with event-day coverage scheduled explicitly.

Use a $25,000 illustrative launch bankroll inside the canonical $10,000-$50,000 startup range. Allocate $10,000 to acquisition exposure, $7,500 to a protected refund and replacement reserve, $3,000 to first-month overhead, and $4,500 to uncommitted cash. Cap cumulative overhead at $750 on Day 7, $1,500 on Day 14, $2,250 on Day 21, and $3,000 on Day 30. The illustrative monthly budget assigns $1,200 to contractor coverage, $1,200 to software and integration access, $300 to insurance and accounting, and $300 to outbound activity. Obtain actual quotations before committing; if a suitable vendor exceeds its allowance, reduce scope rather than silently increasing burn. Acquisition payments are tracked separately from overhead but still consume bank cash. Owner draws reduce available capital and cannot disappear from the liquidity test. A $10,000 launch must shrink inventory exposure, not borrow the protected reserve to imitate this allocation. Do not buy a whole suite merely because its seat-level spread looks attractive. Its deposit, catering minimum, and unsold-seat risk can exceed the bankroll before a single customer settles. Keep all reserves liquid and exclude unsettled exchange receivables from immediately available cash.

Days 2-7 establish enforceable supply and observable inventory behavior. For every proposed purchase, record seller identity, original order evidence, section and row, delivery deadline, transfer restrictions, event status, and cancellation treatment. A suite agreement must expressly permit the intended seat distribution and identify food, alcohol, parking, and admission entitlements. Hospitality components require separate supplier confirmation; possessing an event ticket does not establish authorization to resell an official package. Map purchaser and event jurisdictions before listing. Apply 15 U.S.C. §45c to acquisition methods: prohibit queue bypass, purchase-limit circumvention, and security-measure evasion. Review New York Arts and Cultural Affairs Law Article 25 where applicable, including reseller licensing and ticket-price disclosures. Apply 16 CFR Part 464 to covered live-event price presentations, including mandatory package charges. Evaluate SkyBox, Automatiq, or Logitix against documented marketplace support rather than assumed universal connectivity. Test StubHub, Vivid Seats, SeatGeek, and TickPick individually when available under the actual account agreement. A local auto-hold is necessary but cannot prove instantaneous remote withdrawal. Log acknowledgments and suspend any connector with unbounded lag. Where a test environment is unavailable, use vendor-supervised validation or one controlled real listing; never manufacture customer purchases to generate test activity.

## 2. Days 8-14: Build Contribution Ledgers and Run One Controlled Pilot  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Days 8-10 build the ledger before the sales dashboard. Preserve the scorecard's exact annual relationships: Conservative gross fee basis of $220,000 less $132,000 acquisition cost produces $88,000 retained revenue; subtracting $61,600 of remaining costs produces $26,400 owner profit. Base uses $1,010,000 less $606,000 acquisition cost, leaving $404,000 retained revenue; $212,100 of remaining costs leaves $191,900 profit. Scaled uses $1,800,000 less $1,080,000 acquisition cost, leaving $720,000 retained revenue; $252,000 of remaining costs leaves $468,000 profit. These reconcile to gross-basis margins of 12%, 19.0%, and 26%, and retained margins of 30%, 47.5%, and 65%. Remaining costs must include exchange selling fees, inventory markdowns and write-offs, postponement or cancellation reserves, payment costs, carrying costs, and operating overhead. The 40% retained share is the supplied net-contribution model, not a declaration that every contract receives identical ASC 606 presentation. Determine principal-versus-agent treatment from actual control and contractual responsibilities. Never label the underlying event economy or corporate entertainment budget as operator revenue. Store gross customer consideration, channel fees, and net remittance separately. If an imported exchange payout already deducts a fee, reconstruct the bridge before applying the scorecard; do not deduct that fee twice.

Keep the three operating lanes economically separate even when their headline retained share matches. A multi-exchange example starts with $1,000 customer consideration and $600 acquisition cost. Deduct $100 selling fees, $50 inventory slippage, $25 cancellation reserve, $10 carrying cost, and $50 allocated overhead: owner contribution is $165, not the $400 acquisition spread. A suite example sells eight seats for $1,250 each against a $6,000 suite commitment. Deduct $200 payment costs, $600 catering obligations, $500 markdown allowance, $300 cancellation reserve, $100 carrying cost, and $500 overhead; contribution is $1,800. The suite's entire acquisition commitment remains exposed if buyers disappear. A hospitality example bills $20,000 against $12,000 ticket and supplier acquisition cost, then deducts $600 payment costs, $1,000 service delivery, $800 slippage, $600 cancellation reserve, $200 carrying cost, and $1,000 overhead, leaving $3,800. These illustrative transactions explain mechanics; they do not replace the canonical annual scenarios. Separate supplier deposits, buyer deposits, event delivery, and final settlement dates. Corporate deposits can support procurement only within their contractual refund conditions. They are neither immediately earned profit nor unrestricted owner distributions. Exchange payouts arriving after the event create a different capital cycle from directly invoiced suite seats, even with identical accounting margin.

Days 11-14 run the smallest pilot that can validate the full chain. For P01, use transferable inventory with enough time to withdraw listings, complete delivery, and correct an exception before doors open. For P05-P06, require a signed suite agreement and a seat-level allocation register before accepting money. For P07-P12, quote only supplier-confirmed components and specify substitutions, delivery milestones, and cancellation treatment. Keep P03-P04 option exposure separate from delivered admissions: document what happens when a team fails to qualify, when a series ends early, or when an SSL agreement restricts resale. Contractor B records qualified opportunities and signed orders without counting either as settled sales. Contractor A records every listing change, purchase acknowledgment, hold, transfer, and buyer receipt. The owner approves acquisition and handles unresolved exceptions. Test a contractual replacement charge as high as 200% of the affected order where the actual channel terms support that exposure; do not assume every marketplace uses that formula. Combine unrelated-event replacement exposure with a canceled-event refund obligation and an unsold-inventory markdown shock. Avoid counting mutually exclusive remedies twice. If the protected reserve cannot absorb that stress, reduce inventory before pursuing another buyer, regardless of the apparent quoted margin.

## 3. Days 15-21: Prove Fulfillment, Reconcile Payouts, and Measure Failure  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Week three converts pipeline activity into auditable fulfillment evidence. On Day 15, contractor A checks each upcoming event against the ticketing system's transfer availability and the promised delivery deadline. A barcode image is not adequate proof of a valid transfer; retain the platform's recipient confirmation or another contractually accepted delivery record. Day 16 tests repricing with hard floors derived from acquisition cost, expected selling fees, remaining reserve needs, and the approved exit strategy. Do not let a generic dynamic drop curve sell suite seats below the remaining suite obligation without owner acknowledgment. Day 17 tests one disruption tabletop: postponement, cancellation, or supplier failure. Record who contacts the buyer, who freezes listings, and which contract determines refund timing. Day 18 reconciles the earliest available settlement, including withheld amounts and adjustments. If the event has not occurred or payout is not yet due, mark the order as open with its contractual maturity date; never manufacture completion to satisfy the sprint. Days 19-21 review buyer conversion, supplier delivery reliability, and event-level exposure. Keep acquisition decisions with the owner while contractors operate documented checklists. After-hours coverage must name a reachable primary and backup before any event-day inventory remains listed.

Build a compact daily scorecard with denominators that resist vanity metrics. A completed order requires delivered entitlements, an elapsed event where relevant, and reconciled settlement; retain an appropriate reserve for later contractual claims. Count distinct economically independent orders, not split invoices manufactured from one corporate buyer. Track delivered-on-time orders divided by delivered orders, duplicate fills per accepted order, realized contribution after inventory losses, and owner net margin after all pilot overhead. Measure payout aging from the contractually due date, not from the sale date. Report overdue balances as a share of all due settlement balances, and show absolute dollars alongside percentages. Reconciliation variance equals unexplained settlement differences divided by the expected amount due. Report open inventory at acquisition cost with a separate estimated liquidation value; charging a markdown allowance and later recording the same realized loss requires a reserve release to prevent double counting. Allocate shared overhead consistently across plays, while showing the unallocated launch total so weak plays cannot hide inside accounting choices. When no settlement is due, there is no aging observation; the evidence requirement remains unmet. Pipeline quotations, deposits, and transfer confirmations cannot substitute for demonstrated collection.

Use liquidity coverage to decide whether another sale is safe. Calculate available liquidity as unrestricted bank cash less the next thirty days of committed overhead. Exclude undelivered buyer deposits needed for refunds and do not add unsettled marketplace balances. For the stress denominator, estimate incremental cash needed for a 20% markdown on open unsold inventory, full buyer refunds for one affected event before supplier recovery, and the documented replacement remedy on the largest independent open order. Where a refund deposit has already been excluded from the numerator, exclude the matching funded refund amount from the denominator to avoid double reservation. Apply the actual agreement if its remedy exceeds the illustrative replacement shock. This is a planning stress, not a maximum-loss claim. Maintain the separate $7,500 baseline reserve in the illustrative bankroll even when the current modeled denominator is smaller. A zero-exposure ledger does not establish commercial readiness; collection and fulfillment evidence still control expansion. Track capital-days as cash committed multiplied by days until unrestricted recovery. This exposes why a Super Bowl package purchased months ahead may be less deployable than a smaller nearby resale. Release reserves only against documented extinguishment or reduction of the underlying obligations.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Identify the exact obligation
   - Match the accepted order to unique inventory and the governing channel agreement.
   - Record event, seats, buyer entitlements, delivery deadline, selling fee, refund terms, and replacement exposure before acknowledging availability.
2. Hold inventory and verify withdrawals
   - Apply the local inventory hold and capture acknowledgments from every connected selling channel.
   - Target a local hold within two seconds and remote withdrawal within sixty seconds; suspend any channel that cannot reliably meet the selected operating threshold.
3. Deliver and preserve evidence
   - Transfer through the supported ticketing rail and archive recipient confirmation.
   - For suite or hospitality orders, attach the entitlement schedule and supplier confirmations; escalate any missing component before the buyer's promised delivery deadline.
4. Settle and release only earned cash
   - Reconcile actual bank receipts, fees, deductions, and remaining claim exposure.
   - Update contribution, overdue balances, and reserves without duplicating losses; release owner cash only after obligations and the next thirty days of overhead remain funded.

## 4. Days 22-30 and Beyond: Apply Quantitative Kill, Fix, or Scale Decisions  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Days 22-29 assemble a decision packet rather than chase last-minute volume. Reprice stale inventory, confirm remaining supplier commitments, reconcile each bank receipt, and classify every unresolved exception. Compare plays using realized net contribution and capital-days, not quoted spread. The owner conducts a loss review on every markdown, refund, and delivery failure; contractors bring timestamps and documents rather than recollections. On Day 26, rerun the cash stress with current event concentrations and actual contractual remedies. On Day 27, prepare a continuation budget funded only by genuinely available cash. Cumulative overhead ceilings are $3,000 at Day 30, $6,000 at Day 60, and $9,000 at Day 90. These are maximum authorizations, not instructions to spend: the initial $25,000 allocation cannot fund every extension without releasing inventory capital or generating completed contribution. Do not consume protected reserves to meet the calendar. Day 28 documents contractor capacity and coverage gaps. Day 29 freezes the reporting cutoff and distinguishes open events from completed cohorts. Because the canonical first-deal window is one to three months, a clean but immature pilot normally receives Fix with restricted spending, not an invented Scale result or an automatic commercial failure verdict.

At each checkpoint, apply safety stops first, evidence minimums second, and economic thresholds third. Kill the affected play immediately for fabricated supply, prohibited acquisition methods, an unresolved required license, or inability to fund obligations; stop new sales while fulfilling or refunding existing contracts. A double fill triggers an immediate channel freeze and incident review, even when the overall business remains viable. With adequate completed evidence, Kill thresholds are owner net margin at or below negative 10% on Day 30, negative 5% on Day 60, or zero on Day 90. Evidence minimums are ten independent completed orders across two events, twenty-five across four events, and fifty across six events respectively. Below those minimums, choose Fix without increasing capital; at Day 90, stop paid acquisition if there are no signed orders after sixty qualified buyer conversations and twenty written quotes. Scale requires all checkpoint conditions simultaneously, including net margins of at least 12%, 15%, and 19% respectively. Require at least 99% on-time delivery and zero double fills in the evaluation window. Between Kill and Scale lies Fix: assign one measurable defect, an owner, and a fourteen-day retest. No unresolved safety defect may be averaged away by stronger sales.

Scale is a controlled capital increment, not permission to activate all twelve plays. At Day 30, require liquidity coverage of at least 1.25 times the modeled stress, overdue settlement balances no greater than 5% of due balances, and unexplained reconciliation variance no greater than 1%. Day 60 tightens coverage to 1.50 times, overdue balances to 2%, and variance to 0.5%; Day 90 retains 1.50 times coverage while tightening overdue balances to 1% and retaining 0.5% variance. All checkpoints preserve the baseline reserve, funded overhead, and twenty-percent single-event capital cap. A passing checkpoint permits at most a 25% increase in acquisition exposure for the proven lane; add a second play only after that increment clears a complete settlement cycle. Keep the launch pod at one full-time owner and two contractors until documented exception volume justifies staffing expansion. The canonical Scaled case requires two full-time people plus three contractors, not the original three-person workload stretched indefinitely. Compare annual forecasts with the exact scorecard only after accounting for event seasonality, holiday concentration, and cash duration. A profitable Final Four weekend cannot simply be multiplied by fifty-two. Publish the decision, released capital, retained reserves, corrective actions, and next checkpoint in one dated operating memo.
