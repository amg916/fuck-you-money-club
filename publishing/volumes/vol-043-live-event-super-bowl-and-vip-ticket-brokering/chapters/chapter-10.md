## Executive Summary

Practitioners converge on one line: reselling, even at steep markups, is broadly legal, but defeating a posted purchase limit is what the 2016 BOTS Act punishes, with civil penalties now above $53,000 per violation. Enforcement sat idle until January 2021, when three New York brokers drew a $31 million judgment and paid about $3.7 million. The new wave targets account armies, not just software: Key Investment Group allegedly used 49 accounts for 273 Eras Tour tickets against a six-ticket cap, and REPS allegedly ran up to 40 accounts per name, proxies, multi-session browsers and an error bible to spend $1.3 billion and clear $120.8 million. The live fight is whether real humans on real accounts count as circumvention, while California's AB 1720 debate splits venues and artists against StubHub and a fraud criminologist over 10% resale caps.

## The Practitioner Consensus

The baseline every serious voice on the docket accepts is that the BOTS Act is narrower than its name. Hustle Audit reads the 2016 statute as doing exactly two things: banning circumvention of any security measure, access control or technological control a seller uses to enforce posted purchase limits at public events above 200 capacity, and banning resale of tickets obtained that way by anyone who took part, controlled it or should have known [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:00:49]. The same audit stresses what the law leaves alone: it does not ban reselling, flipping for profit, or buying many tickets by hand within the posted limit, and it does not cap price, with only around 15 states regulating markups, New York capping near 10% over face and California requiring a license to sell above face [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:01:40]. Dot Detective frames the rule in plain English as a ban on getting around per-customer caps, account checks and buying-order rules, enforced by the FTC as an unfair or deceptive practice with civil penalties attached [7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:00:49]. Strat Americas reporting compresses it further: the law makes it illegal to use software to evade online ticket limits or restrictions [d4DslgcIQ7Y @ 00:00:35]. The operator takeaway is blunt: the resale is legal, the limit-beating is the exposure.

Practitioners also agree the enforcement drought is over, that politics is now pushing it, and that the target has shifted from pure software to the account armies brokers build to multiply a single buyer. Hustle Audit documents the gap: signed in late 2016, the act produced zero FTC cases until January 2021, when three New York brokers accused of grabbing more than 150,000 tickets drew a combined judgment above $31 million, mostly suspended on inability to pay, with about $3.7 million actually collected [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:03:29]. The August 2025 Key Investment Group case moved the line: NBC News reports the FTC alleges fake accounts and different credit card accounts bought more than 370,000 Ticketmaster tickets, and legal analyst Danny Cevallos says the case turns on one discovery demand, 'show me the human' [BOIfCHick7o @ 00:02:25]. Dot Detective lays out the parallel REPS fight, where president Brian Archibald insists the company used real people on real accounts and never bots, while the government argues the law covers getting around limits however it is done [7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:02:44]. The consensus warning from Hustle Audit is that whether 49 accounts and 273 tickets count as circumvention without a bot will define how wide the grey zone really is [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:05:45].

The third shared view is that the platform sits inside the problem, not outside it. The Fine Print Audit cites the FTC complaint alleging a senior Ticketmaster executive wrote that the company turned a blind eye as a matter of policy to brokers breaking limits, that one review found five brokers controlling 6,345 accounts holding 246,000 tickets to 2,594 events, and that in 2021 it passed on third-party identity verification because it was 'too effective' [SdsGfL0IdKk @ 00:04:05]. Attorney Domenic Romano argues Ticketmaster profits when tickets are sold, resold and resold again by the very scalpers it blames, calling that the behavior its vertical integration encourages [nQoLincg1zk @ 00:01:16]. NBC News puts the exposure at $53,000 per violation across what it calls hundreds of thousands, potentially millions, of violations [7SO1FTTFaBM @ 00:01:50]. Even the organized broker lobby lines up against software: Gary Adler of the National Association of Ticket Brokers, which counts over 200 professional resellers, says the association's members have long opposed programs that scoop up all the tickets because members cannot compete to buy either, and blames scarcity on how few seats reach the public [111H45dtJsI @ 00:09:18]. Anti-bot is the rare position that brokers and their loudest critics share.

## Exact Street Mechanics, Tools & Numbers

The fullest alleged playbook on the record is REPS and Company of San Diego. Dot Detective walks through the DOJ complaint: a buyer team mostly in the Philippines, power of attorney agreements letting the company open ticket accounts in employees' and partners' names, those names paired with company-controlled email addresses, phone numbers and virtual credit cards, and as many as 40 accounts under one person's name, 19 on average [7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:01:19]. Proxxxymiron, a proxy vendor, adds the masking layer: VPNs, rotating proxies and multi-session browsers to look like thousands of independent buyers, plus an internal error bible of Ticketmaster error messages and bypass instructions so staff could keep buying without interruption [6xTWnxPdGP0 @ 00:00:51]. When a new AXS verification system slowed purchases by around 40%, REPS allegedly answered by assigning a permanent proxy to each account to cut blocks [6xTWnxPdGP0 @ 00:01:25]. Randy Nichols told California lawmakers this tooling is an industry, citing proprietary bots and virtual credit card generators openly on display at broker conferences, which Decibel & Docket host Dave Brooks glossed as IP blockers and multiple Ticketmaster sessions in one browser built to defeat purchase limits [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:18:53]. Every layer of the REPS stack is now an allegation in federal court.

The numbers attached to these operations show the scale of the prize. Dot Detective reports the government's claim that since 2022 REPS spent more than $1.3 billion on over 18.5 million tickets for at least $120.8 million in profit, bought more than eight tickets for at least 83,500 events, and for one Beyoncé Renaissance show at Arrowhead Stadium in October 2023 used at least 1,300 accounts for 7,624 tickets and about $833,000 in profit, with a 2024 Pitbull show taking roughly 700 accounts and nearly a third of the venue [7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:02:07]. Hustle Audit puts Key Investment Group, trading as Epic Seats, at nearly 380,000 tickets between late 2022 and the end of 2023, roughly $57 million spent and about $64 million in resale [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:05:07]. Hidden Price shows the pattern is old: New York's attorney general found one broker bought 1,012 tickets to a single U2 show at Madison Square Garden in one minute on December 8, 2014, and six studied brokers marked up an estimated 49% on average, sometimes past 1,000% [pfp0vvw1nXo @ 00:03:03]. Assemblymember Matt Haney told the AB 1720 hearing that over 90% of resale tickets come from professionals and fans pay on average over 200% above face [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:09:11].

On the sell side, the mechanics that matter are the platform fee split, the payout spread and the all-in display rule that now governs every listing. The Fine Print Audit walks a Ticket News resale example: a Bruno Mars seat listed at $299 showed buyers $422.45, while the seller's projected payout was $254.15 after a $44.85 seller fee, which is 15%, leaving a $168.30 spread between seller and buyer, and under the FTC rule the buyer must see $422.45 first, not $299 [SdsGfL0IdKk @ 00:06:27]. Consumer Context explains that since May 12, 2025 the rule makes resale marketplaces hand sellers the fee data needed to calculate the total, requires sellers to give accurate fee-inclusive prices back, and treats a speculative listing for seats to a sold-out concert the seller does not hold as not 'available' at all [L3_AaKpmtQI @ 00:05:47]. Hidden Price notes a resale company is generally paid a service charge by both seller and buyer, and that StubHub agreed in April 2026 to pay ten million dollars to settle FTC charges that it advertised prices without clearly showing mandatory fees [pfp0vvw1nXo @ 00:07:35]. Bait pricing is no longer a growth hack; it is a line item in a settlement.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Check The Event Against The Statute
   - Confirm the event capacity is above 200 and log the posted per-customer limit before any buy.
   - The BOTS Act only reaches public events above 200 capacity and only bites when posted purchase limits are circumvented; manual buys within the limit sit in the grey zone [Hustle Audit — NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:00:49].
2. Audit Every Buying Account For A Real Human
   - Map each account to the actual person behind it and count accounts per name per event.
   - DOJ pleads up to 40 accounts per name and 1,300 accounts on one Beyoncé show against an eight-ticket cap, while REPS argues real people on real accounts are lawful; that fight is unresolved [Dot Detective — 7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:01:35].
3. Publish All-In Prices With Named Fees
   - Show the fee-inclusive total first and label each fee by what it actually pays for.
   - Since May 12, 2025, vague labels like convenience, service or processing fee are out, and padding a 'taxes and fees' line with seller profit is flagged by FTC guidance [Consumer Context — L3_AaKpmtQI @ 00:04:45].
4. Map State Resale Rules Before Listing
   - Check markup caps, licensing and transfer restrictions in each state where seats are sold.
   - AB 1720 would cap concert resale at 10% over the all-in price [Decibel & Docket — 2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:01:04], and Massachusetts now lets platforms require resale on the original platform once the policy is disclosed [CBS Boston — Wtsvx9NdMgc @ 00:01:54].

> **INSIDER PRO-TIPS — What The Courtroom Fights Reveal About The Line**
> 
> The live cases are teaching brokers where the government will push: identity, account counts per event, and the price a buyer sees first.
> - Treat 'show me the human' as the discovery test: if an account cannot be tied to a specific real person, analyst Danny Cevallos says the government has gone a long way to proving its case [NBC News — BOIfCHick7o @ 00:02:25].

> - Back every order the way NATB members must: a full refund when a canceled event is refunded by the promoter, and a 200% guarantee if a promised ticket is never delivered [BBB National Programs — 111H45dtJsI @ 00:07:47].

## Section 3: Where Practitioners Disagree & Alternative Playbooks

The sharpest split in the resale game is over what the BOTS Act actually reaches. Hustle Audit reads the 2016 statute narrowly: it is aimed at one tool, the automated software that defeats a purchase limit, and it does not ban reselling, flipping for profit, or buying many tickets by hand within the posted limit [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:01:24]. The brokers in the crosshairs run exactly that defense. Key Investment Group, trading as Epic Seats, argues it used no automated software at all, even though the FTC counts 49 separate accounts buying 273 Eras Tour tickets against a posted limit of six [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:05:27]. Reps president Brian Archibald runs the same line for the San Diego shop: real people using real accounts, never bots, and the FTC theory 'is not the law' [7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:02:29]. The government position is that the statute covers getting around the limits however it is done. NBC News legal analyst Danny Cevallos says discovery should make this easy to prove: was this purchase from a human? 'Well, show me the human.' If a broker cannot tie each account to a specific person, Cevallos argues, the government has gone a long way to proving its allegations [BOIfCHick7o @ 00:02:25]. Whichever side wins draws the real legal line for every multi-account buyer.

Price caps split the room harder than bots. At the California AB 1720 hearing, Assemblymember Matt Haney pitched capping concert resale at 10% above what the seller paid including fees, arguing that over 90% of resale tickets come from professional scalpers who often use bots, and that fans pay on average over 200% above face value [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:09:11]. Decibel & Docket host Dave Brooks paused the feed to note that professional resellers argue they play an arbitrage role in the ticketing ecosystem and mark tickets down as well as up, while conceding not everyone agrees they belong there [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:07:24]. UK criminologist Dr. Nicola Harding testified that where caps exist there is a fourfold increase in ticket fraud, because demand displaces to social media and private groups with no money-back guarantees; three in five social-media tickets the research team tried to buy were scams, and Ireland has prosecuted no scalpers since its cap took hold [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:26:25]. Hustle Audit sketches the patchwork brokers already operate inside today: only around 15 states regulate resale price at all, New York caps a markup near 10% over face, California requires a license to sell above face, and the rest of the country mostly leaves price alone [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:01:58].

The blame map splits too. Romano Law founder Domenic Romano says the Ticketmaster line of 'it's not us, it's the scalpers' collapses because the company makes money when tickets are sold, resold, and then resold again by the very scalpers it claims are the problem, behavior its vertical integration encourages [nQoLincg1zk @ 00:01:01]. The Fine Print Audit lays out the FTC allegation in hard numbers: a senior executive wrote that the company turned a blind eye as a matter of policy to brokers breaking ticket limits, one review found five brokers controlling 6,345 accounts holding 246,000 tickets to 2,594 events, and in 2021 the company passed on third-party identity verification because it was 'too effective' [SdsGfL0IdKk @ 00:04:05]. NATB executive director Gary Adler pitches the professional broker trade as anti-bot, arguing bot software hurts members because they cannot compete to buy tickets, and that brokers get blamed for scarcity when so few tickets are made available to the public [111H45dtJsI @ 00:09:18]. Hidden Price backs the scarcity point with New York attorney general data: only about 46% of tickets at top-grossing shows reached the general public, 16% were held for insiders and 38% went to presales. That same probe caught one broker buying 1,012 tickets to a single U2 show at Madison Square Garden in one minute [pfp0vvw1nXo @ 00:02:40].

## Section 4: Firsthand Failure Modes, Traps & Economics Checks

The first economics check is penalty exposure, and it compounds per ticket. Hustle Audit notes BOTS Act violations are treated as unfair or deceptive acts carrying civil penalties now above $53,000 each; the first 2021 sweep against three New York brokers who grabbed more than 150,000 tickets produced a combined judgment above $31 million, but because the brokers claimed they could not pay, about $3.7 million was actually collected [NOBp14Qcy_8 @ 00:03:29]. NBC News correspondent Marquis Francis frames the current scale against Ticketmaster: hundreds of thousands, potentially millions of violations, each carrying over $50,000 in fines [7SO1FTTFaBM @ 00:01:50]. The Reps case shows where industrial volume leads. Dot Detective reports the DOJ alleges Reps ran a buying team mostly in the Philippines under power-of-attorney agreements, paired names with company emails, phones and virtual credit cards at up to 40 accounts per person and 19 on average, and kept an 'error bible' for working around limit messages [7SSPFtppfGE @ 00:01:19]. Proxxxymiron adds that when a new AXS verification slowed purchases by around 40%, Reps reportedly assigned a permanent proxy to each account to cut blocks, and the operation ended in a lawsuit filed on an FTC referral, with the defendants not yet found liable [6xTWnxPdGP0 @ 00:01:25]. Every workaround became a line item in the complaint.

The second killer is selling inventory you do not hold yet. 9NEWS consumer investigator Steve Steger tracked Ticket Secrets LLC, a Colorado broker with ten years of business that sold discounted seats guaranteed only 24 hours before the event; more than 600 buyers in a Facebook group now say they are owed $143,000 [rhYfYkzJGmU @ 00:01:50]. The owner's attorney described a decades-old model of waiting until the last minute to snag discounted seats, busted by two unexpectedly popular Mile High Stadium runs, Zach Bryan and Morgan Wallen, where prices went up and never came down; the company is headed to bankruptcy with its money frozen [rhYfYkzJGmU @ 00:02:27]. Artist manager Randy Nichols described the same failure at festival scale: Coachella short sellers could not cover their shorts and fans were left unable to enter the venue, which Dave Brooks explained as sellers ditching orders once Bieber demand pushed prices up instead of eating the loss [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:19:53]. Dr. Nicola Harding covers the fraud end of the same spectrum, relaying that fraudsters she interviewed price under market because they can sell a ticket they never actually held 10, 15, 20 times over, leaving fans who paid but never get in the room [2Vb_eyHh2aw @ 00:29:45].

The third trap is the refund and disclosure layer. The Current reports that when the concert world shut down, fans holding Rolling Stones and Elton John tickets learned the gap between 'canceled' and 'postponed indefinitely' could mean thousands of dollars: StubHub admitted changing its refund policy under the circumstances, while Ticketmaster declined many refunds and said it had merely clarified its language [9rdi7Mk3v-Y @ 00:00:40]. Gary Adler's NATB code sets the broker-side baseline for keeping customers: if the promoter refunds a canceled event, members must refund in full, and a promised ticket that never arrives triggers a 200% payout so there is an incentive to not do the harm [111H45dtJsI @ 00:07:28]. Hidden Price flags the disclosure penalty on the platform side: since May 12, 2025 the FTC rule requires live-event sellers to show the total price with mandatory fees upfront, and in April 2026 StubHub agreed to pay $10 million to settle charges it advertised prices without clearly showing those fees [pfp0vvw1nXo @ 00:07:35]. Consumer Context adds that the rule reaches resale marketplaces directly: platforms must tell sellers exactly which fees get passed on, sellers must give accurate fee-inclusive prices, and a speculative seller advertising seats to a sold-out concert it does not actually have is not offering available tickets [L3_AaKpmtQI @ 00:05:29].
