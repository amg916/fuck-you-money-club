## 1. Define the revenue denominator before buying the inventory  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Start every cohort with a documented revenue denominator, not the balance displayed in an exchange dashboard. Formula v2.0 supplies $220,000, $1,010,000, and $1,800,000 of annual gross volume and a 40% retained share after ticket or suite acquisition. This chapter normalizes that volume to seller-attributable billings before seller fees, excluding taxes collected for government and exchange buyer fees never earned by the broker. If an exchange statement reports only a net payout, add back its separately identified seller fee before using this waterfall; never subtract that fee twice. The resulting retained amounts are $88,000, $404,000, and $720,000. ASC 606 principal-versus-agent analysis still depends on control of the specified ticket or hospitality service before transfer. A 40% spreadsheet assumption does not establish agency or automatically determine reported revenue. Maintain the scorecard's retained-contribution view alongside the actual accounting presentation, with a reconciliation for supplier pass-throughs, recognized commissions, and customer liabilities. Do not call underlying event attendance spend, corporate travel budgets, or stadium hospitality GMV operator revenue. Allocate mixed packages to their distinct promised services and document when each obligation is satisfied. This prevents advance deposits from inflating both revenue and apparent purchasing capacity.

Separate three businesses before combining their results. Exchange resale buys transferable ticket rights, carries mark-to-market inventory risk, and often waits until after the event for settlement. Suite seat-splitting commits to a block, then distributes permitted individual admissions while sharing catering, host, and minimum-spend obligations. Corporate hospitality assembles admissions and specified services under customer and supplier contracts with different cancellation clocks. For chapter mapping, the 12 operating lanes are multi-exchange drop-curve resale; NFL playoff-option strips; NBA playoff-option strips; regular-season suite splitting; playoff suite splitting; Super Bowl hospitality; World Cup hospitality; F1 hospitality; Masters hospitality; Final Four hospitality; authorized sponsor-allotment redistribution; and retained corporate ticket sourcing. These are the chapter's operating decomposition, not twelve identically funded inventory strategies. A $10,000 resale purchase may be entirely prepaid, a $10,000 suite may require staged deposits, and a $10,000 sourcing engagement may expose only a commission receivable if the customer contracts directly with suppliers. Authenticate allocation rights and redistribution permissions before assigning value to any sponsor offer. Require event-specific proof for claimed official hospitality status. For playoff strips, record which games exist conditionally, who receives refunds for unplayed games, and whether those refunds arrive before the next supplier payment.

The required play, multi_exchange_pos_broadcast_drop_curve_arbitrage, uses supported, authorized SkyBox or Automatiq integrations, with Logitix as another evaluated POS option. Verify actual account and inventory eligibility for StubHub, Vivid Seats, SeatGeek, TickPick, GameTime, and Ticketmaster Resale rather than assuming universal connectivity. A Base-equivalent cohort of twenty fulfilled tickets billed at $505 each produces $10,100 gross and carries $6,060 of sold-ticket acquisition cost. Deduct $707 seller fees, $252.50 incremental inventory impairment, $151.50 expected reversal losses, $360 delivery labor, $180 acquisition CAC, $360 allocated overhead, and $110 financing to retain $1,919, or $95.95 per fulfilled ticket. The impairment allocation covers separate carried inventory losses, not a second charge for markdowns already embedded in the $505 realized price. Reprice against net realizable proceeds, considering fees and transfer deadlines rather than headline asks. Trigger an immediate internal hold when an order arrives and verify remote delisting acknowledgments. These controls reduce duplicate sales without eliminating race conditions. If synchronization fails, pause affected broadcasting, remove residual listings through authorized account interfaces, notify exchanges promptly, and resolve obligations through permitted equivalent replacements or the required cancellation process.

## 2. Reconcile every dollar from gross volume to owner profit  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

The annual waterfall below preserves every canonical Formula v2.0 endpoint. Its intermediate expense allocations are explicit underwriting assumptions, not additional outputs supplied by the scorecard engine. Acquisition consumes 60% of gross volume in each case. Remaining deductions total $61,600 Conservative, $212,100 Base, and $252,000 Scaled, leaving exactly $26,400, $191,900, and $468,000 of owner profit. Gross-basis margins are 12%, 19.0%, and 26%; dividing the same profits by retained revenue produces 30%, 47.5%, and 65%. Weighted seller fees decline from 8% to 7% to 4% because the illustrative mix shifts toward direct suite and hospitality contracts; these are portfolio assumptions, not advertised marketplace rates. The Scaled case requires evidence for that mix change before adoption. Inventory impairment is 3%, 2.5%, and 1% of gross volume; reversal expense is 2%, 1.5%, and 0.5%. Smaller loss ratios require better contract matching and underwriting, not simply more listings. Actual ledger results must replace these allocations monthly while preserving the distinction between original underwriting, revised forecast, and realized economics. Any variance becomes a named bridge item rather than an unexplained adjustment to retained share.

Labor must include the work that automated broadcasting cannot finish: supplier verification, exception handling, customer coordination, transfer chasing, invoice reconciliation, and event-day escalation. Conservative delivery labor assigns $12,000 across two contractors; Base assigns $36,000 across two contractors. Scaled assigns $30,000 across three contractors plus $54,000 of fully loaded nonowner employee expense, totaling $84,000. Those are annual budgets requiring role-specific hours and local pay validation, not asserted market wage schedules. The owner supplies the remaining full-time role in each case, so owner profit includes compensation for their work rather than profit after a separate owner replacement salary. Team counts therefore remain 1 FT + 2 C, 1 FT + 2 C, and 2 FT + 3 C. Acquisition CAC means customer acquisition expenditure, separate from ticket acquisition: outreach, paid lead generation, referral costs, and sales effort purchased externally. Combine delivery labor, CAC, and fixed overhead to obtain monthly operating budgets of $2,500, $7,500, and $11,000. Exchange fees, loss provisions, and financing remain separate variable or capital costs. Never describe that monthly operating budget as the entire cash requirement for running the brokerage.

Use product-level waterfalls to test whether the annual blend is credible. A suite costs $6,000 for twenty permitted admissions. Selling eighteen at $500 generates $9,000; the two empty seats already reduce billings from the $10,000 sellout plan. Deduct $180 collection fees, $180 reversal provision, $450 fulfillment labor, $150 CAC, $300 overhead, and $200 financing, leaving $1,540. Do not subtract another $1,000 for those empty seats. Selling only seventeen seats reduces profit to $1,060 when fees and reversal provision remain 2% each and other costs stay fixed. A fully sold hospitality cohort of four $5,000 packages has $12,000 supplier cost, $400 collection fees, $600 expected reversal expense, $1,200 delivery labor, $600 CAC, $500 overhead, and $500 financing, leaving $4,200. Its 21% margin does not establish the portfolio's 26% Scaled margin; other cohorts must supply the difference. For either product, document access, hosting, transport, and catering obligations independently. Customer deposits remain liabilities until the relevant performance occurs. An unused refundable supplier deposit remains an asset until recovered or impaired, not an instant expense or a second source of earned spread.

## 3. Reserve for failed economics, then stress the assumptions  `🏷️ ILLUSTRATIVE_MODEL`

Maintain separate loss and liquidity ledgers. Expected economic losses reduce profit; a cash reserve or processor hold restricts availability without automatically creating another expense. Build a reversal register for canceled events, postponed events, invalid inventory, rejected delivery, customer disputes, and duplicate-sale replacements. For each exposure, record gross refund liability, expected supplier recovery, unrecoverable fees, replacement cost, and the timing of each movement. A $5,000 customer refund with a collectible $4,000 supplier reimbursement creates $1,000 expected economic loss before incremental expenses, but may require the full $5,000 immediately. A postponement can extend the payout lock without producing a refund entitlement; apply the actual customer and marketplace terms. For cohort forecasting, estimate loss probability multiplied by net loss severity, then add concentration stress for correlated event failures. Reconcile provisions to actual charges and recoveries, releasing excess amounts only when the underlying uncertainty resolves. In financial reporting, classify each item according to its substance rather than treating every forecast allowance as an interchangeable reserve. Never count both a reduced selling price and a separate markdown expense for the same seat. Likewise, track supplier recovery rights without assuming disputed receivables are usable cash.

Validate any claimed 200% double-sale replacement penalty against the applicable exchange agreement, including its calculation basis, alternatives, caps, and additional charges. If a contract actually permits a charge equal to 200% of a $500 sale price, the stated charge is $1,000; that is not proof that every duplicate costs $1,000 or that the charge includes every other liability. Ten such charges would consume $10,000 before any separately payable refunds or sourcing costs. Retain order timestamps, POS hold timestamps, listing identifiers, delisting responses, and issuer transfer acceptance records so the operator can reconstruct the failure without speculation. Under 15 U.S.C. §45c, do not circumvent purchase limits or ticketing security measures; automated distribution of legitimately acquired inventory is operationally different from prohibited acquisition circumvention. SafeTix fulfillment must follow issuer-supported transfer and wallet flows rather than copied rotating barcodes. Apply 16 CFR Part 464 to covered live-event ticket offers, including required upfront total pricing and accurate fee representations. Check New York Arts and Cultural Affairs Law Article 25 for applicable reseller obligations. These requirements belong in cost and delivery design because a nontransferable allocation or incorrectly quoted price can erase an otherwise attractive modeled spread.

Run sensitivities against all three canonical cases using the same denominator and clearly stated cost behavior. A two-percentage-point increase in seller fees removes $4,400, $20,200, and $36,000 of profit respectively. An additional three points of incremental inventory loss removes $6,600, $30,300, and $54,000. Raising reversal losses one point removes another $2,200, $10,100, and $18,000. Applying all three stresses together leaves $13,200, $131,300, and $360,000, before any additional settlement financing. A ten-day persistent extension in the inventory funding cycle requires approximately $3,616, $16,603, and $29,589 more average capital when measured against annual sold-ticket acquisition cost. At a 12% annual borrowing rate, that adds approximately $434, $1,992, and $3,551 of annual interest. This interest is an incremental stress, not a replacement for the existing financing line. Test revenue contraction separately: if volume falls 10%, acquisition and seller fees flex proportionally while all other budget lines stay fixed, resulting profits are $19,360, $158,570, and $403,200. Finally, run event-specific cliff tests; a portfolio average cannot represent losing the only hospitality allocation that supports several prepaid corporate orders.

## 4. Fund the payout lock without confusing profit with cash  `🏷️ OPERATING_FRAMEWORK`

Build a dated cash calendar for every inventory lot and consolidate it into a rolling thirteen-week forecast. Record supplier deposit, supplier balance, customer order, customer collection, event date, transfer deadline, exchange release, bank availability, and possible refund dates. These dates answer different questions. Customer DSO measures collection after the relevant invoice or receivable trigger; a marketplace's post-event payout lock is an additional contractual restriction and should remain separately visible. Buying at day minus sixty and receiving cleared payout at day plus ten creates seventy days of inventory funding even if the customer ordered immediately. Financing $6,060 for seventy days at 12% costs $139.46 using a 365-day year. That exceeds the earlier cohort's $110 allocated financing by $29.46 and reduces its $1,919 profit to $1,889.54 if all other lines remain unchanged. The comparison illustrates why portfolio financing allocations must be reconciled to actual cohort duration. Enter bank-clearing time and business-day release rules explicitly rather than calling every promised payout date available cash. For postponed events, extend the inventory lock and recalculate interest before initiating another buying cycle, even when the original ticket remains valid.

A useful first approximation for steady-state acquisition float is annual acquisition cash cost multiplied by funded days and divided by 365, adjusted for supplier credit and genuinely usable customer collections. At seventy funded days, the Base sold-ticket basis of $606,000 implies $116,219 of acquisition float before unsold inventory, payroll, loss coverage, or seasonal concentration. This is a planning approximation, not the peak cash forecast. The $10,000–$50,000 startup capital range can support a narrow initial book, particularly with short settlement cycles or properly matched customer deposits; it does not finance unrestricted ownership of the Base or Scaled inventory portfolio. Baseline annual interest assumes average funded borrowings of $25,000, approximately $91,667, and $175,000 at 12%. Equity, supplier terms, and permissible customer funding must cover the rest, while committed facilities must accommodate seasonal peaks above those averages. A bank line advertised at $150,000 is not fully available if borrowing-base exclusions or existing draws consume capacity. Keep restricted processor balances, disputed receivables, and refundable customer money out of unrestricted liquidity. Calculate minimum required cash from the worst cumulative dated deficit plus the selected refund shock buffer, not from a percentage of annual profit.

Corporate hospitality can shorten cash gaps only when customer and supplier contracts actually align. On the $20,000 package cohort, an illustrative 50% customer deposit collects $10,000 while a 50% supplier deposit consumes $6,000. The apparent $4,000 surplus remains subject to performance and refund obligations. If the remaining $6,000 supplier balance is due before the final $10,000 customer collection, the cohort reaches a $2,000 acquisition deficit before labor, overhead, and risk coverage. If the customer instead pays thirty days after the event, that receivable persists beyond delivery. Do not finance unrelated events with refundable deposits while ignoring the corresponding refund claim. Set exposure limits by event, supplier, exchange, and corporate customer, then stop new purchases when the cash calendar breaches the available funding envelope. Close each event by matching admissions delivered, exchange statements, customer invoices, supplier credits, contractor time, and bank receipts. Release reserves according to resolved exposures, not because the event date passed. Publish both earned owner profit and distributable cash: debt repayment, inventory replenishment, restricted reserves, and tax funding can make the second materially smaller. This is live-event inventory and service settlement management, not the wholesale electronics buying-group economics addressed in Volume 041.

**SOP — Standard Operating Procedure**

1. Create the inventory-lot economics record
   - Assign event, supplier, rights, channel, acquisition basis, and contractual delivery identifiers before committing capital.
   - Store gross seller-attributable billings, expected fees, realized-price assumptions, incremental inventory losses, and fulfillment obligations in separate fields.
2. Build the dated funding bridge
   - Schedule supplier payments, customer collections, exchange holds, bank clearing, and refund exposures for the full cohort.
   - Calculate peak cumulative deficit, available unrestricted cash, remaining committed borrowing, and the financing expense implied by actual funded days.
3. Execute delivery with verified inventory locks
   - Apply internal POS auto-holds, verify remote delisting, and complete issuer-supported transfer with acceptance evidence.
   - When synchronization fails, pause affected inventory, remove residual listings through authorized interfaces, contact exchanges, and resolve duplicate obligations under the applicable contracts.
4. Close the event and release only resolved exposure
   - Reconcile supplier invoices, customer billings, exchange deductions, contractor time, recoveries, and cleared bank receipts.
   - Bridge forecast profit to actual owner profit, update loss estimates, and calculate distributable cash after replenishment, debt service, reserves, and tax funding.
